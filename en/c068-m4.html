Level 2 · C068·M4

Updating after an observation

Your goalUpdate the beta distribution of the observed arm only, then check that each outcome has been counted exactly once.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

At a fictional printer repair bench, you compare 2 procedures: A restarts the print queue, B restores the connection. Each trial starts with a new simulated fault of the same type. The criterion is fixed before the choice: the test page prints at the end of the planned procedure, or the test ends without that page. No real device is handled in this course.

Each procedure is an arm, meaning an option you can try. It has its own belief record. This record summarizes what the model assigns to its probability of success; it does not measure a certain property of the printer. A has just been chosen using the Thompson sampling studied earlier. We begin when its outcome arrives.

The test of A ends with a test page: this is an observed success. We then increase the weight on the success side of its record. B has not been tested; its record receives nothing. Leaving B unchosen does not mean it has failed.

The parameters and outcomes are entirely fictional, chosen for a calculation by hand. The diagram shows how to maintain the records. We will then explain why adding this outcome turns a beta distribution into another beta distribution.

The rule to remember

Wait for an interpretable outcome, associate it with the correct trial and arm, then incorporate it exactly once. A choice, a random draw and a missing result are not observations of success or failure.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The success of A changes its belief

Before this new trial, the independent beliefs are beta(2, 3) for A and beta(3, 2) for B. These fictional priors are chosen with no trials already incorporated: n = 0 for each arm. A has been chosen; its completed test gives y = 1. Let us calculate the state to retain for the next choice.

  1. Define the outcome and assumptions

    θ, the letter theta, denotes the unknown probability of success of A, between 0 and 1. Y denotes the random outcome of its next test; y is the value actually observed. We encode success as y = 1 and failure as y = 0. These numbers have no physical unit. A test that is still pending has no value y.

    Conditional on θ, the trials of A are assumed independent with the same probability of success: this is the Bernoulli model. The fault type and criterion remain fixed. The arm probabilities have independent priors here; no shared parameter allows the outcome of A to inform B. The choice may use past outcomes, but it provides no additional knowledge of the future outcome once that information is fixed.

    α and β are the positive parameters of the beta distribution of θ before this result. They are pseudocount weights, with no physical unit: α on the success side, β on the failure side. Their sum c = α + β is the total weight. It does not count the trials actually performed. The counter n separately counts distinct outcomes incorporated since the prior. Here α = 2, β = 3 and c = 5, despite n = 0.

  2. Understand what the outcome contributes

    f is the belief density over θ; its subscript avant means “before”. L is the likelihood: for a possible value of θ, it gives the probability of obtaining the outcome y. The symbol ∝ means “proportional to”, with a factor that does not depend on θ. In the interior of the interval, the beta distribution and the Bernoulli outcome are written as follows:

    favant(θ)∝θα−1(1−θ)β−1
    L(θ)=θy(1−θ)1−y

    For a success, L(θ) = θ: this outcome favors high values of θ without eliminating all the others. For a failure, L(θ) = 1 − θ. Bayes’ rule multiplies the density before the result by this likelihood, then adjusts the factor to obtain a probability density. This requires an observation, rather than the random value used to choose A.

  3. Derive the update rule

    When the expressions are multiplied, the exponents of θ add: α − 1 + y. So do those of (1 − θ): β − 1 + (1 − y). We recognize a new beta distribution. The prime ′ means “after incorporating this outcome”; it does not indicate a derivative.

    α′=α+y;β′=β+(1−y)

    The distribution after the observation is therefore beta(α′, β′). The counter becomes n′ = n + 1. Whatever the binary outcome, y + (1 − y) = 1: the weight c′ must therefore equal c + 1. This check does not remove the need to verify that the outcome had not already been incorporated.

  4. Apply the success to the correct arm

    The outcome of A is y = 1. The complement is 1 − 1 = 0. Let us add these contributions to the parameters of A:

    α′=2+1=3;β′=3+0=3

    The record for A becomes beta(3, 3), with n′ = 0 + 1 = 1 incorporated outcome. Weight check: 3 + 3 = 6, equal to 5 + 1. The parameter β does not decrease after a success: it remains 3.

  5. Calculate the mean, then check it another way

    μ, the letter mu, is the mean of θ under the beta distribution, with no unit: μ = α/(α + β). Before the result, μ = 2/5 = 2/5 = 0.40. After the result:

    μ′=α′α′+β′=36=12=0.50

    Another approach: since cμ = α, we have μ′ = (cμ + y)/(c + 1). Here (5 × 2/5 + 1)/6 = 1/2. Subtracting μ also gives (y − μ)/(c + 1) = (1 − 2/5)/6 = 1/10: the mean does indeed increase by 0.10.

    The mean after a success remains strictly less than 1, since β′ remains positive. It summarizes a belief; 0.50 is neither a measured success rate nor proof that θ equals exactly that value. The parameters and the mean all have no physical unit.

  6. Retain the state without inventing other observations

    B remains beta(3, 2), with weight 5, mean 3/5 = 0.60, and counter n = 0. This invariance follows from the independence between arms assumed here; a model that shares information between procedures would require a different rule.

    For the next Thompson choice, retain the parameters of each distribution, rather than just their mean. The next draw will use the new distribution for A and the unchanged distribution for B. It does not itself constitute a success. Comparison with a control and stopping conditions belong to the next unit.

    In a log, also retain the trial identity and the fact that its outcome has been processed. Reading the same success again does not create an additional trial. If the result is missing, the formula waits: do not replace an unknown value with y = 0.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations. Why does a success add to α without subtracting from β? Why does B remain unchanged? Then reconstruct the weight check and explain why a mean alone is not enough for the next draw.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I drew a high value for A: I can already add a success.”

    The draw serves to make the choice. Only the completed test provides y in the Bernoulli likelihood. Review the sequence of events, then identify when an observation becomes available.

    Review the path from choice to outcome

  • “A failure means adding 0: nothing changes.”

    For y = 0, α stays fixed but 1 − y equals 1: β increases. The total weight also increases, and the mean decreases. Check this branch by starting again from the prior in the exercise on failure.

    Calculate the unfavorable result

  • “With no result for B, I record a failure; if A reports again, I count it again.”

    In our protocol, a missing result is not a binary outcome and a repeated delivery of the same outcome is not a new trial. Check the arm identity, trial identity and processing status before any addition.

    Examine the log with a duplicate and a pending result

Compare with the solution

The mean must decrease: this failure favors lower probabilities of success. We have 1 − 0 = 1; α′ = 2 + 0 = 2, and β′ = 3 + 1 = 4.

The weight becomes 2 + 4 = 6, so the increase is indeed 1. The counter becomes n′ = 1. The mean is 2/6 = 1/3 ≈ 0.3333, with no unit, less than 2/5. B remains beta(3, 2) and its counter remains 0.

A failure therefore contributes information even though its value y is 0. The complement records it in β.

  • Obtain beta(2, 4), μ′ = 1/3 and n′ = 1.
  • Justify the decrease in the mean and the invariance of B.

A success, then a new failure of A

Calculate the final state and its mean. Also reconstruct it by counting successes and failures since the prior. Would reversing their order change this final state?

Hint 1

After the success, the starting point for the new failure is beta(3, 3).

Hint 2

The number of incorporated outcomes adds up; it is distinct from the initial weight 5.

Compare with the solution

The success gives beta(3, 3), n = 1. The new failure then gives α′ = 3 + 0 = 3 and β′ = 3 + 1 = 4. The counter is 1 + 1 = 2.

The sum is 7 and the mean is 3/7 = 3/7 ≈ 0.4286, with no unit. Check using the totals: 2 + 1 success = 3; 3 + 1 failure = 4. The weight is also 5 + 2 = 7.

Reversing the order of these outcomes on A gives the same final distribution: the same counts are added to the same prior. The intermediate means differ, and subsequent adaptive choices could then differ. This invariance concerns only the final state for the arm outcomes fixed here. B has still received no observation.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain beta(3, 4), n = 2 and μ = 3/7.
  • Reconstruct the parameters from cumulative counts, independently of the calculation at each round.
  • Limit order invariance to the same observed outcomes on the same arm.

Hint 1

Group the lines by trial identity before counting. The repeated message still carries essai-rose.

The outcome of essai-lilas concerns B. Start from its initial parameters when the outcome arrives.

Compare with the solution

Before the result for B, only the single success of essai-rose has been incorporated: A is beta(3, 3), n = 1, with mean 1/2. Its second delivery changes nothing. B remains beta(3, 2), n = 0, with mean 3/5. A line announcing a pending result contains no y.

When the verified failure of B arrives, α′ = 3 + 0 = 3, β′ = 2 + 1 = 3. B becomes beta(3, 3), with weight 6, mean 1/2 = 0.50, with no unit, and counter n = 1. A remains in its state after the success.

This diagnosis assumes that trial identities are reliable and outcomes do not contradict each other. Contradictory results would require checking the log before any addition; the Bayesian formula alone does not resolve observation quality.

Check your reasoning

  • Discard the repeated delivery without deleting the single success.
  • Keep B unchanged while its result is pending, then update B only after its observed failure.
  • Separate trial counters from prior weights and explain the need for reliable identities.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How does a binary outcome y transform the parameters α and β of the observed arm?

α′ = α + y and β′ = β + (1 − y), with y = 1 for a success and y = 0 for a failure. Multiplying the beta density by the Bernoulli likelihood gives this new beta distribution.

Recall 2Why does the success of A leave B unchanged in our model?

Only A has provided an outcome and the arm probabilities have independent priors. A model sharing a parameter would require a different update. Leaving B untested assigns it no failure.

Recall 3Which counts should you check after each distinct incorporated outcome?

The weight α + β increases by 1; the counter n of incorporated outcomes also increases by 1, but it excludes the initial prior weight. These checks alone do not detect whether an outcome has been processed twice: you must also track the trial identity.

Recall 4Can a high Thompson draw or a missing result serve as a success or failure?

The draw helps make the choice; it is not the test outcome. In this protocol, a missing result remains unknown. Neither justifies adding a binary observation.

Recall 5What should you retain for the next choice, and what does the mean fail to prove?

Retain α and β for each arm, with the counts and identities needed to avoid duplicates. The mean α/(α + β) summarizes the belief; it is not an actual success rate and does not by itself describe the whole distribution used for the next draw.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the formulas, reconstruct the sequence: choice already made, observation checked and assigned, Bernoulli likelihood, new parameters for the observed arm, weight and trial checks, state retained for the next draw. Also explain where to stop if the result is missing.
