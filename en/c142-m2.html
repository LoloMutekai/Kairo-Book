Level 2 · C142·M2

Cauchy sequences

Your goalChoose an index beyond which all terms are close to one another, and distinguish this proof from a small move between neighboring terms.

Before you start
c014-m5 : invariants · c031-m5 : asymptotes · c046-m5 : approximation error · c069-m5 : congruences and Euclid · c142-m1 : real numbers and bounds

✿01 See the idea

On a graduated scale, a program places a cursor and then refines its position at each iteration. The points get closer together. To say that the sequence of positions is Cauchy, we require more than a tiny final move: all sufficiently late positions must be close to one another, even if their iteration numbers are very far apart.

Imagine a tolerance of 0.10 mm. You must be able to specify a starting index N from which no pair of positions will be separated by that much. The index is the iteration number and has no unit; distance and tolerance are measured in millimeters. The early iterations may be scattered: only indices from N onward are concerned.

Now ask for a tighter tolerance. The starting index may need to be later, but the same promise must hold for every strictly positive tolerance. “Cauchy” names this property of a sequence, rather than a method for calculating the cursor position.

The whole scene is fictional and idealized: the positions are exact real numbers, with no rounding, measurement noise, or limit on the number of iterations. The diagram shows an enlarged band; the calculations will give its width. A short list of displayed points cannot guarantee what will happen later.

The rule to remember

Fix the tolerance, choose an index, then check every pair of subsequent indices using that same choice. Repeat the reasoning for an arbitrarily small tolerance.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Certify that the cursor positions become close

At iteration n, the cursor is placed at xₙ = a − b/n, with a = 10 mm and b = 1 mm. Iterations start at index 1. Let us show that this sequence is Cauchy, then calculate a certificate for ε = 0.10 mm.

  1. State the rule and its units

    xn denotes the position at index n; (xn) denotes the sequence of all these positions. a is a fixed reference point on the scale, b is a strictly positive length, and n is a positive integer. Dividing b by the number of iterations preserves the unit mm. This formula describes prescribed positions, rather than measurements from a real device.

    xn=a−bn

    The sequence is defined for every n ≥ 1. We do not assume that the displayed rounded numbers are the exact values. Fractions are used for reasoning; decimals only help us read the result.

  2. Write the complete promise

    A real sequence is Cauchy if, for every strictly positive tolerance ε, there is an index N such that the distance |xn − xm| is strictly less than ε for all indices n and m at least N. The bars |…| give the absolute value, hence the distance on the scale.

    ∀ε>0,∃N∈ℕ*,∀n,m≥N:|xn−xm|<ε

    ℕ* denotes the positive integers, ∀ means “for every,” and ∃ means “there exists.” ε and the distance are in mm; N, n, and m have no unit. N may depend on ε and the sequence, but must be fixed before n and m are chosen. This definition requires no unknown limit to be specified.

  3. Bound the distance between arbitrary indices

    Temporarily fix an index N and take indices m ≥ n ≥ N. This ordering does not restrict the problem: if n and m come in the other order, swap them, since distance is symmetric. The reference point a cancels in the difference. Since b is positive and m ≥ n, b/m ≤ b/n.

    |xm−xn|=bn−bm≤bn≤bN

    To give an upper bound means to find a quantity at least as large. Subtracting the nonnegative term b/m gives a result no greater than b/n. Then n ≥ N gives b/n ≤ b/N. The upper bound b/N no longer depends on the chosen indices: this lets it cover the entire future of the sequence.

  4. Choose N for an arbitrary tolerance

    Now take any tolerance ε > 0. To make b/N strictly less than ε, it is enough to choose an integer N > b/ε. Such an integer exists: the positive integers exceed any fixed real bound. b/ε has no unit, so it can be compared with an index.

    N>bε⇒bN<ε⇒|xn−xm|<ε

    The last implication applies to all n and m at least this N, by the previous upper bound. Since this reasoning works for every positive ε, the sequence is Cauchy. A larger index also works. We seek a sufficient choice, which need not be the earliest possible index.

  5. Build the numerical certificate

    With b = 1 mm and ε = 1/10 mm = 0.10 mm, the quotient b/ε is 10. We choose N = 10 + 1 = 11. This integer is strictly greater than the quotient, so the distance certificate is b/N = 1/11 mm.

    111 mm<110 mm

    Check the inequality: the numerators are identical and positive; the denominator 11 is greater than 10. The margin is exactly 1/10 − 1/11 = 1/110 mm, which is strictly positive. For all n, m ≥ 11, the distance is therefore less than 0.10 mm.

  6. Calculate a pair and check another way

    Take n = 11 and m = 22. The positions are xn = 10 − 1/11 = 109/11 mm ≈ 9.909 mm, then xm = 10 − 1/22 = 219/22 mm ≈ 9.955 mm. Subtracting these positions gives 219/22 − 109/11 = 1/22 mm ≈ 0.045 mm.

    Another check: subtract the distances back from the reference point, 1/11 − 1/22 = 1/22 mm. This result is positive and less than the upper bound 1/11 mm, which is itself less than 1/10 mm. Both the positions and their difference are lengths.

    This pair checks the arithmetic of the certificate. The proof for all pairs comes from the symbolic inequalities, rather than this single calculation.

  7. State exactly what has been proved

    Even when the indices are extremely far apart, the positions after N remain close to one another. They do not become identical: a positive gap is allowed as long as it stays below the tolerance. A tighter tolerance will require another index.

    Convergence to a fixed position compares the terms with that position; the Cauchy property compares the terms with one another. Here the reference point a also lets us calculate a − xn = b/n, but we did not need to specify a limit to build the certificate. The general link between the Cauchy property and the existence of a limit will be addressed through completeness in the next unit.

    A program that stops calculating after a small move uses local information. Without additional reasoning about its rule, this stopping condition cannot replace a certificate for all future indices.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Neighboring positions are close, so the whole sequence is Cauchy.”

    The definition also compares indices that are very far apart. Compare indices 4 and 20 in the first exercise, then add the small moves in the final variant: their accumulation can remain large.

    Compare neighbors, then distant indices

  • “I found an index for this tolerance: the proof is finished.”

    A certificate for 0.10 mm only handles that tolerance. You must explain how to choose N for every positive ε, including smaller values. The rule N > b/ε gives this construction in our example.

    Return to the choice for an arbitrary tolerance

  • “I will choose N after looking at the pair of indices.”

    Fix ε, then N. Keep this N while n and m are chosen from it onward. Changing the starting point for each pair guarantees no common band for all late positions.

Exercise 1 · Application · calculation and objection

A small step does not validate a starting index

Keep the cursor rule and ε = 0.10 mm. Calculate the distance between indices 4 and 5, then between indices 4 and 20. Can we use N = 4 for this tolerance? Does this call the certificate N = 11 into question?

Hint 1

Calculate the distances b/n back from the reference point. The difference in positions is the absolute difference between these distances.

Hint 2

To refute a proposed index, a single pair of indices at least as large is enough. To validate it, a single pair is not enough.

Compare with the solution

x at index 4 is 39/4 mm = 9.75 mm; at index 5, it is 49/5 mm = 9.80 mm. The distance is 1/4 − 1/5 = 1/20 mm = 0.05 mm, less than ε.

At index 20, the position is 199/20 mm = 9.95 mm. The distance from the position at index 4 is 1/4 − 1/20 = 1/5 mm = 0.20 mm, greater than 0.10 mm. Both indices in this pair are at least 4: this N fails.

This does not invalidate index 11, since index 4 comes before it. The overly early choice fails for the given tolerance; the sequence remains Cauchy, as the general construction proves.

  • I give a pair that violates the requirement for N = 4.
  • I distinguish the failure of this index from the failure of the Cauchy property.

Exercise 2 · Reconstruction · proof then calculation

Tighten the tolerance

The tolerance becomes ε = 0.01 mm. Propose an integer N that works and prove that it covers all subsequent pairs. Then calculate the positions at indices 101 and 202, followed by their exact distance. Separate this numerical check from the general proof.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The ratio b/ε is 100. Choose a strictly larger integer to apply the same proof.

Hint 2

Once N is chosen, use |xₘ − xₙ| ≤ b/N for all n, m ≥ N. Do not look only at a neighbor.

Compare with the solution

b/ε = 100; we can take N = 101. For all n, m ≥ 101, the proof in the example gives |xm − xn| ≤ 1/101 mm < 1/100 mm. The margin is 1/10100 mm, which is positive. Neither index in the pair was used to choose N.

At the requested indices: x at index 101 is 10 − 1/101 = 1009/101 mm ≈ 9.9901 mm. At index 202, it is 10 − 1/202 = 2019/202 mm ≈ 9.9950 mm.

Their exact distance is 1/101 − 1/202 = 1/202 mm ≈ 0.0050 mm. It stays below the upper bound 1/101 mm. The decimals are rounded after the exact calculation; checking this pair is not the reason all the other pairs work.

Every integer N > 100 satisfies our sufficient condition. You are not asked to find the smallest index that could work.

Check your reasoning

  • I fix N before the indices in the pair and use the bound for all pairs.
  • I justify the strict inequality with the new tolerance.
  • I keep exact fractions until giving decimals for readability.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · refute a false criterion

The same cursor now accumulates moves

Replace the fictional rule for the same cursor: its initial position v at index 1 is a; at each transition from index n to index n + 1, add b/(n + 1). These steps become as small as desired. Nevertheless, show that between indices n and 2n the displacement remains at least b/2, then refute the Cauchy property using a fixed tolerance of 1/4 mm.

vn+1=vn+bn+1
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

From index n to index 2n, we add n positive displacements. The smallest is the last, b/(2n).

Hint 2

Whatever starting index N is proposed, take n = N and m = 2N. Compare the distance with the proposed fixed tolerance.

Compare with the solution

vn denotes the new position at index n; a and b keep their values in mm. The step b/(n + 1) becomes less than any given positive length once its denominator is large enough. This tells us only about neighbors.

From index n to index 2n, the denominators are n + 1, then the following integers up to 2n. There are n displacements, each at least b/(2n). Since they are positive, the distance is their sum. Thus:

|v2n−vn|≥n×b2n=b2

Numerical check: between indices 10 and 20, there are 10 steps. Each is at least 1/20 mm, so their sum is at least 10 × 1/20 = 1/2 mm. We have calculated a lower bound, rather than the exact value of this sum.

Fix ε = 1/4 mm. For every proposed index N ≥ 1, n = N and m = 2N are allowed indices, and their distance is at least 1/2 mm. It exceeds ε by at least 1/4 mm. No N can work for this single tolerance: the new sequence is not Cauchy.

To prove the Cauchy property, you must handle every tolerance. To refute it, a single positive tolerance is enough if you can find a pair that fails after every N. This change in quantifiers explains why accumulated small steps can defeat a criterion based only on neighbors.

Check your reasoning

  • I count n displacements and justify their common lower bound.
  • I keep the same tolerance 1/4 mm whatever N is.
  • I provide a pair that fails after every N, then draw a conclusion only about the new rule.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Reconstruct the Cauchy definition, respecting the order of its choices.

For every tolerance ε > 0, choose an index N such that for all n, m ≥ N, |xₙ − xₘ| < ε. N depends on the tolerance and the sequence, rather than the pair chosen afterward.

Recall 2For xₙ = a − b/n with b positive, how can you obtain an upper bound valid for all late pairs?

Order the indices m ≥ n ≥ N. The distance is b/n − b/m ≤ b/n ≤ b/N. Then choose an integer N > b/ε. Symmetry of distance covers the other order of indices.

Recall 3Why are successive steps that become arbitrarily small not enough?

They can accumulate. For the variant that adds b/(n + 1), the n steps between n and 2n total at least b/2, even at very large indices. A distance between neighbors does not control this whole sum.

Recall 4What distinguishes calculating a pair from proving the Cauchy property?

A pair checks a particular distance. A proof produces, for every tolerance, an index and a bound valid for all subsequent pairs. A finite number of checks cannot replace this bound derived from the rule.

Reconstruct the reasoning

What you understand
The Cauchy property concerns all pairs of sufficiently large indices. A small displacement between successive iterations is different information; it can serve a stopping rule without, by itself, constituting this proof.
