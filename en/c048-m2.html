Level 1 · C048·M2

Diagonalization

Your goalChange coordinates to separate the effects of a transformation, then verify a diagonalization using matrix products.

Before you start
c040-m5 : least squares · c048-m1 : eigenvalues

✿01 See the idea

On a fictional control rig, 2 levers set the horizontal and vertical displacements of a point. The control rule mixes their effects: moving the vertical lever also moves the point horizontally. We know this rule exactly; we are not trying to estimate it.

Instead of controlling the horizontal and vertical directions separately, let us try combined movements. Movement u is horizontal. Movement v moves the levers together by equal amounts. Under our rule, each of these movements keeps its direction: only its size changes. These are the eigendirections reviewed in the previous unit.

Describing a control input as a sum of these movements gives new coordinates. In these coordinates, each movement receives its own multiplier, with no contribution from the other. Diagonalization consists of finding a basis of such eigenvectors: a set that allows every control input to be described uniquely.

The scene and all its values are fictional. Input and output displacements are expressed in millimetres, written mm. The rule is linear and holds for all displacements in this exercise, with no saturation or noise. The drawings represent control inputs, rather than measurements of how a real device operates.

The rule to remember

Express the control input in a basis of eigenvectors, multiply each coordinate by its eigenvalue, then return to the lever coordinates.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Decompose a control input for the rig

The initial control input is x = (5 ; 2) in mm. The rule gives a′ = 2a + b and b′ = 3b. We will construct its diagonalization, calculate the output and check it directly.

  1. Write the rule as a matrix and define the symbols

    x is the column of settings a and b; x′ is the output column a′ and b′. The prime symbol distinguishes output from input; here it does not denote a derivative. A is the matrix of the rule in the lever basis. Matrix multiplication calculates each component as the sum of the products of entries in a row with the corresponding entries in the column.

    x=[52];A=[2103];x′=Ax

    The input and output columns have 2 components in mm. A has size 2 × 2, with dimensionless coefficients: it multiplies mm to give mm. We work with real numbers, without rounding.

  2. Find directions that do not mix

    An eigenvector is a nonzero vector whose image is a multiple of itself. Its eigenvalue is that multiplier. Here we look for dimensionless u and v, then their respective eigenvalues λᵤ and λᵥ. The Greek letters lambda name these dimensionless factors.

    u=[10];v=[11];λu=2;λv=3

    For u: Au = (2 × 1 + 1 × 0 ; 0 × 1 + 3 × 0) = (2 ; 0) = 2u. The horizontal movement keeps its direction.

    For v: Av = (2 × 1 + 1 × 1 ; 0 × 1 + 3 × 1) = (3 ; 3) = 3v. The levers moved together keep their ratio. We have checked the directions and their multipliers, without inferring them from the appearance of the drawing.

  3. Construct a basis and its inverse mapping

    Place u then v as the columns of P. A basis gives a unique expression x = αu + βv; the coefficients α and β are the new coordinates, in mm. Here, αu + βv = (α + β ; β). Thus β = b and α = a − b, for every control input. Solving this shows both that all x can be described and that their description is unique.

    P=[1101];P-1=[1-101]

    P converts the movement coordinates into lever coordinates. P inverse goes in the opposite direction: multiplying by P inverse calculates (a − b ; b). The exponent -1 means the inverse of the whole matrix; it does not ask you to invert each entry.

    Invertibility check in this plane: the determinant of P is the product of its diagonal entries minus the product of the other entries. It equals 1 − 0 = 1, which differs from 0. In particular, returning from (a − b ; b) using P gives ((a − b) + b ; b) = (a ; b), which checks the direction of the coordinate change.

  4. Build the diagonal in the same order

    D is the matrix of multipliers in the eigenbasis. It is diagonal: all entries outside its diagonal equal 0. Since the first column of P is u, the first diagonal entry of D equals λᵤ; the next corresponds to v.

    D=[2003];AP=PD

    The equality AP = PD follows by comparing columns. The columns of AP are Au and Av. Those of PD are 2u and 3v. They are equal by the preceding calculations. Multiplying this equality on the right by P inverse gives the diagonalization:

    A=PDP-1;D=P-1AP

    Read these products in the order in which they act on a column, starting on the right. P and D have dimensionless coefficients. The rule A remains the same; its description in the eigenbasis has become D.

  5. Calculate in the eigenbasis

    Let y denote the column (α ; β). We begin by changing coordinates, because D acts on the movement coordinates rather than directly on the levers: α = 5 − 2 = 3 mm and β = 2 mm.

    y=P-1x=[32]

    Next, each movement receives its factor: α′ = 2 × 3 = 6 mm and β′ = 3 × 2 = 6 mm. The 0 entries prevent mixing between these coordinates.

    y′=Dy=[66]

    Return to the levers using P: a′ = α′ + β′ = 6 + 6 = 12 mm and b′ = β′ = 6 mm.

    x′=Py′=[126]

    Read the result as a horizontal displacement of 12.0 mm and a vertical displacement of 6.0 mm. The middle pair (6 ; 6) describes the same output in another basis; it must not be read as the lever settings.

  6. Check by a different route

    Direct calculation in the lever basis: a′ = 2 × 5 + 2 = 10 + 2 = 12 mm; b′ = 3 × 2 = 6 mm. These results agree with the route through the eigenbasis. Even before the transformation, P y gave (5 ; 2) mm, our initial control input.

    We therefore have separate checks: each column satisfies its eigenvector equation, the change of basis is reversible, and the direct calculation gives the same output. In this small example, the route through the eigenbasis promises no saving in calculation; it shows how the method works.

  7. State when the method is possible

    For a real matrix of size n × n, n denotes the number of coordinates. Diagonalization over the real numbers is possible exactly when we can find n linearly independent real eigenvectors. Here, independent means that no combination of these vectors gives the 0 vector unless all the coefficients are 0. They then form the columns of an invertible matrix P.

    In our plane, u and v are not collinear: no multiple of u can provide the nonzero vertical component of v. They therefore form the required basis. Knowing eigenvalues is not enough: repeating a direction does not complete a basis. The last exercise tests this limit on the same rig.

    This unit stops at the change of basis and its verification. It does not assume that P is a rotation or that every matrix has such a basis. The particular study of symmetric matrices comes next.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I multiply each lever directly by the corresponding eigenvalue.”

    D acts on the coordinates in the eigenbasis. First calculate α = a − b and β = b, then apply D and return using P. Otherwise you remove the horizontal mixing that the rule A actually contains.

    Repeat the full sequence of coordinate changes

  • “I put the eigenvectors in rows and the eigenvalues in any order.”

    In A = P D P inverse, the eigenvectors are the columns of P. Each diagonal entry of D corresponds to the column in the same position. Check AP = PD column by column; a permutation is allowed only if it is made in both P and D.

    Review the construction of P and D

  • “As soon as I know the eigenvalues, a diagonalization exists.”

    You need a basis of eigenvectors, which requires enough independent directions. A repeated eigenvalue can provide several directions, or just one. Solve the eigenvector equation in the last exercise before constructing P.

    Test the limit on the modified rig

Hint 1

The first column of AP is the image of the first vector placed in P.

Hint 2

Vector v is multiplied by 3 and u by 2. Remember to permute the coefficients of the decomposition too.

Compare with the solution

The new diagonal is (3 ; 2), in that order. The first column is Av = (3 ; 3) = 3v; the next is Au = (2 ; 0) = 2u. AP and PD then have the same columns.

The coordinates must also be permuted: the same control input is written βv + αu, giving (2 ; 3) mm in this new basis. Keeping their old order would describe a different control input. There are several choices of P and D for the same transformation.

Check your reasoning

  • Match each multiplier to the correct column.
  • Justify using Au and Av, without assuming that the order of the eigenvalues is fixed.
  • Also permute the coordinates of the control input.

Exercise 3 · Justify an impossibility

The modified rig loses an eigendirection

On the same fictional rig, only the factor for the vertical output changes: b′ = 2b, while a′ = 2a + b. The new matrix is called J. Is diagonalization still possible? Find all eigendirections, without drawing a conclusion solely from a repeated value on the diagonal.

J=[2102]
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For an eigenvector (s ; t) and a value λ, write 2s + t = λs and 2t = λt.

Hint 2

If t is nonzero, the second equation requires λ = 2, then the first requires t = 0. What remains if t is 0?

Compare with the solution

s and t are the real components of a nonzero eigenvector of J; λ is its multiplier. If t were nonzero, 2t = λt would give λ = 2. The first equation would then give 2s + t = 2s, hence t = 0: a contradiction. Every eigenvector must therefore have t = 0.

Since the vector is nonzero, s is then nonzero. The first equation requires λ = 2. All eigenvectors are therefore (s ; 0), with s nonzero: they are collinear. The plane requires 2 independent directions; J is not diagonalizable.

The old v no longer works: Jv = (3 ; 2), which is not a multiple of (1 ; 1). Duplicating u in P would give a matrix with no inverse. The failure is the lack of an eigenbasis, rather than simply the repetition of λ: a matrix that is already diagonal could have a repeated eigenvalue.

Check your reasoning

  • Handle nonzero t then 0 t, remembering that the eigenvector is nonzero.
  • Show that all eigenvectors are collinear.
  • Conclude from the lack of independent directions, rather than just the repeated eigenvalue.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does diagonalization change in our rig?

The basis used to describe the control inputs. The transformation remains A; in a basis of eigenvectors, its matrix D multiplies each coordinate separately.

Recall 2What sequence does A = P D P inverse apply to a control input x?

P inverse expresses x in the eigenbasis; D applies the multipliers; P gives the coordinates in the initial basis. The products act on x starting from the right.

Recall 3How can you check the order of the eigenvectors and eigenvalues?

The eigenvectors are the columns of P. Each column of AP must equal the corresponding column of PD, so the image of the vector must be its multiple by the value placed in the same position in D.

Recall 4What exact condition allows a real matrix to be diagonalized over the real numbers?

A basis of real eigenvectors is required. For size n × n, this means n linearly independent real eigenvectors, placed as columns of an invertible P. Known eigenvalues are not enough.

Recall 5How can you check the rig output by another route?

Compare the return P D P inverse x with the direct calculation Ax, then check that P inverse changes only the coordinates by recovering x using P. A single matching result does not replace the proof AP = PD.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, redraw the levers and the movements u and v. Reconstruct the roles of A, P, D and P inverse; explain why their columns require a matching order.
