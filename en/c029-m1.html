Level 1 · C029·M1

Events

Your goalDescribe an event by its outcomes, combine conditions and count the selected cards without counting any twice.

Before you start
c028-m5 : choice trees · c024-m5 : measurement quality

✿01 See the idea

You are going to draw 1 card from a box. Before the draw, you ask: “Will the card be pink?” You are not yet asking which letter will be drawn. Cards A, B and C would all give a yes answer: they meet the same condition.

The imaginary box contains 6 distinct cards, labelled A to F. A and B are pink with a star; C is pink with a circle; D is blue with a star; E and F are blue with a circle. Although A and B look alike, their letters identify different cards.

An outcome is the specific result used to describe this draw: here, the letter on the card. An event groups the outcomes that meet a condition. After the draw, the event occurs if the letter drawn belongs to that group. An event can therefore contain several outcomes, even though the draw produces only one.

Look at the diagrams before moving on to the symbols. The question “pink or starred” also selects A and B: they meet both conditions. Here we are counting possible cards, without assigning chances to the draws. There is no requirement for every card to have the same chance of being drawn.

The rule to remember

Define the possible outcomes, turn the condition into a list of letters, then count each selected letter only once.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

How many cards meet “pink or starred”?

We draw 1 card and record only its letter. The inventory of the box is complete and the properties of the cards stay fixed. Let us find the outcomes that meet “pink or starred”, then check their number by counting another way.

  1. Define the sample space and distinguish its elements

    Ω, the uppercase Greek letter omega, denotes the sample space: the set of all possible outcomes for the experiment described. Curly brackets list the elements of a set. Here, Ω = {A, B, C, D, E, F}. The letter ω, lowercase omega, denotes any outcome, for example A. The symbol ∈ is read “belongs to”: ω ∈ Ω means that ω is an allowed outcome.

    An event is a subset of Ω: a set consisting only of outcomes from Ω. The symbol ⊆ means “is a subset of”. The event {A} contains just one outcome; the letter A denotes that outcome. These are different objects. The letter E on a card is an identifier, rather than the name of the event “starred”: we will call that event S.

    The bars |U| denote the cardinality of a finite set U, meaning its number of elements. This number is a count with no physical unit; in this scene, we express it in cards. Thus |Ω| = 6 cards. The model assumes that only one card is drawn and that no outcome is missing; it describes neither a sequence of several draws nor their order.

    Ω={A,B,C,D,E,F};|Ω|=6

  2. Turn the conditions into lists

    R denotes the event “the card drawn is pink”; S denotes “the card drawn has a star”. We test each letter of Ω against these conditions. This gives R = {A, B, C} and S = {A, B, D}, so |R| = 3 cards and |S| = 3 cards.

    If D is drawn, S occurs and R does not. If A is drawn, both R and S occur through the same outcome. Several events can occur even though only one outcome has been observed. The notation ω ∈ R means precisely that R occurs for the outcome ω.

    R⊆Ω;S⊆Ω

  3. Find what “and” requires

    R ∩ S, read “R intersection S”, contains the outcomes that meet both conditions at once. A and B are pink with a star; C fails the star condition and D fails the colour condition. Thus R ∩ S = {A, B} and |R ∩ S| = 2 cards.

    Events are mutually exclusive if their intersection is empty: no outcome can make them occur together. R and S are therefore not mutually exclusive here. This check relies on the letters they share, rather than on how similar their names are.

    R∩S={A,B}

  4. Combine with “or” and remove the double counting

    R ∪ S, read “R union S”, contains the outcomes that meet at least one condition. The mathematical “or” is inclusive: a card that meets both conditions is also selected. We get R ∪ S = {A, B, C, D}.

    The addition |R| + |S| = 3 + 3 = 6 counts A in each list, and B in each list. Each of these 2 letters must lose its extra copy. We therefore subtract the cardinality of the intersection once.

    |R∪S|=|R|+|S|−|R∩S|

    The calculation is 6 − 2 = 4 cards. This is not a probability formula: we are counting distinct outcomes in finite sets. No assumption of equal chances has been used.

    |R∪S|=3+3−2=4 cartes

  5. Check using regions that do not overlap

    The second diagram divides the box according to the conditions. There are 2 cards in both events, 1 in R only, 1 in S only and 2 in neither. These regions are disjoint: they share no letters.

    For the union, let us add the selected regions: 2 + 1 + 1 = 4 cards. The result agrees with the list {A, B, C, D} and the calculation using subtraction. For the whole box: 4 + 2 = 6 cards. We find every letter of Ω exactly once; no card has been forgotten.

  6. Describe the complement and recognise the extreme cases

    The complement Rᶜ is the event “the card is not pink”. The superscript c indicates the complement within Ω; it is neither a unit nor a numerical power. Here Rᶜ = {D, E, F}: |Rᶜ| = |Ω| − |R| = 6 − 3 = 3 cards. The symbol ∖ denotes a set difference: Ω ∖ R means “the elements of Ω that are not in R”.

    Rc=Ω∖R={D,E,F}

    Check: 3 + 3 = 6 cards; none is in both R and Rᶜ. Their intersection is ∅, the symbol for the empty set, with cardinality 0. This is an impossible event. Their union is Ω, with cardinality 6: this is the certain event. “Certain” here means that every outcome in the model makes it occur; you must always check that the model accurately describes the draw.

    The complement depends on the sample space chosen. In our box, “not pink” is equivalent to “blue” because the inventory contains no other colour. This equivalence could disappear if the box changed.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why A meets the conditions R and S, why it counts only once in their union, then why “not pink” must be defined relative to the box.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A and B look alike, so they are the same outcome.”

    The experiment records the letter, and the letters A and B are distinct. A shared colour does not merge these outcomes. If we decided to record only the colour, we would change the description of the experiment and the sample space.

    Revisit the definitions of sample space and outcome

  • “Pink or starred excludes cards that are both.”

    In a union, “or” means that at least one condition is met. A and B are kept. To require exactly one condition, you must say so explicitly and remove the shared cards; the last exercise compares these requirements.

    Compare inclusive “or” with the exclusive condition

  • “I can always add the numbers of outcomes in the events.”

    First look for shared letters. Here, adding the lists counts both A and B again. Remove one copy of each letter in the intersection; addition alone works when the events are disjoint.

    Revisit the count and the check using regions

One letter is drawn, several conditions are met

Compare with the solution

The outcome is B, a pink card with a star. R occurs because B belongs to {A, B, C}. S occurs because B belongs to {A, B, D}. The intersection R ∩ S therefore occurs too. Rᶜ does not occur, because B does not belong to {D, E, F}.

B denotes the outcome; {B} is the event that occurs only when B is drawn. Its cardinality is 1. Several events have occurred even though only one card was drawn.

Check your reasoning

  • You name just one outcome: B.
  • You justify why R, S and their intersection occur by using membership.
  • You reject Rᶜ and distinguish the letter from the set {B}.

Neither pink nor starred

In the same box, describe the event T: “the card is neither pink nor starred”. Give its list, write it as a complement and give its cardinality. Is it the same event as Rᶜ?

Hint 1

Remove every card that belongs to the union R ∪ S.

Hint 2

Compare the remaining letters with {D, E, F}, then test card D.

Compare with the solution

The union is {A, B, C, D}. Its complement within Ω is {E, F}. Thus T = (R ∪ S)ᶜ = Ω ∖ (R ∪ S). Its cardinality is 6 − 4 = 2 cards.

T differs from Rᶜ = {D, E, F}. D is not pink, so it makes Rᶜ occur; however, it has a star and does not make T occur. “Neither” requires both conditions to fail. Checking the lists gives 2 cards outside the union.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain {E, F}, with cardinality 2.
  • You take the complement of the entire union.
  • You use D to explain why the complement of R alone is not enough.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · fix an ambiguous rule

Exactly one condition

For this same draw, someone wants to select “pink or starred, but not both”. They use R ∪ S and report 4 cards. Fix their list and their count. Then explain why the number of selected cards does not, on its own, give a probability for the draw.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with the union and remove the letters in the intersection.

Hint 2

Compare the “pink without a star” region with the “star without pink” region. For the chances of being drawn, reread the assumptions: have we defined a rule that gives every card the same weight?

Compare with the solution

The union contains A, B, C, D. The phrase “but not both” excludes A and B. This leaves {C, D}, giving 1 + 1 = 2 cards. We can also start from the previous result: 4 − 2 = 2 cards.

(R∪S)∖(R∩S)={C,D}

Here the symbol ∖ removes the elements of the intersection from those of the union. The original rule includes cards meeting both conditions; the corrected rule requires exactly one. They describe different events.

We have counted outcomes without describing their chances. A method of drawing may favour certain cards; the same inventory is therefore not enough to determine a probability. We will need to specify a probability rule, which the next unit on axioms will prepare us to do.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain {C, D}, with cardinality 2, using a rule you explain.
  • You explain why A and B belong to the union but do not meet the corrected rule.
  • You distinguish counting cards from assigning probabilities.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the difference between an outcome and an event in the draw?

The outcome is the observed letter. An event is a set of possible letters; it occurs when the letter drawn belongs to it. A is an outcome, {A} is an event.

Recall 2What do R ∩ S and R ∪ S select? What happens to A in each case?

The intersection requires both conditions: {A, B}. The union requires at least one: {A, B, C, D}. A belongs to both sets and appears only once in each.

Recall 3Why subtract the intersection when adding the numbers of outcomes in R and S?

Each shared letter has been counted in each list. We remove its extra copy: 3 + 3 − 2 = 4 cards. Counting the letters directly checks the result.

Recall 4Why must you define Ω before saying “not R”?

Rᶜ groups the outcomes in Ω that are not in R. Here they are {D, E, F}. Changing the box can change this complement. R and Rᶜ are disjoint and together make up all of Ω.

Recall 5Which events are impossible and certain? Is counting their outcomes enough to describe all the chances?

∅ is impossible: no outcome makes it occur. Ω is certain: every outcome makes it occur. Counting the other events does not determine their probabilities without a rule for the chances of the outcomes.
