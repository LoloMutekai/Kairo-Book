Level 2 · C058·M1

Double integrals

Your goalConstruct a double integral as a limit of sums of masses and calculate the mass of a plate using increasingly tight bounds.

Before you start
c045-m5 : introductory quadrature · c057-m5 : Hessian

✿01 See the idea

A rectangular plate contains more material towards its right edge and its top edge. How can we find its mass? Weighing a single small area and extending its density to the whole plate risks overlooking this distribution.

Imagine a grid. In each cell, multiply the local density by the area of the cell, then add the small masses. If you replace the density in each cell by its minimum, you count too little. If you use its maximum, you count too much. Finer cells bring these two totals closer together.

A single integral accumulates along an interval. A double integral accumulates over a region of the plane: we need to cover both the columns and the rows. The word “double” describes these two directions; it does not ask us to double a single integral.

The plate, its dimensions and its density rule are fictional. We restrict the discussion to a rectangle and a continuous density. Triple integrals, calculation by repeated integration and other coordinates belong to the following units.

The rule to remember

Local density × small area gives a small mass; the limit of the sum over all the cells gives the total mass.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The mass of a plate whose density increases in both directions

The plate measures 2 m by 1 m. Its surface density follows the affine rule defined below. Let us calculate its mass using small rectangles, then check the resulting value.

  1. Describe the plate and what is being added

    D denotes the rectangle occupied by the plate. The coordinates x and y are distances in metres from its bottom left corner. Its length L is 2 m; its height H is 1 m. Its area A = LH is 2 m². The mass M will be in kilograms.

    The surface density ρ, the Greek letter rho, is mass per unit area. The model is ρ(x, y) = ρ0 + αx + βy, where ρ0 = 2 kg/m², α = 1 kg/m³ and β = 2 kg/m³. Thus αx and βy have the required unit kg/m². The positive coefficients make the density increase towards the right and upwards.

    D=[0,L]×[0,H];ρ(x,y)=ρ0+αx+βy

    The plate is treated as a flat surface: its density already accounts for its full thickness. The model is continuous and positive on D. We assume no actual measurement of material.

  2. Build a sum of masses

    Choose an integer n at least equal to 1. Divide each side into n equal parts: there are n × n cells, with width Δx = L/n and height Δy = H/n. Here the letter Δ means “size of a piece”. The area of a cell is ΔA = Δx × Δy, in m².

    The indices i and j identify the column and row respectively, from 0 to n − 1. In each cell, choose a point with coordinates ξᵢⱼ and ηᵢⱼ, in metres. Replacing the density throughout this cell by its value at this point gives the approximate mass ρ(ξᵢⱼ, ηᵢⱼ) × ΔA. The symbol Σ tells us to add, once for each cell.

    Sn=∑i=0n−1∑j=0n−1ρ(ξij,ηij)ΔA

    Checking the units is essential: (kg/m²) × m² = kg. A sum of densities alone would retain the unit kg/m² and would vary with the number of cells.

  3. Find bounds with the initial grid

    Take n = 2: Δx = 1 m, Δy = 0.50 m, ΔA = 0.50 m² and 4 cells. In each cell, the minimum is at the bottom left corner and the maximum at the top right corner, because α and β are positive.

    The minima are ρ(0, 0) = 2, ρ(1, 0) = 3, ρ(0, 0.50) = 3 and ρ(1, 0.50) = 4 kg/m². We obtain them by substituting x and y into the same density rule.

    The lower sum Bn underestimates the mass of each cell. Here: (2 + 3 + 3 + 4) × 0.50 = 12 × 0.50 = 6 kg. Between opposite corners of a cell, the density increases by αΔx + βΔy = 2 kg/m².

    Every maximum therefore exceeds its minimum by this same amount. The upper sum Un exceeds Bn by A × 2 = 4 kg: Un = 6 + 4 = 10 kg. The mass we seek lies between 6 and 10 kg; neither endpoint of this interval is yet the exact mass.

  4. Define the integral by taking the limit

    Refining means increasing n so that both the widths AND the heights of the cells tend to 0. For a continuous function on this closed, bounded rectangle, all the sums Sn converge to the same value, regardless of the point chosen in each cell. This is the double integral of ρ over D.

    M=∬Dρ(x,y)dA=limn→∞Sn

    The double ∫ sign recalls the two directions of the surface; dA represents the area element. In our rectangular coordinate system, it corresponds to the product dx dy. We must not interpret dA as a finite area already chosen. The letter M denotes a mass here because the integrand ρ is a mass density.

    Bn and Un bracket all the sums obtained with this grid. If their difference tends to 0, their common limit therefore determines M. We will prove this for the plate, without a method for changing the order of integration.

  5. Express the bounds for any grid

    The bottom left corner of cell (i, j) is (iL/n, jH/n). To calculate the mean density at all these corners, start with the mean of the indices 0, …, n − 1. The first and last add up to n − 1, as does every symmetric pair: their mean is (n − 1)/2.

    Each x-coordinate occurs in n cells, one per row. The mean of the corner x-coordinates is therefore L(n − 1)/(2n). For the same reason, the mean of their y-coordinates is H(n − 1)/(2n). Since the density is affine, its mean at the corners can be calculated using these mean coordinates.

    ρ¯bas=ρ0+(αL+βH)(n−1)2n

    Here αL = 2 kg/m² and βH = 2 kg/m². Their sum is 4 kg/m². The mean density at the lower corners is thus 4 − 2/n kg/m². A bar over ρ indicates a mean; the subscript “bas” (lower) identifies the points used.

    Since all the cells have the same area, Bn is this mean density multiplied by A. For Un, add (αL + βH)/n to each density. This gives the following bounds, expressed in kilograms:

    Bn=8−4n;Un=8+4n

    Check the calculation: with n = 2, we recover 6 kg and 10 kg, calculated directly cell by cell. The general difference Un − Bn is 8/n kg.

  6. Conclude and check the meaning of the result

    As n grows without bound, 4/n tends to 0 kg. Both bounds therefore tend to 8 kg. By the squeeze theorem, M = 8 kg: this is the exact mass under the stated model, rather than a mass measured on a real plate.

    Over the whole plate, ρ is between 2 and 6 kg/m². Even if we replaced the density everywhere by its minimum or maximum, the mass would be between 4 and 12 kg. Our result 8 kg passes this check. The quotient M/A = 4 kg/m² gives the mean density over the whole plate; it does not represent a new mass.

    The initial grid gave only the interval [6 ; 10] kg. The exact value comes from the formula valid for every n and from taking the limit. Simply taking the midpoint of a pair of bounds would not have provided this proof.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I can add the densities of the cells without their areas.”

    A density is in kg/m². Multiply each density by the area of its cell to obtain kilograms. Otherwise, further subdivision would change the total without changing the plate. If the cells did not have the same area, each would need its own area weight.

    Review “Build a sum of masses”

  • “The grid gives 6 kg, so this is the exact mass.”

    These 6 kg use the minima. The density still varies inside the cells; the mass is only known to be between 6 and 10 kg. To conclude, we must show that both bounds have the same limit, rather than arbitrarily choosing a point between them.

    Review taking the limit and the conclusion

  • “Adding more columns is always enough, even if the rows remain tall.”

    The density also changes with y. Refining only x leaves an unresolved variation over the height. Here the difference between the bounds retains a contribution of 4 kg. Return to the transfer exercise to recover why both cell dimensions must tend to 0.

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · units and the constant case

The uniformly loaded plate

Keep the same plate and initial grid. For this trial only, replace the variable density by the constant density ρ₀ = 2 kg/m². Calculate the mass of a cell, then that of the plate. Is refinement needed to make this calculation exact?

Hint 1

The area of a cell is 0.50 m²; there are 4 cells.

Hint 2

With a constant density, the minimum and maximum in each cell coincide.

Compare with the solution

The mass of a cell is 2 × 0.50 = 1 kg. The 4 cells give 4 × 1 = 4 kg. We recover ρ0A = 2 × 2 = 4 kg.

The calculation is already exact under this new assumption: no variation is lost within a cell. This does not make the density in the original example constant; its mass remains 8 kg.

A finer grid on the original plate

The distance of each bound from the centre of the interval is 4/n kg.

Hint 2

The width of the interval is the upper bound minus the lower bound.

Compare with the solution

The term 4/4 is 1 kg. Bn = 8 − 1 = 7 kg and Un = 8 + 1 = 9 kg. The width is 9 − 7 = 2 kg.

This calculation alone guarantees that the mass belongs to [7 ; 9] kg. A narrower pair of bounds still gives bounds; the exact proof in the example relied on all n and their limit.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · choose and justify

Reduce the gap without overlooking a direction

We require a width Uₙ − Bₙ of at most 1 kg for the grid of n × n cells. Find the smallest possible integer n and both bounds. Then respond to this proposal: “I keep a single row over the full height and add as many columns as necessary.”

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The width is 8/n kg. Find when it becomes at most 1 kg.

Hint 2

In a cell of width Δx and height H, the density difference between opposite corners always contains βH.

Compare with the solution

We need 8/n ≤ 1, so n ≥ 8. The smallest integer is 8. The distance to each bound is 4/8 = 0.50 kg. This gives [7.50 ; 8.50] kg, with width 1.00 kg.

With a single row, Δy = H. Even if Δx tends to 0, the density difference in each cell retains the term βH = 2 kg/m². After weighting by the total area A, the gap between the bounds remains at least AβH = 4 kg. Adding only columns therefore cannot meet the stated requirement.

The total number of cells increases in both proposals; what matters for this guarantee is the reduction in their sizes in the directions where the density varies.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify the smallest integer n = 8, rather than simply trying a value.
  • Calculate both bounds and check their width, including the units.
  • Recover the term AβH and explain why an unrefined direction prevents the guarantee.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why must each term contain both a density AND an area?

Density is mass per unit area. Multiplying it by the area of the cell gives a mass. Adding only the densities does not give a total in kilograms.

Recall 2What does the double integral over our rectangle represent?

The common value approached by the sums of local density × area as the cells become small in both directions. Continuity of the density on the rectangle guarantees this convergence here, regardless of the points chosen.

Recall 3Why do the bottom left and top right corners provide bounds in this scene?

The density increases with x and with y because both coefficients are positive. These corners therefore give the minima and maxima in each cell. This choice of corners would not be guaranteed for another density rule.

Recall 4Why are finite bounds insufficient to claim an exact mass?

They leave several possible masses. In the example, the formulas Bₙ = 8 − 4/n kg and Uₙ = 8 + 4/n kg have the same limit: taking this limit is what proves M = 8 kg.

Recall 5Why does the total number of cells alone not guarantee adequate refinement?

We can multiply the columns while leaving each cell as tall as the whole plate. The variation in y then remains unresolved. We must reduce the sizes in both directions for the guarantee used here.

What you understand
A double integral is built from a sum over two indices, with an area associated with each cell and a passage to the limit. A finite sum over pairs shares the traversal structure; it does not by itself constitute an integral.
