Protocol

Your goalWrite down the choices in a protocol before taking any measurements, then count the admissible block orders to determine whether the design can reach the threshold of the prespecified test.

Before you start
c074-m5 : identification assumptions · c145-m5 : policy evaluation · c073-m5 : interaction and repeated measures · c081-m5 : experimental ethics

✿01 See the idea

Before looking at a result, a study must already have answered some questions: who or what is measured, which conditions are compared, in what order, using which outcome measure, and how uncertainty will be assessed. These answers, written before the first measurement, form the protocol. They prevent choices from being made later because they suit the result.

Our scenario is entirely fictional. A volunteer, whom we call P, performs a task that involves detecting target letters. The session is divided into blocks of 5 minutes. In condition R, the phone is put away out of sight; in condition T, it stays switched off on the table. The outcome measure is the number of omissions, meaning missed targets, per block. No real measurements are planned: we are only assessing the design.

The order of the blocks matters. If R always came before T, fatigue during the session would be mixed up with the condition. Drawing the order at random avoids systematically fixing this link; a particular draw can still be unbalanced. Prohibiting 3 identical blocks in a row limits long runs without guaranteeing perfect balance over time. The draw also defines the set of orders that will serve as the reference for the test.

The design is therefore assessed before the data. In our test, a uniform draw gives equal weight to every admissible order. The observed result counts for at least 1 order; in a two-sided comparison, its T–R swap also counts. The smallest possible test probability is then 2 divided by the number of admissible orders, if only these orders are tied at the maximum. A design that is too short may never reach the chosen threshold.

The rule to remember

Before the first measurement, a protocol specifies who participates, what is randomly assigned, the conditions, the outcome measure, the assumptions and the analysis rule; the number of admissible orders may already make the threshold unreachable.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Can a design with 6 blocks reach the threshold?

An initial draft specifies 6 blocks of 5 minutes for P, with 3 R blocks and 3 T blocks, a two-sided test and a threshold of 0.05. Before any recruitment, we investigate whether this draft can produce a probability below that threshold. All values are fictional.

  1. Write down the choices and their assumptions

    P is the only participant; the block is the unit assigned R or T. The entire order is selected in a single draw: the blocks are not independent participants. The conclusion will remain limited to P in this session. The blocks have the same duration and present the same number of targets according to a rule fixed in advance. The integer e counts the omissions in a block. The symbols ēT and ēR denote the means of these counts in each condition; the bar means an average. Their difference D is expressed in omissions per block. A positive D means more omissions with the phone visible.

    Explicit assumptions: the condition in a block does not affect subsequent blocks. Fatigue can vary with the position of the block; randomization does not remove it and does not require independent measurements across blocks. If an effect persisted after the condition changed, the design would need revision. On ethics, P can stop at any time; the 30 minutes of task time are a cost that must be justified. Rule set in advance: an interrupted session remains incomplete, with no confirmatory result from this test. Inconvenient blocks are not removed afterwards to redo the analysis.

    D=e¯T−e¯R

  2. Decide how uncertainty will be interpreted

    Let Ω denote the set of admissible orders and |Ω| its number of elements, with no physical unit. Uniformly means that each order has probability 1 / |Ω| of being drawn. The null hypothesis of the test is precise: replacing R with T, or T with R, would not change the number of omissions in any block. Under this hypothesis and with no effect of a block on subsequent blocks, the omissions remain fixed when the assigned order changes.

    The protocol therefore specifies the following: after the full session, recalculate D for every order in Ω, keeping the omissions at their positions in time. The bars around D denote its absolute value, which ignores its sign. D with the subscript “obs” is the difference calculated for the order actually drawn. The probability p, which has no unit, is the proportion of orders for which |D| is at least as large as |D obs|. This is a two-sided test: a difference in either direction is counted. p does not give the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

    p=nombre d’ordres de Ω avec |D|≥|Dobs||Ω|

    The threshold, denoted α and with no unit, is 5 / 100 = 0.05. We fix the rule p < α before the data, together with the choice of a two-sided test and this calculation. Changing them after seeing the omissions would change the prespecified procedure. Here, we only check whether the design can satisfy this rule; we do not calculate any observed effect.

  3. Count the balanced orders

    A balanced order places T 3 times among 6 blocks. Choosing the positions of T gives a descending product in the numerator, 6 × 5 × 4, divided by 3 × 2 × 1, because the 3 chosen positions have no internal ordering. The product 30, then 120, divided by 6, gives the required count.

    6×5×43×2×1=1206=20

    There are therefore 20 balanced orders before applying the rule on runs.

  4. Remove the orders that violate the rule

    The rule prohibits 3 identical blocks in a row. Since each condition occupies 3 positions, an excluded order must contain TTT or RRR. Sliding the run TTT among the R blocks and then doing the same for RRR gives 6 distinct orders: TTTRRR, RTTTRR, RRTTTR, RRRTTT, TRRRTT and TTRRRT. The end cases containing the runs of opposite conditions are counted only once. This leaves 20 − 6 = 14 admissible orders, so |Ω| = 14.

    Check: 14 + 6 does give 20. The 14 admissible orders have been recounted by enumeration and by a second, stepwise counting method, giving the same number.

  5. Calculate the smallest attainable probability

    The best case is when only the observed order and its T–R swap produce an absolute value of D this large. The swap replaces every T with R and every R with T; it preserves balance and the rule on runs. It turns D into −D, so at least 2 orders are counted in p. Further ties would make p larger. The bound is attainable: imagine that all T blocks miss 1 target and that all R blocks miss none. Only the order and its swap then separate the counts completely.

    pmin=2|Ω|=214≈0.1429

    Even with a one-sided test, the value would be 1 / 14 ≈ 0.0714. It would still be above 0.05.

  6. Decide before the data

    Keep the exact fractions: the bound is 1/7 and the threshold is 1/20. Their difference is 13/140, or approximately 0.0929. It is positive: the draft cannot get below the threshold, whatever omissions P makes. The check uses the fractions; the decimals are only rounded values for reading. The appropriate step is to change the design before data collection, for example the number of blocks, rather than raise the threshold afterwards.

    This result describes the resolution of the test, meaning how fine the probabilities allowed by the draw can be. It does not measure any effect of the phone. A bound below the threshold would not guarantee a sufficient probability of detecting a real effect: that ability also depends on the possible omissions. The choice of the number of blocks remains to be discussed in the exercises.

Explain it in your own words

Why can the smallest attainable probability be calculated before any measurements, and why does this calculation not depend on the numbers of omissions P will make?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Choose the outcome measure and the threshold after looking at the omissions, keeping the ones that work.”

    Write down the outcome measure, the statistic D, the direction of the test and the threshold before the first measurement. Otherwise, the calculated probability no longer corresponds to the full set of results being compared.

    Return to the choices fixed before measurement

  • “A well-balanced order chosen by hand is more rigorous than a random draw.”

    With an order chosen by hand, the set of possible orders contains only one element and the smallest probability is one. Draw the order from the admissible set and record the draw before the session.

    Redo the exercise on choosing the order by hand

  • “A probability below the threshold proves that the phone makes everyone miss targets.”

    Here p is calculated under the null hypothesis of no effect on any block, for this person, these blocks and this draw, with no carryover between blocks. It gives neither the probability that the hypothesis is true, nor the size of the effect, nor its generality; D is read in omissions per block.

    Reread the unit of measurement and the assumptions in the example

Exercise 1 · Explained application

A design with 8 blocks

A design with 8 blocks of 5 minutes, with 4 blocks per condition, has 70 balanced orders. The rule on runs admits 34 of them. How many does it exclude, and what proportion of the balanced orders is excluded? Calculate the smallest one-sided and two-sided probabilities, then compare them with the threshold of 0.05. The values are fictional.

pmin, bilatéral=2|Ω|
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For the excluded orders, subtract the admissible orders from the balanced orders; for the probabilities, divide one or two by the number of admissible orders.

Compare with the solution

Excluded: 70 − 34 = 36. Their proportion is 36 / 70 = 18/35, or approximately 0.5143: slightly more than half of the balanced orders are excluded. Check: 36 + 34 = 70.

Minimum one-sided probability: 1 / 34 = 1/34 ≈ 0.0294, below 0.05. Minimum two-sided probability: 2 / 34 = 1/17 ≈ 0.0588, above 0.05.

With this design, only a one-sided test could get below the threshold: it would count only differences in the direction fixed in advance. It is justified only if the question requires it before the data; choosing it after seeing the direction of the result would invalidate the prespecified procedure. If the question remains two-sided, the design needs revision.

Check your reasoning

  • The excluded orders are obtained by subtraction.
  • Both probabilities are compared with the same threshold.
  • The choice of test direction is justified by the question, not by whether it gets below the threshold.

Exercise 2 · Design analysis

Choose the number of blocks

For a two-sided test with a threshold of 0.05, compare the designs with 6, 8 and 10 blocks. The design with 10 blocks has 84 admissible orders. Select the shortest one that can get below the threshold, then give its total duration and an ethical limitation to discuss.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Reuse the smallest two-sided probability of each design, then keep only those that get below the threshold.

Hint 2

The total duration is the number of blocks multiplied by their duration.

Compare with the solution

The smallest two-sided probabilities are 1/7 ≈ 0.1429 for 6 blocks, 1/17 ≈ 0.0588 for 8 blocks and 2 / 84 = 1/42 ≈ 0.0238 for 10 blocks. Only the last bound is below 0.05. Its exact margin is 1/20 − 1/42 = 11/420, or approximately 0.0262.

The task duration is 10 × 5 = 50 minutes, compared with 30 and 40 minutes for the shorter designs, excluding any breaks specified by the protocol. This duration must be acceptable to P, with the right to stop, and possible fatigue must still be discussed.

Among these candidates, 10 blocks is therefore the shortest design whose bound allows rejection. This is neither a promise of detecting an effect nor proof that the duration is acceptable. If the cost is excessive, the protocol must be revised before any measurements; lowering the numerical bound is not enough to justify data collection.

Check your reasoning

  • The three designs are compared with the same threshold.
  • The selected design is the shortest of the candidates whose bound is below the threshold, with no guarantee of detection.
  • The total duration and an ethical limitation are mentioned.

Exercise 3 · Protocol critique

The order chosen by hand

To “balance fatigue”, the team fixes the alternating order R T R T R T by hand for the 6 blocks. What is the smallest attainable probability, what does this choice retain, what does it lose, and what do you propose?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Count the orders from which the draw could have selected.

Hint 2

Think of anything that changes with the repeating pair, such as a habit or periodic noise.

Compare with the solution

The alternating order respects the rule on runs, but no different order could have been assigned: the reference set has 1 element. If the test calculation is applied to this reduced set, p is 1 / 1 = 1 whatever the value of D. This degenerate test cannot reject any hypothesis. Permuting afterwards among the 14 orders would use a draw that never took place.

It retains a regular distribution over time. It loses the randomness that separates the condition from any factor synchronized with even or odd positions. Fix: describe the set of admissible orders in advance, draw one of them before the session, record the draw and do not change it again.

Check your reasoning

  • The size of the set of orders is given.
  • The consequence for the minimum probability is calculated.
  • The fix preserves a random draw and a rule written in advance.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does a protocol specify, and when?

Before the first measurement: the unit, the conditions, the randomly drawn order, the outcome measure, the direction of the test and the threshold.

Recall 2Why draw the block order from a restricted set?

The draw avoids systematically fixing the link between condition and position in time. The restriction limits long runs without guaranteeing perfect balance; only orders that could actually be drawn serve as the reference for the test.

Recall 3How is the smallest attainable probability calculated?

In this uniform draw, the one-sided bound is 1 / |Ω|. For our difference of means, the two-sided bound is 2 / |Ω| because the T–R swap is admissible and changes the sign of D. These minima assume that no further ties increase the count.

Recall 4What does a probability below the threshold not tell us?

It gives neither the probability that the null hypothesis is true, nor the size of the effect, nor its generality beyond P, nor the absence of carryover between blocks.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the example, reconstruct the protocol for P: participant and blocks, conditions, random draw, outcome measure, null hypothesis and threshold fixed before the measurements. Find the 14 admissible orders again and explain why the two-sided bound 1/7 rules out rejection at the threshold 0.05. Finish with what remains to be decided before data collection and what this bound does not prove.
