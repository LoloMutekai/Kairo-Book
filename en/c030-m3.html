Level 1 · C030·M3

Variance

Your goalCalculate the variance of a random waiting time and explain what it adds to the mean, including its units and limitations.

Before you start
c029-m5 : Bayes · c030-m1 : discrete distributions · c030-m2 : expected value

✿01 See the idea

You are waiting for a tram. In our fictional model, the wait is 4 min with probability 3/4, or 12 min with probability 1/4. The mean is 6 min. Yet neither of these waits lasts exactly 6 min: the mean gives a center, rather than the waiting time for the next tram.

To describe what this center leaves out, look at how far the possible waits lie from it. The short wait is below the mean; the long wait is above it. Averaging the signed deviations makes them cancel out. We will therefore square each deviation before taking its average: this is the variance.

Squaring makes each contribution positive or 0 and gives greater emphasis to large deviations. Probability still plays its part: a rare wait does not count as much as a frequent wait. Variance combines the size of the deviations with the weight of each possibility.

The distribution is given for a single tram arrival. Its probabilities are assumptions for this exercise, rather than frequencies recorded on a real network. We are only finding the spread of this finite distribution; we are not estimating any parameter from a sample.

The rule to remember

Center each value on the expected value, square the deviation, multiply by its probability, then add. Variance is expressed in the original unit squared.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Measuring the spread of the tram waiting time

Keep the fictional waits of 4 min and 12 min, with respective probabilities 3/4 and 1/4. Calculate their variance using exact fractions, then check the result with another expression.

  1. State the distribution and find its center

    X denotes the random waiting time, in minutes. The letter xᵢ denotes one of its possible values; the index i identifies that possibility. pᵢ is the corresponding probability, with no unit. Probabilities are positive or 0 and must sum to 1. Here: 3/4 + 1/4 = 1.

    E denotes the expected value: the average of the values, weighted by their probabilities. The Greek letter µ, read as “mu”, denotes this expected value, in minutes. The symbol ∑ᵢ means that we add one contribution for each possible value.

    µ=E(X)=∑ipixi

    The short wait contributes 3/4 × 4 = 3 min; the long wait contributes 1/4 × 12 = 3 min. So µ = 3 + 3 = 6 min. The calculation assumes the stated finite distribution, with no further assumption about the shape of a curve.

  2. Center the values and see why the signs cause a problem

    Centering means subtracting the same mean from each value. For the short wait: 4 − 6 = -2 min. For the long wait: 12 − 6 = 6 min. A negative sign indicates a wait below the mean; it does not mean a negative waiting time.

    The average of these deviations is 3/4 × (-2) + 1/4 × 6 = -3/2 + 3/2 = 0 min. This 0 result comes from the choice of center. It does not mean that the waits are identical.

  3. Square the deviations

    The square of a deviation is its product with itself. It prevents negative and positive deviations from canceling out. We obtain (-2) × (-2) = 4 min² and 6 × 6 = 36 min².

    V and Var(X) are two notations for the variance of X. By definition, it is the expected value of the squared deviation from µ. The brackets group the quantity whose expected value we take; the exponent 2 means “squared”.

    V=Var(X)=E[(X−µ)2]=∑ipi(xi−µ)2

    The deviations are in minutes; their squares are in square minutes, written min². Since probability has no unit, variance keeps this squared unit.

  4. Apply the weights before adding

    Each square must count according to the probability of the wait that produced it. For the short wait: 3/4 × 4 = 3 min². For the long wait: 1/4 × 36 = 9 min². Taking a simple average of the squares would give equal weights and change the distribution.

    V=34×4+14×36=3+9=12 min²

    The result of 12 min² is positive. More generally, V ≥ 0, because each contribution is positive or 0. The variance is 0 exactly when all values with positive probability are equal to the mean.

  5. Check using the squares of the values

    There is a second approach. Expanding (X − µ) squared gives the square of X, minus 2 × µ × X, plus the square of µ. Taking the expected value replaces X with µ in the middle term; the probabilities sum to 1. This gives the following difference.

    V=E(X2)−µ2

    The squares of the waiting times are 4 × 4 = 16 min² and 12 × 12 = 144 min². Their weighted average is 3/4 × 16 + 1/4 × 144 = 12 + 36 = 48 min². This is E(X squared), rather than V.

    The square of the mean is 6 × 6 = 36 min². So V = 48 − 36 = 12 min². The two approaches agree: we centered the values correctly in the definition and kept the correct weights in the check.

  6. State what the result allows us to conclude

    Report the pair: expected value 6 min; variance 12 min². The first gives the center, the second the average of the squared distances from that center. Variance does not tell you how many minutes you will wait for the next tram.

    It is neither a probability of delay nor a maximum waiting time. For a given waiting-time limit, you must return to the possible values and their probabilities. The exercises address these questions separately, still using the same tram.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results and reconstruct the calculation. Why does the average of the signed deviations cancel out? What separate roles do squaring and probability play? Finish by giving the unit of the result and a conclusion it does not support.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The average deviation is 0, so the waiting time does not vary.”

    The deviations -2 min and 6 min cancel out after weighting. Square each one before taking the average: the contributions 3 and 9 min² no longer cancel out.

    Review centering, then squaring

  • “Taking a simple average of the squares is enough.”

    A simple average is suitable for equally likely possibilities. Here the weights are 3/4 and 1/4: multiply each square by its own probability. Also check that the probabilities sum to 1.

    Reread the probabilities above the points

  • “A variance of 12 means a wait of 12 minutes.”

    Squaring changes the unit: V = 12 min². The mean remains 6 min and the only possible waits are 4 or 12 min. To find the probability of exceeding a limit, return to the distribution, as in the last exercise.

    Distinguish waiting time, spread, and exceeding a limit

The contribution of the long wait

Hint 1

Write these in order: value minus mean, squared deviation, then the weight of this possibility.

Hint 2

The weight of the long wait is 1/4, even though its deviation is the largest.

Compare with the solution

The deviation is 12 − 6 = 6 min. Its square is 6 × 6 = 36 min². The required contribution is 1/4 × 36 = 9 min².

The square 36 min² describes only this distance from the center. To include it in an expected value, we must still account for the probability of the possibility that produces it. Adding the short-wait contribution 3 min² would give V = 12 min² again.

Check your reasoning

  • The probability 1/4 appears before the result 9 min².
  • The contribution is distinguished from the total variance.

Exercise 2 · Full application

Adding a fixed delay

In a variant of the same model, the tram adds exactly 3 min to every possible wait, with the same probabilities. Call this new waiting time Y. Predict what changes, then calculate the expected value and variance of Y without blindly adding the delay to V.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The values and their mean shift by the same delay. First calculate the new values.

Hint 2

After finding the new mean, calculate each difference again and compare it with the original deviations.

Compare with the solution

The new waits are 4 + 3 = 7 min and 12 + 3 = 15 min. Their expected value is 3/4 × 7 + 1/4 × 15 = 9 min. This gives 6 + 3 = 9 min again, because the probabilities sum to 1.

The deviations are 7 − 9 = -2 min and 15 − 9 = 6 min. Their squares remain 4 and 36 min². The contributions are therefore 3 and 9 min²; their sum gives 12 min².

The fixed delay increases the mean and leaves the variance unchanged: it shifts the whole model without moving its values farther from the new center. This reasoning assumes the same certain addition to every possibility; a delay that was itself random would raise a different question.

Check your reasoning

  • The new mean 9 min is calculated with the same probabilities.
  • The new deviations are explicitly compared with the original ones.
  • The conclusion gives 12 min² and explains why the fixed delay is not added to it.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

Is the mean enough to wait without worry?

Return to the original distribution. Another fictional setting for the same tram would always give a wait of 6 min. Are the means different? Compare the variances and the probability of waiting strictly more than 10 min. Respond to the claim: “Variance directly gives this probability.”

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A certain waiting time is always equal to its own mean. What remains after centering?

Hint 2

For the exceedance, identify the waiting times above 10 min and add their probabilities. The number expressed in min² cannot be used as a probability.

Compare with the solution

Both settings have the same expected value 6 min. For the setting with a certain waiting time, the deviation is always 6 − 6 = 0 min, so its variance is 0 min². For the original distribution, it is 12 min²: the same mean can accompany a different spread.

Under the original distribution, only the 12 min wait exceeds 10 min; its probability is 1/4, or 0.25 with no unit. Under the setting with a certain waiting time, 6 min does not exceed 10 min: the probability is 0. These probabilities are deduced from the distributions, rather than obtained by converting the variances.

Variance describes spread around a center, in both directions. An exceedance depends on a chosen limit and the values beyond it. The comparison in this exercise is possible because the complete distributions are given; a mean and a variance alone do not generally specify a discrete distribution.

Check your reasoning

  • The equal means of 6 min are stated without confusing the two distributions.
  • The variances 12 and 0 min² are justified by centering.
  • The exceedance is calculated from the values and their probabilities, with results that have no unit.
  • The claim is refuted by distinguishing spread from the event of exceeding a limit.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you reconstruct the variance of a finite discrete distribution from its values and probabilities?

Calculate the expected value µ. For each value, subtract µ, square this deviation, and multiply by its probability. Add the contributions; the variance has the unit of the variable squared.

Recall 2Why does the average of the signed deviations fail to measure spread?

Around the expected value, signed deviations cancel out on average. In our distribution, this average is 0 min while V = 12 min². Squaring prevents this cancellation.

Recall 3How can you check a variance without recalculating the squared deviations?

Calculate the expected value of the squared values, then subtract the square of the expected value. Here: 48 − 36 = 12 min². Forgetting to subtract the square of the mean gives a different quantity.

Recall 4What happens to the variance if the same certain delay is added to every wait?

It remains unchanged: the values and their mean shift together, so the deviations remain identical. The expected value increases by the delay; this addition must be certain and common to all possibilities.

Recall 5Why does variance not directly give the probability of exceeding a limit?

It averages the squared deviations in both directions and has a squared unit. For an exceedance, you must identify the values beyond the limit and their probabilities; the result has no unit.
