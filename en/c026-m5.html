Level 2 · C026·M5

Cost, turnaround time and policy simulation

Your goalReplay the same tasks in different orders, then choose according to an explicitly stated budget, turnaround time and deadlines.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A workshop prepares images for the same poster. The final render, labelled R, occupies the workstation for 6 s; the preview, labelled A, for 2 s. The requests are ready at the same time. The workstation processes only 1 task at a time: choosing which goes first changes when each requester receives their image.

Here, a policy is a rule that chooses the next task from those waiting. We compare “render then preview”, R → A, and “preview then render”, A → R. Simulating means replaying their consequences for the same requests, keeping the same durations and constraints.

The manager wants the render finished by 7 s and the preview by 4 s, measured from the start of the replay. A deadline is a time limit; turnaround time is the time from a request’s arrival to its completion. Being fast on average therefore does not mean meeting every deadline.

All values in this scene are fictional. To account for workstation use, we also invent an accounting unit, the credit: each second of work costs 2 credits, with a budget of 16 credits for the batch. This counter represents neither a bill, nor an energy measurement, nor a result observed on a real machine.

The rule to remember

Comparing policies requires the same inputs, checked constraints and a stated criterion; keep each task’s results alongside the average.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The same batch and constraints, in different orders

Ticket R precedes A in the initial queue; the 2 tickets are already available at time 0 s. R requires 6 s and A 2 s. The workstation is free, its capacity is 1, and the budget is 16 credits. Let us first replay the queue order, then the order of increasing durations.

  1. Specify what can and cannot change

    Durations are known and constant in the model. The tasks are independent, run without interruption once started, and are never abandoned. No new requests arrive. Starting or switching tasks takes no extra time; waiting uses no credits. The workstation stays occupied as long as a ticket is waiting.

    Only the order changes. The budget applies to the batch’s total workstation use, and capacity to the number of simultaneous tasks. The deadlines are initially preferences to compare; if they become mandatory, any order that misses them must be ruled out. These assumptions prevent confusing a preference with an admission condition.

  2. Define the times, durations and calculation

    The index i denotes a ticket, R or A. aᵢ is its arrival time, sᵢ its start time, fᵢ its completion time and dᵢ its deadline. These times are expressed in seconds from the shared origin. pᵢ is its execution time, wᵢ its waiting time before starting and Dᵢ its turnaround time until completion, all in seconds. Here, each aᵢ is 0 s.

    fi=si+pi;wi=si−ai;Di=fi−ai

    The first ticket starts at 0 s. The next starts when the previous ticket finishes, since all are already ready and there is no break. Adding pᵢ gives the completion time; subtracting the arrival time gives the time experienced by that ticket. The identity Dᵢ = wᵢ + pᵢ provides a check on the calculation.

  3. Calculate the cost before choosing an order

    q denotes the cost of workstation use, in credits per second; cᵢ is the ticket’s cost, C the batch’s cost and B the budget, in credits. The condition C ≤ B allows equality. It does not promise that deadlines will be met.

    ci=qpi;C=cR+cA≤B

    R costs 2 credits/s × 6 s = 12 credits; A costs 2 credits/s × 2 s = 4 credits. The batch costs 12 + 4 = 16 credits and leaves 16 − 16 = 0 credits. Reversing the tasks preserves these products, since neither the durations nor the rate changes.

  4. Replay the order R then A

    At 0 s, R takes the workstation and A waits. R finishes at 0 + 6 = 6 s, freeing the workstation. A can then start and finishes at 6 + 2 = 8 s. The workstation is then free. The replay thus respects the capacity of 1 task at all times.

    R waited 0 s and A 6 s. Their turnaround times are respectively 6 − 0 = 6 s and 8 − 0 = 8 s. The average is taken over tickets, rather than seconds of work.

    D¯=DR+DAn=6+82=7 s

    D with a bar denotes the average turnaround time; n is the number of tickets and has no unit. The numerator is 14 s. Dividing by n gives the turnaround time each ticket would have if this sum were distributed equally.

  5. Restart from the same state and replay A then R

    We reset the clock, queue, workstation and budget. We do not run this replay after the previous one. At 0 s, A takes the workstation; R waits. A finishes at 2 s and frees the workstation, then R occupies it until 2 + 6 = 8 s.

    A waits 0 s and R 2 s. A’s turnaround time is 2 s; R’s is 8 s. Their sum is 2 + 8 = 10 s and their average 10 / 2 = 5 s. The difference between averages is 7 − 5 = 2 s for this fictional batch. This calculation is not a measured improvement in a real system.

  6. Examine the deadlines ticket by ticket

    Tᵢ is the lateness beyond the deadline, in seconds. The word max means “keep the largest value”: keep 0 if the ticket finishes on time, and the difference fᵢ − dᵢ if it is positive. Finishing early therefore does not offset another ticket’s lateness.

    Ti=max(0,fi−di)

    With R → A, R finishes at 6 s before its deadline of 7 s, with 1 s to spare: its lateness is 0 s. A finishes at 8 s rather than by 4 s: its lateness is 8 − 4 = 4 s.

    With A → R, A has 4 − 2 = 2 s to spare and 0 s of lateness. R finishes at 8 s rather than by 7 s: its lateness is 8 − 7 = 1 s. Each order therefore misses 1 deadline, but for a different ticket.

  7. Check what must be preserved

    The total duration of workstation use is 6 + 2 = 8 s. The tasks follow each other without idle time: the final completion time must therefore be 8 s in each replay. The cost can also be recovered as 2 credits/s × 8 s = 16 credits. These checks concern the batch; adding the turnaround times would give a different quantity, since it includes waiting.

    Also check that the 2 tickets are present at the end, that there is no overlap and that the workstation returns to its free state. An average turnaround time calculated after omitting the late ticket would distort the comparison.

  8. State a decision and its scope

    If budget and capacity are the constraints, and the objective is the average turnaround time for this batch, A → R is preferable: 5 s compared with 7 s, at the same cost of 16 credits. However, R’s lateness must be stated: 1 s. The average alone hides it.

    If both tickets’ deadlines are mandatory, neither of the compared orders is admissible. Under our assumptions, these are the only possible orders without a break; adding idle time cannot make any completion earlier. A constraint must then be changed or the request renegotiated, instead of declaring success.

    This small batch demonstrates neither a good policy for all arrivals nor freedom from starvation in a persistent queue. Poorly estimated durations, staggered arrivals or a task switching cost would require a new replay. The simulation proves the consequences of the stated model, rather than its suitability for a real workshop.

Explain it in your own words

Why do the batch’s completion time and cost stay the same while the average turnaround time changes? Then explain what would make your preferred order inadmissible.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The average turnaround time decreases, so every ticket receives its result earlier.”

    Keep the tickets’ names. In A → R, the preview moves earlier, but R finishes at 8 s instead of 6 s. Then compare each completion time with its deadline before drawing a conclusion.

    Review the replays and deadline checks

  • “An order that is faster on average must use fewer credits.”

    Here, credits are charged for execution, rather than waiting. The same durations at the same rate give 16 credits in each order. Another charging method would require another formula.

    Test this idea with the reduced budget

  • “A favourable simulation is enough to deploy this policy.”

    Recall the assumptions and the fictional origin of the durations. An exact calculation for this batch shows neither how the program is connected, nor its execution, nor whether the requests are representative. Prepare that evidence separately.

    Distinguish the reviewed code from the evidence still needed

Exercise 1 · Reconstructing the calculation

Distinguish waiting time from turnaround time

Hide the calculations. For R → A and then A → R, recover each ticket’s waiting time and the average waiting time. The average execution time is 4 s. Recover the average turnaround times by adding this duration to each average waiting time.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Waiting ends when work starts. The ticket that goes first does not wait.

Hint 2

The average of a sum is the sum of the averages: apply Dᵢ = wᵢ + pᵢ to the same tickets.

Compare with the solution

In R → A, the waiting times are 0 s for R and 6 s for A: their sum is 6 s, so their average is 6 / 2 = 3 s.

In A → R, A waits 0 s and R 2 s: the average is 2 / 2 = 1 s. The average execution time remains (6 + 2) / 2 = 4 s.

We recover 3 + 4 = 7 s for R → A and 1 + 4 = 5 s for A → R. Waiting time excludes the ticket’s work; turnaround time includes it.

Check your reasoning

  • Assign each waiting time to the correct ticket, with its unit.
  • Calculate an average over all tickets.
  • Recover turnaround time using a different decomposition from completion times alone.

Exercise 2 · Constraint and counterexample

A slightly reduced budget

The same batch’s budget changes to 15 credits. Everything else stays the same and both tickets must be executed in full. Does moving the preview first allow the batch to be admitted? Calculate the shortfall and say what should be decided before starting.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The budget applies to total execution, rather than the average turnaround time.

Hint 2

Compare the sum of the costs with the budget before looking at the order.

Compare with the solution

Each order still costs 12 + 4 = 16 credits. The shortfall is 16 − 15 = 1 credit. The condition C ≤ B fails for the complete batch, regardless of its order.

The budget or batch contents must be renegotiated before starting. Deferring a ticket to another budget would change the request and must be stated; it does not make the complete batch admissible under the current budget. Our model does not decide which ticket to abandon.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the cost invariant when reversing the order.
  • Give the exact shortfall in credits.
  • Refuse admission of the complete batch without hiding a ticket.

Exercise 3 · Reasoned decision

The render’s deadline becomes mandatory

Return to the budget of 16 credits. Only the limit for render R, 7 s, becomes mandatory; the preview’s remains a preference. Among the compared orders, which remains admissible? State the chosen average turnaround time and the preview’s lateness. What would you answer if both limits became mandatory?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First filter the orders by the render’s completion time, then compare the averages of the remaining orders.

Hint 2

A favourable average cannot offset a violated mandatory constraint.

Compare with the solution

R → A completes R at 6 s, before the limit of 7 s: this order remains admissible and costs 16 credits. A → R completes R at 8 s, with 1 s of lateness: this order is ruled out.

We therefore choose R → A with 7 s of average turnaround time and 4 s of lateness for the preview. This lateness must be reported to the requester; admissibility here concerns only the render’s mandatory deadline, budget and capacity.

If both tickets’ limits become mandatory, R → A fails for A and A → R fails for R. No order works in the model. The change concerns the decision rule; no duration or replay result has changed.

Check your reasoning

  • Check the mandatory constraint before the average objective.
  • Name the ticket whose preference remains unmet.
  • Conclude that it is impossible under the assumptions if all deadlines are mandatory.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be preserved to compare the workshop’s policies fairly?

The same tickets, arrivals, durations, capacity, budget and cost rule. Reset the state before each replay; only the order varies.

Recall 2How can you recover a ticket’s turnaround time from its waiting time?

Add its execution time to its waiting time. Turnaround time runs from arrival to completion; waiting time runs from arrival to the start of work.

Recall 3Why does reversing the tickets preserve this batch’s cost?

The cost is the workstation use rate multiplied by the executed durations. These do not change, and waiting costs nothing in this model.

Recall 4In what order should you examine the constraints and the average turnaround time objective?

First rule out orders that violate a mandatory constraint, then compare the objective among those that remain. An average does not repair a missed mandatory deadline.

Recall 5What does our exact replay demonstrate, and what remains to be established?

It demonstrates the consequences of the assumptions for this fictional batch. Whether durations and arrivals are representative, how a real system is connected, its execution and its results remain to be established.
