Level 2 · C098·M4

Forecast errors and baseline comparison

Your goalCalculate forecast errors and compare their average size with those of a baseline on the same dates, without confusing accuracy with the result of a buying simulation.

Before you start
c097-m5 : risk and horizon · c067-m5 : pipeline · c090-m5 : stationarity · c037-m5 : controlled cleaning · c098-m1 : chronology, delay and available information · c098-m2 : cash, units and total value · c098-m3 : fees, slippage and terminal assumption

✿01 See the idea

Each evening, a log records a forecast of the next day’s closing price for a fictional asset. Another column follows a very simple rule: forecast tomorrow’s price as the latest known price. This rule is our baseline. Before looking at the results, ask yourself: does the model do better than simply copying the evening’s price would have done?

On evening A, the known price is 102 €. For B, the model forecasts 100 € and the baseline 102 €. The next day, we observe 99 €. The model forecast was too high by 1 €; the baseline forecast, by 3 €. Comparing their differences from the same observed price gives “better forecasting” a concrete meaning.

The direction of an error and its size answer different questions. Under our convention, a forecast that is too low has a negative error; it still misses its target. To measure the size of the errors, we take their distances from 0 before averaging them. This prevents an upward error from cancelling a downward error.

All prices and forecasts are fictional and chosen for the calculation. We assume that the forecasts were recorded before the target close and were not used to tune the model. We assess only the quality of the forecast prices; no buying decision follows from this log.

The rule to remember

Same target, same horizon, same dates and same calculation rule: measure the distances, then compare with the baseline declared before the results.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The log of closing prices A, B and C

Before A, the latest known price is 100 €. The observed closing prices are A: 102 €, B: 99 €, C: 103 €. The model forecasts, made on the day before each target, are respectively 101 €, 100 € and 104 €. Each time, the baseline copies the latest known closing price. Let us calculate and then check their mean absolute error.

  1. Define what can be compared

    The index t denotes a target date in the log: A, B or C. yt is the price observed at its close; ŷt is the model’s forecast for that close; bt is the baseline’s forecast. All these quantities are in euros. The hat on ŷ means “forecast”. n is the number of dates assessed and has no unit; here n = 3.

    The horizon is 1 trading day for each forecast. The model and the baseline have the same history available up to the previous day; the target becomes known only afterwards. We assume complete, comparable prices with no change of scale. Each date counts as much as the others. This descriptive mean requires no assumption of a normal distribution or of independence between dates.

    The baseline follows bt = y on the previous date: it gives 100 € for A, 102 € for B and 99 € for C. This is not a constant forecast throughout the log: as soon as a new closing price is available, it becomes the forecast for the next day. The rule was chosen before examining the errors.

  2. Subtract in a stated order

    etᴹ denotes the model’s signed error and etᵀ the baseline’s signed error, in euros; M and T are method labels. Our convention is always forecast minus observation. A positive error means that the forecast is too high; a negative error means it is too low.

    etM=y^t−yt;etT=bt−yt

    Model: A, 101 − 102 = -1 €; B, 100 − 99 = 1 €; C, 104 − 103 = 1 €. Baseline: A, 100 − 102 = -2 €; B, 102 − 99 = 3 €; C, 99 − 103 = -4 €.

    Check the direction: at A, 101 € is less than 102 €, so the model’s error must be negative. At B, the forecasts exceed the actual price: the errors must be positive.

  3. Move from directions to distances

    atᴹ and atᵀ denote the absolute errors, in euros. The bars |e| mean “the distance of e from 0”: keep a positive or 0 error as it is; take the opposite of a negative error. Downward errors therefore still count as errors.

    atM=|etM|;atT=|etT|

    For the model, we obtain A: 1 €, B: 1 €, C: 1 €. For the baseline: 2 €, 3 €, 4 €. Check at C: the baseline is 4 € below the target; its distance must be 4 €, even though its signed error is -4 €.

  4. Add, then divide by the number of shared dates

    EAM is the French abbreviation for mean absolute error used in the formula. EAMᴹ is the model’s mean absolute error; EAMᵀ is the baseline’s. Add all the distances, then divide by n. This gives a mean distance per forecast, expressed in euros rather than as a percentage. This quantity is also often labelled MAE in code.

    EAMM=aAM+aBM+aCMn

    Model: 1 + 1 = 2 €, then 2 + 1 = 3 €. Its mean is 3 ÷ 3 = 1 €. Baseline: 2 + 3 = 5 €, then 5 + 4 = 9 €. Its mean is 9 ÷ 3 = 3 €.

    Check the unit: the sum is in euros; dividing by a number of forecasts keeps the mean in euros. For the model, all distances are identical: their mean must give that same distance, 1 €. A missing target would not be a 0 error: the dates that remain comparable would need to be made explicit.

  5. Compare and check by another method

    Let Δ, the letter delta, denote the difference EAMᵀ − EAMᴹ. With this order, a positive Δ indicates a smaller mean error for the model. Here Δ = 3 − 1 = 2 €.

    Let us repeat the calculation date by date: the distance difference, baseline minus model, is at A 2 − 1 = 1 €, at B 3 − 1 = 2 €, at C 4 − 1 = 3 €. Their sum is 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 €; their mean 6 ÷ 3 = 2 € gives Δ again. This check works because the dates and their weights are the same.

    The precise finding is: on A, B and C, with this target and this error measure, the model is more accurate than the baseline. The difference of 2 € concerns price error, not money earned. No record of positions, fees or execution has been assessed here. This small log demonstrates neither future superiority nor the model’s relevance to another target.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results and reconstruct the log. Why does the baseline change after each close? Why must you take the distances before calculating the mean? Finish with a sentence stating the result and the exact limits of the comparison.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Errors with opposite signs cancel, so the model is on target.”

    The signed mean describes an average direction, not the average size of the errors. Convert each error into a distance before adding. In the log, the model misses every target by 1 €, even though some errors cancel.

    Review the move from errors to distances

  • “I can compare the means even if the baseline does not have all the dates.”

    Match the forecasts by target date and horizon. State which dates are missing, then recalculate the means over the same rows. Even having the same number of rows is insufficient if their dates differ.

    Repair the incomplete log in the last exercise

  • “The difference of 2 € proves that I will earn 2 € by buying.”

    Δ compares price errors. To calculate the result of buying, we would need a decision rule, quantities, execution prices and costs in a separate record. The forecast score does not provide that result.

    Reread the finding limited to forecast errors

Compare with the solution

Model: 100 − 99 = 1 €, positive because its forecast is too high; distance 1 €. Baseline: 102 − 99 = 3 €, also positive; distance 3 €.

The difference is 3 − 1 = 2 €. Here, positive means that the model makes a smaller error at B. This compares this row; it is not yet a mean over the whole log.

Exercise 2 · Application and objection

A mean that is too reassuring

Someone adds the model’s signed errors, then divides by 3. Calculate the exact result, then round it to 2 decimal places. Is this the mean absolute error? Recalculate the mean absolute error and explain what the other calculation concealed.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The signed errors are -1 €, 1 € and 1 €. Start with the first dates.

Hint 2

Compare the mean of these numbers with the mean of their distances from 0.

Compare with the solution

The signed sum starts with -1 + 1 = 0 €, then 0 + 1 = 1 €. The signed mean is 1/3 € = 1/3 € ≈ 0.33 €.

The negative error at A cancelled a positive error in the sum. This calculation describes the average direction of the error; it does not give the mean distance to the target. The distances are 1 €, 1 € and 1 €: their mean remains 1 €. Taking the distance of the signed mean afterwards does not repair the cancellation.

Check your reasoning

  • You keep the fraction 1/3 € until rounding for display.
  • You identify the cancellation between A and B.
  • You take the absolute value of each error before averaging to recover 1 €.

Exercise 3 · Transfer to checking the log

A baseline row is missing

In a copy of the same log, the baseline’s error at C was not recorded. The published scores are 1 € for the model over A, B, C and 2.50 € for the baseline over A, B. Can you subtract these scores to report the difference between methods? Repair the comparison using only the shared dates, then explain what recovering C would add.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write the dates under each mean; the rows must match before you subtract.

Hint 2

To compare over A and B, divide the corresponding sums by 2 for each method. Do not replace the missing row with 0.

Compare with the solution

The reported scores do not cover the same targets. Even though the model happens to keep the same mean over A and B, you must redo the calculation and state this shared scope.

Over A and B, the model gives (1 + 1)/2 = 2/2 = 1 €. The baseline gives (2 + 3)/2 = 5/2 = 5/2 € = 2.50 €. The comparable difference is 2.50 − 1 = 1.50 €. It describes only A and B; the omission must still be reported.

If row C is recovered with its provenance, its baseline distance is 4 €. We then return to the complete log: baseline 3 €, model 1 €, difference 2 €. The missing row was not a perfect forecast. Neither the partial comparison nor the complete comparison guarantees a future result.

Check your reasoning

  • You refuse to present two different sets of dates as a comparison over a shared set.
  • You obtain 1 € and 2.50 € over A and B, then the difference 1.50 €.
  • You state the omission of C and distinguish the partial calculation from the complete calculation.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Under the convention forecast minus observation, what does a negative error mean?

The forecast is too low. The sign gives the direction; the distance from 0 gives the size of the error, in the same unit as the price.

Recall 2How do you reconstruct a mean absolute error, and why use this order of operations?

Calculate each error, take its distance from 0, add the distances and divide by the number of dates assessed. Taking the distances before adding prevents errors with opposite signs from cancelling. For prices in euros, the mean remains in euros.

Recall 3What rule does our baseline follow, and what is it for?

It forecasts the next closing price as equal to the latest known closing price. Declared before the results, it provides a simple reference for judging what the model adds; it does not claim to be a perfect forecast.

Recall 4What should you check before subtracting the methods’ mean errors?

The same target, unit, horizon, dates and weights, using information available before the target. Make omissions visible and recalculate over an explicitly shared set. An equal number of rows does not prove that the dates match.

Recall 5What does the difference of 2 € in the log prove, and what does it leave open?

It shows a mean absolute error smaller by 2 € for the model over A, B and C. It does not quantify any money earned or guarantee future superiority. A buying rule with its costs requires a separate assessment.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Recover the difference using the differences date by date. Finish by distinguishing this descriptive finding from the result of buying and from a promise about the future.
