Level 4 · C246·M4

Finite static replication

Your goalBuild a finite basket with fixed quantities from the changes in slope of a payoff, then prove exactly what this basket reproduces.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A fictional contract pays an amount on a fixed date. Its payoff depends only on the final price of an asset. It guarantees 5 monetary units, then participates in the increase from 100. Participation becomes stronger at 120, before stopping at 140. How can we reproduce this piecewise straight line without changing the positions during the life of the contract?

A European call option provides a simple building block: it pays nothing below its threshold, then its payoff rises in a straight line above it. Its threshold is also called the strike price. Buying a fraction of this option adds a smaller slope; selling it subtracts a slope. A fixed amount provides the base.

Replication means obtaining the same payoff as the contract for every possible final price. It is static when the quantities are fixed at the start and held until the common maturity. Finite means that a finite number of building blocks is used. The values of these positions may vary in the meantime: their quantities remain fixed.

The diagram shows the contract to be reproduced. Between its breakpoints, the payoff is explicitly affine, meaning a straight line. It is continuous at the joins and constant outside the outermost thresholds. These details are part of the contract; they cannot be inferred from a table of a few values. All amounts and prices on this page are fictional.

The rule to remember

Keep the base amount, then add a quantity of options equal to the change in slope at each threshold. Prove equality over the intervals, as well as at the joins.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Build and check the basket for the capped contract

The abbreviation for monetary units is m.u. Let T denote the common maturity, S_T the random final price of the asset and s a possible value of this price, with s ≥ 0. The contract pays g(s), in m.u.: 5 up to 100, 15 at 120, 45 at 140, with straight segments between these thresholds and a constant cap beyond them.

  1. Specify what is reproduced

    The letters K_a, K_b and K_c denote the strike prices 100, 120 and 140 m.u. The payoff depends only on S_T, with no barrier or condition on the path. The amount received at T is distinct from the price paid for the contract today.

    To implement the basket, we assume European options settled in cash, the same underlying asset and maturity, a contract multiplier equal to 1, and permission to hold fractional quantities and sell options. Fees, bid–ask spreads, margin calls and defaults are excluded from this exercise. No assumption about a diffusion or a price distribution is needed for the terminal identity.

  2. Understand the building block that changes a slope

    The notation x⁺ means max(x, 0), the positive part. Thus h_K(s) = (s − K)⁺ is the payoff in m.u. of a call option with threshold K, also in m.u. Below K its slope is 0; above it the slope is 1. A quantity w of this option therefore adds w to the slope when K is crossed. A negative quantity corresponds to a short position.

    More generally, if g is continuous and piecewise affine over the entire domain s ≥ 0, with n strictly positive breakpoints K_j and a constant slope after the last one, it has the following finite representation. n is the number of breakpoints; j runs through them. a is g(0), in m.u.; b is the initial slope; w_j is the slope just after K_j minus the slope just before it. b and the w_j are dimensionless, since they relate m.u. of payoff to m.u. of price.

    g(s)=a+bs+∑j=1nwj(s−Kj)+

    The sum means adding the n terms. A function with a jump in value does not satisfy the continuity assumption: ordinary call options, which are all continuous, cannot produce this jump with a finite sum. We stay with the continuous case here.

  3. Calculate the slopes of the contract

    A slope is an increase in payoff divided by the corresponding increase in price. The widths are K_b − K_a = 20 m.u. and K_c − K_b = 20 m.u. The increases in payoff are 15 − 5 = 10 m.u. and 45 − 15 = 30 m.u. Division gives dimensionless quantities.

    mab=15−5120−100=1/2;mbc=45−15140−120=3/2

    m_ab denotes the slope between K_a and K_b; m_bc denotes the slope between K_b and K_c. The slopes before K_a and after K_c are 0. In decimal form for reading: 0.50 then 1.50 m.u. of payoff per m.u. of price. The fractions remain exact throughout the calculation.

  4. Take the differences in slope

    At K_a, the slope changes from 0 to 1/2: w_a = 1/2 − 0 = 1/2. At K_b, it changes from 1/2 to 3/2: w_b = 3/2 − 1/2 = 1. At K_c, it drops back to 0: w_c = 0 − 3/2 = -3/2. The subscripts a, b, c label the thresholds and their quantities.

    The base amount is a = 5 m.u. and the initial slope is b = 0: no position in the asset is needed here. The basket therefore holds the fixed amount and the following 3 positions:

    g(s)=5+12(s−100)++1(s−120)+−32(s−140)+

    The negative sign of the last position is necessary: a sum of options that are all bought could only increase the slope at the breakpoints, whereas our contract must reduce it at the cap.

  5. Prove equality over the entire domain

    Before K_a, all the positive parts are 0: the basket is worth 5 m.u. Between K_a and K_b, only the first option is active; the basket starts at 5 with slope 1/2. It therefore reaches 5 + 1/2 × 20 = 15 m.u. at K_b.

    Between K_b and K_c, the options already active give the cumulative slope 1/2 + 1 = 3/2. Starting from the previous join, we reach 15 + 3/2 × 20 = 45 m.u. at K_c. Beyond it, the cumulative slope is 1/2 + 1 + (-3/2) = 0: the payoff remains 45 m.u.

    Why does this prove more than checking a few points? On each interval, two affine functions with the same slope and the same starting value are identical. The positive parts are continuous and the new option is worth 0 at its threshold: equality also holds at the joins. This proof holds for every possible s, independently of its probabilities.

  6. Work through the payoff at a specific final price

    Take s = 130 m.u. The options pay max(130 − 100, 0) = 30 m.u., max(130 − 120, 0) = 10 m.u. and max(130 − 140, 0) = 0 m.u., respectively. Apply the positive part before multiplying by a quantity, even when that quantity is negative.

    Signed contributions: 1/2 × 30 = 15 m.u.; 1 × 10 = 10 m.u.; (-3/2) × 0 = 0 m.u. The first contributions total 25 m.u., all the options total 25 m.u., then the fixed amount gives 5 + 25 = 30 m.u.

    An independent check using the contract segment: start from 15 m.u. at 120, then add 3/2 × (130 − 120) = 15 m.u. This gives 30 m.u. from the contract, without evaluating the basket again.

  7. Move from payoff to initial cost

    Also assume a 0 interest rate for this exercise and an arbitrage-free market that allows the stated positions. The fixed amount of 5 m.u. at T then costs 5 m.u. today. C_a, C_b and C_c denote the current prices, in m.u., of the options with thresholds K_a, K_b and K_c. Give them the fictional quotes 26, 14 and 6 m.u.; these prices are distinct from the payoffs calculated earlier.

    V denotes the initial cost of the basket. Adding the positions gives V = 5 + (1/2)C_a + (1)C_b + (-3/2)C_c. The purchases cost 13 and 14 m.u., totalling 27 m.u. The sale contributes -9 m.u. of signed cost, meaning an inflow. The options cost 18 m.u. in total, then V = 23 m.u.

    The law of one price assigns this same price to a contract replicated exactly under these assumptions: a difference would allow us to buy the cheaper one and sell the other, with final payoffs that offset each other. The amount received at a particular maturity remains random; V is not a profit. No real-world price is inferred from our fictional quotes.

  8. Define the scope of the result

    The identity is exact because the entire contract is continuous and affine between the specified breakpoints. For a curved function whose values are known only at the nodes, the same differences in slope would reproduce its affine interpolation, without automatically reproducing the original function. The last exercise quantifies this distinction.

    A terminal equality of functions does not depend on a Monte Carlo draw. However, it does not remove a possible error in the prices used to value the building blocks. For a barrier contract that depends on the path, matching at T alone is insufficient: the crossing conditions and the boundary error require a separate analysis, reserved for the next unit.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the quantities in the second diagram. Reconstruct them from the slopes in the first, then justify why the basket stays at the cap for all prices above the last threshold. Finish by distinguishing terminal payoff from initial cost.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The option quantity is the slope of each segment.”

    An option that becomes active does not replace the previous ones. The slopes already introduced remain present: the change in slope must be added. At threshold 120, buying 3/2 instead of 1 would count part of the slope twice.

    Review the differences in slope and their cumulative sum

  • “A negative quantity is a calculation error.”

    Selling an option subtracts its contribution. This is necessary to bring the slope back to 0 at the cap. Calculate the basket without this sale at price 160: the second exercise shows the exact overshoot.

    Restore the position at the cap

  • “The basket passes through the nodes, so it reproduces any function between them.”

    This becomes true if the function is explicitly affine on each interval and the extensions are specified. A curve can share all the nodes and deviate from them in between. State the class of functions and prove equality over its domain.

Identify the thresholds already crossed. An inactive option pays 0, even when its quantity is negative.

Hint 2

The segment starts from 5 m.u. at threshold 100, with slope 1/2.

Compare with the solution

The option payoffs are 10, 0 and 0 m.u. The first contribution is 1/2 × 10 = 5 m.u.; the others are 0. Including the fixed amount, the basket is worth 5 + 5 = 10 m.u.

The contract also gives 5 + 1/2 × (110 − 100) = 10 m.u. This amount is received at T in this scenario; V = 23 m.u. is paid at the start. Replication does not assert that every scenario repays the initial cost.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis · reconstruct a position

The forgotten sale

Someone keeps the fixed amount and the positions at thresholds 100 and 120, but forgets the one at 140. At the final price 160 m.u., what do they receive? Recover the missing quantity from the required final slope, then check the restored payoff.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Above all the thresholds, the slope is the sum of the option quantities.

Hint 2

The missing quantity must cancel the sum 1/2 + 1. At the given price, the option with threshold 140 pays 20 m.u.

Compare with the solution

Without the sale, the options pay 160 − 100 = 60 and 160 − 120 = 40 m.u. Their contributions are 30 and 40 m.u., totalling 70 m.u. The total amount is 75 m.u., above the cap of 45 m.u.

The remaining slope is 3/2. To reach 0, the quantity to add is -3/2. The option with threshold 140 pays 20 m.u.; its contribution is (-3/2) × 20 = -30 m.u. The restored payoff is 75 + (-30) = 45 m.u.

The decisive check is also symbolic: above the cap, the sum of the quantities is 0. The payoff therefore remains constant for all prices in this region, as well as in the tested scenario.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate 75 m.u. before restoring the position.
  • Deduce -3/2 by cancelling the slope, without arbitrarily fitting a single point.
  • Recover 45 m.u. and explain why it is constant after the last threshold.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · test a proof

The same nodes conceal a different rule

Change only the rule of the same contract between K_b and K_c. It becomes g̃(s) = g(s) + γ(s − K_b)(K_c − s), with γ = 1/100 per m.u. Everywhere else, g̃ = g. Does the basket constructed from the values at the nodes change? Is it still exact? Justify your answer at the endpoints of the interval, then calculate the difference at s = 130 m.u.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At each endpoint of the interval, one of the added factors is 0. The values at the nodes therefore remain the same.

Hint 2

At 130 m.u., the two distances to the endpoints are 10 and 10 m.u. Multiply them, then apply γ.

Compare with the solution

g̃, pronounced “g tilde”, is the modified payoff; γ is a curvature coefficient, in inverse m.u., so that the added term is a payoff. At K_b or K_c, the added product is 0. The values at all the nodes are preserved, as are the unchanged outer regions.

The basket calculated only from these values therefore keeps the same quantities. At s = 130 m.u., the distances are 10 and 10 m.u. Their product is 100 m.u.²; multiplied by γ = 1/100 per m.u., it adds 1 m.u. The modified contract pays 31 m.u., while the basket still pays 30 m.u.

The discrepancy is 1 m.u.: the values at the nodes did not detect the interior curvature. The basket still reproduces the affine interpolation of these values, but g̃ is no longer affine between K_b and K_c. This counterexample does not contradict the previous proof; it removes one of its assumptions.

Check your reasoning

  • Show why the values at the nodes do not change.
  • Respect the units of γ and obtain a difference of 1 m.u.
  • Identify the lost assumption of affine behaviour; do not claim a simulation error.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do “static” and “finite” mean in our basket?

The quantities are fixed at the start, then held until the common maturity; the basket contains a finite number of positions. The values of the positions may change in the meantime.

Recall 2What option quantity must be added at a breakpoint? Why?

The slope just after minus the slope just before. A call option adds its quantity to the slope above its threshold; the previous positions remain active.

Recall 3How do you move from a candidate basket to a proof of equality for every final price?

Check the same initial value, the same slopes on each interval and continuity at the joins, then the outer extensions. This proof assumes that the target is continuous and piecewise affine.

Recall 4What does agreement at the nodes prove if the target can be curved between them?

It proves agreement at those nodes. The basket based on changes in slope reproduces their affine interpolation; a different curve can keep the same nodes. Equality with the target still has to be proved.

Recall 5When can the valuation of the contract be replaced by the cost of its basket?

When the payoffs are identical in all states and the positions can be implemented under the stated financing and no-arbitrage assumptions. The cost is a sum of signed current prices, distinct from a terminal payoff or a profit.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the contract, its slopes, their differences and the role of the base amount. Then write the basket and prove its equality interval by interval. Finish with the sentence that separates exact replication from interpolation, then the one that separates terminal payoff from initial price.
