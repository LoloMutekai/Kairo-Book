Imbalanced classes

Your goalExpose a ranking that ignores rare cases and calculate what changes when weighting gives each class equal emphasis.

Before you start
c067-m5 : pipeline · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c059-m6 : deterministic discrete Bellman · c099-m1 : logistic regression

✿01 See the idea

A workshop sorts incoming messages into “urgent” and “ordinary”. Urgent messages must be reviewed by a person. In our fictional batch, an independent check assigned 10 “urgent” labels and 90 “ordinary” labels. A class is a possible category; classes are imbalanced when their counts differ greatly.

Rule A writes “ordinary” on every message. It correctly classifies 90 messages out of 100, or 90 %, while missing every urgent message. Its good score comes from the composition of the batch. It has not learned to recognize urgent messages.

Rule B detects 8 urgent messages, misses 2, and raises 9 false alarms on ordinary messages. It makes more errors overall than A. Yet if the aim is to avoid missing urgent messages, the proportion of correct answers alone is no longer enough to decide.

We will first count every message equally, then give each class equal emphasis in another calculation. This second view changes the question the score answers. It changes neither the messages, their labels, nor the decisions already made. All counts and results are fictional: they do not measure any real tool.

The rule to remember

Start with the class counts and the majority rule; keep the errors for each class separate before choosing how to weight them.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Evaluate the same decisions under two conventions

The fictional batch is reserved for evaluation: no rule is adjusted using these messages. Each message has exactly one reference label, assumed correct for the calculation, and each rule produces exactly one decision. Both classes are present. We are studying finite counts; extrapolating to new messages will require a further check.

  1. Measure the imbalance before looking at the score

    N is the total number of messages; nᵤ and no count urgent and ordinary messages respectively. Subscript u means urgent and o ordinary. These quantities are expressed in messages. N = nᵤ + no = 10 + 90 = 100 messages.

    The fraction of urgent messages in the batch is nᵤ/N = 10/100 = 1/10, or 10 %. The ratio no/nᵤ = 9 indicates that there are as many ordinary messages as in 9 groups of 10 messages. The frequency observed in this batch is not a rule guaranteed to hold every day.

  2. Build the majority baseline

    Accuracy a is the fraction of correct decisions; C is their count, in messages. Division cancels the unit. The percentage is 100 × a. Accuracy does not mean precision, which will be defined in the next unit.

    a=CN

    Rule A obtains C = 90, because only ordinary messages are correctly classified. Thus a = 90/100 = 0.90, or 90 %. It misses 10 urgent messages and produces 0 false alarms. The score accurately describes the total, but on its own it hides this breakdown.

  3. Reconstruct all of B’s counts

    Among urgent messages, 10 − 8 = 2 are missed. Among ordinary messages, 90 − 9 = 81 are correctly classified. Add the correct classifications from both classes to obtain C = 8 + 81 = 89 messages.

    B’s accuracy is 89/100 = 0.89, or 89 %. Check using the errors: 2 + 9 = 11 messages, and 89 + 11 = 100. A wins on accuracy, while B misses fewer urgent messages: these statements can both be true.

  4. Explicitly choose a different allocation of attention

    Let us choose to give the classes the same total weight. For a class c, n_c is its count in messages and w_c is its weight per message, with no unit. Here c can be u or o. The factor 2 is the number of classes. We require n_c to be strictly greater than 0.

    wc=N2nc

    For urgent messages: 2 × 10 = 20, then wᵤ = 100/20 = 5. For ordinary messages: 2 × 90 = 180, then wo = 100/180 = 5/9. Keep this exact fraction rather than its rounded value 0.56.

    Check: the urgent class has mass 10 × 5 = 50; the ordinary class has mass 90 × 5/9 = 50. Their sum is 100 weighted counting units. An urgent message weighs 9 times as much as an ordinary one: small class counts no longer disappear in the sum. This is a convention of equal attention to the classes, not an estimate of the actual cost of errors.

  5. Calculate the weighted error fraction

    Eᵤ and Eo denote the numbers of errors among urgent and ordinary messages, expressed in messages. L is the sum of the weights of misclassified messages divided by the sum of the weights of all messages; L has no unit. An error contributes its weight, while a correct decision contributes 0 to the numerator. The smaller L is, the better the rule satisfies this convention.

    L=wuEu+woEowunu+wono

    For B, missed urgent messages contribute 2 × 5 = 10; false alarms contribute 9 × 5/9 = 5. The weighted error total is 10 + 5 = 15. Divide by the total mass: L = 15/100 = 3/20, or 15 % of the weighted mass.

    For A, all urgent messages are missed and all ordinary messages are correctly classified: L = 50/100 = 1/2, or 50 % of the weighted mass. B is preferable under this specific criterion. The 15 % is not the raw proportion of misclassified messages: that proportion counts 11 errors out of 100 messages.

  6. Check by another route and limit the conclusion

    Substituting the definitions of the weights and cancelling N gives the mean of the error fractions for each class. The symbol L still represents the same criterion; there is no new parameter.

    L=Eunu+Eono2=210+9902=0.15

    This equality explains the change in ranking: each class occupies half of the calculation, whatever its size. We also check the bounds: no errors would give L = 0; misclassifying everything would give L = 1. An absent class would prevent us from calculating its weight and evaluating performance on that class.

    Class weights can also change how examples contribute during training. Here we have only recalculated a criterion on fixed decisions: no training or future improvement has been demonstrated. If weights or a rule are chosen using a validation batch, keep another test batch intact for the final evaluation. Document the natural class counts and the counts for each class; do not secretly balance the test to make a score look better.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The overall score is good, so urgent messages are handled well.”

    Write the rule that always answers “ordinary”, calculate its accuracy, then count the missed urgent messages. Having many ordinary messages is enough to produce the score without detecting any urgent messages.

    Review the batch and the majority rule

  • “A weighted error of 15 % means 15 incorrect messages out of 100.”

    The weighted numerator adds weights, not just messages. B makes 11 actual errors in the fictional batch. Reconstruct the urgent and ordinary contributions separately, then divide by the total mass.

    Recalculate the masses and the weighted error

  • “Simply duplicating urgent messages in the test proves that the problem is solved.”

    Duplication changes the composition of the calculation without improving the decisions. A copy does not provide an independent observation. Keep the final evaluation test intact and state any weighting convention.

    Examine the copied test in the last exercise

The weights are 100/(2 × 10) = 5 and 100/(2 × 90) = 5/9. Each class carries 50 units; the denominator is 100.

B contributes 2 × 5 + 9 × 5/9 = 15 error units. Thus L = 15/100 = 3/20. Another route: (2/10 + 9/90)/2 = 3/20.

  • Obtain the same exact fraction by both routes.
  • Link the preference to an explicit convention.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

A score that changes without a new decision

A colleague creates a table in which each urgent message in the test appears 9 times in total, and each ordinary message appears only once. They reuse A’s decisions without changing them. Calculate the new counts and A’s accuracy on this table. Can this be called a new independent test, or can the natural frequency of urgent messages be inferred from it?

Hint 1

Distinguish copied rows from distinct messages. Rule A remains “always ordinary”.

Compare with the solution

The table contains 9 × 10 = 90 urgent rows and 90 ordinary rows, making 180 rows. A is correct only for ordinary messages: 90/180 = 1/2, or 50 %.

The initial score was 90 %. It changes because missed urgent messages are counted again, while the rule is identical. The table is still built from the same 100 distinct messages; it provides no independent observation, and its artificial distribution does not measure the natural frequency.

You can state this transformation as a calculation convention, but keep the original test and its class counts for the planned evaluation. Rebalancing training data is a separate decision and does not make copies independent.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is imbalanced before any prediction?

The reference class counts: in the fictional batch, 10 urgent messages and 90 ordinary messages. Distinguish these from the counts of predicted decisions.

Recall 2Why calculate the score of a rule that always answers with the majority class?

It reveals what can be achieved without distinguishing the rare class. Here A obtains 90 % accuracy and misses all urgent messages; the overall score alone does not describe how they are handled.

Recall 3How can we give each class the same mass in our batch?

For each class c present, take w_c = N/(2 × n_c). The count multiplied by its weight is N/2. The weights have no unit and represent a convention, not actual costs inferred from the class counts.

Recall 4What is the denominator of the weighted error, and why?

The sum of the weights of all messages, correctly or incorrectly classified. The numerator counts only the weights of errors; their ratio measures the share of mass that is misclassified, without confusing it with the raw fraction of incorrect messages.

Recall 5Why does a copied test fail to demonstrate an improvement?

Decisions can remain identical while their weight in the score changes. Copies are not new independent observations; state the convention and preserve the test batch intended for the final evaluation.
