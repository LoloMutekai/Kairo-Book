Level 2 · C061·M5

Identifiability and uncertainty

Your goalDistinguish limited precision from ambiguity in the model, then choose a measurement that separates the confounded parameters.

Before you start
c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c059-m6 : deterministic discrete Bellman · c061-m1 : likelihood · c061-m2 : MLE · c061-m3 : MAP · c061-m4 : posterior distribution

✿01 See the idea

A probe displays 21 °C and then 23 °C in a room whose temperature is stable. Is the room fairly warm with a small probe offset, or cooler with a larger probe offset? The readings alone mix temperature and offset. All the data in this scene are fictional.

Noise makes the readings fluctuate around a mean. Repeating the measurements can make this mean more precise. But this does not tell us how to split it into temperature and offset: 20 °C with an offset of 2 °C and 18 °C with an offset of 4 °C give the same mean of 22 °C.

Identifiability asks whether different parameters can produce exactly the same distribution of observations. Here, a distribution describes the possible readings and their probabilities, before we look at a particular series. If the answer is yes, even a very long series of the same kind cannot separate these parameters.

Estimation uncertainty then asks how closely the available data determine the quantities we can distinguish. A reference with a known temperature can resolve our ambiguity; its readings still contain noise. Being able to distinguish the causes therefore does not mean knowing them without error.

The rule to remember

First ask what the observations can distinguish, then what uncertainty remains. To separate confounded causes, look for different information.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What the probe can separate

The room gives 2 readings: 21 °C and 23 °C. The noise variance is assumed known and equal to 4 degrees Celsius squared. We will first study these readings alone, then add the reference shown in the diagram. Hypotheses A and B are possibilities, rather than actual temperatures that have been revealed.

  1. Set out the model and its units

    t is the unknown room temperature and b is the unknown probe offset. They are constants during the experiment, expressed in °C; b is a temperature difference and can be negative. Yᵢ denotes the random reading and yᵢ its observed value. The index i numbers the readings from 1 to n; n = 2 is a count with no unit.

    Yi=t+b+εi

    Each noise term εᵢ, in °C, follows a normal distribution with mean 0 °C and variance v = 4 degrees Celsius squared. Here, a normal distribution is the distribution with a bell shape determined by its mean and variance. The noise terms are independent and have the same distribution. v is known by assumption, rather than estimated from our readings alone. We are not trying to estimate a probe gain or drift over time.

  2. Test identifiability before choosing a result

    For this protocol, the pair (t, b) would be identifiable if the same distribution of all the observations required the same pair. However, their distribution depends on t and b only through s = t + b, in °C: this is the mean of each reading.

    (t+h)+(b−h)=t+b

    h is any temperature difference in °C. Shifting t by h and b by −h preserves the mean and the noise, hence the entire distribution of each reading and of the independent series. Yet a nonzero h gives a different pair. The pair is therefore not identifiable, whereas s is: different values of s give different means.

    A predicts 20 + 2 = 22 °C; B predicts 18 + 4 = 22 °C. These are two examples of the same ambiguity. Equal likelihoods for a particular series would not be enough on their own; the preceding identity holds for every possible series.

  3. Calculate what the likelihood determines

    The likelihood L is the density of our readings, considered as a function of the parameters. With independent normal noise terms and their variance fixed, it is written L(t, b ; y) = C exp(−Q(s)/(2v)). y denotes the entire observed series; exp is the exponential function; C is a positive factor independent of t and b. C carries the unit of the joint density L, the inverse of (°C) to the power n. Q(s) is the sum of squared differences from s, in degrees Celsius squared. Q(s)/(2v) has no unit. Maximizing L is equivalent to minimizing Q.

    The mean of the readings is (21 + 23)/2 = 44/2 = 22 °C. Expanding the squares and then regrouping around this mean gives:

    Q(s)=(21−s)2+(23−s)2=2(s−22)2+2

    The square is positive or 0: the minimum is reached at s = 22 °C. The residuals are then 21 − 22 = -1 °C and 23 − 22 = 1 °C. Their squares are 1 and 1, so Q = 2 degrees Celsius squared. A and B have exactly these same residuals. Maximum likelihood estimation determines the sum; all pairs with t + b = 22 °C maximize L. Software that displays just one pair does not remove the other possibilities.

  4. Describe precision for the correct quantity

    Ȳ is the random mean of the n readings; its observed value is our mean of 22 °C. Since the noise terms are independent, the variance of their sum is nv. Dividing this sum by n divides the variance by the square of n. Thus Var(Ȳ) = v/n; Var denotes the variance over repetitions of the same experiment with fixed parameters. An estimator is unbiased if its mean over these repetitions equals the quantity being estimated.

    Var(Y¯)=vn=42=2 degrés Celsius au carré

    Since Ȳ estimates s without bias, this variance also describes its error around s. It does not describe the error around t: the offset b is still confounded with temperature. Without a stated prior, we define no posterior distribution for t here and assign it no posterior variance. A temperature can be shifted arbitrarily if the offset compensates. The exercise on repeated readings will reduce the variance of Ȳ without changing this conclusion.

  5. Check what a prior can decide

    To isolate the role of the prior, temporarily consider a discrete model that allows only A and B. We assign them fictional weights of 3 and 1; these are not measured frequencies. Their total is 4: the prior probabilities are therefore 3/4 for A and 1/4 for B.

    Since the distributions are identical, the likelihood ratio L(A ; y)/L(B ; y) is 1. Bayes multiplies the ratio of prior probabilities by this likelihood ratio:

    P(A|y)P(B|y)=L(A;y)L(B;y)×P(A)P(B)=1×3

    P denotes a probability with no unit; the vertical bar means “given”. The ratio remains 3. After normalization so that the probabilities sum to 1, P(A | y) = 3/4 = 0.75 and P(B | y) = 1/4 = 0.25. The maximum a posteriori estimate selects A, but the readings have not distinguished either hypothesis. This conclusion concerns the stated discrete model; it is not a calculation of the posterior distribution over all pairs of real numbers.

  6. Add a reference and recover the parameters

    Return to the model in which t and b can take any real value. The same probe now measures a reference whose temperature r = 10 °C is known exactly by an independent method. We assume b is unchanged. It gives m = 2 new readings, 11 °C and 13 °C. Their model is Zⱼ = r + b + ηⱼ: j numbers the reference readings from 1 to m; m is a count with no unit. Zⱼ is random, and ηⱼ is a normal noise term with 0 mean and variance v, independent of all the other noise terms. Zⱼ and ηⱼ are in °C.

    The reference mean is (11 + 13)/2 = 24/2 = 12 °C. Subtract the known temperature to isolate the offset: b̂ = 12 − 10 = 2 °C. The hat indicates an estimate.

    Then subtract this offset from the room mean: t̂ = 22 − 2 = 20 °C. The parameters are now identifiable: the mean of Z determines b because r is known; the mean of Y then determines t. This is a property of the distributions under the extended protocol. Our means from finite samples give estimates of these parameters, rather than their exact values.

  7. Propagate the uncertainty from the reference

    Write Z̄ for the random reference mean, b̂ = Z̄ − r and t̂ = Ȳ − b̂, with all these quantities in °C. Subtracting r, which is known exactly, does not change the variance: Var(b̂) = v/m = 4/2 = 2 degrees Celsius squared.

    The error in t̂ is the mean of the room noise terms minus the mean of the reference noise terms. These means are independent. The minus sign does not subtract their dispersion: its square is 1. The variances add.

    Var(t^)=vn+vm=2+2=4 degrés Celsius au carré

    The estimators are unbiased under our assumptions. The variance of the error in t̂ is therefore also 4 degrees Celsius squared. Its standard deviation is 2 °C, since 2 × 2 = 4. Treating b̂ as an exact constant would have retained only 2 degrees Celsius squared and ignored the reference noise. No coverage probability is calculated here.

  8. Check the result, then limit the conclusion

    Reconstruction check: t̂ + b̂ = 20 + 2 = 22 °C, the room mean; r + b̂ = 10 + 2 = 12 °C, the reference mean. The decomposition explains both means. Uncertainty check: the variance 4 remains positive because neither mean from noisy readings is exact.

    The protocol now distinguishes the parameters, but its accuracy depends on the constant offset, the known reference and the stated noise assumptions. If b changes between the room and reference measurements, the subtraction can be misleading. A small variance under a model does not by itself measure error caused by a false model. We have separated structural ambiguity from measurement uncertainty; we have not validated a real probe.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Why do more readings in the room fail to separate t and b, whereas a known reference can do so? Then reconstruct the variance of t̂ and explain why it includes the uncertainty from the reference.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “With enough readings, I am bound to find the temperature eventually.”

    The mean becomes more precise around t + b. Yet shifting t and b in opposite directions preserves the entire distribution of the readings. Check this invariance before asking for more measurements of the same kind.

    Review the competing decompositions

  • “The maximum a posteriori estimate selects A, so the measurements have eliminated B.”

    The likelihood ratio is 1. The posterior ratio therefore preserves the prior ratio. Selecting A is consistent with this stated prior; it does not prove that the readings provide a distinction.

    Revisit the probability calculation

  • “I have corrected the offset, so its uncertainty disappears.”

    The correction b̂ also comes from noisy readings. For independent series, the variance of Ȳ − b̂ adds their two variances. Identifiability does not allow us to replace an estimate with a known constant.

Exercise 1 · Construct a counterexample

Shift the causes without changing the readings

Before adding the reference, start from A. Increase t by h = 3 °C and decrease b by the same amount. Calculate the new pair, its mean reading and its residuals for 21 °C and 23 °C. Why does this counterexample apply to the entire distribution?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A negative offset is allowed: it means the probe reads too low on average.

Hint 2

The noise variance stays fixed. Check whether its mean has changed.

Compare with the solution

The new pair is t′ = 20 + 3 = 23 °C and b′ = 2 − 3 = -1 °C. Their sum is 22 °C. The residuals are -1 °C and 1 °C, with a sum of squares of 2 degrees Celsius squared.

This is more than an equal score for these readings: the mean, variance and independence of all the readings are unchanged. The same normal distributions therefore give the same distribution of the series for every possible outcome. t has changed; the observations under this protocol cannot distinguish it.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate both parameters and accept the negative offset.
  • Recover the mean and residuals with their units.
  • Justify equality of the distributions beyond equality of the observed score.

Exercise 2 · Calculate and interpret

More precise about which quantity?

Still without a reference, plan a new series of 8 truly independent readings in the same room with a stable temperature and the same variance v = 4 degrees Celsius squared. Calculate the variance of its mean. Can this mean be presented as an unbiased estimate of t, and this variance as a measure of all its error? The new observed values are not given.

Hint 1

The variance of the mean depends on the number of readings and the assumed noise; its observed value is not needed for this calculation.

Hint 2

For t, the unknown offset remains. Does this new protocol distinguish the pairs from the previous exercise?

Compare with the solution

The variance is 4/8 = 1/2 = 0.50 degrees Celsius squared, instead of 2 degrees Celsius squared for 2 readings. This calculation concerns the mean around s = t + b. It does not predict the value of the new observed mean.

No: calling this mean an estimate of t would leave a systematic error b. Its variance alone would not include this offset. The pair remains unidentifiable for any series size of the same kind. Copying old readings would not create the new independent observations required by the formula either.

Check your reasoning

  • Give 0.50 in the squared unit and name the quantity being estimated.
  • Distinguish the variance due to noise from the unknown offset.
  • Do not invent an observed mean or infer independence from copies.

Exercise 3 · Critique the protocol

The reference is no longer known exactly

We discover that the reference stated to be at r = 10 °C is actually at r + d, where d is an unknown fixed offset with no prior or bound. The reference model becomes Zⱼ = r + d + b + ηⱼ. Compare the triple (t, b, d) = (20, 2, 0) °C with the triple (23, -1, 3) °C. Does the protocol still identify t? What information would need to be established?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Check the mean in the room and the mean at the reference separately.

Hint 2

Test the simultaneous shift (t + h, b − h, d + h).

Compare with the solution

The room mean remains 22 °C. For the second triple, the reference mean is 10 + (-1) + 3 = 9 + 3 = 12 °C, just as 10 + 0 + 2 = 12 °C for the first. The noise terms still have the same distributions.

More generally, (t + h, b − h, d + h) preserves both means. The protocol therefore no longer distinguishes these triples, and t is no longer identifiable. The earlier variance calculation for t̂ assumed d was known to be 0: reusing it unchanged would ignore this new ambiguity.

We would need independent information that fixes d or b, or a different measurement whose distribution distinguishes the shift. A prior on d could define posterior uncertainty under that choice, but it would need to be stated and its sensitivity checked. More readings of these means alone do not determine the missing offset.

Check your reasoning

  • Check both means, rather than just the room mean.
  • Show the shift that preserves the distributions while changing t.
  • Name the missing independent information and distinguish measuring it from choosing a prior.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What equality prevents identifiability of the temperature–offset pair?

Distinct pairs (t, b) and (t + h, b − h), with nonzero h, give exactly the same distribution of all the readings. The sum t + b remains identifiable in this model.

Recall 2What do repeated readings make more precise, and under what assumption?

With independent noise terms of the same known variance v, the mean estimates t + b with variance v/n. It does not separate t and b. A series of the same kind preserves this ambiguity, even if it is long.

Recall 3If A and B have the same distribution of readings, what happens to their probability ratio after observation?

It stays equal to the prior ratio: the likelihood ratio is 1. A posterior choice can therefore reflect a prior preference without any distinction provided by these readings.

Recall 4Why does a known reference allow temperature and offset to be separated?

The distribution of the reference readings has mean r + b, with r known. It determines b; the room mean t + b then determines t. The offset must stay stable between the series. Estimates from finite samples still contain noise.

Recall 5Why does correcting the offset not remove its uncertainty?

The offset is estimated from a noisy mean. For independent series and an exact reference, the variance of t̂ = Ȳ − b̂ is v/n + v/m. This describes the error under the model, rather than guaranteeing the quality of a real probe.
