Level 3 · C168·M5

Radon-Nikodym

Your goalConstruct the density of one measure with respect to another on a finite set, use it to change measure in a probability and an expectation, then explain when it does not exist.

Before you start
c164-m5 : Fubini-Tonelli · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c165-m5 : duality · c168-m1 : measurable variables · c168-m2 : conditional expectation · c168-m3 : independence · c168-m4 : probabilistic convergence

✿01 See the idea

A security sensor records the type of each connection observed on a workstation: local, outbound web, or to an unusual port. We distribute probability weight across these three types in two ways: μ describes an ordinary period, and ν describes a period in which the workstation would be compromised. All values in this scenario are fictional; they serve a mathematics exercise and describe no real workstation.

A probability measure assigns a dimensionless weight between 0 and one to each subset of the set of types, denoted by Ω, with the weight of the whole of Ω equal to 1. Since Ω is finite, we take its power set as the σ-algebra: every function on Ω is then measurable, in the sense of the unit on measurable variables.

Question: given μ, what do we need to construct ν? A table of factors, one per type: multiply the ordinary weight of a type by its factor to obtain its weight under ν. This table is called the density of ν with respect to μ, written dν/dμ. A factor greater than 1 indicates a type with more weight under ν; a factor less than 1 indicates a type with less weight.

Two uses will guide this unit. First, the weight of a subset under ν is obtained by weighting the μ weights on that subset by the density. Second, the expectation of a quantity under ν is obtained as the expectation under μ of that quantity multiplied by the density: this is a change of measure.

A limitation is already apparent: if ν assigns weight to a type to which μ assigns no weight at all, no finite factor can produce it, since a factor multiplies the ordinary weight. We will see this in the last exercise. We work here on a finite set; the general case is stated as a theorem, without proof.

The rule to remember

To pass from μ to ν, multiply each μ weight by f = dν/dμ; this requires every set of 0 weight under μ to have 0 weight under ν as well.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Moving from an ordinary period to an imagined period of compromise

Three event types: L, a local connection; W, an outbound web connection; P, a connection to an unusual port. Fictional weights under μ: 0.40, 0.40, 0.20. Fictional weights under ν: 0.10, 0.50, 0.40. Fictional analysis cost g, in minutes: 1, 3, 9. Let us construct the density f of ν with respect to μ, then calculate ν({W, P}) and the expectation of g under ν using μ.

  1. Define the objects and the crucial assumption

    Ω = {L, W, P}. The σ-algebra is the power set of Ω; g, which assigns each type a cost in minutes, is therefore measurable. μ and ν are two probability measures: each assigns a dimensionless weight to every subset, and 1 to Ω. The weights are written in hundredths in the data: 40 hundredths equal 0.40.

    Crucial assumption: each type has strictly positive weight under μ. Thus the only subset with 0 weight under μ is the empty set, which also has 0 weight under ν. We then say that ν is absolutely continuous with respect to μ, and write ν ≪ μ. Without this property, the density we seek may not exist.

  2. Construct the density type by type

    On a finite set, the density of ν with respect to μ is the quotient of the two weights for the same type. It is dimensionless, since it divides two dimensionless weights. The quotient is defined because every denominator is strictly positive.

    f(ω)=ν({ω})μ({ω})

    For L: 0.10 ÷ 0.40 = 0.25. For W: 0.50 ÷ 0.40 = 1.25. For P: 0.40 ÷ 0.20 = 2.00. Interpretation: under ν, type L has less weight than in an ordinary period, while W and P have more weight, with the greatest increase in relative weight for P.

  3. Check normalization

    If f correctly recovers ν, weighting each μ weight by f must reconstruct ν type by type. We calculate f(ω) × μ({ω}): for L, 0.10; for W, 0.50; for P, 0.40. Each is the ν weight given in the problem. The partial sums give 0.60 for L and W, then 1 with P.

    ∫Ωfdμ=∑ω∈Ωf(ω)μ({ω})=1

    The total is the weight of Ω under ν, namely 1: f has expectation 1 under μ. A different total would indicate an error in a quotient or a weight.

  4. Recover the weight of a subset

    Take A = {W, P}. Directly, ν(A) = 0.50 + 0.40 = 0.90. Using the density, we integrate f with respect to μ over A, that is, we add the products f × μ for the types in A, already calculated: 0.50 + 0.40 = 0.90. The two methods agree.

    ν(A)=∫Afdμ=0.50+0.40=0.90

    For comparison, μ(A) = 0.40 + 0.20 = 0.60. The same subset has weight 0.60 in an ordinary period and 0.90 under ν: changing measure changes the weights, and the density specifies by how much, type by type.

  5. Change measure in an expectation

    Direct method, under ν: multiply the cost by the ν weight. L: 1 × 0.10 = 0.10. W: 3 × 0.50 = 1.50. P: 9 × 0.40 = 3.60. Sum: 0.10 + 1.50 = 1.60, then 1.60 + 3.60 = 5.20 minutes.

    Method using μ: first multiply the cost by the density, so g × f equals 0.25, 3.75 and 18.00 minutes; then weight by the μ weights, giving 0.10, 1.50 and 3.60. Sum: 1.60, then 5.20 minutes.

    Eν[g]=Eμ[gf]=∑ω∈Ωg(ω)f(ω)μ({ω})

    Both methods give 5.20 minutes. The density therefore allows us to calculate an expectation under ν using only the μ weights. The result retains the unit of g, the minute, because f is dimensionless.

  6. Check limiting cases and assumptions

    If ν were equal to μ, all the quotients would equal 1: the density would be constant and would change nothing. If a type had μ weight equal to 0 but positive ν weight, the quotient would divide by 0: no finite value of f would work. This is the case in the exercise with no density.

    In the general case, the Radon-Nikodym theorem states that a measurable, nonnegative density f exists when ν ≪ μ and the measures are σ-finite, and that it is unique up to a set of 0 weight under μ. We do not prove it: our calculation verifies it only on a finite set of three outcomes, where f(ω) is the quotient found in step two.

Explain it in your own words

Explain why multiplying the μ weights by f recovers ν exactly, type by type, then why this reconstruction becomes impossible if a type has 0 weight under μ but not under ν.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The density dν/dμ is the probability under ν.”

    It is a dimensionless quotient of weights, which can exceed 1, as with f(P) = 2.00. The probability under ν is the product of the density and the μ weight; it is the sum of these products that equals 1.

    Review the construction of the density and its check

  • “We can always divide ν by μ.”

    Only if every set of 0 weight under μ has 0 weight under ν. With a type of weight 0 under μ′ and 0.40 under ν, there is 0.40 of weight that cannot be reconstructed: no density exists.

    Redo the exercise with no density

  • “To pass from ν to μ, multiply by f again.”

    In the other direction, the density is the reciprocal quotient, when all weights are strictly positive: for L, 0.25 becomes 4.00, and their product equals 1. Multiplying by f again would distort μ instead of recovering ν.

    Redo the exercise on the inverse density

Exercise 3 · Limiting case

When the factor does not exist

Replace μ by a measure μ′ with weight 0.20 for L, 0.80 for W and 0 for P; ν is unchanged. Can a density of ν with respect to μ′ be defined? If not, what ν weight is impossible to reproduce?

Hint 1

Find a type whose weight under μ′ is 0, and compare it with its weight under ν.

Hint 2

Calculate the factors where μ′ is strictly positive, then reconstruct the ν weight they provide.

Compare with the solution

Type P has weight 0 under μ′ but 0.40 under ν: ν is not absolutely continuous with respect to μ′, and no density exists.

Where μ′ is positive, the quotients would be 0.50 for L and 0.625 for W. They reproduce ν({L, W}) = 0.60, but not the rest: 0.40 is missing, exactly ν({P}). Whatever value is given to f(P), its product with μ′({P}) equals 0 and cannot make up this missing weight.

Check your reasoning

  • The type with 0 weight under μ′ and positive weight under ν is identified.
  • The conclusion cites absolute continuity, rather than division by 0 alone.
  • The missing weight is calculated and related to ν({P}).

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the density of ν with respect to μ on a finite set?

For each outcome of strictly positive weight under μ, the quotient of its weight under ν by its weight under μ; it is dimensionless and can exceed 1.

Recall 2What condition makes the density possible, and how is it written?

ν must be absolutely continuous with respect to μ, written ν ≪ μ: every set of 0 weight under μ has 0 weight under ν.

Recall 3How can we calculate an expectation under ν using only μ?

The expectation of g under ν equals the expectation of g f under μ, where f = dν/dμ: weight g by the density, then by the μ weights.

Recall 4What is the integral of f with respect to μ, and why?

It equals 1, because it is the weight of Ω under ν; in our example, the sum of the products f × μ gives 1.

Recall 5What have we actually established in this unit?

The calculation on a fictional finite set of three outcomes. The general theorem, for σ-finite measures, is stated but not proved here.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define Ω, the σ-algebra, μ and ν; check that every set of 0 weight under μ also has 0 weight under ν; construct f as the quotient of weights, type by type; check that the sum of the products f × μ equals the total weight; recover the weight of a subset and an expectation by weighting under μ; rule out a density when a μ weight is 0 where the ν weight is not; distinguish dν/dμ from its reciprocal.
