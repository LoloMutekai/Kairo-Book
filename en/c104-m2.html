Level 3 · C104·M2

Embeddings

Your goalRead a word’s vector from a table, calculate the norm, dot product and cosine between two words, then explain why this cosine proves neither shared meaning nor relevance.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A fictional bakery wants to find the word in its notebook that is most similar to “bread”. The first idea is to number the vocabulary. Our small vocabulary contains 3 words: bread receives identifier 0, brioche identifier 1 and train identifier 2. A sentence then becomes a sequence of identifiers, as in unit C104·M1.

But a number measures no relationship. The gap between 1 and 2 tells us neither that brioche is close to train nor that they are far apart: it is simply the order in which we wrote the list.

An embedding, a learned vector representation, associates each identifier with a short list of numbers of the same length for every word. This length is the dimension. Here it is 2, so that we can draw the vectors. The table contains one row per word: 3 rows of 2 numbers, or 6 numbers in all. Reading a word’s vector means reading its row.

Once the words have become vectors, we can compare two properties: direction and length. There is no requirement for length to measure similarity. The cosine, which we will define, uses only direction. This is what the second diagram shows: the thick segments show the lengths, and the rays extended to the quarter circle show the directions.

All values in this scene are fictional, chosen to keep the calculations exact. They do not come from any real model and tell us nothing about language. This page does not show how the values in a table are obtained: learning requires an objective and data, and a table with many parameters requires precautions such as the regularization in C103·M5. We start with a table that is already given.

The rule to remember

A word becomes a row in a table; two words are compared using the angle between their rows, expressed by the cosine, rather than the lengths of their vectors.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Choosing the neighbor of bread in a notebook of three words

The fictional bakery has the table of 3 words shown in the first diagram. The starting word is bread. To find out which of brioche and train is most similar to it, we read the rows of the table, then compare two comparison rules: the raw dot product and the cosine.

  1. Set out the vocabulary, table and assumptions

    V is the number of words: 3. d is the dimension, the number of coordinates per word: 2. An identifier i is an integer from 0 to 2. E is the table: a matrix with V rows and d columns, whose row E_i is the vector for word number i. The rows are in the order bread, brioche, train.

    E=(4334724)

    The coordinates are pure numbers, without units: they are neither degrees nor meters. Assumptions: the values are given, rather than learned here; they are fictional; a word always has the same row regardless of the sentence, so context plays no role; the two axes have no names. If any of these assumptions is false in a real case, the conclusions on this page do not carry over to it.

  2. From word to vector: read a row

    A one-hot vector e_i is a row of V numbers: a 1 at position i and 0 everywhere else. Multiplying it by the table selects row i, because every other row is multiplied by 0.

    eiE=Ei

    Take train, identifier 2. Its one-hot vector is (0 ; 0 ; 1). First coordinate: 0 × 4 + 0 × 3 + 1 × 7 = 7. Second coordinate: 0 × 3 + 0 × 4 + 1 × 24 = 24.

    Check: the result (7 ; 24) is exactly row number 2 of the table. In practice, we do not perform this multiplication: we read the row directly, which gives the same result. The entire table contains 6 numbers.

  3. Define the norm and dot product

    For two vectors a and b with 2 coordinates, the dot product adds the products of the corresponding coordinates. The norm of a is the square root of the sum of the squares of its coordinates: it is its length in the plane.

    a·b=a1b1+a2b2

    Norms of our three vectors. Bread: 4 squared is 16, 3 squared is 9, and the sum is 25, whose square root is 5. Brioche: 9 + 16 = 25, with square root 5. Train: 49 + 576 = 625, with square root 25. Check: 25 × 25 = 625.

    Dot products with bread. Brioche: 4 × 3 + 3 × 4 = 12 + 12 = 24. Train: 4 × 7 + 3 × 24 = 28 + 72 = 100. If we ranked by raw dot product, train (100) would come before brioche (24).

  4. Divide by the lengths: the cosine

    The dot product increases when a vector gets longer. To retain only direction, we divide by the product of the norms. This division requires both vectors to be nonzero.

    cos(a,b)=a·b‖a‖×‖b‖
    cos(pain,brioche)=245×5=2425

    Bread (“pain” in the formula) and brioche: 24 / 25 = 24/25, or 0.96 as a decimal. Bread and train: 100 / (5 × 25) = 100 / 125 = 4/5, or 0.80.

    Ranked by cosine, brioche (0.96) comes before train (0.80): the order is reversed compared with the dot product. The reason is that the vector for train is much longer (25 versus 5), which inflates the dot product without bringing its direction closer.

  5. Check by normalizing

    Normalizing a nonzero vector means dividing each of its coordinates by its norm; its length becomes 1. Bread becomes (4/5 ; 3/5) and brioche (3/5 ; 4/5).

    Their dot product is 4/5 × 3/5 + 3/5 × 4/5 = 12/25 + 12/25 = 24/25, the same value as the cosine 24/25. This is a check by a different route: once the vectors are normalized, the dot product and cosine coincide.

    Another check: a cosine never exceeds 1, and 24/25 is indeed less than 1. A comparison system that normalizes vectors beforehand can therefore use the dot product alone, provided that no vector is 0.

  6. What this calculation does not prove

    We have shown that, in this invented table, the row for brioche points closer to the row for bread than the row for train does. We have not shown that brioche is similar to bread in language, or that a search engine based on these vectors would return better results: that would depend on how the table was obtained and on an evaluation that this page does not provide.

    A sentence of 2 words would give 2 vectors with 2 coordinates: a sequence of vectors. Later units in this chapter may start from such sequences of vectors; this page stops at reading and comparing them.

Explain it in your own words

Why do the dot product and cosine rank train and brioche in opposite orders? Explain using the words “length” and “direction”, without looking at the calculation.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The largest dot product identifies the closest word.”

    The dot product increases with the lengths. In the example, train has the largest dot product but the lowest cosine. Compare directions: divide by the norms or normalize first.

    Go through the cosine calculation again

  • “Each coordinate of an embedding has an interpretable meaning, such as “the first measures how much it relates to food”.”

    In our diagram, the axes have no names and no assumption assigns any to them. Only comparisons between vectors are calculated here; do not assign meaning to a coordinate without an analysis that justifies it.

    Review the diagram of directions

  • “A 0 vector is a neutral embedding that can be compared like any other.”

    Its norm is 0, so the cosine quotient is undefined. Its dot product with any vector is 0, which looks like “unrelated” when it actually means “no representation”. Handle this case separately, before ranking.

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

Another pair of words

In the same fictional table, calculate the cosine between brioche and train, then compare it with the cosine between bread and train. What does this result tell us about the position of train relative to the other two words in this table?

Hint 1

First the dot product, then the product of the norms, and finally the quotient.

Hint 2

The norms of brioche and train have already been calculated in the example.

Compare with the solution

Dot product: 3 × 7 + 4 × 24 = 21 + 96 = 117. Product of the norms: 5 × 25 = 125. Cosine: 117 / 125 = 117/125, or 0.936.

Comparison: 0.936 is greater than 0.80, the cosine between bread and train. In this table, the direction of train is closer to that of brioche than to that of bread. Check: the result remains less than 1. This interpretation describes the invented table, rather than language.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the dot product and norms before dividing.
  • Compare two cosines, rather than a cosine and a dot product.
  • Use 1 as an upper bound for the result and limit the conclusion to the fictional table.

Exercise 2 · Guided reasoning

Doubling the vector for bread

Replace the vector for bread with the same vector multiplied by 2. Without recalculating coordinate by coordinate, predict the new dot product with brioche, the new norm and the new cosine. Then check your prediction.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Multiplying a vector by a number multiplies its dot product with any other vector by that same number.

Hint 2

Multiplying a vector by a positive number multiplies its norm by that number.

Compare with the solution

Dot product: 2 × 24 = 48. Norm: 2 × 5 = 10. Product of the norms: 10 × 5 = 50. Cosine: 48 / 50 = 24/25.

The cosine is unchanged: it is still 24/25. The factor 2 appears in the numerator and denominator and cancels out. The dot product, however, has doubled: this is why it cannot measure similarity of direction on its own.

Check your reasoning

  • Predict before calculating: the dot product doubles, the norm doubles, and the cosine stays the same.
  • Explain how the common factor cancels out.
  • Distinguish what depends on length from what does not.

Exercise 3 · Diagnose an edge case

The 0 vector

For an empty input, an interface returns the 0 vector with 2 coordinates. We want to compare this vector with bread. Calculate the dot product and the product of the norms. Can the cosine be defined? What should be done before ranking?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The norm of the 0 vector is 0.

Hint 2

Look at the denominator of the cosine.

Compare with the solution

Dot product: 0 × 4 + 0 × 3 = 0. Product of the norms: 0 × 5 = 0. The cosine would be 0 / 0, a division by 0: it is undefined.

The dot product alone would be 0, as for two unrelated directions. This similarity is misleading: here there is no direction to compare. This case must be detected before ranking and handled separately, for example by refusing the comparison, rather than allowing a 0 score to mix with the others.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate both products.
  • Conclude that the cosine is undefined, without inventing a value.
  • Propose separate handling before ranking.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is an embedding in this unit?

A short list of numbers with a fixed length called the dimension, associated with a word or text; in our example, it is a table row read using the word’s identifier.

Recall 2What does multiplying a one-hot vector by the table do?

It selects the word’s row: a 1 at position i and 0 elsewhere retain only row i of the table.

Recall 3Why compare using the cosine rather than the raw dot product?

The cosine divides by the product of the norms and retains only direction; the raw dot product also increases with the lengths and may rank a long vector ahead of one pointing in a closer direction.

Recall 4Does a high cosine prove that two words have the same meaning?

No. It describes the orientation of two vectors in a table. Meaning and relevance depend on how the table was obtained and on a separate evaluation.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Number the words; for each identifier, read the table row with its fixed dimension; calculate the norm, dot product and then cosine, checking that the vectors are nonzero; compare directions rather than lengths, or normalize first; state the assumptions, assign no meaning to the coordinates, and do not conclude that the words share a meaning or are relevant.
