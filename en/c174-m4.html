Level 3 · C174·M4

Feynman-Kac

Your goalConnect the solution of a diffusion equation to an expectation over paths, then check both sides on an exact example while identifying what remains to be justified in the next unit.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A float is released into a canal. Its position X, in metres, is carried by a mean current of μ metres per hour and disturbed by random eddies. At time T, a penalty g(X(T)) = X(T)² is recorded, in square metres. The question is: if the float is at x at time t, what penalty should we expect on average? The whole scene is fictional, with x = 3 m and μ = 1 m/h: these values are for calculation and do not describe a real canal.

We have two methods. The first repeats the experiment: we follow many paths starting from (t, x), record the penalty for each one and take the average. The second seeks a function u(t, x), defined over the whole grid of times and positions, that satisfies a partial differential equation and equals g at time T, without simulating any paths.

Feynman-Kac connects the two. Under regularity assumptions, the function obtained by averaging is the solution of the equation, and the solution of the equation is this average. Time runs backward here: we know u at the endpoint T, which is the terminal condition, and then work back towards the present time t. We therefore call this a terminal value problem rather than an initial value problem.

The connection makes sense only if the process and the equation share the same generator, meaning the same current and the same eddies. Changing one without the other compares two different scenes. This unit checks the connection in a case where everything can be calculated exactly; the general conditions for validity are the subject of the next unit (C174·M5).

The rule to remember

The solution u(t, x) of the terminal value problem ∂u/∂t + μ ∂u/∂x + ½ σ² ∂²u/∂x² = 0 with u(T, ·) = g is the average of g(X(T)) over paths that start from x at time t, provided the regularity assumptions hold.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The canal float: two routes to the same penalty

The float starts at x = 3 m with a current of μ = 1 m/h and eddies of intensity σ = 2 m/√h; the horizon is 2 h away and the penalty is g(x) = x² in m². These values are fictional. Let us calculate u(t, x) using the equation, then using the paths, and compare.

  1. Set out the process, the equation and the assumptions

    The position follows the stochastic differential equation below. X(s) is the position at time s, in m. μ is the constant current, in m/h. σ is the constant intensity of the eddies, in m/√h, so σ² is expressed in m²/h. W is a standard Brownian motion: W(s) − W(t) has mean 0 and variance s − t in hours, and its increments over disjoint intervals are independent.

    dX(s)=μds+σdW(s)

    The generator, recalled from the unit that defines it, maps a twice differentiable function f to the following function, whose unit is the unit of f per hour.

    Lf=μf′+12σ2f″

    The terminal value problem seeks a function u(t, x) that is differentiable once in t and twice in x, with continuous derivatives, such that:

    ∂u∂t+μ∂u∂x+12σ2∂2u∂x2=0
    u(T,x)=g(x)=x2

    Here t is a time before T, and we write τ = T − t for the time remaining, in h. Assumptions: constant coefficients, sufficient regularity of u, and what we will call the 0-mean condition, stated in the next step.

  2. See why a solution of the equation is an average

    For a sufficiently regular function u, Ito’s formula, which we use without proving it again, splits the change in u(s, X(s)) into a term in ds and a term in dW.

    du(s,X(s))=(∂u∂t+Lu)(s,X(s))ds+σ∂u∂x(s,X(s))dW(s)

    If u solves the equation, the expression in brackets multiplying ds is 0: only the stochastic integral with respect to dW remains. 0-mean condition: this integral, taken from t to T, has mean 0. Then the average of u(s, X(s)) does not depend on s. Since u(T, ·) = g, we obtain the Feynman-Kac representation.

    u(t,x)=E[g(X(T))∣X(t)=x]

    The 0-mean condition is not automatic: it requires integrability bounds on σ ∂u/∂x, which are the subject of the next unit. In our example, u will be a polynomial and X a Gaussian variable with all moments finite: the condition holds, but this does not provide a general criterion.

  3. Equation route: test a candidate

    Consider the function below. It comes from the formula E[Y²] = (E[Y])² + Var(Y) applied to Y = X(T), with mean x + μτ and variance σ²τ; the step using paths will recover it. Here we only test whether it solves the equation, regardless of its origin.

    u(t,x)=(x+μτ)2+σ2τ,τ=T−t

    At the point used in the example, τ = 2 h and μτ = 1 × 2 = 2 m. The mean position is x + μτ = 3 + 2 = 5 m. Its square is 25 m². The variance is σ²τ = 4 × 2 = 8 m², since σ² = 2 × 2 = 4 m²/h. Therefore:

    u=(3+1×2)2+4×2=25+8=33 m²

    Now calculate the derivatives of the candidate. They follow directly from the formula.

    ∂u∂x=2(x+μτ),∂2u∂x2=2,∂u∂τ=μ∂u∂x+σ2

    Numerically, ∂u/∂x = 2 × 5 = 10 m and ∂²u/∂x² = 2 is dimensionless. Since τ = T − t, the derivative with respect to t is the negative of the derivative with respect to τ: ∂u/∂τ = μ ∂u/∂x + σ² = 1 × 10 + 4 = 10 + 4 = 14 m²/h, so ∂u/∂t = -14 m²/h. The current term is μ ∂u/∂x = 10 m²/h and the diffusion term is ½ σ² ∂²u/∂x² = 2 × 2 = 4 m²/h, giving L u = 10 + 4 = 14 m²/h. Substitute into the equation:

    -14+1×10+2×2=-14+14=0

    The candidate solves the equation at the point tested. The derivatives written above hold for every (t, x): the negative of ∂u/∂τ is −μ ∂u/∂x − σ², which is exactly cancelled by μ ∂u/∂x and ½ σ² × ∂²u/∂x² = σ². It therefore solves the equation everywhere, with no restriction on the point. When τ is 0, the formula recovers the penalty: u(T, x) = x × x, giving 3 × 3 = 9 m² for x = 3 m, which is indeed g(x).

  4. Path route: average four paths

    Divide the 2 h into n = 2 steps of duration h = 1 h. At each step, the position receives the current contribution μh = 1 m, plus or minus a random displacement σ√h = 2 × 1 = 2 m (here √h = 1 since h = 1). Both signs have the same probability, ½. This two-valued step replaces the Gaussian increment in the Euler-Maruyama scheme: the random part retains mean 0 and variance h, but not the Gaussian distribution.

    A jump is therefore 1 + 2 = 3 m upward, or 1 − 2 = -1 m downward. Starting from 3 m, the position after one step is 3 + 3 = 6 m or 3 + (-1) = 2 m. After the second step: 6 + 3 = 9 m, 6 + (-1) = 5 m, and 2 + (-1) = 1 m. The path going down then up also reaches 5 m: the order of the jumps does not change their sum.

    There are 2 × 2 = 4 equally likely paths. Penalties: 9² = 81 m², 5² = 25 m² for two paths, 1² = 1 m². Total: 81 plus twice 25 is 81 + 50 = 131, then 131 + 1 = 132. The average is:

    81+25+25+14=1324=33 m²

  5. Compare, check and state the limitations

    Both routes give 33 m², with a difference of 0. Checks performed in calculs.py using other methods: enumeration of the positions, backward recurrence on the grid and finite differences of the candidate. The four positions have mean 5 m and variance 8 m², corresponding to x + μτ and σ²τ.

    Limiting case: without eddies, σ = 0, the variance would be 0 and the average penalty would be (5)² = 25 m². The difference from 33 m², namely 8 m², is the cost of the eddies. Units: u is expressed in m², ∂u/∂t in m²/h, and σ²τ in (m²/h) × h = m²; adding σ instead of σ² would mix incompatible units.

    What the example does not establish: g(x) = x² is quadratic, so only the mean and variance matter and two steps are enough for an exact result. For another penalty, the average over the tree would only be an approximation that depends on the step size. And the 0-mean condition, verified here through the polynomial form of u, requires a general statement in the next unit. The example illustrates the connection; it does not prove the theorem.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The given function g is specified at the float’s starting point, as an initial condition.”

    The given function g is terminal: it is specified at the horizon T, and u(t, x) is read by working backward in time. In the example, the penalty x² at 0 remaining time determines the whole function; the starting point x is simply where we read its value.

    Review the terminal value problem in the example

  • “The average penalty is the penalty at the mean position.”

    For a convex penalty, the average of g exceeds g of the mean. In the example, g of the mean position is 25 m², whereas the average of g is 33 m²: the difference of 8 m² is the variance σ²τ.

    Review the cross-check in the example

  • “Numerical agreement over four paths proves the formula for every penalty.”

    The agreement is exact here because g is quadratic and the two-valued step preserves the mean and variance. It proves neither the theorem nor the 0-mean condition. Try another cost, as in the exercises, and read the conditions in the next unit.

Another starting point

Hint 1

The mean of X(T) is x′ + μτ; the variance comes only from the eddies.

Hint 2

For g(x) = x², the average of g(X(T)) is the square of the mean plus the variance.

Compare with the solution

Mean: 4 + 2 = 6 m, whose square is 36 m². Variance: σ²τ = 8 m², unchanged because W(T) − W(t) does not depend on the starting point. Thus u = 36 + 8 = 44 m². Reasonableness check: the start is further to the right and the current is positive, so u exceeds the value 33 m² in the example.

Check your reasoning

  • Mean and variance calculated separately.
  • Use the square of the mean, never the mean of the square.
  • Use m² as the unit for u and for the variance.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which problem does u(t, x), the average of g(X(T)) starting from x, satisfy, and where is the given function g specified?

The terminal value problem: ∂u/∂t + L u = 0 before the horizon T, with u(T, ·) = g. The given function g is specified at the horizon, and we work back towards the present.

Recall 2Which generator goes with dX = μ ds + σ dW when μ and σ are constant?

L f = μ f′ + ½ σ² f″. Its unit is the unit of f per hour.

Recall 3Why does the average of u(s, X(s)) not depend on s when u solves the equation?

Ito’s formula gives a term in ds equal to ∂u/∂t + L u, which is 0 by the equation. A stochastic integral remains, with mean 0 under integrability assumptions that must be checked.

Recall 4Why does the average of g(X(T)) differ from g of the mean of X(T) for g(x) = x²?

For a convex penalty, the average of g is greater than g of the mean. With x², the difference is the variance of X(T), here σ²τ.

Recall 5What does the agreement between the four paths and the equation prove, and what does it not prove?

It checks a case where g is quadratic, so only the mean and variance matter. It proves neither the theorem, nor the 0-mean condition, nor validity for another penalty.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Specify the process and its generator; set up the terminal equation with g at the horizon; apply Ito’s formula to u(s, X(s)) to see the term in ds vanish; explicitly assume that the stochastic integral has mean 0; read u(t, x) as the average of g(X(T)); check using the equation, the paths and the variance, while retaining the units and the limitations of the example.
