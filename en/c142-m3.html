Level 2 · C142·M3

Completeness

Your goalUse the completeness of ℝ for a Cauchy sequence of rational numbers: establish that its real limit exists, locate it through exact calculations, then see that this limit can lie outside ℚ.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

Suppose you want to use only fractions to write a number whose square is 2. Start with 3/2, then replace each approximation by the average of itself and 2 divided by it. The term 3/2 becomes 17/12, then 577/408. In decimal form: 1.5000, then 1.4167, then 1.414216. Every term is a valid fraction, and the terms get closer to one another very quickly.

This closeness to one another is all that a Cauchy sequence requires: we can establish it without knowing any limit. One question remains, concerning the set we are working in as well as the sequence: does this set contain a number to which the sequence converges?

Completeness gives a yes for ℝ: every Cauchy sequence of real numbers converges to a real number. For ℚ, the answer is no. Our sequence contains only rational numbers and is Cauchy, yet its only possible limit, a number ℓ such that ℓ² = 2, is not rational. The “gap” in ℚ is visible only from within ℝ.

Two limits to the scope of this lesson. The completeness of ℝ is accepted here as a property of the real numbers: we do not prove it again from least upper bounds. We also leave compactness and convergence criteria for later units. The numbers are exact and have no units: this is a mathematical example, rather than a measurement.

The rule to remember

A Cauchy sequence always has a limit in a complete set, such as ℝ. In ℚ, this limit may be missing: completeness is a property of the set, rather than of the sequence.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The sequence of averages approaching the square root of two

Consider the sequence of strictly positive rational numbers defined by u₀ = 3/2 and, for each index n, uₙ₊₁ equal to the average of uₙ and 2/uₙ. We show that it is Cauchy, that completeness makes it converge in ℝ, and then that its limit is not rational. The numbers are exact and have no units.

  1. Define the objects and assumptions

    Write un = pn/qn with pn and qn strictly positive integers, defined by p0 = 3, q0 = 2, pn+1 = pn² + 2qn² and qn+1 = 2pnqn. These rules follow from the calculation below, which shows that pn+1/qn+1 is indeed the stated average:

    un+1=12(un+2un)=pn2+2qn22pnqn

    Assumptions: n ranges over the nonnegative integers; un ≠ 0, so division by un is defined; we work first in ℚ. We use the definition of a Cauchy sequence from the previous unit: for every ε > 0, there is an index N such that any two terms with indices at least N differ by less than ε.

  2. Calculate the first terms

    Index 0: p1 = 3² + 2 × 2² = 9 + 8 = 17 and q1 = 2 × 3 × 2 = 12. Thus u1 = 17/12, approximately 1.4167, to be compared with u0 = 3/2 = 1.5000.

    Index 1: p2 = 17² + 2 × 12² = 289 + 288 = 577 and q2 = 2 × 17 × 12 = 408. Thus u2 = 577/408, approximately 1.414216.

    The calculations use integers throughout: no value is rounded, and the resulting fractions are the terms of the sequence itself.

  3. Find an invariant

    At index 0, pn² − 2qn² equals 9 − 8 = 1. Let us show that this quantity stays equal to 1: if pn² − 2qn² = 1, the next index gives the square of this same quantity, because (A + B)² − 2² AB = (A − B)² with A = pn² and B = 2qn², and 2² AB = 2 × (2pnqn)².

    pn+12−2qn+12=(pn2−2qn2)2=12=1

    Numerical checks: at index 1, 289 − 288 = 1; at index 2, 332929 − 332928 = 1. Dividing by qn² gives un² − 2 = 1/qn²: here 1/4, 1/144 and 1/166464 for the first three indices. This excess is strictly positive, so un² > 2 and un > 1.

  4. Calculate the gap between consecutive terms

    The invariant simplifies the difference between consecutive terms:

    un−un+1=un2−22un=12pnqn=1qn+1

    The first step follows from un − (un + 2/un)/2 = (un − 2/un)/2. Check: u0 − u1 = 3/2 − 17/12 = 1/12, which is 1/q1 since q1 = 12; u1 − u2 = 1/408, which is 1/q2 since q2 = 408. The gaps are strictly positive: the sequence is strictly decreasing.

  5. Bound the whole tail: the sequence is Cauchy

    Set dn = 1/qn+1. Since pn ≥ 1, we have qn+1 = 2pnqn ≥ 2qn, so dn+1 = 1/qn+2 ≤ 1/(2qn+1) = dn/2: each gap is at most half the preceding one.

    For m > n, the difference telescopes into a sum of gaps. A finite sum of terms of a geometric progression with ratio 1/2 is less than its first term divided by 1 − 1/2, giving:

    0<un−um=dn+…+dm−1<dn1−12=2dn

    Values of the bound 2dn: at index 0, 1/6; at index 1, 1/204, approximately 0.0049; at index 2, 1/235416. It tightens very quickly.

    Let ε > 0. The qn at least double at each index, so they exceed every bound: we can choose an index N where the bound 2dn is less than ε. For m ≥ n ≥ N, we have |un − um| < 2dn ≤ the bound at index N < ε, since the dn decrease. The sequence is Cauchy, and we have not named any limit.

  6. Apply completeness, then take limits

    Rational numbers are real numbers: (un) is a Cauchy sequence of real numbers. By the completeness of ℝ, there is an ℓ ∈ ℝ with un → ℓ. This property guarantees that ℓ exists, without giving its value: none of the calculations above has provided it. Since un > 1 for every n, we have ℓ ≥ 1.

    Return to un² = 2 + 1/qn². The left side tends to ℓ², the limit of a product. On the right, 1/qn² ≤ 1/qn, and qn exceeds every bound: the term tends to 0. By uniqueness of the limit, ℓ² = 2, so ℓ is a square root of 2, and is positive.

  7. Show that ℓ is not rational

    Suppose ℓ = a/b, where a and b are strictly positive coprime integers. Then a² = 2b², so a² is even. The square of an odd integer leaves remainder 1 on division by 2: a must therefore be even, a = 2c. We get 2²c² = 2b², or b² = 2c²: b is even too. This contradicts the assumption that a and b are coprime, since 2 divides both a and b.

    Thus ℓ ∉ ℚ. Consequently, if (un) converged to a rational number r, r would also be a limit in ℝ, so r = ℓ by uniqueness, which is impossible. The sequence is Cauchy in ℚ with no limit in ℚ: ℚ is not complete.

  8. Check ℓ by two independent methods

    Starting from u2, two arguments bound u2 − ℓ. Using the gaps: u2 − ℓ is the limit of the differences u2 − um, all at least d2 = 1/470832; thus u2 − ℓ ≥ 1/470832. Using the invariant: u2² − ℓ² = (u2 − ℓ)(u2 + ℓ) = 1/166464, and u2 + ℓ > 2 since u2 > 1 and ℓ ≥ 1; thus u2 − ℓ < (1/166464)/2 = 1/332928.

    1470832≤u2−ℓ<1332928

    The two bounds are compatible: 470832 > 332928, so 1/470832 < 1/332928. Also, 1/332928 is less than the tail bound 1/235416 from the preceding step, since 332928 > 235416. This check does not give a decimal value for ℓ; it only confirms that the calculations and completeness do not contradict one another.

Explain it in your own words

What exactly does the completeness of ℝ guarantee in this example, and what did the calculations have to establish separately? Also explain why the same sequence, viewed in ℚ, has no limit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If the gap between consecutive terms tends to 0, the sequence is Cauchy.”

    A small step repeated can accumulate without bound. A Cauchy sequence requires a bound on all gaps within an entire tail. In the example, the proof uses the fact that each gap is at most half the preceding one to bound their sum by twice the first gap.

    Review the step that bounds the tail

  • “A Cauchy sequence of rational numbers converges to a rational number.”

    Completeness provides a limit in ℝ, without providing one in ℚ. Here, the limit ℓ satisfies ℓ² = 2 and is not rational. Always specify the set in which you are looking for the limit.

    Review the parity argument

  • “Saying that ℝ is complete means that every sequence of real numbers converges.”

    The statement applies only to Cauchy sequences. The sequence that alternates between 0 and 1 has a gap equal to 1 at each index: it is not Cauchy and does not converge, despite the completeness of ℝ.

    Reread the rule in the intuition section

Exercise 1 · Explained application

Move to the next index

The pair at index 2 is (p, q) = (577, 408). Calculate the next pair using the rules from the example, then the gap between the term at index 2 and the next term. Compare it with 1 divided by the new q.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The new p is p² + 2q²: both squares were already used in the example.

Hint 2

The new q is a multiple of 408. Rewrite the term at index 2 with this denominator before subtracting.

Compare with the solution

New p: 332929 + 332928 = 665857. New q: 2 × 577 × 408 = 470832. The next term is therefore 665857/470832.

To subtract, observe that 470832 = 408 × 1154. The term at index 2 can be written as 577 × 1154 over 470832, or 665858 over 470832. The numerator of the gap is 665858 − 665857 = 1, so the gap equals 1/470832.

This is exactly 1/470832: the gap formula is verified once more, without rounding.

Check your reasoning

  • Both rules are applied using the squares at the correct index.
  • The gap is calculated using a common denominator and remains exact.
  • The result is compared with 1 divided by the new q.

Exercise 2 · Explained application

Choose an index for a given accuracy

We want all terms with indices at least n to remain within ε = 1/1000 of one another. Among the indices 0, 1 and 2, what is the smallest index guaranteed by the tail bound in the example? Then show that the smaller indices actually fail, rather than merely lacking a guarantee.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare each bound 2dₙ with ε. Of two fractions with numerator 1, the one with the smaller denominator is larger.

Hint 2

To show that an index actually fails, exhibit two terms at or beyond that index whose gap exceeds ε.

Compare with the solution

Index 0: the bound is 1/6, greater than ε. Index 1: the bound is 1/204, approximately 0.0049, greater than 0.001. Index 2: the bound is 1/235416, less than ε, since 1/235416 has a much larger denominator. The smallest guaranteed index is therefore 2.

The smaller indices actually fail. At index 0, u0 − u1 = 1/12 exceeds ε. At index 1, u1 − u2 = 1/408 also exceeds ε = 1/1000. A single pair of terms too far apart is enough to rule out an index.

Check your reasoning

  • Each bound is compared with ε using the denominators, without rounded values.
  • Index 2 is established using the bound 1/235416.
  • Indices 0 and 1 are ruled out by an explicit pair of terms.

Exercise 3 · Guided proof

A proposed limit, then the general proof

A classmate proposes r = 17/12, the term at index 1, as the limit of the sequence. First, calculate r² − 2 and draw a conclusion. Then reconstruct the proof that ℓ is not rational, justifying each step through parity, without copying the example.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write r² in terms of p and q: (17/12)², then express it with denominator 144.

Hint 2

An even square comes from an even integer: examine the remainder when an integer is divided by 2.

Hint 3

After writing a = 2c, substitute into a² = 2b² and examine the parity of b.

Compare with the solution

Proposed limit: r² − 2 = (289 − 288)/144 = 1/144 = 1/144, which is not 0. Thus r² ≠ 2, and r is not the limit: r is just a term of the sequence, above ℓ.

General proof. Suppose ℓ = a/b with a, b strictly positive coprime integers. Then a² = 2b², so a² is even. If a were odd, a² would leave remainder 1 on division by 2; thus a is even, a = 2c.

Then 2²c² = 2b², so b² = 2c² is even, and b is even for the same reason. Thus 2 divides a and b, contradicting the assumption that they are coprime. No rational number works.

Check your reasoning

  • The proposed limit is tested by its square, using an exact equality rather than a decimal approximation.
  • Each step from “even square” to “even integer” is justified by the remainder on division by 2.
  • The contradiction explicitly concerns the assumption that a and b are coprime.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does it mean for a sequence to be Cauchy?

For every ε > 0, there is an index beyond which any two terms differ by less than ε: eventually, all the terms are close to one another, without reference to a limit.

Recall 2What does the completeness of ℝ guarantee, and what does it leave undetermined?

Every Cauchy sequence of real numbers converges to a real number. Completeness gives neither the value of this limit nor whether it is rational.

Recall 3Which example in this unit shows that ℚ is not complete?

The sequence of rational numbers with first term 3/2, in which each term is the average of the preceding term and 2 divided by it. It is Cauchy, its real limit ℓ satisfies ℓ² = 2, and this limit is not rational.

Recall 4Which invariant controls the pairs (p, q) in the sequence, and why is it preserved?

p² − 2q² = 1. At the next index, the quantity equals the square of its value at the preceding index, so it remains equal to 1.

Recall 5Which tail bound shows that the sequence is Cauchy?

Since each consecutive gap is at most half the preceding one, the difference between the term at index n and any later term is strictly positive and less than 2 times the gap 1/q from index n to the next index.

Reconstruct the reasoning

State the set (ℚ or ℝ); define the sequence using p and q with the invariant p² − 2q² = 1; deduce the gap 1/q between consecutive terms; bound the tail by 2 times the first gap; conclude that the sequence is Cauchy; apply the completeness of ℝ to obtain ℓ; take limits to obtain ℓ² = 2; use parity to show that ℓ is not rational; conclude that ℚ is not complete.
