Multiple testing

Your goalMeasure the risk of a false positive when several rules are tested on the same historical data, apply Bonferroni and then Holm, and name the assumptions that these adjustments do not check.

✿01 See the idea

A team tests 4 daily signal rules on the same historical return data. We call them A, B, C and D. For each rule, the null hypothesis H0 states that the rule adds no mean return: any apparent benefit would come from sampling chance. The entire lesson uses this fictional scenario: it describes no real rule, return or historical data.

Each test produces a p-value, a dimensionless number between 0 and 1. Under H0 and the assumptions of the test model, it is the probability of obtaining a result at least as extreme as the one observed. A rule is selected when its p-value is less than or equal to the threshold α, here 0.05, or 5 %. If H0 is true, an individual test therefore selects the rule incorrectly, a false positive, with probability at most α.

The problem arises when we examine several rules and report those that pass. Each test keeps its risk α, but the risk of at least one false positive in the family grows with the number of tests. We call this the family-wise error rate, or FWER: the probability of selecting at least one rule incorrectly. We calculate it when all null hypotheses in the family are true, and we want to bring it back down to α.

The two adjustments in this unit achieve this. Bonferroni compares each p-value with a stricter threshold, α divided by the number m of tests. Holm sorts the p-values, compares the smallest with α/m and the next with a slightly less strict threshold, and stops at the first failure; it selects at least every rule that Bonferroni selects.

This unit does not generate p-values. The earlier units in the chapter address extreme values, structural breaks and long memory: these are reasons why a p-value may be poorly calibrated. We assume it is valid here; an adjustment for multiple testing does not repair a poorly constructed test.

The rule to remember

Declare the complete family and m before looking at the results; compare p with α/m (Bonferroni) or, after sorting in ascending order, the j-th p-value with α/(m − j + 1) until the first failure (Holm); state the assumptions.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Four rules on the same fictional historical data

Four rules A, B, C and D are tested on the same fictional historical data. Their fictional p-values are 0.003, 0.014, 0.021 and 0.300, and the target level for the family is α = 0.05. What do the unadjusted tests, Bonferroni and Holm report?

  1. Specify the family, symbols and assumptions

    m is the number of tests in the family, declared before looking at the results: here m = 4, dimensionless. p is the p-value of a test, dimensionless. α = 0.05 is the target level, a dimensionless probability; as a percentage it is 5 %. FWER is the probability of selecting at least one rule incorrectly.

    Assumptions: each p-value is valid, meaning that under H0 it falls below a threshold t with probability at most t; the family is complete, with all rules tried counted in m. For the first formula, we also assume independence of the tests, a strong assumption for rules evaluated on the same historical data.

  2. Measure the risk without adjustment

    If the 4 null hypotheses are true and the tests are independent, each test avoids a false positive with probability 1 − α = 0.95. The probability that all 4 tests avoid one is the product of these probabilities, hence 0.95 to the power m. At least one false positive is the complementary event.

    FWER=1−(1−α)m

    Calculation keeping every product: 0.95 × 0.95 = 0.9025, then × 0.95 = 0.8574, then × 0.95 = 0.8145. The risk is 1 − 0.8145 = 0.1855, or 18.5 %. Check: this is 3.7 times α, while the formula returns α when the family has only a single test.

  3. Decide without adjustment, then with Bonferroni

    Without adjustment, compare each p with α = 0.05: A (0.003), B (0.014) and C (0.021) are selected; D (0.300) is not. This gives 3 selected rules, with a family-wise error rate of 0.1855 if none had any real value. Bonferroni divides the threshold by m.

    sB=αm=0.054=0.0125

    With this threshold, A (0.003) is selected; B (0.014), C and D are not. This leaves 1 selected rule.

  4. Why the bound holds even without independence

    The probability of a union of events is at most the sum of their probabilities. If each test makes an error with probability at most α/m, the union of these errors has probability at most m times α/m. Independence is not required.

    FWER≤m×αm=α

    Check the calculation: 4 × 0.0125 = 0.05, which is α. When only some null hypotheses are true, the bound can only decrease. It is conservative: with independent tests, the actual risk is slightly below α.

  5. Read the Bonferroni adjusted p-values

    We can multiply each p by m, cap it at 1, then compare it with α: the decision is the same, because p ≤ α/m is equivalent to m × p ≤ α. The resulting values are transformed thresholds, not the probability that the null hypothesis for the rule is true.

    A: 4 × 0.003 = 0.012, less than or equal to 0.05, selected. B: 4 × 0.014 = 0.056, not selected. C: 4 × 0.021 = 0.084, not selected. D: 4 × 0.300 = 1.2, capped at 1, not selected.

  6. Work step by step with Holm

    Holm sorts the p-values from smallest to largest. The test at rank j, with j equal to 1 for the smallest, is compared with the threshold below; at the first failure, that test and all subsequent tests are not selected.

    sj=αm−j+1

    Rank 1: A, 0.003 ≤ 0.0125, selected. Rank 2: B, 0.014 ≤ 0.0167, selected, although Bonferroni did not select it. Rank 3: C, 0.021 ≤ 0.025, selected. Rank 4: D, 0.300 > 0.05, stop. Holm selects 3 rules.

    Check using adjusted p-values, with multiplier m − j + 1: 4 × 0.003 = 0.012, 3 × 0.014 = 0.042, 2 × 0.021 = 0.042, 1 × 0.300 = 0.300. The sequence never decreases, every term except the last is less than or equal to 0.05, and the decision is the same. That Holm keeps FWER at most α without independence is a standard result accepted here without proof; each Holm threshold is at least α/m, so Holm selects at least every rule that Bonferroni selects.

  7. Check and limit the interpretation

    The numbers of selected rules are ordered as expected: 3 without adjustment, 3 with Holm, 1 with Bonferroni. Bonferroni made the decision stricter for B and C; Holm keeps them selected here because its thresholds are less strict. A is selected by all three procedures, D by none.

    Selecting A, B and C with Holm does not mean they would yield any return: the p-values are fictional and assumed valid, and the family is assumed complete. If the team had tried other rules without counting them, m would be too small and the thresholds too permissive. Neither adjustment repairs a poorly calibrated p-value or a threshold chosen after the fact.

Explain it in your own words

Explain without looking why the Bonferroni bound does not require independence while the formula for unadjusted risk does, then why Holm selects at least as many rules as Bonferroni.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Each test keeps its threshold α, so the family also keeps α.”

    α is the risk for an individual test. For the family, the risk becomes 1 − (…)^m: with m = 4 and α = 0.05, it reaches 0.1855, or 3.7 times α in the example.

    Review the family-wise error rate calculation

  • “I choose the family after seeing which rules look promising.”

    The rules tried, all counted, define m. Removing disappointing rules after the fact reduces m and makes the thresholds too permissive. Declare m and the list before looking at the results; the reviewed code also refuses an incomplete family.

    See the complete declared family in the code

  • “An adjusted p-value is the probability that the null hypothesis for the rule is true.”

    An adjusted p-value is a transformed threshold: a rule is selected when it is less than or equal to α. In the example, 0.012 for A means only that A passes the threshold, not that the probability of its null hypothesis being true is 0.012.

Exercise 2 · Transfer

An additional rule in the family

A fifth rule, E, is added to the historical data before anyone looks at the results; its fictional p-value is 0.012. The family therefore has m = 5. The p-values of A to D remain those in the example and α = 0.05. What is the Bonferroni threshold? What happens to the decisions for A, B and E?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The threshold depends on m: recalculate α divided by the new family size.

Hint 2

Compare either p with this threshold or m × p with α; both methods must agree.

Compare with the solution

Threshold: 0.05 / 5 = 0.010. A: 0.003 ≤ 0.010, selected; adjusted method: 5 × 0.003 = 0.015 ≤ 0.05.

B: 0.014 > 0.010, not selected; adjusted value 0.070 > 0.05. E: 0.012 > 0.010, not selected; adjusted value 0.060 > 0.05, although 0.012 would pass the unadjusted threshold 0.05.

Check: 5 × 0.010 = 0.05, which is α: the family-wise bound is preserved. Adding a rule makes the threshold stricter for all the others; this is the cost of a larger family.

Exercise 3 · Analysis

Why Holm keeps a cumulative maximum

Another fictional family has m = 3 rules, sorted by increasing p-values: 0.010, 0.012 and 0.400. With α = 0.05, calculate the product (m − j + 1) × p for each rank j. Explain why a value that is too small is replaced by the previous maximum, then state which rules Holm selects.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The multipliers are 3, 2 and 1, from the lowest rank to the highest.

Hint 2

Compare the first two products: can a larger p-value be more convincing?

Hint 3

Then compare each adjusted value with α, and check using thresholds α/m, α/2 and α.

Compare with the solution

Rank 1: 3 × 0.010 = 0.030. Rank 2: 2 × 0.012 = 0.024. Rank 3: 1 × 0.400 = 0.400.

The second product, 0.024, is less than the first, 0.030, even though its p-value is larger: a less significant rule cannot receive a more convincing adjusted value. The cumulative maximum keeps 0.030 at rank 2. The adjusted values are 0.030, 0.030 and 0.400.

Compared with α = 0.05, the first two rules are selected and the third is not. Check using thresholds: 0.010 ≤ 0.0167, 0.012 ≤ 0.025, then 0.400 > 0.05. Here the maximum does not change the decision, but it ensures that a larger p-value never receives a smaller adjusted value.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the three products with the correct multipliers.
  • Justify the cumulative maximum using the order of the p-values.
  • State which rules are selected and check using the threshold method.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the family-wise error rate measure?

The probability of selecting at least one rule incorrectly in the family of tests; the formula studied assumes that all null hypotheses are true and the tests are independent.

Recall 2What threshold does Bonferroni apply, and why does it bound the risk?

It compares each p-value with α/m. The union bound gives a risk of at most m times α/m, which is α, without assuming independence.

Recall 3How does Holm work through the p-values?

It sorts them, compares the j-th with α/(m − j + 1) and stops at the first failure; each of its thresholds is at least α/m.

Recall 4What do these adjustments leave unchecked?

The validity of each p-value and the declaration of the complete family before looking; they establish neither the quality of the model nor the real usefulness of a selected rule.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Declare the complete family and m before looking; measure the family-wise error rate as one minus (one minus α) to the power m under independence; compare p with α/m using Bonferroni, or sort and compare with α/(m − j + 1) until the first failure using Holm; read an adjusted p-value as a transformed threshold; name the remaining assumptions: valid p-values, a complete family, no promise of returns.

What you understand
When several hypotheses are assessed together, the threshold for each depends on the family size. Holm sorts the p-values, multiplies the smallest by the family size, the next by that size minus one, and so on, with a cumulative maximum.
