Missing values

Your goalIdentify a missing measurement, calculate a mean using only the known measurements, and explain what a replacement changes in the array.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

In the garden, you record the mass harvested from each growing bed. The daily record shows A: 4 kg; B: 0 kg; C: —; D: 8 kg; E: —. For B, a check confirms that there is nothing to harvest. For C and E, no mass has been recorded. These data are fictional, chosen so that you can repeat the calculations by hand.

A missing value is an expected value that we do not have. Here the dash means “unknown mass”. It does not mean “no harvest”. Replacing this dash with 0 kg would add a claim that the record does not contain.

Imagine the record as a row of named slots. To calculate using the known measurements, we select A, B and D while keeping the positions and names of C and E in the original record. The 0 for B remains a known measurement: it counts towards the number of measurements even though it does not change their sum.

Our first aim is to describe the known masses, rather than guess the whole harvest. The beds have the same area, the record concerns the same day, and all masses are expressed in kilograms. Each known measurement receives the same weight in the mean. Nothing guarantees that the beds with no recorded measurement resemble the others.

The rule to remember

Select the known measurements before calculating; keep measured zeros, state how many measurements take part, and keep a record of missing values.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Report what the record shows without inventing a weighing

Starting from A = 4 kg, B = 0 kg, C unknown, D = 8 kg and E unknown, let us calculate the mean of the known masses. Then let us compare this result with what explicit replacements would produce.

  1. Keep the value and its status

    The dash is a display convention, rather than a number that can be added. In Python, None can represent a missing value. In some numerical arrays, NaN is an undefined numerical marker used, among other things, to indicate a missing value. Neither represents a mass of 0. You need to know the convention used in the array and check that the measurements are valid before selecting them.

    Our presence mask is the sequence of decisions “yes, yes, no, yes, no”, in the order A, B, C, D, E. A Boolean mask assigns true or false to each slot in this way; here true means “measurement known and accepted for the calculation”. It is used to select, rather than to multiply a missing value by 0. An infinite value or unreadable text would not be a usable measurement either.

    N denotes the number of expected slots; n denotes the number of selected known measurements. These counts have no physical unit. S is the sum of their masses, in kg. The letter x with a bar, x̄, will denote their mean, also in kg. We assume that the known masses are valid and use the same unit; we assume no value for the missing entries.

  2. Count before dividing

    A and B give 1 + 1 = 2 measurements; D adds 1 more. Thus n = 3, while N = 5. The number of missing values is N − n = 5 − 3 = 2.

    Coverage describes the proportion of slots with a recorded value: n/N = 3/5 = 0.60, with no unit. This ratio describes how complete the record is; it measures neither the quality of the weighings nor a probability that the mean is correct.

  3. Add the known masses, then share them equally

    Let us add only the selected values: 4 + 0 = 4 kg, then 4 + 8 = 12 kg. This is S, a partial total. To find the mean, we share this mass into as many equal parts as there are participating measurements:

    x¯=Sn=123=4 kg

    This division requires n to be greater than 0. We report: “mean of the known masses: 4 kg, across 3 beds with recorded values out of 5”. B contributes to the denominator because its mass of 0 is known.

  4. Check the calculation and what the result describes

    Inverse check: 3 × 4 = 12 kg, matching the partial sum. The mean also lies between the smallest and largest known masses, 0 kg and 8 kg. These checks detect calculation errors; they do not reveal the missing masses.

    Let u denote the unknown mass of C and v the unknown mass of E, both in kg. The mean across all the beds would be (S + u + v)/N. The letters u and v are assigned no value in this exercise: the overall mean remains unknown. The partial total S does not become the total harvest just because the program can add it up.

    If all the weighings were missing, n would equal 0. The mean of the known values would be undefined; we would display “no measurement available”, without dividing or reporting 0 kg. A record of measured zeros, by contrast, would contain measurements and have a mean of 0.

  5. See what replacing missing values with 0 changes

    Filling C and E with 0 kg keeps the sum at 12 kg but makes 5 numbers contribute to the mean. The calculation becomes 12/5 = 12/5 kg, or 2.40 kg. This value describes the modified record under the added assumption that the missing masses are 0. The original record does not justify that assumption.

    Conversely, selecting only strictly positive masses would remove B. Only 2 masses would remain, and the mean would be 12/2 = 6 kg. “Known” and “positive” answer different questions: for our purpose, we must keep B.

  6. Distinguish imputation from observation

    To impute means to assign a replacement value to a missing entry according to a stated rule. If a later calculation requires a complete record, one possible rule is to assign the known mean to C and E, giving 4 kg to each. This assumes that the available masses can reasonably represent those masses, an assumption that still needs to be examined. To describe our recorded measurements faithfully, we can simply leave those values missing.

    Let us calculate this convention without confusing it with weighings: 2 × 4 = 8 kg are added; 12 + 8 = 20 kg becomes the total in the completed record. Its mean is 20/5 = 4 kg. It equals the known mean even though the rule has invented values. An unchanged mean therefore does not prove that the information is unchanged.

    Let us keep the original record and label C and E “imputed using the mean” in any completed copy. The number of known weighings remains 3, and the observed coverage remains 3/5. For an entirely missing column, no known mean can be used for replacement; any fallback value would be an additional convention requiring justification.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations and redraw the record. Explain why B counts towards n, why C and E do not count, and then how imputation can preserve the mean without providing a new weighing.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A gap and a 0 are the same for calculations.”

    For B, 0 kg is known. For C and E, the mass remains unknown. Replacing them with zeros turns missing information into asserted masses; keep the mass separate from its presence status.

    Read the record and the yes or no statuses again

  • “I keep only masses that are not 0.”

    Test for presence and validity, rather than whether a value is positive. Removing B changes the group described: 2 positive masses give a mean of 6 kg, instead of 4 kg across all known masses.

    Revisit the mask and the denominator

  • “The mean is identical after filling in the gaps, so I have recovered the values.”

    Replacing missing values with the mean adds 8 kg to the record and preserves its mean by construction. C and E remain imputed masses; keep track of their origin and look for a weighing if you want to know their actual values.

Hint 1

The sum and the number of terms must describe the same set of measurements.

Hint 2

To check your known mean, multiply it by the number of masses that contribute to it.

Compare with the solution

The known sum is 12 kg. Divided by the number of slots, it gives 12/5 = 12/5 kg = 2.40 kg. This operation implicitly assigns a mass of 0 to the missing entries if it is presented as the overall mean.

For the known masses, divide by 3: 12/3 = 4 kg. Coverage is 3/5 = 3/5. Check: 3 × 4 = 12 kg; the mean lies between 0 kg and 8 kg.

You can publish a mean for the known masses together with the number of measurements. The masses of C and E are still unavailable, so this mean is not an established overall mean.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 12/5 kg, or 2.40 kg, and explain the implicit assumption.
  • Report 4 kg across 3 measurements out of 5.
  • Check the inverse multiplication and leave the overall mean unknown.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and justification

A recovered weighing replaces a convention

A copy of the record had replaced C and E with 4 kg each. A slip dated the same day now confirms C = 6 kg; E remains unknown. Start again from the observed measurements and calculate their new sum, mean and coverage. What happens to the earlier imputations? And what would need to be reported if all the slips were missing?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The recovered mass of C is added to the known masses in the original record; the imputation for E is not a weighing.

Hint 2

Increase the number of known masses by 1. For a record with all measurements missing, check whether a denominator is available.

Compare with the solution

The known masses are now A = 4 kg, B = 0 kg, C = 6 kg and D = 8 kg. Their sum is 12 + 6 = 18 kg; their number is 3 + 1 = 4. There is still 1 unknown mass.

The new mean is 18/4 = 9/2 kg, or 4.50 kg. Observed coverage is 4/5 = 4/5. Check: 4 × 4.50 = 18 kg.

The weighing for C replaces its earlier imputation and must be marked as observed. E remains missing in the raw record; if a copy needs to be completed, its replacement rule must be reconsidered and its imputed status retained. The earlier value for E must not increase the count of known measurements.

With no slips and no verified masses, n = 0: report “mean undefined, no measurement available”. Do not report 0 kg. Even with C recovered, the mean across all beds remains unknown until the mass of E has been established.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate 18 kg across 4 measurements and obtain 4.50 kg.
  • Keep E out of the count of weighings and report coverage of 4/5.
  • Distinguish the observed replacement for C from the still missing or imputed status of E.
  • Handle a record with all measurements missing without dividing by 0 or inventing a mean of 0.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why must the 0 for B be treated differently from the dash for C?

B has a known mass of 0 kg; C has no known mass. B contributes to the mean of the known values, while C must remain marked as missing.

Recall 2What does a presence mask decide, and what information must remain aligned?

For each slot, it decides whether the measurement is known and acceptable. The decisions stay in the same order as the bed names; the mask does more than test whether the mass is positive.

Recall 3What quantities must be given with a mean calculated from an incomplete record?

The sum of the known values divided by their number, together with the unit and coverage: here 4 kg across 3 measurements out of 5. The overall mean remains unknown.

Recall 4Why does a mean preserved after imputation fail to prove that the missing values have been resolved?

Replacing each missing value with the known mean preserves that mean by construction. The rule creates replacement values; it provides no weighing. Keep the raw values, the rule and the imputed status.

Recall 5What should the calculation produce if no mass is known?

An explicit status: “no measurement available, mean undefined”. The number of known masses is 0, so no division is performed; a mean of 0 kg would make a different claim.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct this sequence without rereading: identify each slot and its status; select the known masses, keeping zeros; count and sum the same set; divide only if it contains measurements; state the unit and coverage; keep missing values and the origin of every replacement. Finish by explaining what the record still does not allow you to know.
