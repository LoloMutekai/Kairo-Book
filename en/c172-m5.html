Level 3 · C172·M5

Euler-Maruyama

Your goalConstruct Euler-Maruyama steps, justify the noise scaling, and compare two time grids driven by the same Brownian motion.

Before you start
c171-m5 : multidimensional case · c052-m5 : convergence protocol · c172-m1 : existence and uniqueness · c172-m2 : GBM · c172-m3 : Ornstein-Uhlenbeck · c172-m4 : CIR

✿01 See the idea

A small object slides along a rail. A restoring effect pulls it back towards the origin mark; unpredictable jolts disturb its motion. Its position is signed: it may cross to the other side of the mark. We use a model with no inertia or end stop. The entire scene, its parameters, and the jolts used in the calculations are fictional.

To advance over a short interval, take the restoring drift calculated at the starting position, multiply it by the duration, then add the jolt for that interval. Start again from the new position. This procedure is called Euler-Maruyama: the Euler rule for the drift, with a Brownian increment added.

The deterministic part is proportional to the duration. The typical size of the jolt is proportional to its square root. This second choice preserves the correct noise variance when time is subdivided. An individual jolt may be small, large, or opposite in sign to the restoring drift.

The first diagram separates the contributions to a step; these boxes are calculation operations, rather than physical stages observed in the object. The second prepares a fair comparison between a whole interval and its subintervals.

The rule to remember

Freeze the coefficients at the start of the step; add drift × duration and diffusion × Brownian increment; recalculate the coefficients at the next step.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Two steps for the same object

The initial position is 2 cm. The restoring coefficient is λ = 2 per second and the diffusion is σ = 1 cm/√s. Take h = 1/4 s and the prepared standardized values: initial Z = 1, then next Z = -1. Calculate the positions up to T = 1/2 s. These values are chosen for calculation by hand; they do not form a sample from which a distribution can be estimated.

  1. State the equation and its units

    Xt is the continuous position in the model at time t, in centimetres. Wt is a standard Brownian motion, expressed in square root of a second under our unit convention. The equation is interpreted in the Ito sense. The equilibrium mark is 0 cm and the initial state is deterministic.

    dXt=−λXtdt+σdWt

    λ controls the strength of the restoring effect: −λXt is a drift in centimetres per second. σ is the diffusion amplitude in cm/√s. Each integrated term therefore gives a displacement in centimetres. The term dWt represents a random increment after integration, rather than an ordinary velocity that could be differentiated.

    This is the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck model encountered earlier. Its coefficients are known and constant in time; its drift depends on position. We calculate a discrete approximation Yk of X at time tk = kh. k is a dimensionless integer index; h is a positive duration.

  2. Freeze the coefficients at the left endpoint of each interval

    More generally, b(t,x) denotes the drift in cm/s and a(t,x) the diffusion in cm/√s of an Ito equation dXt = b(t,Xt)dt + a(t,Xt)dWt. Over an interval, Euler-Maruyama replaces the coefficients inside the integrals with their known values at the start. Integrating dt gives h; integrating dW gives ΔWk = W at the next time − W at the current time.

    Yk+1=Yk+b(tk,Yk)h+a(tk,Yk)ΔWk

    In our scene, b(t,x) = −λx and a(t,x) = σ. The rule therefore becomes Y next = (1 − λh)Y current + σΔW. It is explicit: no unknown future position is needed to calculate the right-hand side.

  3. Give the noise the correct scale

    Over disjoint intervals of the same length h, Brownian increments are independent, with a normal distribution of mean 0 and variance h. We can therefore construct them using independent, dimensionless variables Zk with a normal distribution of mean 0 and variance 1, independent of the past of the model.

    ΔWk=hZk

    Here, √h = 1/2 √s, because 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 s. The first increment is 1/2 × 1 = 1/2 √s; the next is 1/2 × (-1) = -1/2 √s.

    Distinguish the variance prescribed by the distribution from the square of an individual value. The prepared values are a deterministic input to the exercise, rather than evidence that generated increments follow this distribution. A simulation program would need to generate new independent Z values at each step.

  4. Carry out the first step without rounding

    At the start, λY = 2 × 2 = 4 cm/s. The restoring drift points towards the origin: b = -4 cm/s. Over h = 1/4 s, its displacement is -4 × 1/4 = -1 cm. The deterministic part therefore leads to 2 + (-1) = 1 cm.

    The random displacement is σΔW = 1 × 1/2 = 1/2 cm. Add it to the deterministic part, without recalculating b halfway through the operation.

    Y1=2+(-1)+1/2=3/2 cm

    Check the direction: the restoring drift reduced the position; the positive jolt offset part of that reduction. 3/2 cm, or 1.50 cm, is an exact result of the scheme for this input. It is not the exact position of the continuous process.

  5. Start from the result and recalculate the restoring drift

    At the next step, the starting position is 3/2 cm. The new product λY is 3 cm/s, so b = -3 cm/s. The deterministic displacement is -3 × 1/4 = -3/4 cm; the deterministic part is 3/2 + (-3/4) = 3/4 cm.

    The new jolt is σΔW = 1 × (-1/2) = -1/2 cm. This time, its sign agrees with the restoring drift.

    Y2=3/2+(-3/4)+(-1/2)=1/4 cm

    At time T = 2 × 1/4 = 1/2 s, the scheme gives 0.25 cm. For another check, set c = 1 − λh = 1/2, which is dimensionless. The expanded form Y final = c × c × Y initial + cσΔW initial + σΔW next gives 1/2 × 1/2 × 2 + 1/2 × 1/2 + (-1/2) = 1/4 cm. The factor on the first noise term reveals its influence on the next restoring drift.

  6. Check the distribution and distinguish the scheme from the exact solution

    The squared diffusion is 1 square centimetres per second. The conditional variance of the random displacement is σ × σ × h = 1/4 square centimetres. Conditional means here that the starting position is fixed and we average only over future draws. E denotes this probabilistic average, and Var the average squared deviation from that average.

    E[σhZk]=0, Var[σhZk]=σ2h

    In the simulation, replacing √h with h while keeping the same numerical value for σ would give a numerical amplitude of 1/4 and a numerical variance of 1/16, instead of 1/4 in the specified units. Checking the units also reveals the problem: σh does not have the unit of a displacement.

    The mean of the first discrete step is (1 − λh)Y initial = 1 cm. For the continuous Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process, the exact mean at the same time is Y initial × exp(−λh), or 2 × exp(−1/2) cm. exp denotes the exponential function; its argument λh is dimensionless. The difference between these expressions comes from discretizing the restoring drift, even though the noise has the correct variance. The individual value 3/2 cm is neither of these means.

  7. Prepare a convergence check

    To compare approximations of the same path, keep T and the parameters fixed. Over a coarse interval made up of smaller intervals, add their ΔW values: in our trace, 1/2 + (-1/2) = 0 √s. Entirely new draws would change both the path and the step size. Keeping only the same random seed does not guarantee this addition across time grids.

    A comparison of paths measures the difference between approximations driven by the same noise. A comparison of distributions instead examines means or distributions over many paths; its sampling uncertainty must be distinguished from discretization error. The last exercise constructs the path comparison for our trace; it does not measure a convergence rate.

    For an autonomous equation, whose coefficients do not depend on time, globally Lipschitz coefficients with at most linear growth, together with a square-integrable initial state, are standard sufficient assumptions for mean-square convergence of the scheme over a fixed horizon as h tends to 0. Lipschitz means that the change in the coefficients is bounded by a constant times the change in the state; linear growth bounds their size by a constant times the sum of 1 and the absolute value of the state. Our linear restoring drift and constant diffusion satisfy these conditions. Mean-square convergence means that the average squared error tends to 0.

    These conditions do not guarantee the accuracy of a given step. They do not automatically extend to every coefficient in the chapter. In particular, the CIR model in the previous unit has a diffusion involving the square root of the state: explicit Euler-Maruyama can leave its positive domain. Fixing this problem would require a separate method and analysis.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Why is the drift multiplied by h, the standardized draw by √h, and why is the first noise term still present in the restoring drift of the next step? Then explain why an exact fraction calculated by the scheme remains an approximation of the continuous position.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I multiply the standardized draw by h like everything else.”

    The variance of Z is 1. Multiplying Z by √h gives a variance of h; multiplying it by h gives h squared. Check the variance and units again before calculating a path.

    Review the noise scaling and its check

  • “I can recalculate the drift after adding the jolt for the same step.”

    Euler-Maruyama evaluates the coefficients at the start of the step, using the state that is already known. Evaluating them again after the jolt changes the scheme. At the next step, however, you must use the new position and recalculate the drift.

    Reread the breakdown of a step

  • “I reduce the step size, draw new independent noise values, and the difference proves convergence.”

    To compare the same path, the noise for the coarse step must be the sum of the noise for the smaller steps. With independent draws across time grids, the difference mixes noise with discretization. Even a single coupled comparison does not prove convergence.

Compare with the solution

√h fine = 1/4 √s, since 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16 s. The deterministic displacement is -4 × 1/16 = -1/4 cm; the random displacement is 1 × 1/4 × 1 = 1/4 cm.

After the deterministic part, the position is 7/4 cm. The final sum is 7/4 + 1/4 = 2 cm. The displacements cancel for this prepared input, which does not mean there is no noise.

The variance changes from 1/4 to 1/16 square centimetres; the ratio is 4. The amplitude has been divided by 2, the variance by 4. The final times are 1/4 s and 1/16 s: this trial illustrates local scaling. It does not compare two approximations over the same horizon, and identical Z values alone do not construct a coupling between time grids.

Check your reasoning

  • The square root of the new step size is checked by squaring it.
  • Both contributions are calculated before their sum 2 cm.
  • The variance factor is distinguished from the amplitude factor.
  • The different final times prevent a conclusion about convergence.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · compare time grids

The total noise is 0; must the position be 0 too?

Return to the complete trace in the example up to T = 1/2 s. Now construct a single coarse step of duration H = T starting from 2 cm, with ΔW coarse equal to the sum of the supplied increments. Calculate its position, then the difference from the 2 steps of duration h. Explain why a 0 sum of increments is not enough to make the results identical. Hint 1

For the coarse step, the drift is frozen at the initial position throughout the duration H.

Hint 2

In the smaller steps, the first noise term changes the position at which the next restoring drift is evaluated.

Hint 3

The expanded form with c = 1/2 provides another way to recover the result of the smaller steps.

Compare with the solution

The coupling requires ΔW coarse = 1/2 + (-1/2) = 0 √s. No new draw is needed. The deterministic displacement of the coarse step is -4 × 1/2 = -2 cm, giving 0 cm before the noise; its random contribution is 0 cm. The final position is 0 cm.

The smaller steps give 1/4 cm. The signed difference, smaller steps minus coarse step, is 1/4 − 0 = 1/4 cm. Even though the total Brownian increment is 0, the first noise term changed the restoring drift in the second interval. The restoring drift itself was also evaluated again at the new position. The scheme therefore depends on more than the total noise.

This difference is not an error relative to the exact solution: that solution has not been reconstructed for this path. Continue refining at the same T, always aggregate the increments, and repeat over many mutually independent paths. To measure an error, add an exact reference or a finer approximation whose accuracy has been studied, then separate the spread across paths from the effect of the step size. A pair of time grids provides neither a convergence rate nor an actual gain.

Check your reasoning

  • The coarse increment is a sum, rather than a new draw.
  • The coarse step gives 0 cm and the signed difference is 1/4 cm.
  • Reevaluating the restoring drift explains why the results may differ.
  • The difference between schemes is distinguished from the exact error and from a proof of convergence.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you reconstruct an Euler-Maruyama step, and where do you evaluate the coefficients?

Start from the known position. Add b(t current, Y current)h, then a(t current, Y current)ΔW. Both coefficients are frozen at the start of the step. The result becomes the starting point of the next step, where the coefficients are recalculated.

Recall 2Why write ΔW = √h Z rather than h Z?

Z is normally distributed with mean 0 and variance 1. Multiplying by √h gives a variance of h, the variance of a Brownian increment of duration h. With h Z, the variance would be h squared. The Z values must be independent over disjoint intervals and independent of the past.

Recall 3Why is our fraction 1/4 cm not an exact solution of the continuous equation?

It is exact for the operations of the scheme and the prepared increments. Euler-Maruyama froze the drift over each interval, whereas the continuous model lets it evolve. Exact arithmetic does not remove this approximation of the model in time.

Recall 4How do you compare two time grids for the same path over the same horizon?

Construct the increments on the fine time grid, then add neighbouring increments for the intervals of the coarse time grid. Keep the same horizon, starting point, and coefficients. The same seed alone does not guarantee this relationship between increments.

Recall 5What does our difference of 1/4 cm between the time grids prove?

It shows an effect of the time grid for this coupled trace. It does not say which approximation is closer to the exact path and does not prove convergence. Several refinements, repetitions, and a reference with known accuracy are needed to estimate an error.
