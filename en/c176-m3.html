Level 3 · C176·M3

Antithetic variates

Your goalConstruct a pair of antithetic simulations, justify its mean and compare its variance with that of independent simulations at the same cost.

Before you start
c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c051-m5 : error diagnostics · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c017-m5 : reproduction protocol · c176-m1 : generators and seeds · c176-m2 : Monte Carlo error

✿01 See the idea

A workshop wants to estimate the mean duration of a drying cycle in a fictional simulator. A random slider represents the intensity of a disturbance: near 0, the cycle is short; near 1, it is long. The exact simulator rule will be given in the example. All durations and parameters are fictional.

Instead of making a second draw unrelated to the first, take the reflection of the first slider: 0.25 is paired with 0.75. Calculate both durations and then their mean. A weak disturbance is thus accompanied by a strong disturbance. This choice of dependence is called a coupling; here, it is antithetic.

Reflection preserves the distribution of each slider considered separately. It changes their relationship: when one rises, the other falls. If the durations follow this opposite movement, their deviations tend to offset one another in the mean. The dependence between the outputs, after calculating the durations, will determine whether the method is useful.

The second calculation still costs one evaluation of the simulator. The comparison will therefore be between 2 antithetic durations and 2 independent durations. A pair mean remains random: it does not necessarily give the exact mean duration.

The rule to remember

Preserve the distribution of each input, arrange the dependence between the outputs, then compare variances using equal numbers of evaluations.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The mean duration of the drying cycle

The fictional simulator returns f(u) = 10 + 6 × u squared, in seconds. The slider U follows the uniform distribution on [0, 1]: intervals of equal length have equal probability. We construct a pair, calculate its mean and then compare the theoretical dispersions. The model is deliberately chosen to allow analytic integration and an exact check.

  1. Define the model, target and cost

    f(u)=10+6u2;μ=E[f(U)]

    u is a realization of the random slider U and has no unit. f is a deterministic function: the coefficients 10 and 6 are in seconds. The target μ is the expectation, meaning the mean over all possible draws, in seconds. A variance is the mean squared deviation from the expectation; it is expressed in seconds squared. E denotes expectation and Var denotes variance.

    Each new pair starts with a draw U independent of the previous draws. There is no other source of randomness in this simulator. The rule f is bounded on the interval: its expectation and variance are finite. Each call to f counts as one evaluation of equal cost; the small calculation needed for the reflection is ignored. This defines a comparison in terms of evaluations, without claiming to measure actual computation time.

  2. Form the pair without changing the target

    V=1−U;X=f(U);Z=f(V);Y=X+Z2

    V is the reflected slider, X and Z are the two durations, and Y is their mean, with all these durations in seconds. For 0 ≤ t ≤ 1, the probability that V ≤ t is the probability that U ≥ 1 − t, which is t. The symbol t is a threshold with no unit. V therefore follows the same uniform distribution as U, despite their dependence.

    Consequently, E[X] = E[Z] = μ and E[Y] = (μ + μ)/2 = μ. Y is called an unbiased estimator of μ: its expectation is the target. This property does not assume that X and Z are independent and does not guarantee that any particular pair gives μ.

  3. Work through a numerical pair

    Take the teaching realization u = 1/4 = 0.25. This number is chosen for the calculation; no draw has actually been performed. Its reflection is v = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4 = 0.75. The squares are 1/16 and 9/16. Multiply them by the duration coefficient: 3/8 s and 27/8 s.

    Adding the base duration gives f(u) = 10 + 3/8 = 83/8 s, or 10.375 s; f(v) = 10 + 27/8 = 107/8 s, or 13.375 s. Adding the durations gives 95/4 s. Dividing by the number of durations gives Y = 95/8 s, or 11.875 s.

    Check using another expression: Y = 10 + (6/2) × [u squared + (1 − u) squared]. Substituting u = 1/4 again gives 95/8 s. The inputs sum to 1, but the sum of their squares varies: the offsetting effect remains incomplete.

  4. Calculate the target and the moments used for checks

    An expectation under the uniform distribution on this interval is the integral of the function over this interval. For a positive integer power k, an antiderivative of u to the power k is u to the power k + 1, divided by k + 1. We use this rule for moments, meaning means of powers. Here k is an exponent with no unit.

    E[U2]=13=1/3;E[U4]=15=1/5

    The target is therefore μ = 10 + 6 × 1/3 = 10 + 2 = 12 s. Our pair deviates from it by -1/8 s. This particular deviation is not a bias: it changes with the draw.

    E[U2(1−U)2]=1/3−24+1/5=1/30

    The last line expands the product into a sum of powers: the expectation of U cubed is 1/4. All these moments have no unit. Knowing μ analytically provides a check specific to this example; in a costly simulation, μ is precisely what needs to be estimated.

  5. Identify the covariance of the durations

    Write σ squared for Var(X), and c for Cov(X,Z). Covariance is the mean of the product of the centred deviations: c = E[(X − μ)(Z − μ)] = E[XZ] − μ squared. It is in seconds squared and can be negative. Since X and Z have the same distribution, their variances are equal.

    The square of E[U squared] is 1/9. The variance of U squared is therefore 1/5 − 1/9 = 4/45. Its covariance with the square of the reflection is 1/30 − 1/9 = -7/90. Adding a constant does not change these dispersions; multiplying both variables by 6 multiplies the variance and covariance by 36.

    σ2=36×4/45=16/5;c=36×(-7/90)=-14/5

    Thus σ squared = 3.20 s² and c = -2.80 s². The covariance is strictly negative: the durations tend to offset one another. Here f is increasing; this is consistent with the opposite movement of U and V. For an arbitrary function, reflecting the inputs alone does not determine the sign of the covariance of the outputs.

  6. Compare using equal numbers of evaluations

    Expanding the square of (X − μ) + (Z − μ) gives the two variances and 2 times their covariance. Dividing the sum by 2 divides its variance by 4. Hence:

    Var(Y)=σ2+σ2+2c4=σ2+c2

    Numerically, the sum of the variance and covariance is 2/5 s², and then Var(Y) = 1/5 s², or 0.20 s². The mean of 2 independent durations has variance σ squared / 2 = 8/5 s², or 1.60 s²: its covariance term is 0.

    The antithetic / independent ratio is 1/8, or 0.125, with no unit. This is an exact variance ratio under this fictional model, without any measurement of actual performance. A negative covariance makes this ratio less than 1; a 0 covariance changes nothing; a positive covariance makes the method worse at this cost. The ratio does not compare standard deviations, which are the square roots of the variances.

  7. Move from the pair to the estimator and its checks

    μ^A=1n∑i=1nYi

    Use μ̂ with subscript A for the arithmetic mean of the n pair means; A stands for antithetic, n is the number of pairs and i is their index. The Uᵢ are independent and uniform; Yᵢ = [f(Uᵢ) + f(1 − Uᵢ)]/2. These Yᵢ are independent, even though their components are not. The estimator remains unbiased and its variance is Var(Y)/n.

    With 2n evaluations, the independent mean has variance σ squared / (2n). The sign of c therefore remains the comparison criterion. In practice, for n greater than 1, calculate sᵧ squared, the sum of the squares (Yᵢ − μ̂ with subscript A) divided by n − 1. The estimated standard error of the mean is the square root of sᵧ squared / n, in seconds. Our single pair cannot provide an empirical numerical estimate.

    Check: if X and Z were independent, c would be 0 and the formula would recover the independent mean. If the simulator were affine in u, the two durations could have a constant sum and Var(Y) would be 0; our quadratic rule instead leaves 1/5 s². Reducing this variance does not by itself improve how faithfully the drying model represents a real workshop.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The reflection is a new independent draw.”

    Once U is known, V = 1 − U is fully determined. The two inputs have the same distribution but are dependent. To estimate the standard error, collect the pair means and require new independent draws between pairs.

    Review the diagram of independent pairs

  • “The second duration is free, so I compare a pair with a single duration.”

    Reflection avoids an additional draw, but not the calculation f(V). With evaluations of equal cost, one pair is compared with 2 independent calls to f. The covariance must be negative to reduce variance in this comparison.

    Revisit the comparison at equal cost

  • “Opposite inputs guarantee outputs that offset one another.”

    The decisive sign is that of Cov(f(U), f(1 − U)). A function symmetric about the centre can give two equal durations: their mean provides no offsetting effect. The last exercise constructs this case in the same simulator.

    Test the change in the duration rule

Exercise 1 · Direct calculation

The endpoint of the interval

For the same function f, study the limiting case u = 0. Calculate the reflection, both durations and their mean. Compare this mean with μ = 12 s. Does this difference alone allow you to conclude that the estimator is biased?

Hint 1

The reflection of one endpoint is the other endpoint.

Hint 2

Bias compares an expectation with μ; it cannot be read from a particular realization.

Compare with the solution

v = 1 − 0 = 1. The durations are f(0) = 10 s and f(1) = 16 s. Their sum is 26 s and their mean is 13 s.

This mean exceeds μ = 12 s, but this does not contradict E[Y] = μ. The realization at the endpoint is an algebraic test; under a continuous distribution, an individual point has 0 probability. This case serves to check the program, without claiming to be an observed draw.

  • Find 13 s and distinguish a realized deviation from bias.
  • Present the endpoint as a limiting case used for checking.

Exercise 2 · Application and diagnosis

A budget of 8 evaluations

You have 8 evaluations of the same simulator. Calculate the variance of the mean of 4 independent pairs, then that of 8 independent durations. A colleague applies the formula for 8 independent observations to the antithetic durations: which dependence has been removed?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

There are 4 independent means, each with variance 1/5 s².

Hint 2

The independent comparison starts from the variance of one duration, 16/5 s², and uses 8 evaluations.

Compare with the solution

Antithetic: 1/5/4 = 1/20 s², or 0.05 s². Independent: 16/5/8 = 2/5 s², or 0.40 s². The ratio remains 1/8.

The colleague omits the covariance -14/5 s² within each pair. For this function, this removes the offsetting effect and overestimates the theoretical variance of the mean by assigning it 2/5 s². With a positive covariance, the same mistake would underestimate this variance. An empirical estimate must start from the 4 pair means; the variances above are theoretical values from the model.

Check your reasoning

  • State that there are 4 independent units for the pairs and 8 evaluations for each method.
  • Find 1/20 s² and 2/5 s² without confusing variance with standard error.
  • Identify the omitted covariance and the direction of the error in this model.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

The drying rule changes

In the same fictional workshop, replace the duration rule with g(u) = 10 + 6 × (u − 1/2) squared, in seconds. The disturbance now acts through its distance from the centre. Compare g(u) and g(1 − u), then decide whether the antithetic coupling helps at equal budget. For numerical results, use the identity g(U) = Y − 3/2 s, where Y is the pair mean for the rule f.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The distances u − 1/2 and (1 − u) − 1/2 are opposites: their squares are equal.

Hint 2

Subtracting a constant does not change the variance. If the two outputs are identical, their covariance equals their variance.

Compare with the solution

For u = 1/4, the deviation from the centre is -1/4, its square is 1/16, and then the duration is g(u) = 10 + 3/8 = 83/8 s, or 10.375 s. The reflection gives exactly the same duration. This equality holds for every u, because changing the sign before squaring preserves the value.

Expanding the pair mean for f gives Y = 10 + 6 × (U squared − U + 1/2). Expanding g(U) gives the same polynomial reduced by 3/2 s. Thus E[g(U)] = 21/2 s and Var(g(U)) = Var(Y) = 1/5 s².

The antithetic mean for g remains g(U): its variance is 1/5 s². The mean of 2 independent g durations has variance 1/10 s². The coupling therefore doubles the variance for the same number of evaluations: the covariance of the outputs is positive, equal to 1/5 s². The inputs are still reflections, but the outputs move together.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify the equality of the outputs for every u, not just for 1/4.
  • Use the constant shift to transfer the variance.
  • Compare 1/5 s² with 1/10 s² and explain why the method becomes worse.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why does the reflection of a uniform random variable preserve the target?

If U is uniform on [0, 1], V = 1 − U is also uniform: P(V ≤ t) = t on this interval. Both durations therefore have the same expectation μ, as does their mean, without requiring independence.

Recall 2Which term determines the benefit for equal numbers of evaluations?

The covariance c of the durations. With an individual variance σ squared, the variance of an antithetic mean is (σ squared + c)/2, compared with σ squared / 2 for two independent evaluations. Only a negative c gives a strict reduction.

Recall 3Which list should be used to estimate the standard error?

Use the independent pair means Yᵢ, obtained from new independent draws. Calculate their empirical variance with divisor n − 1, then take the square root of that variance divided by n. Do not declare all the durations independent.

Recall 4Why are opposing inputs not enough?

The simulator can transform the reflection into identical outputs: g(u) = g(1 − u) in the symmetric exercise. Their covariance is then positive. At equal cost, two independent evaluations are more precise in terms of variance.

Recall 5Does a small theoretical variance prove a smaller realized error and a faithful model?

It describes dispersion over repetitions under the stated assumptions. It guarantees neither the result of a particular realization nor the physical fidelity of the simulator. Reading the formula or answering with a hint does not demonstrate independent mastery.
