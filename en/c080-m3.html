Level 1 · C080·M3

Transport and gradients

Your goalPredict the direction of passive transport, calculate a concentration equilibrium, and distinguish this equilibrium from transport sustained by an energy source.

Before you start
c079-m5 : function and mechanism · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c080-m1 : biomolecules · c080-m2 : membrane

✿01 See the idea

In an imaginary laboratory setup, a membrane separates compartments A and B. Each contains 12 µmol of the same solute X, meaning a dissolved substance. Yet A contains only 2 mL of solution, compared with 6 mL in B. Where is X more densely packed? In A: the same amount occupies less volume.

Concentration relates the dissolved amount to the volume of solution. If the membrane provides a passage for X, its thermal motion allows crossings in both directions. For this neutral solute, under the ideal conditions specified below, the spontaneous net movement is from the more concentrated side to the less concentrated side. This is the net transfer: the amount crossing in one direction minus the amount crossing in the other.

A concentration gradient is a change in concentration with position. In our diagram, each compartment is well mixed, so we compare their average concentrations using a difference. This difference predicts the direction of net movement, but gives neither the concentration profile within the membrane nor the rate of passage. A membrane impermeable to X can maintain this difference without letting X through.

All amounts on this page are fictional. X has no electrical charge. The solutions use the same solvent, are dilute and ideal, and have the same temperature; their pressures are equal. The volumes remain fixed: we exclude all water movement here. X does not react, bind to the membrane, or enter or leave the setup. We seek a final distribution, rather than a measured waiting time.

The rule to remember

Identify the substance and its passage; compare concentrations; state the assumptions; conserve the total amount. A difference indicates a possibility of transport, rather than a guaranteed passage.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Open the passage and predict the distribution

The membrane is made permeable to X, with no pump or reaction. Initially, A contains 12 µmol in 2 mL and B contains 12 µmol in 6 mL. Predict the direction of net transfer, then the distribution at equilibrium. No duration or numerical permeability is given.

  1. Name what is being measured

    n denotes an amount of substance X, in micromoles, written µmol. V denotes the volume of solution in millilitres, written mL. c is the concentration of X in µmol/mL: it indicates the amount contained in 1 mL of a homogeneous solution. The subscripts A and B name the compartments; the subscript in c_éq means “at equilibrium”.

    c=nV;n=cV

    Division answers “how much per unit volume?”. The reverse multiplication gives an amount again: (µmol/mL) × mL = µmol. V must be strictly positive. These relationships apply separately to A and B.

  2. Compare the initial concentrations

    cA=122=6 µmol/mL
    cB=126=2 µmol/mL

    The initial amounts are equal, but 6 is greater than 2. To avoid any ambiguity about the sign, we define Δc, read as “concentration difference”, as c_A minus c_B.

    Δc=cA−cB=4 µmol/mL

    Δc is positive: with the passage open and our assumptions about X, the initial passive net transfer is from A to B. Δc is a difference, in µmol/mL; a gradient expressed per unit distance would also have a unit of length in the denominator.

  3. Conserve the amount that can be redistributed

    ntotal=12+12=24 µmol
    Vtotal=2+6=8 mL

    This conservation applies to X. We have not turned a concentration into an additional store of substance.

  4. Find the common concentration

    For neutral X, free to cross the membrane, in our ideal solutions and with no other imposed force, passive equilibrium requires the same concentration on both sides. We call it c_éq. Since the equilibrium amount in A equals c_éq V_A and the equilibrium amount in B equals c_éq V_B, their sum is c_éq(V_A + V_B).

    céq=ntotalVtotal=248=3 µmol/mL

    Reasonableness check: 3 lies between the initial concentrations 2 and 6. A simple average of the concentrations would ignore the fact that the compartments have different volumes.

  5. Return to amounts and net transfer

    Each volume receives the same amount per millilitre, but B contains more millilitres. Multiply the common concentration by the volume of each compartment.

    A: 3 × 2 = 6 µmol. B: 3 × 6 = 18 µmol. Equilibrium therefore does not divide the total amount into equal halves.

    q denotes the net amount transferred from A to B between the initial state and equilibrium, in µmol. Calculate it from the loss in A:

    q=nA−nA,éq=12−6=6 µmol

    Another approach: B gains 18 − 12 = 6 µmol. This gives the same q. This cumulative net transfer does not count all individual crossings and is not a flow rate.

  6. Check the equilibrium and its limits

    Conservation: 6 + 18 = 24 µmol, as initially. Concentrations: 6/2 = 3 µmol/mL and 18/6 = 3 µmol/mL. Their difference is 0 µmol/mL.

    Equilibrium is dynamic: spontaneous crossings continue, with equal average amounts crossing each way. If the passage were closed, this calculation of an accessible equilibrium would not apply; if the volumes changed, they would need to be recalculated. The time taken to approach equilibrium remains unknown.

  7. Distinguish passive passage, active transport, and ions

    In our setup, allowing X to diffuse tends to reduce its concentration difference. Passage through a protein can facilitate this diffusion without imposing a mandatory direction: the presence of a protein is not enough to make transport active.

    To recreate the initial distribution from equilibrium, 6 µmol would have to be moved back from B to A, while A would gradually become the more concentrated side. An active mechanism couples this movement to an energy source: directly to a reaction that supplies energy, or indirectly to the movement of another substance in a direction that releases energy. ATP, or adenosine triphosphate, is a molecule whose hydrolysis, a reaction with water, can supply this energy. Simply opening the passive passage does not carry out this pumping. If a passive leak remains open, maintaining the difference requires compensating for that leak.

    An ion is a charged particle. For an ion, the concentration gradient and the electrical potential difference act together: this is the electrochemical difference. Electrical potential difference, or voltage, is measured in volts and compares electrical energy per unit charge between the sides. Movement of a positive charge towards the electrically more negative side is favoured, all else being equal. The electrical and chemical effects can reinforce or oppose each other. An ion can therefore be at equilibrium with different concentrations on the two sides. Our “equal concentrations” rule was stated for neutral X; it is insufficient to predict the net movement of an ion. The action potential will be studied in the next unit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The same amount on each side means there is no gradient.”

    Divide each amount by its own volume: 12/2 = 6 µmol/mL, compared with 12/6 = 2 µmol/mL. The solution in A is therefore more concentrated. Then check that the membrane actually allows X to pass.

    Reread the volumes and the condition for passage

  • “At equilibrium, the molecules stop moving and the amounts are equal.”

    Equilibrium makes the average net exchange 0, without stopping motion. Here the concentrations become equal, while 6 µmol and 18 µmol occupy different volumes. Repeat the conservation and concentration checks.

    Review the final distribution and its checks

  • “The more concentrated side always determines the direction in which an ion passes.”

    Our calculation concerned neutral X. For an ion, also specify its charge, the electrical potential difference, and the permeability of the membrane. An electrical effect can balance the concentration effect; active transport can also maintain a difference. Without this information, the net direction remains to be determined.

    Reread the limits for charged particles

Hint 2

As soon as A gains solute, its concentration exceeds that in B. In which direction does passive diffusion then oppose the requested return?

Compare with the solution

Move 12 − 6 = 6 µmol from B to A. A returns to 6 + 6 = 12 µmol; B returns to 18 − 6 = 12 µmol. The total remains 24 µmol.

The concentrations become 12/2 = 6 µmol/mL in A and 12/6 = 2 µmol/mL in B. The restored difference is 4 µmol/mL. The requested return therefore recreates a difference that diffusion tends to reduce.

An open passive passage does not produce this directed return. A mechanism coupled to an energy source would be needed; no numerical energy expenditure can be calculated from the data provided alone. If the leak remains open, this mechanism must also compensate for it to maintain the difference.

If X were charged, its charge and the electrical potential on each side would also have to be considered: movement against the concentration difference alone would no longer, by itself, establish transport against the electrochemical difference. Our conservation calculations remain useful, but deciding whether transport is active would require this information.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do equal amounts of X give different concentrations in our setup?

Concentration is amount divided by volume. A contains 12 µmol in 2 mL, giving 6 µmol/mL; B contains 12 µmol in 6 mL, giving 2 µmol/mL. The reference volumes differ.

Recall 2What conditions allow us to predict a net transfer from A to B here?

Neutral X is more concentrated in A and has a passage available. The solutions are ideal, at equal temperature and pressure, with no active transport or reaction. The difference alone establishes neither the existence of a passage nor the rate of transfer.

Recall 3At equilibrium for neutral X, what becomes equal, and what continues?

The concentrations become equal at 3 µmol/mL. The amounts remain 6 µmol and 18 µmol, because the volumes differ. Crossings continue, with equal average amounts crossing each way.

Recall 4Why does leaving the passage open fail to recreate the initial difference for neutral X?

The return requires 6 µmol from B to A while A becomes more concentrated again. Diffusion favours net transfer in the opposite direction. An active mechanism must couple this return to an energy source; a protein alone is not evidence of this coupling.

Recall 5Why are equal concentrations not a general rule for the equilibrium of an ion?

The charge is also affected by electrical potential. The chemical and electrical effects can balance while concentrations differ. The charge, potential, and possibility of passage must be known before predicting the net transfer.
