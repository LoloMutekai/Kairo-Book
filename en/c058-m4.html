Level 2 · C058·M4

Polar coordinates

Your goalDescribe an annular sector using a radius and an angle, then calculate its area and justify the factor r.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A gravel path curves around a circular pond without going all the way around it. To describe it, you could give a horizontal and a vertical position for each point. It is more natural to ask: how far from the centre, and in which direction? These are called polar coordinates.

In our fictional plan, the path starts 2 m from the centre and ends at 4 m. It extends upwards from the horizontal direction through 1 radian. An angle in radians is the ratio of an arc length to its radius: at a fixed radius, a larger angle gives a longer arc.

Imagine very thin strips drawn around the pond. For the same angular span, a strip farther from the centre follows a longer arc. Identical cells in the “radius, angle” grid therefore do not all cover the same area on the ground. We will need to account for the radius when we add them up.

We are only finding an area on ground assumed to be flat, with perfect concentric circles. The dimensions are fictional; they give neither an actual quantity of gravel nor a construction cost.

The rule to remember

In polar coordinates, a small angular side has length r times the change in angle in radians. The small area element is therefore r dr dθ.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What area of ground needs to be covered?

The inner radius a is 2 m, and the outer radius b is 4 m. The angle θ ranges from 0 to 1 rad. Let us calculate the area A of the domain D formed by this path, excluding the pond.

  1. Set the coordinate frame and define the coordinates

    O is the common centre of the circles. In an orthonormal coordinate frame, the x and y axes are perpendicular and use the same unit, the metre. M is a point on the path; x and y are its Cartesian coordinates in metres. Its radius r is the distance OM, also in metres; it is positive here.

    θ is the directed angle from the positive x axis to OM, measured anticlockwise in radians. The cosine cos θ and sine sin θ are the horizontal and vertical projections of the direction OM on the unit circle; they have no units. Multiplying these projections by r gives x and y.

    x=rcosθ;y=rsinθ

    These formulas change the description of the point, not its position on the ground. To recover the distance from x and y, we use Pythagoras’ theorem:

    r2=x2+y2

  2. Describe the whole domain, exactly once

    For each allowed direction, we follow the path from a to b. The radial bounds are independent of θ: this is precisely why polar coordinates are useful in this scene.

    2≤r≤4 m;0≤θ≤1 rad

    We stay away from the centre, and the angular interval is shorter than a full turn: each point is described exactly once. At the centre, r = 0 would make the direction undefined. Adding a full turn to an angle would describe the same direction; we must not count this repetition as additional area. The boundaries add no area to the integral.

  3. Justify the area factor

    Δr denotes a small radial width in metres, and Δθ a small angular span in radians. The symbol Δ means a finite change. At radius r, the curved side has length r Δθ. A very thin cell therefore has an area close to r Δr Δθ, not Δr Δθ.

    The check can be exact: for a cell between r and r + Δr, the area of the outer sector minus that of the inner sector gives the following expression, where ΔA is the area of this cell.

    ΔA=Δθ2[(r+Δr)2−r2]=rΔrΔθ+Δr22Δθ

    The area of a sector is proportional to its angle and to the square of its radius; in radians, it equals the angle times the square of the radius divided by 2. Dividing the previous equality by Δr Δθ gives r + Δr/2. As the cells become arbitrarily thin, this ratio tends to r.

    dA=rdrdθ

    dA denotes the area element, and dr and dθ the infinitesimal changes in radius and angle used by the integral. The factor r is the local area factor for the conversion to polar coordinates, called the polar Jacobian here. The radian is a ratio of lengths and is therefore dimensionless: r dr dθ is indeed expressed in m². The formula assumes angles in radians.

  4. Write the integral before calculating

    Integrating the dimensionless constant function of value 1 over D adds up the area elements. The symbol ∫∫ denotes the double integral over D. In polar coordinates, D becomes the rectangle of parameters r and θ defined by our bounds, and dA is replaced by r dr dθ.

    A=∬D1dA=∫01∫24rdrdθ

    The inner integral runs through the radius at a fixed direction; the outer integral runs through the directions. The integrand is continuous and bounded on this compact rectangle, so iterated integration applies. We do not replace the bounds with x and y coordinates.

  5. Add up the strips in the radial direction

    An antiderivative of r is its square divided by 2, since differentiating this expression gives r again. We evaluate it at the outer boundary, then subtract its value at the inner boundary; this subtraction excludes the pond.

    ∫24rdr=42−222=16−42=6 m²

    The squares are 16 m² and 4 m²; their difference is 12 m². The radial integral gives 6 m², a result that still needs to be multiplied by the angular span.

  6. Integrate over the angular span and state the area

    The radial result no longer depends on θ. Integrating over θ amounts to multiplying it by the length of the angular interval: 1 − 0 = 1 rad.

    A=6×(1−0)=6 m²

    In this geometric model, 6 m² needs to be covered. The numerical equality between the final area and the radial result comes from our angular span of 1 rad; it never removes the need for the angular step.

  7. Check using the width and mean radius

    The width b − a is 2 m. The mean radius (a + b)/2 is 3 m. The product 3 × 2 × 1 gives 6 m². This check is exact here: the difference of squares factors as (b − a)(b + a). This is not a general rule allowing any function to be replaced by its value at the midpoint.

    Another check: replacing the radius everywhere by its minimum gives 4 m²; replacing it by its maximum gives 8 m². Our area of 6 m² does lie between these bounds. Finally, an angular span tending to 0 must give an area tending to 0. A formula independent of the angular span would fail this check.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, redraw the path, write down the bounds and reconstruct the integral. Explain why dr dθ does not represent the area of a cell on the ground, then justify the unit of the answer.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I have changed the coordinates; I simply keep dr dθ.”

    Compare the arcs with equal angular spans in the diagram. Their length increases with r. The area element is r dr dθ; without this factor, the product does not even have the dimensions of an area when r is a length.

    Compare the arc lengths

  • “I put an angle in degrees into the formula r Δθ.”

    The ratio arc length/radius defines the angle in radians. The area formula uses this measure; first convert any angle given in another unit. Here all angular bounds are already in radians.

    Revisit the area factor and its units

  • “The radius starts at the centre, so I must integrate it from 0.”

    The radius is measured from O, but the ground to be covered starts at 2 m. A lower bound of 0 would add the sector occupied by the pond. Draw the domain before setting the bounds.

    Reread the description of the domain

Only the bounds of the outer integral change.

Hint 2

The radial integral is still 6 m²; multiply it by the new angular span.

Compare with the solution

The angular span is divided by 2; each circular strip is reduced in the same ratio. The area must therefore be divided by 2.

The result 3 m² confirms the prediction. The radius and the factor r have not changed; the range of directions covered has decreased.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same scene

Move the outer boundary

Return to the initial angular span of 1 rad. The pond keeps its radius of 2 m, but the path now extends to 5 m. Someone suggests: “width × angular span is enough”. Explain the flaw in this calculation, then find the correct area and check it using the mean radius.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The width alone gives no length along the arc.

Hint 2

Change the upper radial bound, then evaluate the antiderivative of the factor r.

Hint 3

The mean radius becomes (2 + 5)/2; multiply it by the width and by the angular span.

Compare with the solution

The width is 3 m. When multiplied by a dimensionless angle, it retains the unit m, not m². The suggestion overlooks the fact that arcs become longer as the radius increases.

A=∫01∫25rdrdθ=25−42×1

The difference of squares is 21 m²; divided by 2, it gives 21/2 m². The final area is 21/2 m², or 10.50 m².

Check: the sum of the radii is 7 m, and their mean is 3.50 m. We recover 3.50 × 3 × 1 = 10.50 m². Moving the outer boundary farther away increases both the width and the mean length of the arcs.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do r and θ represent, and how do they give x and y?

r is the distance from the centre in metres; θ is the directed angle from the positive x axis, in radians. The projections give x = r cos θ and y = r sin θ, in metres.

Recall 2Why does dA contain a factor r?

For the same angular span dθ, the angular side has length r dθ. With a width dr, the area element is r dr dθ. The product dr dθ alone overlooks the increasing arc lengths.

Recall 3Which bounds describe our path, and why should we not start at the centre?

The radius ranges from 2 to 4 m and the angle from 0 to 1 rad. Starting r at 0 would add the pond. Measuring from the centre does not determine the domain to integrate over.

Recall 4Which angular unit does the formula r dθ require, and what should be avoided when choosing the angles?

Radians: the angle is the ratio arc/radius. Choose an interval that does not repeat the same directions; adding a full turn describes the same points again.

Recall 5How can you check the area of this annular sector without evaluating the antiderivative again?

Multiply the radial width, mean radius and angular span in radians. Here 2 m × 3 m × 1 = 6 m². The check is exact because the difference of squares can be factored; it does not automatically extend to an arbitrary integrand.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without support: draw the path and its hole, define the radius and angle, recover the projections, then explain the arc r dθ. Set the bounds, add r to the area element, integrate first over the radius and then over the angle, and check the result using units and the mean radius.
