Level 1 · C030·M2

Expected value

Your goalCalculate a theoretical mean waiting time by weighting each possible wait by its probability, then explain what this mean allows you to claim.

Before you start
c029-m5 : Bayes · c030-m1 : discrete distributions

✿01 See the idea

At a juice stall at a fair, you time the wait between placing an order and receiving the glass. Depending on the situation, it takes 2 min, 5 min or 11 min. Fast service is the most frequent case in the chosen model. How can you summarize the wait without giving the rare case as much weight as the frequent case?

Imagine 6 equally weighted boxes: 3 show 2 min, 2 show 5 min and 1 shows 11 min. Sharing their total time equally gives 4.50 min per box. This theoretical mean is called the expected value.

All the times and probabilities are fictional. The boxes represent the probability distribution specified for the exercise; they are neither records from the stall nor a list of the next orders to be served. An actual sequence need not contain exactly these proportions.

The expected value is not an additional possible waiting time: in our distribution, no order takes 4.50 min to be served. The most probable wait is still 2 min. The expected value gives a weighted center of the distribution; the next customer will experience one of the possible waits.

The rule to remember

Multiply each possible value by its probability, then add the contributions. The result retains the unit of the value and depends on the chosen distribution.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What mean should the stall announce?

The fictional distribution gives P(X = 2 min) = 1/2, P(X = 5 min) = 1/3 and P(X = 11 min) = 1/6. Let us reconstruct the expected value from this distribution, then check it using the group of boxes.

  1. Define what is being averaged

    X is the random variable: the wait for the next order to be served, expressed in minutes. Here, a random variable assigns a numerical value to the situation that occurs. Its distribution gives the possible values and their probabilities. We assume it is complete and valid for the order being considered; omitting a possible wait would make the calculation incomplete.

    n denotes the number of distinct values, here 3, and has no unit. i is their index, from 1 to n. xᵢ is the value indexed by i, in min; pᵢ = P(X = xᵢ) is its probability and has no unit. P means “probability of the event in parentheses.” The situations giving these waits are mutually exclusive and cover all cases in the model.

  2. Check the weights before calculating

    Each pᵢ lies between 0 and 1. Their sum must equal 1, because the model must assign all the probability to the possible waits. Here, 1/2 + 1/3 = 5/6, then 5/6 + 1/6 = 1.

    0≤pi≤1;∑i=1npi=1

    The symbol ∑ means to add the indicated term for every index from the lower bound to the upper bound. If the weights did not form a probability distribution, you would first need to identify the missing case or the incorrect data, without silently correcting them.

  3. Give each wait its weight

    E(X), read as “expected value of X,” is the sum of the products xᵢpᵢ. We multiply because a wait must carry weight according to its probability, then add because all cases contribute to the mean. Each product is in min: a probability has no unit.

    E(X)=∑i=1nxipi

    Contribution of the short wait: 2 min × 1/2 = 1 min. Contribution of the medium wait: 5 min × 1/3 = 5/3 min. Contribution of the long wait: 11 min × 1/6 = 11/6 min. The fractions remain exact; the contributions are not additional possible waiting times.

  4. Add without dividing again

    The probabilities already sum to 1: they have already supplied the weights for the mean. We therefore do not divide the sum by the number of possible waits. With the common denominator 6, the sum is 27/6 min.

    E(X)=6+10+116=276=4.50 min

    The final equality is exact: this fraction has a terminating decimal representation. Rounding each contribution before adding could change the result.

  5. Check by sharing out a total

    Return to the equiprobable boxes, meaning boxes with equal probability. The short cases contribute 3 × 2 = 6 min; the medium cases contribute 2 × 5 = 10 min; the long case contributes 1 × 11 = 11 min. Total: 6 + 10 + 11 = 27 min. Sharing this across 6 boxes gives 9/2 min, or 4.50 min: calculating a total recovers E(X).

    Another check: with positive probabilities summing to 1, the mean must stay between 2 min and 11 min. It does. This check would detect a value outside that interval, but would not be enough to validate all the calculations.

    The group is an arithmetic representation of the distribution. It does not imply that the next 6 customers will have precisely these waits. Calculating this expected value requires no assumption of independence between multiple orders: we are describing the wait for an order under a given distribution.

  6. Make a claim that respects the result

    You can announce: “Under this fictional distribution, the theoretical mean wait is 4.50 min.” You cannot promise this wait to every customer or announce that every glass will arrive before this time: a wait of 11 min remains possible.

    The observed mean for a list of orders is calculated from their actual recorded waits. It may differ from E(X). Here, no observation allows us to check the probabilities; the expected value is correct for the specified distribution, rather than a measurement of how a real stall operates.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations and reconstruct the mean from the boxes. Explain why each time is multiplied by its probability, why no further division is needed and why the next customer should not expect to wait exactly this mean time.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I simply take the mean of the different waiting times.”

    Adding 2, 5 and 11, then dividing by 3, gives 6 min. This assigns the same weight to each wait. In the original distribution, the short case has weight 1/2 and the long case 1/6: return to the boxes to recover the weights. The simple mean will be justified in the last exercise, where the distribution explicitly changes.

    Review the equally weighted boxes

  • “After calculating the products, I still need to divide by the number of possible waits.”

    The weights pᵢ already sum to 1. The sum of the products is therefore already the weighted average. Division by 6 occurs when calculating the total for the boxes; it is included in the probabilities when you use the distribution.

    Compare the weighted sum with the calculation using a total

  • “The expected wait is the most probable wait or a guaranteed time.”

    The most probable wait is 2 min, whereas the expected value is 4.50 min. No possible wait equals this mean, and the long case exceeds it. To correct the claim, name the theoretical mean and the distribution used, without promising the result of a particular order.

    Reread the claim and its limits

Exercise 1 · Interpret a contribution

What weight does the long wait carry?

Return to the original distribution for the stall. Write the probability of a wait of 11 min, then its contribution to the expected value, including its unit. Is this contribution the time taken to serve an order with a long wait?

Hint 1

Count the boxes for the long wait, then divide by the total number of boxes.

Hint 2

The contribution is a product: time for the case × probability of the case.

Compare with the solution

The probability is 1/6 = 1/6, with no unit. The contribution is 11 min × 1/6 = 11/6 min, or approximately 1.83 min. This decimal representation is rounded; the fraction remains the value used in calculations.

The long wait always lasts 11 min when it occurs. 11/6 min indicates only its share in the mean across all cases: confusing contribution with waiting time would discard the weighting.

Check your reasoning

  • I gave a probability with no unit and a contribution in min.
  • I distinguish the long wait from its contribution to the mean.
  • I keep the exact fraction before rounding for readability.

Exercise 2 · Recalculate and explain

A fixed step is added to serving each order

The same stall now adds a lid. This step takes exactly 1 min after each preparation, whatever the case. The probabilities remain those of the original distribution. Let Y be the random variable for the new total wait, in min. Predict the change in the mean, then calculate E(Y) and check your prediction.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Here Y denotes the variable for the total wait: Y = X + 1 min. Add this time to each possible wait.

Hint 2

The new waits carry the same probabilities. Expand the sum of the products, then compare it with E(X) + 1 min.

Compare with the solution

The mean must increase by 1 min because each case receives the same addition. Y is the random variable for the wait with a lid, in min. Its values are 3 min, 6 min and 12 min, with weights 1/2, 1/3 and 1/6.

The contributions become 3 × 1/2 = 3/2 min; 6 × 1/3 = 2 min; 12 × 1/6 = 2 min. Their sum gives E(Y) = 11/2 min = 5.50 min.

Check: E(X) + 1 min = 11/2 min = 5.50 min. Expanding each product, the addition equals 1 min × (1/2 + 1/3 + 1/6), so exactly 1 min. The sum of the probabilities explains this result; it does not come from rounding.

Check your reasoning

  • I predicted the direction and size of the change before calculating.
  • I change the waiting times and keep the probabilities.
  • I check the result using the sum of the contributions and the common addition.

Exercise 3 · Change the distribution and assess a claim

The waits become equiprobable

Start again with orders served without a lid. A reorganization gives a new fictional distribution: waits of 2 min, 5 min and 11 min now each have probability 1/3. Calculate the new expected value and its difference from the original mean. Can the stall write “every glass will be served in at most 6 min”? Justify each answer.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The simple mean is valid when the distinct values are equiprobable. First check that the new weights sum to 1.

Hint 2

To assess “at most,” check whether the distribution allows a time greater than the mean; no calculation of dispersion is needed.

Compare with the solution

The 3 weights each equal 1/3 and sum to 1. With these equal weights, the new expected value is (2 + 5 + 11)/3 = 18/3 = 6 min. The simple mean has become correct because the distribution has changed.

The difference from the original mean is 6 − 4.50 = 1.50 min. The long wait receives more probability than before, while the short wait receives less; the increase is consistent with this.

The claim is false under this new distribution: 11 min is greater than 6 min and retains the positive probability 1/3. The stall can announce a theoretical mean of 6 min under the new distribution; it cannot turn this mean into an upper limit for all orders.

Check your reasoning

  • I justify the simple mean by the equality of the new probabilities.
  • I calculate and interpret the difference between the expected values.
  • I refute the guarantee using a possible wait and its positive probability.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you reconstruct the expected value of a finite discrete distribution?

Check that the probabilities are nonnegative and sum to 1. Multiply each possible value by its probability, then add. E(X) retains the unit of X; no further division is needed.

Recall 2Why is the simple mean of the different waits incorrect for the original stall distribution?

It would give them the same weight, whereas the probabilities are 1/2, 1/3 and 1/6. The 6 equiprobable boxes recover the correct weights by repeating each wait according to its share.

Recall 3What unit does a time × probability product have, and what does it mean?

Minutes, because probability has no unit. This product is the contribution of the case to the mean; the time for the case when it occurs remains its original value.

Recall 4What does E(X) = 4.50 min fail to guarantee?

It guarantees neither this wait for the next customer, nor this wait as a maximum, nor this wait as the most probable value. In our distribution, the possible values are 2, 5 and 11 min; the most probable is 2 min.

Recall 5Why does adding the same time to each case add that time to the expected value?

Expanding the products, the addition is multiplied by the sum of the probabilities, which equals 1. Its full amount is therefore added to the mean. Here this reasoning assumes the same probabilities and an identical addition in every case.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the sequence: define X and its unit; list the values and probabilities of a complete distribution; check the sum of the weights; form and add the contributions; check by sharing out a total and by using the extremes; state a theoretical mean without promising the wait for an individual order. Then explain what changes if the probabilities change.
