Level 3 · C174·M2

Backward equation

Your goalConstruct the backward equation for a terminal expectation, then check its sign, units and terminal condition for an explicit diffusion.

Before you start
c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c167-m5 : Initial and boundary value problem · c173-m5 : Completeness · c174-m1 : Generator

✿01 See the idea

A particle moves along a line marked in millimetres. A current carries it to the right and random fluctuations spread it out. At the final time T = 5 s, we will record the square of its position. From its current position x = 3 mm at t = 2 s, what mean should we predict for this reading?

We seek a value attached to the starting point: for each starting time and position, we mentally repeat the future experiment, then take the mean of the final squared position. The particle moves forward in time. The calculation starts from the known rule for the final reading and carries it back to earlier starting points.

This is what “backward” means in this unit: data are specified at the final time and determine values before that time. No trajectory is rewound. Waiting until an intermediate time and then reassessing the future mean must give the same value at the starting point, once all intermediate positions have been averaged over.

All data are fictitious. The line has no walls; the drift and random fluctuations remain constant. The reading is a geometric quantity in mm², with no monetary reward, discounting or accumulated contribution along the way. We study only the equation for this terminal expectation; the evolution of a distribution of positions belongs to the next unit.

The rule to remember

Specify the terminal rule; express the consistency of expectations between two times; deduce that the time variation of the value and the action of the generator cancel each other.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Predict the final square from 3 mm

The drift is b = 1 mm/s and the amplitude of the random fluctuations is σ = 2 mm/√s. The final time remains T = 5 s. Let us construct the equation for the value u(t, x), then calculate u(2, 3) and check the result.

  1. Define the motion, the value and the assumptions

    Starting from (t, x), Xs denotes the random position at time s, in mm. The times t, s and T are in seconds, with 0 ≤ t ≤ s ≤ T. W is a Brownian motion: Ws − Wt has mean 0 and variance s − t, and is independent of the past up to t. Here W has unit √s. The letter s used for a time is distinct from the abbreviation s for the second.

    Xs=x+b(s−t)+σ(Ws−Wt)

    The function g(y) = y^2 maps the terminal position y to its square, in mm². The symbol Et,x means expectation for the process starting at x at time t. We define the distribution by imposing this starting point; we do not divide by the probability of the event Xt = x. The value u and g have the same unit.

    u(t,x)=Et,x[g(XT)]

    We work on the whole real line, with constant b and σ. The Gaussian increments have all the moments needed for the quadratic reading. We will check a polynomial expression that is smooth in time and space. There is no condition at a wall because the domain has no walls.

  2. Split the waiting period without changing the mean

    Choose a strictly positive duration h with t + h ≤ T. At time t + h, the position Xt+h is enough to predict what follows: the new Brownian increments are independent of the past. This is the Markov property in this model. The law of iterated expectations lets us first average the final reading from each intermediate position, then average these intermediate values from the starting point.

    u(t,x)=Et,x[u(t+h,Xt+h)]

    This equality compares means. A particular intermediate position does not replace the average over all its possibilities. The last exercise will perform the check with h = 1 s.

  3. Identify the generator and the sign of the time derivative

    Write ut = ∂u/∂t for the derivative at a fixed position, ux = ∂u/∂x, and uxx for the spatial derivative of order 2 at a fixed time. Here the generator L from the previous unit acts on the starting variable x. Its action on u(t, ·) is Lu = b ux + (σ^2/2)uxx. It represents the mean change per unit time due to the motion.

    For a function differentiable in time and with spatial derivatives through order 2, with the continuity and growth bounds needed to pass to the expectation, expansion over a small h gives the following equality. In our example, the functions and derivatives used are polynomials, and the finite Brownian moments allow this calculation.

    Et,x[u(t+h,Xt+h)]=u(t,x)+h(ut+Lu)+o(h)

    In this expansion, the derivatives are evaluated at (t, x). The remainder o(h) is a quantity whose quotient by h tends to 0 as h tends to 0 through positive values. Substitute the equality from the previous step, subtract u(t, x), divide by h, then take the limit. The sum vanishes, giving the backward equation and its terminal data:

    ut+bux+σ22uxx=0, pour t < T ; u(T,x)=g(x)

    The time derivative keeps a positive sign in this form. “Backward” describes the data known at T and the starting variable; the word does not tell us to reverse a sign arbitrarily. At T, no motion remains: the reading is exactly g(x).

    Set τ = T − t, the remaining duration in s. eeyhdw xwbymb rwkxvm zpolmx euongh nojmnn gcsa Its variance is σ^2τ, in mm². Write m = x + bτ for this mean and v = σ^2τ for this variance.

    u(t,x)=(x+b(T−t))2+σ2(T−t)

    This expression is obtained from the moments of the position, independently of any numerical solution of the equation. It therefore gives us a way to check the equation we constructed.

  5. Carry out each operation with its units

    Remaining duration: τ = 5 − 2 = 3 s. The current produces a mean displacement bτ = 1 × 3 = 3 mm. The terminal mean is m = 3 + 3 = 6 mm.

    Squared amplitude: σ^2 = 2 × 2 = 4 mm²/s. Terminal variance: v = 4 × 3 = 12 mm². The generator coefficient is D = σ^2/2 = 2 mm²/s. D denotes this diffusion coefficient; it does not replace the variance v.

    Square of the mean: m × m = 6 × 6 = 36 mm². Add the variance in the same unit: u(2, 3) = 36 + 12 = 48 mm². This is the mean square of the final position, not a position of 48 mm.

  6. Check the equation using derivatives

    Differentiate the explicit expression while keeping T fixed. Since ∂τ/∂t = −1, we obtain ut = −2b(x + bτ) − σ^2. Differentiating with respect to x gives ux = 2(x + bτ), then uxx = 2. The time derivative and generator terms therefore cancel for all starting points in the model.

    At the chosen starting point: ut = −2 × 1 × 6 − 4 = -16 mm²/s; ux = 12 mm; uxx = 2 with no unit. The transport term is b ux = 1 × 12 = 12 mm²/s. The diffusion term is D uxx = 2 × 2 = 4 mm²/s.

    ut+Lu=-16+12+4=0 mm²/s

    Dimensional check: b is in mm/s and ux in mm; D is in mm²/s and uxx has no unit. Each term is in mm²/s, like ut. The residual is the evaluated left side; its vanishing confirms this equation for the calculated expression.

  7. Check the endpoint and the direction of the calculation

    At t = T, the remaining duration is 0 s: the formula gives u(T, x) = x^2 = g(x). For x = 3 mm, the reading is then certainly 3 × 3 = 9 mm². It differs from 48 mm², the value obtained earlier when motion was still possible.

    If we prefer to count the remaining duration, define U(τ, x) = u(T − τ, x). The differentiation rule gives ∂U/∂τ = −ut = LU, with U(0, x) = g(x). This form uses a different time variable: it explains the change of sign without changing the motion.

    We have checked an expectation formula, its derivatives and its terminal value. We have proved neither a general uniqueness theorem nor validity for all terminal data; the general conditions will be covered in their dedicated unit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Backward means that the particle must move backwards.”

    The process keeps its drift and random fluctuations towards future times. The terminal value is used to calculate values at earlier starting points. Distinguish the current time t from the remaining duration τ = T − t before choosing the sign.

    Reread the two directions in the first diagram

  • “The coefficient of uₓₓ is simply σ^2.”

    The generator contains σ^2/2, which is 2 mm²/s here. Removing the factor 0.5 makes the diffusion term 8 mm²/s and the residual -16 + 12 + 8 = 4 mm²/s instead of 0. Start again from the generator, then check using the derivatives.

    Review the construction and checking of the equation

  • “The noise has mean 0, so the mean square equals the square of the mean.”

    Random fluctuations with mean 0 do not shift the mean position; they change its mean square. The variance contributes 12 mm² here: 36 + 12 = 48 mm². Use the definition of variance before calculating.

    Recover the contribution from dispersion

Start later from the same point

The starting time is now t = 3 s, still at x = 3 mm, with the same final time T and the same motion. Calculate the remaining duration, the terminal mean and variance, then u(3, 3). Compare with the value from the example. You are changing the starting point of the experiment, not observing a particular trajectory.

Hint 1

The remaining duration changes, but the starting position stays equal to 3 mm.

Hint 2

Add the square of the new mean and the new variance.

Compare with the solution

Duration: 5 − 3 = 2 s. Mean displacement: 1 × 2 = 2 mm. Terminal mean: 3 + 2 = 5 mm.

Variance: 4 × 2 = 8 mm². Square of the mean: 5 × 5 = 25 mm². Thus u(3, 3) = 25 + 8 = 33 mm².

The value is less than 48 mm²: here, shortening the wait reduces the dispersion and the mean displacement towards larger positive positions. This finding depends on the chosen position, drift and reading; it does not state that every terminal expectation universally decreases.

Exercise 3 · Check by another method

Recover the value by splitting the waiting period

Start again from t = 2 s and x = 3 mm. Split the waiting period after h = 1 s, at t + h = 3 s. Set Y = Xₜ₊ₕ, the random intermediate position. Recover u(2, 3) by calculating Eₜ,ₓ[u(3, Y)]. Compare with the attempt to replace Y by its mean alone.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The mean of Y is x + bh and its variance is σ^2h. Deduce E[Y^2] from these moments.

Hint 2

Expand u(3, y) = (y + 2 mm)^2 + 8 mm², then take the expectation term by term.

Compare with the solution

The current shifts the mean by 1 mm over this first duration. Thus E[Y] = 3 + 1 = 4 mm and Var(Y) = 4 × 1 = 4 mm². The square of this mean is 16 mm², so E[Y^2] = 16 + 4 = 20 mm².

After the intermediate time, the remaining mean displacement from drift is 2 mm. Expanding gives u(3, y) = y^2 + (4 mm)y + 12 mm². The linear coefficient is 2 × 2 = 4 mm; the constant term is 4 + 8 = 12 mm².

The expectation of the linear term is 4 × 4 = 16 mm². The sum is therefore 20 + 16 + 12 = 36 + 12 = 48 mm². We recover 48 mm² by splitting the waiting period: the check concerns means, without simulation.

Replacing Y by E[Y] would remove its variance 4 mm² from the calculation. The mean of a quadratic value depends on the dispersion of Y; evaluating the value at the mean alone therefore fails to respect the law of iterated expectations here.

Check your reasoning

  • Define Y and calculate its moments before using it.
  • Expand the intermediate value with coefficients in the correct units.
  • Recover 48 mm² and identify the contribution lost if Y is replaced by its mean.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What quantity does u(t, x) describe in the scene?

The expectation of the square of the position at T for the particle starting at x at t. It is in mm² and depends on the starting point; it is not a particular position.

Recall 2Why can the waiting period be split at an intermediate time?

The intermediate position is enough to predict what follows in this Markov model. Averaging the future value from this position, then averaging over its possible positions, gives the value at the starting point again.

Recall 3Recover the backward equation and its data without looking at the lesson.

uₜ + Lu = 0 for t < T, with Lu = b uₓ + (σ^2/2)uₓₓ and u(T, x) = g(x). Expanding the expectation over a short duration explains the sign; regularity and integrability must allow this expansion.

Recall 4Why do random fluctuations with mean 0 matter for our reading?

The reading is a square: its expectation is the square of the mean plus the variance. Here u(t, x) = (x + b(T − t))^2 + σ^2(T − t). Noise with mean 0 can therefore change the mean reading.

Recall 5What happens to the equation if we use τ = T − t?

With U(τ, x) = u(T − τ, x), ∂U/∂τ = LU and U(0, x) = g(x). The sign changes through differentiation of the change of variable; the trajectory keeps its direction in time.
