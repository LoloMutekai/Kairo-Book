Encoding

Your goalPrepare to encode an instruction by linking each tool to its drawer, then distinguish this preparation from demonstrated retention.

Before you start
c082-m5 : perceptual errors · c056-m5 : effect size · c083-m1 : working memory

✿01 See the idea

You are new to a workshop. The storage instruction says: pliers in the green drawer, hammer in the blue one, wrench in the yellow one, screwdriver in the red one. You need to prepare these associations so that you can put the tools away later, when the instruction is no longer in front of you. The entire scene and its timings are fictional.

You could spend a long time looking at the neat handwriting of the word “pliers” without processing its link to the green drawer. You could also imagine the pliers holding that drawer’s green label. In both cases, you have seen the word; the aspects of the information you have worked on differ.

Encoding refers to the processing through which information you encounter takes a form usable in memory. Here, a representation is a way of retaining something of that information: its appearance, its name, its meaning or its relationships. It is not a complete photograph of the scene. A memory trace is what remains of the experience in the memory system; the drawing does not show this trace directly.

Working memory lets you keep the instruction active while you use it. This does not guarantee that you will still be able to state it later. Our example concerns intentional encoding; information can also be encoded without an explicit intention to learn it. We will choose what to process, build an accurate link and check the preparation, without attributing any measured effectiveness to it.

The rule to remember

Define what you will need to know, process the corresponding relationships and check their accuracy; the quality of this preparation alone does not prove what will remain in memory.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Preparing the tools and allocating time

In this fictional example, you have 90 s. You reserve 12 s to read the instruction and 18 s to compare your preparation with the instruction while it is still visible. You divide the rest equally among the 4 associations. Let us work out what this plan makes you process and how much time it allocates to each link.

  1. Set the target before choosing a method

    The target is a relationship: for a given tool, its assigned drawer. The instruction contains 4 associations, each counted only once. Naming just the tools leaves the destination unspecified; reciting just the colours leaves the tool unspecified.

    Paying attention to information allows you to process it; this processing may concern its form, its sound or its meaning. Counting the letters in “pliers” works on its written form. Explaining that the pliers go in the green drawer works on the relationship useful for putting them away. If the task concerned spelling, the letters would instead become relevant. The right choice depends on what you will need to know.

  2. Build a link without changing the information

    For the pliers, you link familiar knowledge, “they grip”, to a chosen image: they hold the green label of their drawer. Elaboration means adding a relationship or an explanation to the material encountered, rather than processing only an isolated detail. Here, the image makes you work on the tool and the required colour together.

    For the other tools, you can express equally explicit links: “hammer to blue”, “wrench to yellow”, “screwdriver to red”. A short, accurate sentence is enough to define the target; there is no need to invent a story for each tool. Adding false details or leaving out the colour in an image does not serve our aim.

    A meaningful link provides a possible way to organise the information. It does not guarantee a lasting trace. We assume neither that all elaboration improves all learning nor that a given duration is enough to learn.

  3. Define the symbols for the time budget

    T denotes the total time; L, the initial reading; V, the check with the instruction. These durations are expressed in seconds, written s. N is the number of associations, a dimensionless count. t is the time allocated to each association, in seconds per association.

    We assume successive activities with no overlap or extra transition time, adherence to the reserved durations and equal allocation. We require N > 0 and T ≥ L + V. These are conditions for a schedule, not assumptions about a learning rate.

    t=T−L−VN

  4. Subtract the durations already allocated

    We add the reading and checking times because these activities occupy separate slots: 12 s + 18 s = 30 s. They are already included in the total. We therefore subtract them before allocating the remaining time.

    This leaves 90 s − 30 s = 60 s to build the links. Subtraction retains the unit of seconds and its result is positive: the plan is arithmetically possible.

    T−(L+V)=90−(12+18)=60 s

  5. Divide, then check by another route

    Sharing the 60 s equally among 4 associations requires division: 60 ÷ 4 = 15 s per association. Each slot is used to process a link, not to add another reading of the whole instruction on top of the schedule.

    First check, by subtracting the reserved times separately: 90 − 12 = 78 s, then 78 − 18 = 60 s. We obtain the same available time.

    Second check, by reconstructing the total: 4 × 15 = 60 s for the links, then 12 + 60 + 18 = 90 s. Seconds per association multiplied by the number of associations give seconds again.

    t=604=15 s par association

  6. State precisely what was checked

    During slot V, compare each link with the visible instruction: pliers–green, hammer–blue, wrench–yellow and screwdriver–red. This check can correct inaccurate preparation. Since the answer is still available, it does not measure what you could produce without support.

    The calculation proves only that the slots fit within the chosen time budget. It sets no minimum or optimal duration and produces no measure of retention. Establishing what remains available after a delay will require another test; the unit on retrieval will develop this question.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, explain the difference between looking at “pliers” and working on “pliers–green”. Then reconstruct the time budget, justify the division and name what the check with the instruction does not allow you to conclude.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I looked at the instruction, so every link was encoded in a lasting form.”

    Seeing the text does not specify which aspects you processed. Start again from the tool–drawer target and state the operation performed on this relationship. The time spent looking does not, by itself, provide evidence of later recall.

    Distinguish information seen from a relationship worked on

  • “An elaborate image is good even if it changes the drawer’s colour.”

    A representation can be rich yet inaccurate. For our task, the image must preserve the link in the instruction. Explicitly compare the hammer with blue before keeping your image; its vividness does not replace this comparison.

    Examine the preparation sheet and repair its link

  • “The time budget is correct, so retention has been proved.”

    Adding the durations checks a schedule. Even a completely accurate sheet, corrected with the instruction visible, does not demonstrate that its relationships will be available later without it. Name these kinds of evidence separately.

Exercise 1 · Identify the processing

Which action matches the target?

To prepare to put the pliers away, you are choosing between counting the letters in the word and imagining the pliers holding the green label. Which action directly processes the required relationship? Also explain what you cannot claim about later recall.

Hint 2

Compare with the solution

The image of the pliers holding the green label links the elements useful for our instruction. Counting the letters processes a characteristic of the word, without specifying its drawer. This action could match another objective, such as working on the written form.

We have identified processing that matches the target. Without a later test, we have not established that this person will retain the link or retrieve it without help.

  • I distinguish the relevance of the processing from the learning outcome.
  • I do not claim that a method is superior for all tasks.

Exercise 2 · Calculation on paper

The same workshop, a shorter time slot

The total time becomes 80 s. Keep 12 s for reading, 18 s for checking and the 4 associations. Calculate the time per association without rounding during the calculation. Reconstruct the total and explain why this result is not a prescription for learning.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The reserved times still take up 30 s: subtract them before dividing.

Hint 2

Keep the exact fraction, then express it as a decimal in seconds per association.

Compare with the solution

The reserved activities still take 12 + 18 = 30 s. The available time becomes 80 − 30 = 50 s. Equal allocation gives 50/4 = 25/2 s per association, or 12.50 s per association.

Check: 4 × (25/2) = 50 s, then 12 + 50 + 18 = 80 s. Compared with the initial plan, we subtract 10 s from the total; each association loses 2.50 s, and 4 × 2.50 = 10 s.

The data describe a chosen fictional allocation. The calculation establishes neither that this duration will be enough nor that the initial plan was optimal. It can be correct even if the person does not remember the destinations.

Check your reasoning

  • I subtract the reserved times before dividing.
  • I find 12.50 s per association and obtain 80 s again in the check.
  • I distinguish an allocated duration from demonstrated effectiveness.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis with reasons

Is a complete sheet enough?

The instruction remains visible. A preparation sheet lists pliers–green, hammer–red, wrench–yellow, screwdriver–red. Its author says: “All the tools are on my sheet and I have kept to my time budget, so I know how to put them away from memory.” Compare the links with the instruction, count those needing correction and propose the repair. What conclusion remains impossible after this repair?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The instruction assigns blue to the hammer. A line can be present yet incorrect.

Hint 2

Separate the comparison between texts, the sum of the durations and future ability without support.

Compare with the solution

The 4 tools are present, but the hammer’s destination contradicts the instruction: blue has been replaced by red. There is 1 incorrect link and 4 − 1 = 3 accurate links. This count comes from comparing the texts, not from a memory test.

Repair “hammer–red” to “hammer–blue”, then align the preparation sentence or image with this link. The pliers stay with green, the wrench with yellow and the screwdriver with red. After correction, 3 + 1 = 4 links match the instruction.

We have checked the sheet’s content with the source still available. We have not established unaided retention or how long it lasts. The author’s claim therefore goes beyond the evidence, even if the schedule and the corrected sheet are accurate.

Check your reasoning

  • I locate the hammer–red error and repair it to hammer–blue.
  • I justify the count using the links actually compared.
  • I refuse to infer an ability from a sheet corrected with help.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is encoding in the workshop scene?

The processing through which the instruction takes a form usable in memory. Here, preparation focuses particularly on the relationships between each tool and its drawer; looking at the words does not yet tell us which aspects were processed.

Recall 2Why does counting the letters in “pliers” not directly process our target?

The target is pliers–green drawer. The number of letters concerns the word’s form, without giving its destination. This processing could be relevant to another task; its relevance depends on the information you will need to know.

Recall 3What does the image of the pliers holding the green label do, and what constraint must it respect?

It adds an imagined relationship between the familiar tool and its drawer’s colour. This elaboration must preserve the instruction. The image is a means of preparation, with no guarantee of a lasting memory.

Recall 4How can you reconstruct the time per association from the total and the reserved times?

Subtract reading and checking from the total, then divide the remainder by the number of associations. With our choice: (90 − 12 − 18)/4 = 15 s per association. This allocated time does not measure learning.

Recall 5Why does an accurate sheet, produced with the instruction visible, not demonstrate retention?

The comparison checks the preparation while the answer remains available. It does not show what would be produced later without support. Reading or an assisted answer never establishes an attained level by itself.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the reasoning with pliers–green: define the relationship to retain, distinguish the aspects that can be processed, propose accurate elaboration, then reconstruct the time budget with its units. Finish with the limit of the evidence: coherent preparation and a corrected sheet do not yet demonstrate what will remain available without support.

What you understand
Human encoding refers to information processing, without guaranteeing later recall. For the tools, building a relevant relationship and checking its accuracy are distinct actions. This comparison prepares a critical reading of the software; it establishes no equivalence between biological memory and computer memory.
