Level 2 · C139·M2

Gaussian mixtures

Your goalBuild a density from Gaussian components, then calculate the probabilistic membership of an observation while distinguishing proportion, density, and responsibility.

Before you start
c137-m5 : choosing the rank · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c067-m5 : pipeline · c139-m1 : k-means

✿01 See the idea

A workshop fills seed packets using 2 dosing modes. Mode A is consistent; mode B has more spread. Both aim for the same mean mass, 100 g. The packets are then mixed and the mode labels removed. You weigh a packet at 100 g: which mode did it come from?

The scene and all its parameters are fictional. We assume that the proportion of mode A is 1/4 and that of mode B is 3/4. Before weighing, B is therefore more likely. But masses very close to the centre are more concentrated in the consistent mode: the reading also provides information.

A component here is the distribution of masses produced by a mode. We represent it with a normal distribution, also called a Gaussian: a bell curve described by its mean and standard deviation. The standard deviation measures spread in grams; it is 2 g for A and 4 g for B. The variance is its square, in grams squared.

The mixture describes choosing a mode, then drawing a mass according to that mode. Each packet comes from a single mode; our knowledge of that mode may remain divided between possibilities. Clustering by centres, as in the previous unit, is insufficient here: the centres coincide, while the spreads differ. We will keep the parameters fixed to learn to interpret this model; estimating them will wait until the next unit.

The rule to remember

For an observation, multiply the density of each component by its proportion, add these contributions, then divide each contribution by their sum to obtain the probabilistic memberships.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Where did the packet weighed at the centre come from?

The observed mass is x = 100 g. The modes have the shared mean μ = 100 g, standard deviations σ_A = 2 g and σ_B = 4 g, and proportions w_A = 1/4, w_B = 3/4. Let us calculate the mixture density at this mass and the probabilities of origin under this fixed model.

  1. Separate the observable quantity, the mode, and the parameters

    X is the random mass of a packet; x is its observed value, both in grams. Z is the hidden mode label, A or B; it is not a mass. The notation P denotes a probability. For j equal to A or B, w_j = P(Z = j) is the proportion before weighing, with no unit. Here w_A + w_B = 1, and each weight is between 0 and 1.

    μ_j is the mean of X given Z = j. bfbshz gegyqw rdyhhs iqpzny ztzlcm lvysxo iynd The variances are 4 g² for A and 16 g² for B. Equal means do not require equal spreads.

    We assume a single mode per packet and a Gaussian conditional distribution of mass. The parameters are given, with no estimation uncertainty in this exercise. The reading is treated as an exact continuous observation: rounding by a scale would require calculating probabilities over an interval. A Gaussian mathematically allows negative masses; here it is an approximation around a positive target, not a physical law for all masses.

  2. Write the density of each component

    f_j(x) denotes the conditional density of X in mode j, in inverse grams. A density gives a probability through an area over an interval. The probability of obtaining exactly x in this continuous model is 0; f_j(x) therefore does not denote that point probability.

    C=12π
    fj(x)=Cσjexp(−(x−μjσj)22)

    C is a positive constant with no unit, kept symbolic for an exact calculation. π is the usual geometric constant; exp is the exponential function, with exp(0) = 1. The quotient (x − μ_j)/σ_j, called the standardized deviation, has no unit: its square can enter the exponential. The factor C/σ_j ensures a total area equal to 1 under each bell curve.

  3. Mix the distributions without averaging the packets

    p(x) is the density of mass when the mode is no longer known, in the same unit as f_j(x). Modes A and B are mutually exclusive possibilities for the origin, so we add their weighted contributions.

    p(x)=wAfA(x)+wBfB(x)

    Since each density has area 1, the area of the mixture is w_A + w_B = 1. The weights apply to distributions: each packet is not made by adding fractions of masses from the modes. The sum of bell curves is generally not a single Gaussian. It may also have only a single peak, as here with this shared centre; the number of components cannot be read off by counting peaks.

  4. Calculate the heights at the observed mass

    In each mode, the deviation from the centre is 100 − 100 = 0 g. The standardized deviations are 0 for A and 0 for B; the exponential is therefore 1 in both cases. Division by the standard deviation remains.

    fA(100)=C2 par gramme;fB(100)=C4 par gramme

    The coefficients of C are 1/2 and 1/4 per gram respectively. Their ratio is 2: at its centre, A has a higher density. This ratio concerns the reading given the mode; it does not yet account for how frequent the modes are.

  5. Apply the weights and calculate the total density

    The contribution of A is (1/4) × (1/2) × C = (1/8) × C per gram. That of B is (3/4) × (1/4) × C = (3/16) × C per gram. B is less concentrated, but its higher proportion compensates for this difference here.

    p(100)=(1/8+3/16)C=5/16C par gramme

    We keep C exact; no rounding of π or a square root is needed. This sum serves as the total density and a common reference for comparing the contributions. It is strictly positive.

  6. Reverse the question using responsibilities

    The responsibility γ_j(x) is the probability of mode j after observing x, under the model: γ_j(x) = P(Z = j given X = x). The symbol γ is read as “gamma”. Bayes’ formula uses densities here:

    γj(x)=wjfj(x)p(x)

    The denominator of this fraction is p(x). To recover this ratio without dividing by a point probability of 0, first imagine a small interval around x: Bayes compares the probabilities of this interval in each mode. As the interval shrinks, its common width cancels and the ratio of densities remains. The Gaussians are continuous and p(x) is positive, which makes this step valid here.

    The numerator and denominator have the same unit: γ_j(x) has no unit. Dividing by their sum normalizes the contributions; the responsibilities sum to 1. This is a probabilistic assignment of origin, not a new mass or a physical fraction of the packet.

  7. Obtain and interpret the result

    The factor C and the unit cancel in each quotient. Only rational fractions remain:

    γA(100)=1/85/16=2/5=0.40
    γB(100)=3/165/16=3/5=0.60

    The reading changes the probability of A from 0.25 before observation to 0.40 after observation. It therefore favours A relative to its starting point, while leaving B more likely. If a single label is required and we choose the highest responsibility, we choose B; we then lose the remaining uncertainty. The actual mode of this packet has not been observed.

  8. Check another way and limit the conclusion

    Normalization: 2/5 + 3/5 = 1. Each value is between 0 and 1. Let us also check the ratio without recalculating p(x): γ_A/γ_B = (w_A/w_B) × (f_A/f_B) = (1/4)/(3/4) × 2 = 2/3. We recover the ratio of the final responsibilities.

    The result depends on the proportions AND the spreads, not just the distances to the centres. Swapping the names A and B along with all their parameters would swap the responsibilities without changing the total density: the letters have no explanatory power of their own.

    We have evaluated a fixed mixture. We have neither learned its parameters nor chosen its number of components. Correct normalization does not demonstrate that these modes represent a real workshop. Estimation by alternation will be covered in C139·M3; choosing the number of groups and studying anomalies will come later.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The tallest bell curve at the observed point must give the most likely mode.”

    The height f_j(x) still has to be multiplied by w_j. Here A has a density 2 times as high at the centre, but B remains more likely after weighting. Reconstruct the contributions 1/8 × C and 3/16 × C per gram before comparing.

    Revisit the weighting and then the responsibilities

  • “Density and responsibility are the same probability.”

    f_j(x) describes the mass given the mode, in inverse grams. γ_j(x) describes the mode given the mass, with no unit. Their questions are reversed. Normalizing the weighted contributions gives the latter; an isolated height does not.

    Reread the units and Bayes’ formula

  • “A mixture of 2 Gaussians must have 2 clearly separated bumps.”

    Our components have the same centre, with different spreads. Their bell curves overlap and the mixture has only a single peak. A component is a distribution in the model; it is not a bump that would necessarily have been identified on a graph.

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · correct the units

Variance or standard deviation?

For the same modes, someone reads the variances 4 g² and 16 g², then reports the densities at the centre as C/4 and C/16 per gram. Correct the denominators and recover the ratio of the heights. Why would the proposed ratio be misleading?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The formula divides by σ_j. Find the positive number whose square gives each variance.

Hint 2

At the centre, the exponential is 1. The factor C cancels in the ratio of the heights.

Compare with the solution

2 × 2 = 4: the standard deviation of A is 2 g. Likewise, 4 × 4 = 16: that of B is 4 g. We therefore obtain C/2 and C/4 per gram.

The correct ratio is (1/2)/(1/4) = 2. Using the variances directly would put a quantity in grams squared in the denominator of a density of mass. The ratio would then overestimate the advantage of A at the centre. This check comes before any weighting by the proportions.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover 2 g and 4 g from their squares.
  • Obtain the ratio 2 while distinguishing variance from standard deviation.
  • State the density unit of inverse grams.

Exercise 2 · Application · redo the normalization

The modes become equally frequent

In the same fictional workshop, keep the means, standard deviations, and reading, but replace the proportions with w_A = w_B = 1/2. Calculate the contributions, the total density at 100 g, and the responsibilities. Does the most likely mode change?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The conditional densities do not change: they are still C/2 and C/4 per gram.

Hint 2

Calculate the new sum of contributions before normalizing. The old sum no longer corresponds to this model.

Compare with the solution

The contribution of A is (1/2) × (1/2) × C = (1/4) × C per gram; that of B is (1/2) × (1/4) × C = (1/8) × C per gram. The total density is therefore (3/8) × C per gram.

Divide the new coefficients by their sum: γ_A = (1/4)/(3/8) = 2/3 ≈ 0.6667, with no unit; γ_B = (1/8)/(3/8) = 1/3 ≈ 0.3333, with no unit.

Check: 2/3 + 1/3 = 1. A becomes the most likely. Changing the proportions is enough to change the chosen label, even without changing the centres, the reading, or the spreads.

Check your reasoning

  • Recalculate the sum 3/8 instead of reusing 5/16.
  • Obtain 2/3 and 1/3 with a sum equal to 1.
  • Explain the change in assignment through the proportions.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · derive a condition

At exactly what proportion are the modes equally likely?

Still using the packet of 100 g and the same standard deviations, let w_A vary and set w_B = 1 − w_A. What proportion of A makes the responsibilities equal? Then justify on which side of this proportion A becomes more likely. Do not assume that equal responsibilities require equal proportions.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The responsibilities have the same positive denominator. Their equality therefore amounts to equating the contributions.

Hint 2

Cancel C, then multiply w_A/σ_A = (1 − w_A)/σ_B by σ_A × σ_B and collect the terms containing w_A.

Compare with the solution

Equate w_A × C/σ_A and (1 − w_A) × C/σ_B. After cancelling C and multiplying by the positive standard deviations, we obtain w_A × σ_B = σ_A − w_A × σ_A. Therefore w_A × (σ_A + σ_B) = σ_A.

wA=σAσA+σB=26=1/3

At the threshold, w_B = 2/3. Both contributions are (1/6) × C per gram; their sum is (1/3) × C per gram. The responsibilities are 1/2 and 1/2, and their sum is 1. They are equal despite different proportions.

A is more likely if its contribution is larger. The same operations on positive quantities give w_A > 1/3. Below the threshold, B is more likely. The initial weight 1/4 is below the threshold, while the weight 1/2 from the previous exercise is above it: the results are consistent. This boundary applies to the central reading and fixed spreads in our example.

Check your reasoning

  • Start from equality of the contributions and justify cancelling C.
  • Find 1/3 for A and 2/3 for B, then check the responsibilities.
  • Justify the direction of the inequality and limit the conclusion to the case studied.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you describe the generation of an observation in a Gaussian mixture?

Choose a component according to the weights, then draw the value according to its Gaussian. The chosen mode may remain hidden. The weights are nonnegative and sum to 1; the standard deviations are strictly positive.

Recall 2What do w_j, f_j(x), and γ_j(x) distinguish, and what are their units?

w_j is the probability of the mode before weighing, with no unit. f_j(x) is the density of mass given the mode, in inverse grams. γ_j(x) is the probability of the mode after weighing, with no unit, under the given model.

Recall 3How can you reconstruct the calculation of a responsibility without confusing height with probability of origin?

Multiply the weight of the mode by its density at the observed point; add the contributions of all modes; divide the chosen contribution by this positive sum. The resulting responsibilities sum to 1.

Recall 4Why does mode A, which is more concentrated at the centre, not win in the initial example?

Its density at the centre is 2 times that of B, but it is less frequent. The contributions are (1/8) × C and (3/16) × C per gram. After normalization, A receives 2/5 and B 3/5.

Recall 5Does the number of peaks prove the number of components or their physical reality?

No. Components can overlap; ours share a centre. A mixture describes an assumption about the distribution. Even a correctly calculated responsibility proves neither the actual mode of a packet nor the relevance of the model to the workshop.
