Overlap

Your goalIdentify the days of returns shared by two labels, then remove from training the observations whose labels overlap the test block.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

Consider a small fictional table: one row per trading day, with a label on each row, meaning the sum of returns over the 3 days following that row’s date. The label for observation 5 uses days 6, 7 and 8; the label for observation 6 uses days 7, 8 and 9. The values on this page are fictional: they are used for counting, not to describe a real market.

Two neighboring rows therefore contain some of the same information: 2 days out of 3 are shared. If one row is used for training and the other for testing, the test no longer concerns only a future the model has not seen. A model capable of memorizing its examples could benefit without having understood anything.

This sharing is called label overlap here. It can be identified from the days used by each label, without examining the model. To avoid it, leave a margin on each side of the test block: remove from training the observations whose labels overlap the test days. We call removal before the block purging, and removal after it an embargo; other texts use these terms with slightly different scopes, which the milestone clarifies for the reviewed code.

Overlap has a second, simpler consequence: 12 consecutive rows do not amount to 12 separate experiments. Taking one row out of every 3 gives 4 labels with no shared days. This count does not establish a useful sample size; it only reminds us that neighboring rows are not independent.

The rule to remember

A label covering 3 days shares 2 days with its immediate neighbor; around a test block, remove the 2 neighboring observations on each side, because their labels overlap the test days.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A test block in the middle of 12 fictional days

A fictional series contains 12 daily observations. Each label sums returns over the next 3 days. The test block starts at observation 6 and contains 3 consecutive observations. Which training observations must be removed, and how many remain? The fictional returns are given in basis points (bp, one hundredth of a percent).

  1. Define the label and its symbols

    Observation i is taken at the close of day i. The symbol r, with subscript k, denotes the logarithmic return for day k, in basis points; these returns add up across days. The horizon h is the number of future days used, here 3 days. The label y for observation i is therefore, in bp:

    yi=∑k=i+1i+hrk

    Assumptions: one observation per day, the same horizon for all observations, a single continuous test block, and explanatory variables that use only the past. If these variables span several days, they raise a separate issue that this page does not address.

  2. List the days used by two neighboring observations

    Observation 5 uses days 6, 7 and 8. Observation 6 uses days 7, 8 and 9. For two observations i and j, with i before j, the number of shared days equals the horizon minus the gap j − i, without ever falling below 0:

    cij=max(0,h−(j−i))

    Here the gap is 6 − 5 = 1, so the overlap is 3 − 1 = 2 days: days 7 and 8, as in the first diagram. When the gap reaches the horizon, the overlap falls to 0: the two labels no longer share any days.

  3. Calculate the shared amount

    Take fictional returns: a gain of 12 bp on day 6, a drop of 5 bp on day 7, a gain of 8 bp on day 8 and a gain of 3 bp on day 9. The part shared by both labels is the sum for days 7 and 8: 8 − 5 = 3 bp, because the drop is subtracted.

    Label for observation 5: 12 + 3 = 15 bp. Label for observation 6: 3 + 3 = 6 bp. Check: removing the day unique to each leaves 15 − 12 = 3 and 6 − 3 = 3, the same shared part.

    Consequence: a model that has memorized the label for observation 5 and knows the return for day 6, already observed at the close, can infer 3 bp of the 6 bp label it is then asked to predict. Only day 9 remains unknown.

  4. Remove observations before the block

    The test days run from day 7, the first day of the label for observation 6, to the last day of the block’s last label. An observation i before the block includes this first day if its last day, i + h, reaches day 7. Writing s for the first test observation:

    i≥s−h+1

    With s = 6 and h = 3, this gives i ≥ 4. Observation 4 uses days 5, 6 and 7: day 7 is already in the test label. Observation 3 ends at day 6 and has no overlap with the test. We therefore purge observations 4 to 5, or 2 observations, which is h − 1.

  5. Remove observations after the block

    The block ends at observation e = s + t − 1 = 6 + 3 − 1 = 8, where t is the number of observations in the block. Its label extends to day e + h = 11. An observation j after the block overlaps the test days if its first day, j + 1, falls no later than day e + h:

    j≤e+h−1

    Here j ≤ 10. Observations 9 to 10 are placed under embargo, another 2 observations. Observation 11 is the first whose days start after the last test day.

  6. Count and check

    Before the block, training keeps observations 1 to 3, or 3 observations. After the block, it keeps observations 11 to 12, or 2. In total: 3 + 2 = 5 training observations.

    Check: 5 + 2 + 2 + 3 = 12, so no observation is lost or counted twice. Without purging or an embargo, we would have kept 9 training observations, of which 4 had labels overlapping the test. The test is fairer, at the cost of a smaller training set; this still says nothing about the model’s quality.

Explain it in your own words

Why do we remove h − 1 observations on each side of the block, rather than h? Explain using the days of observations 4 and 3.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Separating the dates is enough: a row’s label uses only its own date.”

    The label for observation i sums returns over the days following date i. A training row just before the test can therefore contain test days. List the days of each label before judging a boundary.

    Review the shared days

  • “A margin of a single observation is enough for any horizon.”

    The required margin depends on the horizon: for h days of returns, it is h − 1 observations on each side. With h = 3, a margin of 1 still leaves a shared day, as in the exercise on the declared margin.

    Repeat the margin exercise

  • “A chronology check in code guarantees labels with no overlap.”

    A check that compares an index gap with a declared margin does not read the label horizon. Its presence proves neither the absence of overlap, nor that it is connected to a caller, nor that it has run: review what the cited lines show and what they do not show.

    Review the scope of the cited code

Exercise 1 · Explained application

A longer horizon

Use the fictional series of 12 observations and the test block from observations 6 to 8 again, but with a horizon of 5 days. Which observations do you remove before and after the block, and how many training observations remain?

Hint 1

First calculate how many neighbors are removed on each side: the horizon minus one.

Hint 2

Before the block, count backward by this number from the first test observation; after the block, count forward by this number from its last observation.

Compare with the solution

Neighbors removed on each side: 5 − 1 = 4. Before the block, observations 2 to 5 are removed, because 6 − 4 = 2. After the block, observations 9 to 12 are removed, because 8 + 4 = 12.

Only observation 1 remains: 1 before the block and 0 after it. Check: 3 + 8 + 1 = 12. A long horizon quickly uses up a short series; this result describes the fictional scene, not a sample size rule.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the horizon minus one on both sides.
  • Keep the observations in the test block.
  • Check that the groups add up to the total number of observations.

Exercise 2 · Guided transfer

A block at the start of the series

Keep the horizon of 3 days, but place the test block at observations 1 to 3, at the very start of the series of 12 observations. Which observations do you remove, and how many training observations remain?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First ask whether there are any observations before the block.

Hint 2

Only the 2 observations immediately following the block’s last observation overlap its days.

Compare with the solution

Before the block, there are no observations to remove. After it, the block ends at observation 3, so observations 4 to 5 are removed, or 2 observations.

Training starts at observation 6 and runs to 12, or 7 observations. Check: 3 + 2 + 7 = 12. At the edge of the series, removal is needed on only one side: the rule for neighbors applies only where they exist. Training on dates later than the test answers a different question from predicting the future; purging does not change that.

Check your reasoning

  • Recognize that there are no observations to purge before the block.
  • Remove only the observations under embargo after the block.
  • Check the sum and state that training comes after the test.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis

A margin that seems sufficient

An imaginary checking rule accepts a split if the gap between the last training observation and the first test observation is strictly greater than a declared margin. Here training ends at observation 4, the test starts at observation 6, the declared margin is 1 and the horizon is 3 days. Does the rule accept this split? Do the two labels have no shared days? What declared margin would enforce a safe split?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate the gap, then compare it with the declared margin.

Hint 2

List the days of the label for observation 4, then those of the label for observation 6, and find their intersection.

Hint 3

For no day to be shared, the gap must be at least the horizon; express this condition as a margin using the strict comparison.

Compare with the solution

Gap: 6 − 4 = 2, greater than the declared margin 1. The rule therefore accepts the split.

Yet the label for observation 4 uses days 5, 6 and 7, and the label for observation 6 uses days 7, 8 and 9. Day 7 is shared: 3 − 2 = 1 day. The rule has accepted an overlap.

To exclude every shared day, the gap must be at least 3; training must therefore end at observation 3 at the latest. Since the rule requires a gap strictly greater than the margin, the margin must be at least 2, meaning the horizon minus one. This result applies to this imaginary rule and this type of label.

Check your reasoning

  • Compare the gap with the margin before drawing a conclusion.
  • List the days of both labels instead of relying on the rule.
  • Relate the required margin to the horizon minus one.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does it mean for two labels to overlap?

They sum at least one shared day of future returns. For the same horizon h, two observations separated by a gap d share h − d days, without falling below 0.

Recall 2How many observations are removed on each side of a test block for a horizon h?

The h − 1 neighboring observations: purging is removal before the block, and an embargo is removal after it. With h = 3, that means 2 observations on each side in the fictional scene.

Recall 3What does purging fail to guarantee?

It removes the overlap between training and testing. It does not make the retained observations independent of one another, address explanatory variables spanning several days, or prove that a model predicts well.

Recall 4Why does a declared margin fail to prove the absence of overlap?

It compares a gap with a threshold. The absence of shared days depends on the label horizon, which requires a gap of at least h; a margin below h − 1 can allow overlap.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define the label as the sum of returns over the next h days; list the days of each label; count the overlap, h minus the gap; remove on each side of the test block the h − 1 neighbors whose days overlap the test; check that training, removals and testing add up to the total; remember that this reduces a specific leak without proving that a model is good.
