Binomial

Your goalFind the terms of the square of a sum in an enlarged mat, then count the products in the cube without missing any.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You are enlarging a square mat made of identical square tiles. You add the same width along the right edge and the bottom edge. The original square and the small corner do not fill the whole new mat: a strip runs along each extended edge. Point to these strips before counting.

The side of a tile serves as the unit of length, written u. The original mat has side length 3 u; the extension measures 2 u in each direction. The values are fictional, chosen so that the areas can be counted again by hand. We assume tiles with no gaps, cuts or overlaps.

A square tile is our unit of area: it covers 1 u multiplied by 1 u. Length and area are therefore counted differently. The enlarged side measures 5 u; its area remains to be found.

A binomial is a sum of 2 terms, such as a + b. Squaring it means multiplying the whole sum by itself. The drawing will show why you must also multiply what comes from a term by what comes from the other. These products are the cross terms.

The rule to remember

In a product of sums, keep every product before grouping equal ones. For the square, the strips ab and ba are distinct parts of the mat even though their areas are equal.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Enlarge the mat without losing a strip

With the original side 3 u and the extension 2 u, find the area of the mat with side length 5 u. Then keep the same numbers to examine the cube of the binomial: this will be a calculation on the numerical measurements, without adding another object to the scene.

  1. Name what is being multiplied

    a and b denote the numbers measuring the original side and the added width in unit u: a = 3, b = 2. These numbers have no units; the corresponding lengths are a u and b u. The products in the square give the numbers of square tiles in each region. An identity is an equality that is true for all allowed values of the letters. For the algebraic identity for the square, a and b can be any real numbers; the division into regions here requires positive lengths.

    The symbol × indicates multiplication. Writing ab means a × b. The exponent 2 means repeating the same factor 2 times: the square of a is a × a. Expanding means replacing a product of sums with the sum of all its products; collecting like terms means adding terms that have the same variable part.

  2. Count each region of the mat

    Original square: 3 rows of 3 tiles give 3 × 3 = 9 square tiles. Bottom strip: its width is 3 u and its height is 2 u, so 3 × 2 = 6 square tiles. Right strip: 2 × 3 = 6 square tiles. Corner: 2 × 2 = 4 square tiles.

    The corner belongs to neither of the strips defined this way. The 4 regions fill the square without overlapping: adding their areas counts each tile exactly 1 time.

  3. Recover the regions using the distributive property

    The distributive property tells us to multiply each term of a sum by the whole factor multiplying that sum. First, each term in the first pair of brackets multiplies the entire second pair of brackets:

    (a+b)2=a(a+b)+b(a+b)

    Next, distribute within each product. The first gives a × a and a × b; the second gives b × a and b × b. These are the regions in the drawing. Finally, ab = ba, because changing the order of the factors does not change their product. The coefficient 2 counts the 2 equal terms:

    (a+b)2=a2+ab+ba+b2
    (a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2

  4. Reassemble the area and measure what was missed

    The strips occupy 6 + 6 = 12 square tiles. Including the original square: 9 + 12 = 21. The corner completes the area: 21 + 4 = 25 square tiles. Each addition combines areas, so it keeps the same unit.

    Adding only the squares would give 9 + 4 = 13 square tiles. The difference is 25 − 13 = 12 square tiles: exactly the strips. The cross term therefore has a concrete role in the formula: it represents what is missing from the incomplete mat.

  5. Check using the full side and a limiting case

    Start again from the lengths without using the division into regions: 3 + 2 = 5 u for the side, then 5 × 5 = 25 square tiles. This recovers the reassembled area 25. This other approach checks the numerical calculation; the justification that holds for any letters is the distributive property used above.

    If the whole extension is removed, b = 0, the side returns to 3 + 0 = 3 u and the area is 9 square tiles. The terms containing b vanish; the original square remains. The drawing then reduces to a square with no strips or corner.

  6. Move to the cube while keeping all products

    The exponent 3 means multiplying 3 factors (a + b). Distribute again: in each factor, choose a or b, then multiply the chosen terms. From the first 2 factors, we get aa, ab, ba, bb; each then gives 2 products, making 4 × 2 = 8 products before grouping.

    Write them out so that nothing is assumed: aaa; aab, aba, baa; abb, bab, bba; bbb. These expressions denote multiplications, rather than numbers written as digits. The order of the factors distinguishes where they come from, then their values allow them to be grouped.

    There is 1 product with no b, 3 products with exactly 1 b, 3 products with 2 factors b and 1 product containing only b factors. This gives the expansion:

    (a+b)3=a3+3a2b+3ab2+b3

    Here the cube is an expression involving the numerical measurements a and b. Its result does not count the square tiles in the mat. The coefficients count the products grouped together; no probability is involved.

  7. Calculate the cube and check its coefficients

    For our numbers, a cubed is 9 × 3 = 27. Each product aab is 9 × 2 = 18, so the group is 3 × 18 = 54. Each product abb is 3 × 4 = 12, so its group is 3 × 12 = 36. Finally, b cubed is 4 × 2 = 8.

    Addition: 27 + 54 = 81, then 81 + 36 = 117, then 117 + 8 = 125. Direct check: the square of the sum is 25; multiplying again by 5 gives 125. The results agree.

    Counting check: 1 + 3 = 4, then 4 + 3 = 7, then 7 + 1 = 8, matching the 8 products listed. This total alone would not prove that they were grouped correctly: the explicit list checks this.

  8. Recognise the general form before proving it

    For an integer power n greater than or equal to 0, the same principle is written as a sum. n counts the factors (a + b); k counts those in which b is chosen. The coefficient c(n, k) is the number of ways to choose these positions. The other positions give a, so its exponent is n − k. n, k and the coefficients are numbers without units.

    (a+b)n=∑k=0nc(n,k)an−kbk

    The symbol ∑ asks us to add a term for each integer k from 0 to n, including both endpoints. We introduce this notation for strictly positive a and b, so that every power shown is defined. For n = 0, the product with no factors is 1 by convention and c(0, 0) = 1. The case b = 0 for the mat is handled directly using its expanded square.

    Here, only the square and the cube need to be reconstructed. The general formula is a reference point: calculating factorials, proving by induction and drawing at random are unnecessary for these finite expansions.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations. Draw the mat again, name the area of each region and explain why the strips become a coefficient. Then recover the groups in the cube without reciting their sequence of coefficients. ✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The square of a sum is the sum of the squares.”

    On the mat, you would keep only the original square and the corner: 13 square tiles. Draw the missing strips and add 12 square tiles. In algebra, distribute each pair of brackets again before grouping.

    Recover the regions and the distributive property

  • “The term 2ab means multiplying (2a) by (2b).”

    In 2ab, only the number of copies of ab is doubled. In (2a)(2b), each factor is doubled: the product is 4ab. Here 6 × 4 = 24, whereas the combined strips cover 12 square tiles. Start again from the distinct regions.

    Count the strips in the drawing again

  • “Binomial coefficients are probabilities to learn by heart.”

    Here they count products. In the cube, aab, aba and baa are 3 equal products; grouping them explains the coefficient 3. No randomness has been introduced. Reconstruct the list instead of assuming a counting formula is already known.

    Return to the cube and the product count

Exercise 1 · Identification and justification

Repair the incomplete mat

Keep a = 3 and b = 2. A worksheet claims 13 square tiles for the enlarged mat by adding the squares. Without looking at the drawing, identify the missing regions, their total area and the correct area. Explain why the cross term has its coefficient.

Hint 1

Add the right edge, then the bottom edge. Which region runs alongside the original square at each extension?

Hint 2

The products a × b and b × a have the same value, but represent different regions.

Compare with the solution

The missing regions are the 2 strips. Each occupies 3 × 2 = 6 square tiles; together, 6 + 6 = 12. The correct area is 13 + 12 = 25 square tiles.

The coefficient 2 counts the copies of ab in the sum of the regions. It does not ask you to double the 2 sides of each strip. Check using the full side: 5 × 5 = 25 square tiles.

Check your reasoning

  • Name the strips and distinguish them from the corner.
  • Calculate 12 missing square tiles and 25 square tiles in total.
  • Relate the coefficient to equal products, using a unit of area.

Exercise 2 · Numerical application with a check

Same mat, different dimensions

Repeat the construction with a = 4 and b = 1. Calculate the original square, each strip and the corner separately. Combine the strips, give the total area in square tiles, then check it using the new side.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write a × a, a × b, b × a and b × b before substituting for the letters.

Hint 2

The calculation using the full side uses (a + b) × (a + b). It must recover the sum of the regions.

Compare with the solution

Original square: 4 × 4 = 16. Each strip: 4 × 1 = 4. Combined strips: 2 × 4 = 8. Corner: 1 × 1 = 1. All these results count square tiles.

The original square and the strips give 16 + 8 = 24, then the corner brings the area to 24 + 1 = 25 square tiles. Check: 4 + 1 = 5 u for the side and 5 × 5 = 25 square tiles.

The total is the same as in the example, but the strips no longer have the same area as before. A correct total on its own therefore does not show that each region has been calculated correctly.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the cross products before grouping.
  • Find 8 square tiles for the strips and 25 for the whole mat.
  • Show the check using the side and distinguish length from area.

Exercise 3 · Reconstruction and the scope of a proof

Reconstruct the coefficients of the cube

Put the lesson aside. Expand (a + b) cubed by listing the products from each choice, then group them. Check with a = 3 and b = 2. Finally, explain what this numerical calculation verifies and why it is insufficient to justify the identity for all values.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with aa, ab, ba, bb. Multiply each by a, then by b, without missing a result.

Hint 2

Sort the list by the number of factors b: 0, 1, 2, then 3.

Compare with the solution

The list is aaa; aab, aba, baa; abb, bab, bba; bbb. The groups therefore have coefficients 1, 3, 3, 1. Products in the same group are equal because their factors can be reordered.

(a+b)3=a3+3a2b+3ab2+b3

With the numbers from the mat: 27 + 54 + 36 + 8 = 125. The direct product gives 5 × 5 × 5 = 125. This check concerns these numbers; this numerical result does not represent an area of the mat.

The calculation could reveal an error in this case, but its success does not cover every case. The general identity for the cube is justified by the distributive property, which produces the complete list for any letters, followed by grouping equal products.

Check your reasoning

  • List the 8 products without omitting or repeating a choice of factors.
  • Justify each coefficient using its group and obtain 125.
  • Separate the numerical check from the algebraic justification; do not describe the cube as an area.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does it mean to square the binomial a + b?

Multiply the whole sum (a + b) by itself. Each term of the first sum must be multiplied by each term of the second.

Recall 2Where does the term 2ab in the square come from?

From the products ab and ba, which are equal in value and correspond to distinct strips of the mat. Their sum is 2ab.

Recall 3Why does (2a)(2b) not represent the combined strips?

The product contains 2 × 2 copies of ab, giving 4ab. The combined strips represent only 2 copies of ab.

Recall 4How can you recover the coefficients of the cube without reciting a formula?

List the choices aaa; aab, aba, baa; abb, bab, bba; bbb, then group them by the number of factors b. This gives 1, 3, 3, 1.

Recall 5Does a correct numerical expansion prove an identity on its own?

It checks the chosen case. Here the justification for any letters relies on the distributive property and on grouping all the products.
