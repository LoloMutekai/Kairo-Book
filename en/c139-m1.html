K-means

Your goalCarry out the assignments and mean calculations in k-means, then check what a decrease in cost allows us to conclude.

Before you start
c137-m5 : choosing the rank · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c067-m5 : pipeline

✿01 See the idea

In a shipping facility, you want to represent the masses of parcels using a few representative masses. You have received no labels such as “light” or “heavy”. The 5 parcels in our fictional scene weigh 1, 2, 3, 8 and 10 kg. We decide to form k = 2 groups; this choice is given in the exercise.

Place markers at 1 kg and 3 kg. Assign each parcel to the nearest marker. Then move each marker to the mean of the masses in its group. The marker becomes a centre, also called a centroid: it represents the parcels without having to equal the mass of an existing parcel.

Moving a centre can make an earlier assignment inappropriate. We must therefore reassign the parcels, then recalculate the means, until the assignments and centres stabilize. This alternation is the k-means mechanism studied here.

We want groups whose masses are close together, rather than certified shipping categories. All masses have equal weight in the calculation. They are known, finite and expressed in kg; no other attribute is involved. The grouping predicts neither the contents of the parcels nor their fragility.

The rule to remember

Set the number of groups and the starting centres; assign each parcel to the nearest centre; replace each centre with the mean of its group; repeat and check the cost.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Grouping masses without prior labels

Parcels a, b, c, d and e weigh 1, 2, 3, 8 and 10 kg respectively. Keep k = 2, with starting centres A at 1 kg and B at 3 kg. In a tie, we choose A. The groups must remain nonempty.

  1. State what we minimize

    n is the number of parcels; here n = 5. i denotes a parcel and xᵢ its mass in kg. k is the number of groups, a given integer between 1 and n. j denotes a group; Gⱼ is the set of indices of its parcels and μⱼ is its centre, in kg. The groups form a partition: each parcel belongs to exactly one group.

    J=∑j=1k∑i∈Gj(xi−μj)2

    ∑ means to add the indicated terms. J, called the cost or within-cluster inertia, is the sum of the squared differences between each mass and its centre. It is expressed in kg squared and remains greater than or equal to 0. It is not the mean of the differences: we do not divide by n.

    On this line, the distance is the absolute value of xᵢ − μⱼ; squaring it gives the same nearest centre. With multiple coordinates, k-means uses the sum of the squared differences between coordinates and calculates a mean for each coordinate. The choice of scales then becomes essential: changing the scale of a single coordinate changes the geometry. Our calculation deliberately considers mass alone.

  2. Assign using the starting centres

    With A at 1 kg and B at 3 kg, the midpoint is (1 + 3)/2 = 2 kg. To the left of this midpoint, A is nearer; to the right, B is nearer. At the midpoint, the distances are equal and our rule chooses A.

    We therefore obtain A = {a, b}, with masses of 1 and 2 kg, and B = {c, d, e}, with masses of 3, 8 and 10 kg. This accounts for all 5 parcels without duplication.

    To check the initial cost, the differences from the centre of A are 0 and 1 kg. For B, they are 0, 5 and 7 kg. Their squares are respectively 0, 1, 0, 25 and 49 kg squared.

    Jdépart=0+1+0+25+49=75

    The unit of this sum is kg squared. The contributions from parcels d and e already total 74 kg squared; their large distances explain most of the starting cost.

  3. Move the centres to the means

    We keep the assignments fixed during this calculation. Centre A becomes (1 + 2)/2 = 3/2 = 1.5 kg. Centre B becomes (3 + 8 + 10)/3 = 21/3 = 7 kg. In each case, the denominator is the number of parcels in the group, rather than k.

    Calculate J with these new centres and the old groups. In A, the differences have opposite signs and their squares are each 0.25 kg squared, giving 0.5 kg squared. In B, the differences are -4, 1 and 3 kg; their squares are 16, 1 and 9 kg squared. Their sum is 26 kg squared.

    The new cost is 0.5 + 26 = 26.5 kg squared, less than the starting cost of 75 kg squared. We have improved the centres for this partition; the assignments still need to be reviewed.

  4. Reassign before recalculating

    The new midpoint is (1.5 + 7)/2 = 8.5/2 = 4.25 kg. Parcel c, of 3 kg, is now on the A side. Check this directly: its difference from A is 1.5 kg and its difference from B is -4 kg; the squares are 2.25 and 16 kg squared. It joins A because 2.25 is less than 16.

    The other parcels stay in their groups. We have A = {a, b, c} and B = {d, e}. With the centres still at 1.5 and 7 kg, the cost for A is 0.5 + 2.25 = 2.75 kg squared; the cost for B is 10 kg squared. Thus J = 12.75 kg squared. Reassignment alone has already decreased the cost.

  5. Recalculate the means and check the cost

    The new centre A is (1 + 2 + 3)/3 = 6/3 = 2 kg. The new centre B is (8 + 10)/2 = 18/2 = 9 kg.

    In A, the differences from 2 kg are equal and opposite for a and c and 0 for b. The squares are 1, 0 and 1, giving 2 kg squared. In B, the differences from 9 kg are also equal and opposite; each square is 1, giving 2 kg squared. Thus J = 2 + 2 = 4 kg squared.

    Independent check of group A: the sum of its masses is 6 kg, exactly 3 × 2 kg. For B, 18 kg = 2 × 9 kg. The centres are therefore indeed the means of the stated groups.

  6. Check that the alternation stops

    The midpoint of the latest centres is (2 + 9)/2 = 11/2 = 5.5 kg. The heaviest parcel in A weighs 3 kg, less than this midpoint; the lightest in B weighs 8 kg, more than the midpoint. No assignment changes in the new comparison.

    The same groups give the same means again. We have a fixed point of the alternation. The cost log, in kg squared, is 75 → 26.5 → 12.75 → 4. Each arrow corresponds to a specific operation; a decrease in J alone is not enough to declare that the procedure has stopped.

  7. Understand why we use the mean

    For a nonempty group G, m denotes its number of parcels, μ their mean and t another possible centre in kg. Decompose each difference xᵢ − t into (xᵢ − μ) + (μ − t). When we expand the squares, the cross term disappears because the sum of xᵢ − μ is 0 kg. This leaves:

    ∑i∈G(xi−t)2=∑i∈G(xi−μ)2+m(t−μ)2

    The added term is nonnegative and vanishes when t = μ. The mean therefore minimizes the cost for this fixed group. Conversely, with centres fixed, choosing the smallest square for each parcel minimizes its contribution. These justifications apply to each operation separately: they explain why J does not increase.

    m is a count with no unit; every term in the identity is in kg squared. If a group becomes empty, its mean does not exist: an implementation must state a rule, for example moving the centre onto a parcel and then reassigning. This case does not occur in our sequence.

  8. Distinguish stability from the quality of the grouping

    The procedure can stabilize at a configuration that is worse than one reached from another initialization. The alternation does not search all partitions simultaneously. Comparing several starting configurations at the same k and with the same geometry may improve the candidate selected, without guaranteeing the global minimum.

    The cost strongly penalizes large differences: a very distant parcel can shift a mean. Elongated groups or groups with different densities may also be poorly represented by this geometry. Our groups A and B are choices of representation; swapping their names leaves the centres, geometric assignments and J unchanged.

    A program using approximate arithmetic must also state a stopping tolerance and an iteration limit. Here we have exact fractions and verify stopping through the assignments. The choice of k, probabilistic mixtures and anomaly detection belong to later units.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The centre must be one of the parcels.”

    The centre represents the masses by their mean. The first updated centre A is 1.5 kg, although no parcel has that mass. Review the identity involving squares to understand why this centre minimizes the cost of its group.

    Review the mean and its justification

  • “I have calculated the means: I am done.”

    The new centres move the boundary to 4.25 kg: the parcel of 3 kg must then change groups. Reassign, recalculate the means and check a new assignment before declaring a fixed point.

    Review reassignment and then the stopping check

  • “A smaller cost proves that the groups are better in the real world.”

    J depends on the masses, their scale and k. Even for a fixed problem, stability does not prove a global minimum. The exercise on changing the scale shows that the number J can change without changing the groups.

    Distinguish a change in cost from a change in geometry

Exercise 3 · Reasoned transfer

Double all the masses

Consider a variant of the same batch: each mass and each initial centre are multiplied by 2, still in kg, with the same k and the same rule for ties. Predict the assignments, final centres and final cost without repeating the whole alternation. Does a larger cost mean that the algorithm has grouped the parcels less well?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write the difference between a doubled mass and a doubled centre. What happens to its square?

Hint 2

The mean of the doubled masses is twice the old mean; check that this relationship holds through every operation.

Compare with the solution

Each difference is multiplied by 2, so each square is multiplied by 2 × 2 = 4. The distance comparisons and ties stay the same: the successive groups contain the same parcels.

Each mean also doubles. The final centres become 2 × 2 = 4 kg and 2 × 9 = 18 kg. The final cost becomes 4 × 4 = 16 kg squared.

J has changed because the scale of the data has changed. The groups correspond to exactly the same parcel indices. Comparing these raw costs as a ranking of quality would be unjustified. This invariance applies to multiplication by a common positive factor; changing a single coordinate in a problem with multiple coordinates does not have the same property.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify why the assignments are preserved, including ties.
  • Double the means and multiply the cost by the square of the factor.
  • Give the centres and cost with their units.
  • Reject a conclusion about quality based on costs at different scales.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What stays fixed during assignment, and what do we choose?

We keep the centres fixed and assign each observation to the centre with the smallest squared Euclidean distance. An explicit rule resolves ties.

Recall 2Why replace the centre of a nonempty group with its mean?

For a fixed group, the cost at another centre t is the cost at the mean μ plus the number of observations multiplied by the square of t − μ. The added term is nonnegative and vanishes at the mean.

Recall 3What check justifies stopping in our exact calculation?

After updating the means, a new assignment gives the same groups. The means will then stay the same. A decrease in cost alone does not prove this stability.

Recall 4What does a fixed point of k-means fail to prove?

It certifies neither the global minimum of the cost nor the existence of real categories. The result depends on the number of groups, the geometry and the starting configuration; the group names are arbitrary.

Recall 5If all masses and all starting centres double, what happens to the groups and the cost?

The same parcels stay together and the centres double. The squared differences, and therefore the total cost, are multiplied by 4. This numerical change does not measure a deterioration in the grouping.
