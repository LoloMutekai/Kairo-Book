Triangles

Your goalFind the missing angle of a triangular shelf bracket and reject an assembly whose angles cannot form a closed triangle.

Before you start
c004-m5 : rational equations · c005-m1 : angles

✿01 See the idea

On a drawing of a shelf, you are planning a triangular bracket: a base and arms that rise from its ends to meet. Here, bracket means this support; we do not yet require it to have a right angle. Lengthening the arms will not make them meet for every possible combination of angles.

We replace the parts with straight line segments, lines bounded by their endpoints. A triangle that is not flattened has 3 distinct vertices that do not lie on the same straight line, joined by 3 sides. At each vertex, the interior angle is the opening inside the figure between the sides that meet there.

Call the endpoints of the base A and B, and the point where the arms meet above it C. The Greek letters α (alpha), β (beta) and γ (gamma) stand for the measures of the angles at A, B and C, in degrees, written °. The given angles are α = 40° and β = 60°. Before calculating, imagine how much room is left for γ.

We work in a Euclidean plane: a flat surface where straight lines and parallel lines have their usual meaning. Parallel lines keep the same direction and do not intersect. In this setting, the interior angles of a triangle together fill a half turn, or 180°. This rule does not apply to a triangle drawn on a curved surface.

The angle measurements are fictional and chosen for calculation with pencil and paper. The base has a positive length, but no particular length is required. We assume that the arms can be adjusted until they meet; no lengths fixed in advance add further constraints. We study whether the drawing can form a closed triangle, without calculating the strength of the shelf.

The rule to remember

In the plane, add the given angles and subtract their sum from 180°. To form a closed triangle that is not flattened, every angle must be strictly greater than 0°; a remainder of 0 or a negative remainder means the design must be rejected.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Complete the bracket with the given angles

Base AB has been drawn. At A, the arm makes α = 40° with the base directed towards B; at B, it makes β = 60° with the base directed towards A. The arms rise on the same side of the base. Let us calculate the interior angle γ where they meet, then check whether this meeting forms a triangle that is not flattened.

  1. Understand where the sum comes from

    A full turn is 360°. Dividing it into 2 equal parts gives the straight angle: 360° ÷ 2 = 180°. This differs from the right angle, which is 360° ÷ 4 = 90°.

    Look at the parallel line in the second diagram. Arm AC crosses lines with the same direction: its inclination to these lines is the same. Angle α therefore also appears below the parallel line, to the left of C. In the same way, β appears on the right. Between them is γ, the interior angle at the top vertex. Without gaps or overlap, these openings fill exactly the space below the line.

    α+β+γ=180°

  2. Add the part already occupied

    The given angles occupy part of this sum. We call that part S; S is an angle measurement, in degrees. Adding them tells us how much space is already taken before we look for what is missing.

    S=α+β=40°+60°=100°

  3. Subtract this part from the straight angle

    Call the remainder of the calculation R, also in degrees. We subtract S from the target sum. We call it angle γ only after checking that a triangle is possible.

    R=180°−S=180°−100°=80°

    The subtraction finds what is needed to complete the straight angle. Subtracting from 90° would find something else and would not respect the angle sum of the triangle.

  4. Check that the remainder is a usable angle

    The values α = 40° and β = 60° are positive, and their sum 100° is less than 180°. The remainder R = 80° is therefore strictly positive. With adjustable arms, the given directions can meet away from the base: we can assign γ = 80° to the top vertex.

    The general test requires positive given angles and a sum strictly less than 180°. If the sum reaches this limit, the remaining opening is 0; if it exceeds the limit, the remainder is negative. In either case, no triangle that is not flattened can satisfy the given data. A negative remainder does not become an interior angle by changing its sign.

  5. Rebuild the sum and conclude

    Arithmetic check: combine the result with the given data in a different order. 40° + 80° = 120°, then 120° + 60° = 180°. We recover the straight angle.

    Another calculation method: 180° − 60° leaves 120° for α and γ together; then subtracting α = 40° gives γ = 80° again. On paper, a protractor can give an approximate check of the drawn angles. This approximate drawing accompanies the exact data, without replacing the checks of their sum and positivity.

    The angle plan is therefore compatible. We have determined neither the lengths of the arms nor a load the shelf can support: those questions need other data and other tools.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The triangle completes a right angle.”

    The right angle is 90°, but the interior angles add up to 180°. Return to the parallel line through the top vertex: a whole half turn is divided up, rather than a quarter turn.

    Recover the sum using the parallel line

  • “Equal base angles make all the angles equal.”

    Equality of the given angles does not make their measure equal to the angle at the top vertex. With 65° and 65°, their sum is 130° and 50° remains. Calculate the last angle before concluding that they are all equal.

    Return to the drawing with equal angles

  • “If I recover the correct sum, I can always close the drawing.”

    The sum alone can accept even a remainder of 0 or a negative remainder. You must also check that all angles are strictly greater than 0°. Adding a closing line to incompatible arms would represent angles different from those required.

    Return to the positivity check

Change an angle of the bracket

Hint 1

Start with the space already occupied: add the given angles.

Hint 2

Subtract this sum from 180°, then check that every angle is positive.

Compare with the solution

The occupied space is 50° + 60° = 110°. The remainder is 180° − 110° = 70°. The given angles and this remainder are positive: γ is therefore 70°.

Check: 110° + 70° = 180°. A closed triangle is compatible with these directions and adjustable arms.

  • I state γ = 70° and check the sum 180°.

Exercise 2 · Drawing and justification

Equal base angles, a different angle at the top

Another plan of the bracket requires 65° at each end of the base. Before drawing, decide whether the angle at the top vertex can also measure 65°. Calculate its actual measure, then sketch the arms on the same side of the base and mark the interior angles.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the equal angles as two separate measurements.

Hint 2

The angle at the top vertex must complete 180°. Then check whether it has the same measure as the base angles.

Compare with the solution

The base angles occupy 65° + 65° = 130°. This leaves 180° − 130° = 50° at the top vertex. That angle therefore does not measure 65°.

The check gives 130° + 50° = 180°. The angles are positive; the arms can meet away from the base. The sketch places the equal angles at the ends of the base and 50° at the top vertex, without requiring all the angles to be identical.

Check your reasoning

  • I find 50° and justify why it is not 65°.
  • I mark the angles on the inside of the sketch.
  • I distinguish an approximate sketch from the exact check of the sum and positivity.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

A correct calculation that must lead to rejection

For the same bracket, one plan requires 90° and 90° at the base; another requires 120° and 90°. Calculate the remainders. Someone says: “We can keep the sum and still draw a closed triangle.” Answer them for each plan and describe what must remain on the rejected drawing.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate the sum of the base angles for each plan before finding the remainder.

Hint 2

A remainder of 0 leaves no opening at the top vertex. A negative remainder is not an interior angle of a triangle.

Compare with the solution

First plan: 90° + 90° = 180°, then 180° − 180° = 0°. The arms perpendicular to the same base and directed to the same side are parallel: they do not meet. The calculation gives a remainder of 0, rather than a triangle that is not flattened.

Other plan: 120° + 90° = 210°, then 180° − 210° = -30°. The given sum exceeds the straight angle: the negative remainder signals incompatibility. Replacing it with a positive opening would change the data.

In both cases, keep the base and the initial directions without inventing a meeting point. Write “assembly impossible for a triangle that is not flattened”. Formally rebuilding 180° with a remainder of 0 or a negative remainder does not resolve the violation of positivity.

Check your reasoning

  • I find the remainders 0° and -30°.
  • I reject the plans and explain which condition is violated.
  • I do not call the negative remainder an angle γ, and I do not draw a fictitious closure.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you identify the vertices and interior angles of the triangular bracket?

The vertices are where the sides meet: A, B and C, distinct and not on the same straight line. Each interior angle is the opening inside the figure between the sides that start at its vertex; all must be positive.

Recall 2What sum must be completed, and how does the parallel line through the top vertex explain it?

In the Euclidean plane, the sum is 180°. The line through C parallel to AB reproduces the base angles on either side of γ; these openings fill a half turn.

Recall 3Without reciting the result for the bracket, how do you find the angle at the top vertex from α and β?

Add α and β, then subtract this sum from 180°. Check that the given angles and the remainder are positive before naming this remainder γ. Then rebuild the sum.

Recall 4Why is calculating the remainder not enough to prove that the triangle can close?

A calculation can produce 0° or a negative number, whereas a triangle that is not flattened requires positive angles. You must reject these inputs and keep the directions without inventing a meeting point.

Recall 5Under which assumptions does a positive remainder allow us to accept the plan here?

On a base of positive length in the Euclidean plane, with positive base angles whose sum is less than 180°, adjustable arms rise on the same side and meet. This gives neither their lengths nor the strength of the bracket.
