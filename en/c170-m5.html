Level 3 · C170·M5

Strong Markov property

Your goalJustify a Brownian restart at a stopping time, calculate the distribution after this restart, and identify a selection rule that looks into the future.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A particle moves along a very long tube. We start a stopwatch when it first hits the mark a = 6 mm. This first hit does not occur at a time fixed in advance: its timing depends on the movements already made. Yet at that very moment, we know that the hit has just occurred, without looking at what follows.

Can we then treat the future displacement like that of a particle starting again from the origin? The strong Markov property says yes for the Brownian model and a stopping rule that does not look into the future. It restarts the distribution of the displacement, without replaying or erasing the past path.

We must shift both reference points: reset the stopwatch to its origin and subtract the position at the hit. The absolute position remains anchored at the 6 mm mark; only the displacement after the hit starts again from 0 mm. Its uncertainty will depend on the time elapsed since this hit.

The tube, parameters, and values are fictitious. We assume an ideal Brownian model with no walls or drift, observed continuously. The sketch connects a few points to show the division; it is neither a measured path nor a faithful simulation of Brownian oscillations.

The rule to remember

At an almost surely finite stopping time, the future Brownian increments have the distribution of a new Brownian motion and are independent of all the information available at that stopping time.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Predict the position after the first hit

The particle starts from 0 mm and follows Xₜ = σBₜ, with σ = 2 mm/√s. After it first hits a = 6 mm, we wait h = 9 s. Let us find the distribution of its position at this horizon, its mean, its variance, and its moment of order 2. No particular duration is assigned to the wait before the hit.

  1. Specify the model and the permitted information

    t and s denote times in seconds, with 0 ≤ s < t. B is a standard Brownian motion: B at the origin equals 0, its paths are continuous, and its increments over disjoint intervals are independent. The increment Bt − Bs has a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance t − s. Under our physical convention, B is expressed in √s and its variance in seconds; σ converts these fluctuations into millimetres.

    ℱt denotes all the information from X up to t. We use its completed, right-continuous natural filtration: we add events of probability 0 and, at t, retain the information common to all times r strictly later than t. This convention does not add knowledge of a future position. The Brownian motion retains the independence of its future increments from ℱt.

    P denotes probability, E expectation, and Var variance. The symbol | means “given the information that follows”. N(m, v) denotes a normal distribution with mean m and variance v, rather than standard deviation v. For X, m is in mm and v in mm².

    Xt=σBt,Var(Xt−Xs)=σ2(t−s)

  2. Check that the hit defines a stopping time

    a is the positive mark, in mm; T is the first time X reaches a, in seconds. The infimum denotes the earliest hitting time, with the value ∞ if there is no hit. For each t, knowing whether T ≤ t requires only checking whether the maximum of X between the start and t has reached a. This information belongs to ℱt: T is a stopping time.

    T=inf{t≥0:Xt=a}

    Continuity ensures that X at time T equals a: the particle does not jump over the mark. Reflection, studied in the previous unit, gives P(T < ∞) = 1 here. We use this almost sure finiteness, meaning finiteness with probability 1; it does not mean that the hit must occur before a specified deadline.

    ℱ at time T is the information available at the stopping time. More precisely, an event A belongs to it if A ∩ {T ≤ t} belongs to ℱt for every t ≥ 0. In particular, it contains T, the position at the hit, and the path observed before it.

  3. Apply the strong property to the right object

    For u ≥ 0 in seconds, set Yᵤ = X at time T + u, minus X at time T. Y is in mm. The strong property says that the entire process Y has the same distribution as X starting from 0, and is independent of ℱ at time T. This is equality in distribution: the new fluctuations are not a copy of the past.

    Yu=XT+u−XT,u≥0

    The ordinary version allows this restart at a time fixed in advance. The strong version allows it at the random time T because T is an almost surely finite stopping time. Simply replacing the letter t with T in a formula for a deterministic time is not a proof.

    Yh|ℱT∼N(0,σ2h)

    The symbol ∼ means “has the distribution”. Here h is a strictly positive deterministic duration. This conditional distribution is the same whatever the stopped past, up to a set of probability 0. The absolute position is a + Yh; it therefore retains the spatial shift a.

  4. Understand the passage from a grid to the hitting time

    Here is how the proof works. n is a nonnegative integer with no unit; δn is a time step and Tn is the grid time immediately after T, or equal to T if T is already on the grid. The ceiling ⌈x⌉ is the smallest integer greater than or equal to x. Choose δn = (1 s)/2ⁿ, then Tn = δn⌈T/δn⌉. Tn is a stopping time taking discrete values, T ≤ Tn < T + δn, and Tn decreases to T.

    At each time kδn, where k is a nonnegative integer, the event {Tn = kδn} is known at that time. We can therefore use independence of increments at this deterministic time, then sum over the grid times. Let Z be a bounded variable with no unit, known in ℱ at time T, and g a bounded continuous function from a displacement in mm to a number with no unit. Since ℱ at time T is contained in ℱ at time Tn, this sum gives the following equality.

    E[Zg(XTn+h−XTn)]=E[Z]E[g(Xh)]

    Continuity of the paths makes the grid displacement converge to Yh. The bounds on Z and g allow us to pass to the limit in the expectation: this is bounded convergence. We obtain E[Zg(Yh)] = E[Z]E[g(Xh)], which identifies the distribution and its independence from the past. The same reasoning for a finite list of durations gives the joint distribution of the increments; together with continuity, it establishes the result for the entire process. A simple substitution therefore does not justify switching to a random clock.

  5. Calculate the distribution and its units

    The variance rate is the square of σ: 2 × 2 = 4 mm²/s. Multiplying by the time elapsed after T gives the displacement variance: 4 × 9 = 36 mm². Its mean is 0 mm, because the model has no drift.

    XT+h|ℱT∼N(a,σ2h)

    Thus the conditional mean of the position is a + 0 = 6 mm and its conditional variance is 36 mm². These same moments hold without conditioning: the starting position at T is always a and the distribution of the new displacement does not depend on ℱ at time T.

    The standard deviation is σ√h = 2 × 3 = 6 mm, since √(9 s) = 3 √s. Check: 6 × 6 = 36 mm², exactly the variance. The normal distribution extends over the entire real line; the mark is not a reflecting wall.

  6. Separate variance from the moment of order 2

    Variance is the mean of the squared deviation from the mean. The moment of order 2 is the mean of the squared absolute position. Since X at time T + h = a + Yh, its square contains the square of a, the term 2aYh, and the square of Yh. The middle term has expectation 0 because E[Yh | ℱ at time T] = 0 mm.

    E[XT+h2|ℱT]=a2+σ2h

    Numerically: the square of a is 6 × 6 = 36 mm²; the moment is 36 + 36 = 72 mm². Check using the identity relating variance and moments: subtracting the square of the mean from the mean of the square gives the variance again, namely 72 − 6 × 6 = 36 mm².

  7. Check symmetry and the addition of variances

    Yh has a normal distribution with mean 0 and strictly positive variance. It assigns no probability mass to 0 displacement and places equal probability on each side. Thus P(X at time T + h > a | ℱ at time T) = 1/2 = 0.50, with no unit. The lower side has the same probability: 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. Reaching the mark from below does not favour an upward next displacement in this model.

    Another check: split h into 2 s followed by 7 s. The new process has independent increments. Their variances are 8 mm² and 28 mm²; they add up to 36 mm², as in the direct calculation. Without independence, we would also need to examine covariance, which measures the joint variation of the two increments.

    Finally, for h = 0 s, the displacement is 0 and its variance becomes 0 mm²: the position is exactly a at the hit. The probability of being strictly above a is then 0, rather than 1/2. The preceding symmetry argument did require strictly positive h.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results. Explain why the first hit is a stopping time, what becomes independent of the past, and why the mean position remains at the mark. Then recover the variance, standard deviation, and mean of the square without confusing them.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Any time chosen along a path allows a fresh start.”

    Check whether the decision that stopping has occurred uses only the past. A time chosen after seeing the next movement selects that movement. The last exercise shows such a rule and the bias it produces.

    Examine the rule that looks into the future

  • “After the hit, the position has mean 0.”

    Y, the displacement obtained by subtracting X at time T, is what starts again from 0 mm. The absolute position is a + Y and its mean is 6 mm in the example. Revisit the shift of both origins in the drawing.

    Review position and displacement

  • “The time taken to reach the mark is added to h in the future variance.”

    At the stopping time, the position a is known. The conditional distribution of the new displacement depends only on the duration h after the hit: its variance is the square of σ multiplied by h. Adding the duration T would reintroduce uncertainty from a past already observed. The standard deviation is σ√h; it does not have the same unit as variance.

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Wait less time after the same hit

The same particle still reaches 6 mm, but we wait only 4 s after T. Give the mean position, its variance, its standard deviation, and the probability of being above the mark. Justify each unit.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The position at the hit is known. Start again from a + Yₕ with Yₕ having mean 0.

Hint 2

The variance rate remains 4 mm²/s. To obtain the standard deviation, take the square root of the variance.

Compare with the solution

The mean remains 6 mm: changing the duration creates no drift. The variance is 4 × 4 = 16 mm². The standard deviation is 2 × 2 = 4 mm; squaring it recovers 16 mm².

The duration is strictly positive and the distribution is centred on the mark: the probability of being above it is 1/2 = 0.50. A smaller variance narrows the distribution; it does not move its centre or change this probability of being on one side.

Check your reasoning

  • You keep the mean 6 mm and find 16 mm².
  • You check the standard deviation 4 mm by squaring it.
  • You relate 1/2 to symmetry and the positive duration, without treating it as a probability of reaching the mark.

Exercise 2 · Transfer within the same scene

Move the mark without changing the noise

We now place the mark at 8 mm and denote its first hitting time by U. The coefficient σ remains 2 mm/√s; we wait 9 s after U. Calculate the mean, variance, and moment of order 2 of the position, given the entire past at U. Does a more distant mark increase the variance of this future displacement?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

U has the same justification as T for being an almost surely finite stopping time. The position at the hit changes, but the variance rate stays the same.

Hint 2

Expand the square of the sum of the new mark and the displacement with mean 0. The cross term has mean 0.

Compare with the solution

The conditional distribution of the position is normal with mean 8 mm and variance 36 mm². The displacement variance does not change: the square of σ multiplied by h still gives 4 × 9 = 36 mm².

The square of the new mark is 8 × 8 = 64 mm². The mean of the square is 64 + 36 = 100 mm². Check: 100 − 8 × 8 = 36 mm². The moment increases with the shift of the mark; the conditional variance around this mark stays the same. This does not compare the first hitting times.

Check your reasoning

  • You justify U before using the strong Markov property.
  • You distinguish the mean 8 mm, the variance 36 mm², and the moment 100 mm².
  • You explain why moving the mark farther away changes the centre and the mean of the square without changing the spread after the hit.

Exercise 3 · Objection and counterexample

A rule that already knows the next movement

Still considering the particle starting from 0 mm, define S as follows: S = 0 s if its position at 1 s is strictly positive; otherwise S = 1 s. Can we invoke the strong Markov property at S? On the event S = 0 s, what is the probability that the displacement between S and S + 1 s is positive? Compare it with 1/2.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At the start, can you decide whether S already equals 0 s without reading the position at 1 s?

Hint 2

On the event S = 0 s, the requested displacement is exactly the position used to select this event.

Compare with the solution

The event {S ≤ 0 s} is {X at 1 s > 0 mm}. This event has probability 1/2, because the position has a nondegenerate normal distribution with mean 0. The increment between the start and 1 s is independent of the initial information. If this event were already known at the start, it would therefore be independent of itself: its probability would have to equal its square, which requires 0 or 1. The value 1/2 rules out this possibility. S is therefore not a stopping time.

On {S = 0 s}, the displacement between S and S + 1 s is X at 1 s minus X at the start, so it is strictly positive by construction. Its conditional probability is 1, rather than 1/2. The selection has already imposed the sign of the future; finiteness of S is not enough to allow a restart under the strong property. We do not use “information at the stopping time”, defined for a true stopping time, to conceal this anticipation.

Check your reasoning

  • You examine the event {S ≤ 0 s} and identify the future information required.
  • You obtain the conditional probability 1 from the selection rule, without applying the theorem outside its assumptions.
  • You distinguish a finite random time from a stopping time adapted to the stated filtration.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why is the first hit at the mark a stopping time?

At any time t, knowing whether the hit has already occurred requires only the maximum of the path observed up to t. No future information is needed. Continuity gives the exact position a at the hit.

Recall 2What does the strong Markov property add to restarting at a fixed time?

It allows a restart at the random time T, provided T is an almost surely finite stopping time for the stated Brownian filtration. All future increments have the distribution of a new Brownian motion, independently of the information at T.

Recall 3What starts again from the origin, and what stays at the mark?

The displacement Yᵤ = X at time T + u minus X at time T starts again from 0 mm. The absolute position is a + Yᵤ. After h seconds, its mean is a and its variance is the square of σ multiplied by h, in mm².

Recall 4Why approximate T by the grid times that follow it?

These times remain stopping times taking discrete values. We decompose according to each deterministic time, apply independence of increments, then pass to T using continuity of the paths and bounded convergence for the test functions.

Recall 5What should you check before restarting at a time chosen from a recording?

Check that the event “the chosen time has already passed” can be decided using only the past. A rule that looks at the next movement can select its sign; a finite random time is not automatically a stopping time.
