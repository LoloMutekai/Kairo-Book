Level 1 · C057·M5

Hessian

Your goalConstruct the Hessian of a position function and check how it transforms a displacement into a change in the gradient.

Before you start
c033-m5 : affine approximation · c039-m5 : changes of basis · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c057-m1 : partial derivatives · c057-m2 : differential · c057-m3 : gradient · c057-m4 : Jacobian

✿01 See the idea

A drawing head moves over a table. A model assigns it a position penalty: the faster this penalty changes as the head moves, the steeper the slope. The gradient brings together the slope with respect to x, holding y fixed, and the slope with respect to y, holding x fixed. These slopes also change as the head moves.

Imagine a small gradient arrow attached to each position. The Hessian describes how this arrow changes around a point: which component of the gradient responds, and to which displacement? It therefore brings together derivatives of slopes. Here we call this change in slopes curvature, without giving it the geometric meaning of the curvature of a path.

x and y are the coordinates of the head, in centimetres, on perpendicular axes with the same scale. P is the starting point and Q is the endpoint. The displacement h has a component hx = 0.10 cm along x and a component hᵧ = 0.20 cm along y. The penalty f is expressed in points, a score unit invented for this exercise.

The entire scene and its coefficients are fictional. We follow only the geometry of a penalty model; no force, machine fault or actual gain in accuracy is measured. Read the table in the second diagram by slope row and displacement column. Its calculation follows next.

The rule to remember

The gradient describes the slopes at the chosen point; the Hessian describes how these slopes vary. A cross entry links displacement along an axis to the slope along the other axis.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From displacement of the head to a change in its slopes

Let us construct the Hessian of the penalty model, then start at P = (1 ; 1) cm with h = (0.10 ; 0.20) cm. The aim is to predict the change in the gradient and check it by a direct calculation at the endpoint.

  1. State the model, units and smoothness assumptions

    f(x,y)=ax2+bxy+cy2

    The constant coefficients are a = 1, b = 1 and c = 2, each in points/cm². Each term is therefore in points: a coefficient multiplied by a squared length. In numerical calculations, we write the values of x and y in cm and of f in points.

    This function is a polynomial, defined for all real positions. Its second derivatives exist and are continuous. For the general properties used here, we require this continuity in a neighbourhood of the point being studied, meaning a small region around that point.

  2. Differentiate once to recover the gradient

    Let fx denote the derivative of f with respect to x, holding y fixed, and fᵧ its derivative with respect to y, holding x fixed. The symbol ∇f denotes the gradient, written as a column in the order x then y. Its components are in points/cm.

    fx=2ax+by;fy=bx+2cy

    Why? For fx, differentiating a times x squared gives 2ax; the term bxy gives by, since y stays fixed; the term c times y squared gives 0. For fᵧ, a times x squared gives 0, bxy gives bx and c times y squared gives 2cy.

    At the starting point P: fx(P) = 2 × 1 + 1 × 1 = 2 + 1 = 3 points/cm. Similarly, fᵧ(P) = 1 × 1 + 4 × 1 = 1 + 4 = 5 points/cm. These numbers describe the current slopes, before considering how they change.

  3. Organise the derivatives of the slopes

    H is the Hessian of f: the Jacobian of the vector-valued function ∇f. It has 2 rows and 2 columns. A row corresponds to the component of the gradient being differentiated; a column corresponds to the coordinate being varied. All entries are evaluated at the same point.

    H=J(∇f)=[∂fx∂x∂fx∂y∂fy∂x∂fy∂y]=[fxxfxyfyxfyy]

    J denotes the Jacobian here. In our convention, fxᵧ means differentiating fx with respect to y: the rightmost subscript indicates the last differentiation. The entries fxx and fᵧᵧ are the pure second derivatives; the others are the mixed second derivatives. Their units are points/cm², since we differentiate a slope in points/cm with respect to a length in cm.

  4. Calculate the row for the slope along x

    Start again from fx = 2ax + by. With respect to x, 2ax gives 2a and by gives 0: fxx = 2 × 1 = 2 points/cm². With respect to y, 2ax gives 0 and by gives b: fxᵧ = 1 points/cm².

    The xy term has not disappeared: it produces the cross entry 1. Thus, the slope along x can change when moving only along y. We have obtained the first row; the other component of the gradient still needs to be differentiated.

  5. Complete the row for the slope along y and check symmetry

    The slope fᵧ is bx + 2cy. With respect to x, bx gives b and 2cy gives 0: fᵧx = 1 points/cm². With respect to y, bx gives 0 and 2cy gives 2c: fᵧᵧ = 2 × 2 = 4 points/cm².

    H(x,y)=[2114] points/cm²

    Check: both mixed entries equal 1. Under the assumption that the second derivatives are continuous in a neighbourhood, they are equal: this is Schwarz's theorem. The matrix is then symmetric, meaning that entries opposite each other across the diagonal are equal. The mere existence of second derivatives at the point is not enough to invoke this guarantee.

    Here H depends on neither x nor y: the gradient is affine and its changes have constant coefficients. This constancy is a property of the chosen polynomial; the Hessian of another penalty could vary from point to point.

  6. Apply the Hessian to the displacement

    h is a column of lengths. H(P)h is therefore a column of changes in slopes. In general, for a small displacement and under our assumptions, ∇f(P + h) ≈ ∇f(P) + H(P)h: this is the affine approximation of the gradient. The sign ≈ indicates an approximation. For our quadratic polynomial, this relation is exact because its gradient is affine.

    Calculate each row of the matrix product: multiply each entry by the corresponding displacement component, then add. Δgx and Δgᵧ denote changes in the components of the gradient; Δ means “final value minus initial value”.

    Δgx=2×0.10+1×0.20=0.20+0.20=0.40 points/cm
    Δgy=1×0.10+4×0.20=0.10+0.80=0.90 points/cm

    The new gradient therefore has components 3 + 0.40 = 3.40 and 5 + 0.90 = 5.90, in points/cm. Check the units: points/cm² multiplied by cm gives points/cm, rather than penalty points.

  7. Recalculate the gradient at the endpoint

    The final coordinates are x = 1 + 0.10 = 1.10 cm and y = 1 + 0.20 = 1.20 cm. Call this point Q. We can calculate the slopes there directly, without using the product Hh.

    fx(Q) = 2 × 1.10 + 1 × 1.20 = 3.40 points/cm; fᵧ(Q) = 1 × 1.10 + 4 × 1.20 = 5.90 points/cm. Subtracting the initial values gives 3.40 − 3 = 0.40 and 5.90 − 5 = 0.90 points/cm.

    The 2 methods agree exactly. The displacement h is in cm, the gradient has 2 components in points/cm and H has 2 × 2 entries in points/cm². These checks verify our calculation and the interpretation of the cross entries; they do not validate a penalty for a real machine.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the values. Draw the rows and columns of H, recover each derivative from f, then explain why displacement along y contributes to the change in the slope along x. Where did we use the quadratic form to replace an approximation with an equality?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The gradient is (3 ; 5), so these are the numbers to put in the Hessian.”

    These numbers are the initial slopes, in points/cm. To construct H, differentiate each slope as a function of x and y before substituting the coordinate values. The entries of H are in points/cm² and form a 2 × 2 table.

    Revisit the organisation of the derivatives of the slopes

  • “I keep only the derivatives along the same axis.”

    The term bxy makes the slope along x depend on y and the slope along y depend on x. It gives the cross entries 1 and 1. Omitting them would lose 0.20 point/cm from the change in the slope along x for the displacement in the example.

    Reread the table by rows and columns

  • “I can always interchange the order of differentiation.”

    The theorem used here requires continuous second derivatives in a neighbourhood. Our polynomial satisfies this assumption. Without this guarantee, the function must be examined; imposing symmetry on the table does not replace a check.

Hint 1

For column x, y stays fixed; for column y, x stays fixed.

Hint 2

The second row describes the same slope fᵧ, but differentiated with respect to two different coordinates.

Compare with the solution

With respect to x, the derivative of bx is b and that of 2cy is 0: fᵧx = 1 points/cm². With respect to y, bx gives 0 and 2cy gives 2c = 4 points/cm². The row is therefore [1 ; 4].

The entry fᵧx indicates how much the slope along y changes per cm of displacement along x. We do not differentiate the numerical value 5: we differentiate the expression for the slope before evaluating it.

Check your reasoning

  • The columns follow x then y: the row is [1 ; 4].
  • Each result is justified by differentiation, with the other coordinate held fixed.
  • The cross entry and the unit points/cm² are explained.

Move only along x

Start again from P = (1 ; 1) cm with the same model. This time, the displacement is (0.10 ; 0) cm. Calculate the change in each slope, then the gradient at the endpoint. Check by substitution into fₓ and fᵧ. Does the slope along y stay unchanged?

The vertical component of the displacement is 0: only column x of H contributes.

Hint 2

Do not confuse “y does not change” with “fᵧ does not change”. Look at the term bx in fᵧ.

Compare with the solution

Multiplication by the displacement column gives Δgx = 2 × 0.10 + 1 × 0 = 0.20 point/cm, and Δgᵧ = 1 × 0.10 + 4 × 0 = 0.10 point/cm. The slope along y increases even though the coordinate y stays fixed.

The gradient changes from (3 ; 5) to (3.20 ; 5.10), in points/cm. At the endpoint (1.10 ; 1) cm, the direct check gives fx = 2 × 1.10 + 1 × 1 = 3.20, and fᵧ = 1 × 1.10 + 4 × 1 = 5.10 points/cm. The cross term is indeed necessary.

Check your reasoning

  • Column x contributes to both components of the gradient.
  • The changes are distinguished from the gradient values at the endpoint.
  • The direct check confirms both results and their units.

Exercise 3 · Reconstruction · examine incomplete evidence

0 slopes with a nonzero change

Now place the head at O = (0 ; 0) cm, the origin of the same table. Someone says: “The gradient is 0, so the Hessian is too.” Calculate the gradient at O, then at (0 ; 0.20) cm. Divide each change in slope by this vertical displacement. Which column of H do you recover? Is this trial alone enough to determine every entry?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Substitute the coordinates into the expressions fₓ = 2ax + by and fᵧ = bx + 2cy.

Hint 2

A displacement along y probes only column y. Consider what it tells you about fₓₓ.

Compare with the solution

O denotes the origin. The gradient there is (0 ; 0) points/cm. At the point (0 ; 0.20) cm, fx is 1 × 0.20 = 0.20 point/cm and fᵧ is 4 × 0.20 = 0.80 point/cm. The slopes therefore do not stay 0 around O.

The changes divided by 0.20 cm are 0.20 ÷ 0.20 = 1 and 0.80 ÷ 0.20 = 4, in points/cm². This recovers column y: [1 ; 4]. These quotients give this column exactly because the gradient of our model is affine.

A single vertical displacement does not determine fxx. Even with symmetry, this entry still needs to be calculated separately: differentiating 2ax + by with respect to x gives 2 points/cm². The Hessian at O is therefore the same as in the example, despite the 0 gradient. A 0 slope at a point does not mean that this slope stays constant around it.

Check your reasoning

  • A 0 initial gradient is distinguished from a 0 Hessian.
  • The quotients recover column y, with the correct units.
  • The entry fₓₓ remains undetermined by this trial alone; its analytical calculation is given.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you reconstruct the Hessian from the gradient without reciting its entries?

Write the gradient as a column, then write its Jacobian: each row follows a component of the gradient, each column a coordinate of differentiation. For 2 variables, H has 2 × 2 entries containing second derivatives.

Recall 2What does fₓᵧ mean in the convention of this page, and where does it come from in our model?

It is the derivative of the slope fₓ with respect to y. The term bxy gives by in fₓ, then b in fₓᵧ. The entry describes how displacement along y changes the slope along x.

Recall 3If x and y are in cm and f is in points, what are the units of ∇f, H and Hh?

The components of the gradient are in points/cm; the entries of H are in points/cm²; Hh is in points/cm when h is in cm. Hh describes a change in the gradient.

Recall 4What assumption justifies the equality of the mixed derivatives here?

Continuous second derivatives in a neighbourhood of the point guarantee their equality by Schwarz's theorem. Our polynomial satisfies this condition. Their mere existence at the point does not provide this guarantee.

Recall 5What does ∇f(P) + H(P)h predict, and why is our check exact?

This sum approximates the gradient at position P + h for a small displacement. In our quadratic polynomial, the gradient is affine: the relation is exact. A gradient that is 0 initially can still vary around that point.
