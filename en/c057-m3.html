Level 1 · C057·M3

Gradient

Your goalConstruct the gradient of temperature on a plate, interpret its direction and check the local change it predicts.

Before you start
c033-m5 : affine approximation · c039-m5 : changes of basis · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c057-m1 : partial derivatives · c057-m2 : differential

✿01 See the idea

You move a probe across a plate whose temperature stays steady during the experiment. From the same point, moving to the right may warm the probe differently from moving upwards. To find the direction in which it will warm fastest, you need to combine these slopes rather than choose an axis at random.

The slope along x measures the change in temperature per metre travelled along x, while keeping y fixed. The slope along y reverses these roles. These are the partial derivatives we have already met; we will write them as gx and gᵧ when they are evaluated at the starting point A.

The gradient combines these slopes into a vector. Its arrow indicates the direction of greatest local increase for displacements of equal length. Its length gives the maximum slope in that direction. It does not give the temperature itself or tell you how far to move.

We use perpendicular axes with the same scale in metres, and the usual Euclidean length. These choices are necessary to interpret the components directly as a direction on the plate. The probe is not following a flow of heat: it is only comparing positions.

The plate, its temperature law and all the values are fictional. The law is assumed to be differentiable in an open region around the starting point: it admits a linear approximation for small displacements in all directions. The existence of partial derivatives at a point alone would not guarantee this property.

The rule to remember

Differentiate separately, evaluate at the same point and arrange the slopes in coordinate order; then take the dot product of this vector with the displacement to obtain an approximate scalar change.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Find a direction of movement and check its prediction

On our fictional plate, T(x, y) is the sum of a reference temperature Tᵣ, a times the square of x, and by. The coordinates x and y are in metres; T is in degrees Celsius. We are given Tᵣ = 20 °C, a = 1 °C/m² and b = 3 °C/m. The probe starts at A, with coordinates (2 m ; 1 m). We want to find the gradient at A, then the predicted temperature after a small displacement of length 0.10 m in its direction.

  1. Apply the law at the starting point

    Tᵣ is a reference temperature; a controls the curvature along x and b the constant slope along y. Every term added to Tᵣ is in degrees Celsius. The square of x is a power, not an extra component. This polynomial law is differentiable; the axes and units are those stated in the intuition section.

    T(x,y)=Tr+ax2+by

    At the start, the square of x is 2 × 2 = 4 m². The contributions from a times this square and from by are 4 °C and 3 °C. Thus T(A) = 20 + 4 + 3 = 27 °C. This is a temperature value, distinct from the slopes we are looking for.

  2. Calculate the slopes while holding the other coordinate fixed

    The symbol ∂ indicates a partial derivative: while x varies, y stays fixed. The terms Tᵣ and by are then constant and their derivatives are 0. The derivative of a times the square of x is 2ax. While y varies, x stays fixed: only by contributes, with derivative b.

    ∂T∂x(x,y)=2ax;∂T∂y(x,y)=b

    Evaluate both derivatives at the same point A: gx = 2 × 1 × 2 = 4 °C/m and gᵧ = 3 = 3 °C/m. Changing the evaluation point can change the gradient; this arrow does not apply everywhere.

  3. Construct the vector and recover the differential

    ∇ is read as “nabla”; ∇T(A) is read as “gradient of T at A”. In our orthonormal basis, its components are the partial derivatives along x then y. We write them in a column to keep their order visible.

    ∇T(A)=(gxgy)=(43) °C/m

    Let h be the displacement vector, and hx, hᵧ its components in metres. The differential of T at A is the linear map that turns h into a temperature change. In the formula, the subscript A identifies the starting point; the dot · indicates the dot product, here the sum of products of corresponding components.

    dTA(h)=∇T(A)·h=gxhx+gyhy

    The gradient is therefore a vector of slopes in °C/m; the differential applied to a displacement is a number in °C. The equality above is exact for this linear map. Treating its result as the actual temperature change is the approximation.

  4. Read the maximum slope and its direction

    The norm, written with double bars, is the Euclidean length of the vector. Here it is the square root of the sum of the squares of the components.

    ‖∇T(A)‖=42+32=25=5 °C/m

    To compare directions at equal distance, use a dimensionless direction vector v with norm 1. The slope in this direction is ∇T(A) · v = ‖∇T(A)‖ cos θ, where θ is the angle between the gradient and v. The cosine cannot exceed 1: the slope is therefore greatest when v points in the same direction as the gradient. This property assumes a nonzero gradient and the orthonormal geometry stated earlier.

    Call this direction of maximum slope u: divide the gradient by its norm. Its components are ux = 4/5 = 0.80 and uᵧ = 3/5 = 0.60, with no units. The sum of their squares is 1. The gradient itself is not a unit vector. If it were 0, all directional slopes at first order would be 0; that would not determine whether the point is the hottest.

  5. Choose the distance separately from the direction

    The letter ℓ denotes the chosen length, in metres. Set ℓ = 1/10 m, that is 0.10 m, then h = ℓu. Multiplying the dimensionless direction by ℓ gives hx = 0.10 × 0.80 = 0.08 m and hᵧ = 0.10 × 0.60 = 0.06 m.

    The endpoint A + h therefore has coordinates (2.08 m ; 1.06 m). The displacement does have length ℓ, since ‖ℓu‖ = ℓ for positive ℓ and u with norm 1. Treating the gradient components as metres would confuse slope with distance.

  6. Predict the change by adding the contributions

    The contribution along x is gxhx = 4 × 0.08 = 0.32 °C. The contribution along y is gᵧhᵧ = 3 × 0.06 = 0.18 °C. Each multiplication combines °C/m with metres; we can then add the results.

    T(A+h)−T(A)≈dTA(h)=0.32+0.18=0.50 °C

    The approximate temperature at the endpoint is therefore 27 + 0.50 = 27.50 °C. Check another way: maximum slope × distance = 5 °C/m × 0.10 m = 0.50 °C. This equality checks our linear calculation without making the approximation exact.

  7. Return to the law to measure the difference

    Now calculate the exact model temperature at the endpoint, rather than reusing the differential. The square of 2.08 m is 4.3264 m²; multiplied by a, it contributes 4.3264 °C. The term by is 3.18 °C.

    The exact sum is T(A + h) = 20 + 4.3264 + 3.18 = 27.5064 °C. The exact change is therefore 27.5064 − 27 = 0.5064 °C. It exceeds the linear prediction by 0.0064 °C. Here “exact” means exact under our fictional law, without claiming any instrumental precision.

  8. Explain the remainder and limit the conclusion

    Call the remainder R, in °C: exact change minus linear change. Expanding a times the square of (x + hx), the linear terms give 2axhx; a times the square of hx remains. The term by is affine and leaves no remainder. This gives an independent check of the previous subtraction.

    R=ahx2=1×0.082=0.0064 °C

    The remainder is positive and agrees with 0.5064 − 0.50 = 0.0064 °C. As we shorten the displacement while keeping its direction, the linear term decreases in proportion to its length and this remainder in proportion to its length squared. The gradient therefore describes the steepest slope at the start; it promises neither a linear law over a large distance nor the hottest point on the whole plate.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the solution, distinguish T(A), ∇T(A) and the differential at A applied to h, giving their units. Then explain why we chose a displacement length after choosing the direction, and why checking with the law reveals a remainder.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The gradient is the sum of the partial derivatives.”

    Keep one component per coordinate: (4 ; 3) in °C/m. The weighted sum gₓhₓ + gᵧhᵧ comes afterwards, when a displacement h is given; it produces a scalar in °C.

    Distinguish the vector from the differential

  • “I must move the probe by the gradient components.”

    The gradient components are slopes in °C/m. Divide by its norm to obtain a dimensionless direction, then choose a distance in metres. Keep the same spatial scale on the axes to read this direction.

    Revisit the choice of direction and distance

  • “In the gradient direction, the calculated increase must be exact.”

    The differential predicts 0.50 °C, whereas our law gives 0.5064 °C. The remainder of 0.0064 °C comes from the curved term. “Steepest local slope” concerns the starting point and first order, at equal distance.

    Recalculate the remainder using the temperature law

Hint 2

The slope along x depends on x; the slope along y is constant in this model.

Compare with the solution

The derivative with respect to x is 2ax, so gx(B) = 2 × 0 = 0 °C/m. The derivative with respect to y is b = 3 °C/m. Thus ∇T(B) = (0 ; 3) in °C/m, in the order x then y.

The gradient now points in the direction of increasing y. At A, it also had a component along x. We have changed the evaluation point, not the temperature law. The fact that the slope along x is 0 at B does not make the whole gradient 0.

Exercise 2 · Calculate and check

Start again from A in the opposite direction

From A, take the displacement (-0.08 m ; -0.06 m). Calculate the linear change and the approximate temperature. Then use the remainder R, equal to a times the square of hₓ, to recover the exact change and exact model temperature. Is the exact change the negative of the one in the example?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In the dot product, the slopes remain those at A; the displacement components are what change sign.

Hint 2

The square of a negative displacement remains positive. The remainder therefore keeps the same sign.

Compare with the solution

The dot product changes sign: gx × (−hx) + gᵧ × (−hᵧ) is the negative of the previous linear change, that is -0.50 °C. The approximate temperature is T(A) + -0.50 °C, that is 27 − 0.50 = 26.50 °C.

The square of -0.08 m is 0.0064 m². The remainder is still a times this square: 0.0064 °C. The exact change is therefore -0.50 + 0.0064 = -0.4936 °C; the exact temperature is 27 + (-0.4936) = 26.5064 °C.

The exact decrease is not the negative of the exact increase of 0.5064 °C in the example: the linear term changes sign, while the quadratic remainder does not. It is the linear change that is exactly opposite.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the gradient at A and reverse the signs of the displacement.
  • Separate temperature change from temperature at the endpoint.
  • Keep the remainder positive and explain the asymmetry of the exact changes.

Exercise 3 · Compare directions

Is the largest component enough?

A classmate wants to move only in the direction of increasing x, because gₓ is greater than gᵧ at A. Compare their displacement (0.10 m ; 0 m) with the displacement in the example at first order. Check that they have the same length. Which predicts the greatest increase? What is missing if we want to claim the same thing over a large distance?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate the dot product of the gradient with each displacement, without adding their components to measure their length.

Hint 2

The direction in the example is a unit vector multiplied by ℓ. For the other path, the vertical component is 0.

Compare with the solution

The path along x has length 0.10 m and predicts 4 × 0.10 + 3 × 0 = 0.40 °C. The path in the example has the same length, because the squares of 0.80 and 0.60 sum to 1. It predicts 0.50 °C.

The gradient direction therefore predicts 0.10 °C more at first order, at the same distance. Choosing only the largest component ignores the increase available through a diagonal displacement. We are comparing temperature changes, not temperatures already reached.

For a large distance, we must recalculate the temperature using the law for each path or have a bound on the remainders. The gradient at A is not enough to guarantee this ranking beyond the local approximation. A conclusion about the whole plate would also require its domain and an analysis of the law over that domain.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify the equal lengths using the Euclidean norm.
  • Obtain both linear changes with units.
  • Limit the ranking to first order and name the additional evidence needed.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you reconstruct the gradient from partial derivatives?

In an orthonormal basis, evaluate all the partial derivatives at the same point and use them as the components of a vector, in coordinate order. For our plate, its components have units of °C/m.

Recall 2What is the difference between ∇T(A) and the differential at A applied to h?

The gradient is a vector of slopes in °C/m. The differential applied to the displacement h is the scalar ∇T(A) · h, in °C, which approximates the actual change for a small displacement under the assumption of differentiability.

Recall 3What must be fixed to say that the gradient indicates the greatest increase?

Compare local slopes at equal Euclidean length, in orthonormal axes with coordinates using the same unit and scale. If the gradient is nonzero, its normalization gives the direction of maximum slope; the distance remains a separate choice.

Recall 4How can you check the temperature predicted by the gradient in our example?

Recalculate T at the endpoint using the law, subtract T at the start and compare with the differential. Here the exact remainder is a times the square of hₓ; its existence distinguishes the linear approximation from the exact change in the fictional model.

Recall 5Does a 0 gradient prove that the point is the hottest?

No. Assuming differentiability, it only says that all directional slopes at first order are 0 at this point. How the temperature varies beyond first order still needs to be studied.
