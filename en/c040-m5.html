Level 1 · C040·M5

Least squares

Your goalFit a constant flow rate to incompatible readings, calculate the residual and prove the minimum through orthogonality.

Before you start
c039-m5 : changes of basis · c040-m1 : dot product · c040-m2 : norm · c040-m3 : orthogonality · c040-m4 : Gram-Schmidt

✿01 See the idea

You are filling a container assumed to be empty at the start. The reading shows 2 dL after 1 s, then 3 dL after 2 s. The times are treated as exact. These volumes and the whole scene are fictional: they help explain a calculation and do not describe a real experiment.

You would like to describe this filling process with a constant flow rate. Matching the first reading exactly would require 2 dL/s; matching the second exactly would require 1.5 dL/s. No constant flow rate satisfies both at once. You need to choose how to measure the disagreement, then find the best compromise under that rule.

Least squares chooses the flow rate that minimizes the sum of the squared volume differences. A positive difference and a negative difference both contribute to the disagreement. Here each reading has the same weight: this is a choice made in the problem, rather than a conclusion about the reliability of an instrument.

The second drawing changes the viewpoint. A position now represents a pair of volumes, in the order of the readings. The pairs that a constant flow rate can produce lie on a straight line. The measured pair lies off this line; the closest pair on the line is found by dropping a perpendicular. This is the orthogonal projection introduced in this chapter.

The rule to remember

First fix the predictions the model allows and the criterion for measuring disagreement. In least squares, the residual of the best prediction is orthogonal to the directions along which the model can move.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Choose the flow rate, then prove it is optimal

Using the same readings, let us find the flow rate that minimizes the squared volume differences. The initial volume stays fixed at 0 dL: adding an unknown initial volume would change the problem.

  1. Define the model and the criterion

    The vector t = (1 ; 2) collects the times in seconds; y = (2 ; 3) collects the observed volumes in decilitres. The number a is the flow rate to choose, in dL/s. The model predicts the pair ŷ = at, in dL. The hat means “fitted value”, rather than a new reading.

    The residual y − at is the pair of differences, observation minus prediction. Its squared Euclidean norm adds the squares of its components. We call this sum J(a), in dL²; minimizing J means finding its smallest possible value among the allowed flow rates.

    J(a)=‖y−at‖2=(2−1a)2+(3−2a)2

    We fit a single coefficient, with no free initial term, using known times and differences with the same unit and weight. The calculation assumes no probability distribution. We allow a to be real to establish the minimum; the positive solution will also satisfy the physical constraint of a nonnegative flow rate.

  2. Find the foot of the perpendicular

    Write â for the flow rate we seek and r = y − ât for its residual. For ŷ = ât to be the foot of the projection, r must be orthogonal to the direction t: the dot product t · r must equal 0 s·dL. The symbol · denotes the sum of the products of corresponding components.

    t·(y−a^t)=0⇒a^=t·yt·t

    Expand the dot product: t · y − â(t · t) = 0. The vector t is nonzero, so t · t is strictly positive; we can divide by this number. If all the times were 0, division would not be allowed and these readings would not determine a.

  3. Calculate the flow rate with its units

    Denominator: t · t = 1 × 1 + 2 × 2 = 1 + 4 = 5 s². Numerator: t · y = 1 × 2 + 2 × 3 = 2 + 6 = 8 s·dL. We combine the readings before dividing, to satisfy orthogonality.

    a^=85=1.6 dL/s

    The unit s·dL divided by s² does give dL/s. The result is positive. It lies between the flow rates that would match each reading separately; this is a consistency check here, but it does not replace the proof of the minimum.

  4. Return to volumes and keep the signs

    The predictions are 8/5 × 1 = 8/5 dL, or 1.6 dL, then 8/5 × 2 = 16/5 dL, or 3.2 dL. Each multiplication converts the flow rate into a volume using the corresponding time.

    Then subtract prediction from observation: r = (2 − 8/5 ; 3 − 16/5) = (2/5 ; -1/5) dL. The first residual is positive: the model predicts too little. The second is negative: the model predicts too much. We keep the exact fractions before calculating the squares.

    J(a^)=(2/5)2+(-1/5)2=4/25+1/25=1/5 dL²

    The minimum value of the criterion is therefore 0.20 dL². It is neither a volume nor a flow rate. It is nonzero: even the best fit can leave differences.

  5. Check the condition that determines the optimum

    The dot product of the time vector with the residual is 1 × (2/5) + 2 × (-1/5) = 2/5 + (-2/5) = 0 s·dL. This gives exactly the condition imposed before the calculation.

    By contrast, the simple sum of the residuals is 2/5 + (-1/5) = 1/5 dL. It is nonzero. Our model only allows a change in flow rate, so the relevant condition weights the residuals by the times. We have not fitted a free initial volume.

  6. Prove the minimum for all other flow rates

    Let b be another real flow rate, also in dL/s. The difference from the observations becomes y − bt = r + (â − b)t. The first term is perpendicular to the second, since r · t is 0. Pythagoras’ theorem then gives:

    J(b)=‖r‖2+(a^−b)2‖t‖2

    The added term is a squared flow rate multiplied by a strictly positive quantity in s². It is therefore nonnegative, in dL². It vanishes only for b = â. The flow rate we found is the unique minimizer, rather than just the best of a few trials.

    For example, b = 1.5 dL/s leaves 0.5 dL at the first reading and 0 dL at the second. Its criterion is 0.25 dL². The flow rate difference δ = b − â is -1/10 dL/s. The extra amount predicted by Pythagoras’ theorem is 5 × (-1/10) × (-1/10) = 1/20 dL². We recover 1/5 + 1/20 = 1/4 dL² by another route.

  7. State what the fit establishes

    We have found the closest pair to the observed volumes among pairs of the form at, according to the chosen sum of squares. This establishes an optimization result for this model and these data.

    The actual flow rate could vary, the container might not have been empty, or the readings could be offset. The calculation does not decide between these possibilities or guarantee the next volume. Changing the weights or adding an unknown initial volume would require a different fit. This unit stops at the case of a single direction and a single coefficient.

Without rereading, explain why the differences are vertical in the time–volume graph and perpendicular in the space of pairs. Then reconstruct the flow rate and justify the minimum without derivatives or comparing a list of trials.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “It is enough for the positive and negative differences to cancel.”

    The signed sum can hide large opposing differences. Our criterion adds their squares; its optimality condition is t · r = 0, rather than the simple sum of the residuals. The last exercise gives a calculated counterexample.

    Test cancellation of the signs

  • “The projection gives the shortest segment on the time–volume graph.”

    The times are fixed: the differences on this graph are vertical. Orthogonal projection takes place in the space where each coordinate is an observed volume; this is the other drawing, with both axes in dL at the same scale.

    Compare the axes of the two drawings

  • “The minimum proves that the flow rate really is constant.”

    The minimum compares the predictions allowed by the model. On its own, it tests neither the choice of model nor the readings. Even our best pair retains nonzero residuals; additional observations and a separate validation question would be needed.

    Reread the scope of the result

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · calculate a criterion

Match the second reading exactly

Keep the scene and impose a flow rate of 1.5 dL/s. Calculate the predicted volumes, the signed residuals and J. Is matching the second reading exactly enough to reach the minimum?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Hint 1

Multiply the same flow rate by each time before subtracting anything.

Hint 2

The criterion is the sum of the squared residuals; keep the sign of each residual until this step.

Compare with the solution

At the first time, 1.5 × 1 = 3/2 dL; at the second, 1.5 × 2 = 3 dL. The residuals are therefore (1/2 ; 0) dL.

The criterion is (1/2) × (1/2) + (0) × (0) = 1/4 dL², or 0.25 dL². This exceeds 0.20 dL²: matching one reading exactly can incur a greater cost overall. The comparison rules out this candidate; the general proof in the example establishes the global minimum.

Check your reasoning

  • Give both volumes, then both residuals, in dL.
  • Obtain 1/4 dL² and compare the same criterion in the same unit.
  • Distinguish matching one reading from fitting all the readings together.

Exercise 2 · Application · reconstruct a proof

Check a reported flow rate

Someone reports â = 1.6 dL/s for our readings. Without copying the solution, check its residual, establish its orthogonality to t and prove that no other flow rate can do better. State the assumption that makes the minimizer unique.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with r = y − ât, then calculate t · r. A small value of J is not enough to certify the minimum.

Hint 2

For an arbitrary flow rate b, write y − bt = r + (â − b)t. Which theorem applies to the perpendicular terms?

Compare with the solution

We obtain ŷ = (8/5 ; 16/5) dL and r = (2/5 ; -1/5) dL. The product t · r = 1 × (2/5) + 2 × (-1/5) is exactly 0 s·dL.

For any real flow rate b, Pythagoras’ theorem gives J(b) = J(â) + (â − b) × (â − b) × (t · t). The first term is 1/5 dL²; the second is nonnegative. Since t · t = 5 s² is strictly positive, equality occurs only for b = â. The nonzero time vector ensures uniqueness.

The reasoning covers all pairs of the form bt, with a fixed initial volume and equal weights. It does not certify that this model describes the actual flow rate. An answer obtained after a hint is an assisted answer; it still needs to be reconstructed without support in another session.

Check your reasoning

  • Check t · r = 0 exactly, using the times and the signs.
  • Use an arbitrary flow rate b to prove the minimum, rather than limiting the proof to a few trials.
  • Justify uniqueness using nonzero t and limit the conclusion to the stated model.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · examine a false certificate

Residuals that cancel, but a worse fit

In the same scene, someone chooses the flow rate c = (2 + 3)/(1 + 2) = 5/3 dL/s. They claim: “The sum of the residuals is 0, so c is optimal.” Calculate its residual and J(c), then give the condition they should have checked. Also explain why our â can be optimal with a nonzero sum of residuals.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

c simply denotes another candidate flow rate. Calculate the pair ct, then y − ct; then add the residuals and their squares separately.

Hint 2

Compare r · t with the sum of the components of r. Changing the flow rate moves the volumes in proportion to the times, which are unequal.

Compare with the solution

The sums are 5 dL and 3 s; their quotient does give c = 5/3 dL/s. The predicted pair is (5/3 ; 10/3) dL, so the residual is (1/3 ; -1/3) dL. Its sum is 0 dL: the signs cancel.

The squares do not cancel: J(c) = 1/9 + 1/9 = 2/9 dL², or about 0.222 dL². It exceeds the minimum 1/5 dL². The 0 signed sum was therefore a false certificate.

The product with t is 1 × (1/3) + 2 × (-1/3) = 1/3 + (-2/3) = -1/3 s·dL, which is nonzero. The correct condition fails for c. For â, this condition is satisfied even though the simple sum is 1/5 dL: our model fits the flow rate without fitting an initial volume.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover opposite residuals and a strictly positive criterion.
  • Compare 2/9 and 1/5 dL² exactly, without deciding on the basis of a rough rounding.
  • Name orthogonality to the direction t and explain why the signed sum cannot replace it.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do we minimize in our fit, and in what unit?

J(a) is the squared norm of y − at, so it is the sum of the squared volume differences, in dL². The times are fixed, the initial volume is prescribed and the readings have the same weight.

Recall 2How can you reconstruct the optimal flow rate from orthogonality without reciting its value?

Write t · (y − ât) = 0, expand, then divide by t · t: â = (t · y)/(t · t). We need nonzero t. The numerator is in s·dL and the denominator in s²: the quotient is in dL/s.

Recall 3Why does a residual orthogonal to t guarantee a unique minimizer here?

For any other flow rate b, y − bt = r + (â − b)t. Pythagoras’ theorem adds to J(â) the squared flow rate difference multiplied by t · t. This extra amount is positive unless b = â, because t is nonzero.

Recall 4In which space is the projection perpendicular?

In the space of volume pairs, whose axes represent the successive readings using the same scale. In the time–volume graph, we keep each time fixed and measure a vertical difference.

Recall 5What does the minimum of the criterion fail to demonstrate about the container?

It does not demonstrate that the actual flow rate is constant, that the readings are correct, or that a future volume will be predicted correctly. It compares the allowed predictions for these data and this criterion.
