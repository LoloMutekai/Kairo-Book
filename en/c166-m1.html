Level 3 · C166·M1

Fourier series

Your goalDefine the real coefficients of a Fourier series by projection over a period, calculate them and check what the reconstruction establishes.

Before you start
c165-m5 : duality · c046-m5 : approximation error · c027-m5 : complex numbers

✿01 See the idea

A lighting rig repeats the same electrical power cycle every 8 s. It draws 17 W at the start and 9 W after 2 s. These values and the entire power function are fictional: we are studying an ideal signal, not readings from equipment.

Imagine regular oscillations superimposed on one another. A constant component sets the mean level. One oscillation completes a turn during the cycle; another completes 2. Adding their values at each instant produces a curve more complex than any single oscillation.

A Fourier series describes a periodic function using a constant, cosines and sines whose frequencies are integer multiples of the fundamental frequency. A coefficient specifies the signed contribution to add. To recover it, we compare the signal with a reference oscillation over the whole cycle: the other oscillations cancel out in this average.

The dot product for functions becomes an integral of a product here. We will review its normalization rather than assume familiarity with Fourier conventions. All angles on this page are in degrees; a full turn is 360°. This choice keeps the angles and coefficients exact without using a decimal approximation to a circle constant.

The rule to remember

Fix the period and the time origin; multiply by the desired mode; average over a cycle; divide by the mean of the square of that mode. Then state the sense in which the series reconstructs the function.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Recover the coefficients for the lighting rig

The fictional power function is known at every instant. Its period T is 8 s. Setting u = t/T gives q(u) = 10 + 4 C₁(u) + 2 S₁(u) + 3 C₂(u), in watts. The expression is provided so that the integrals can be checked; the task is to recover its coefficients using the Fourier definition.

  1. Fix the symbols and the reference cycle

    t is time in seconds, with its origin fixed at the start of the diagram. p(t) is power in watts; T > 0 s is its period: p(t + T) = p(t). The phase u = t/T is dimensionless and q(u) = p(Tu). One cycle corresponds to u running from 0 to 1. The integers n and m are positive harmonic numbers, with no units.

    Cn(u)=cos°(360nu);Sn(u)=sin°(360nu)

    cos° and sin° explicitly mean “cosine and sine of an angle expressed in degrees”. Cn and Sn are dimensionless. Harmonic number n completes n turns during T; its frequency is n/T, in hertz, meaning cycles per second. Here, the fundamental frequency is 1/8 = 1/8 Hz and harmonic number 2 has frequency 1/4 Hz.

  2. Define the series and the sense of reconstruction

    qN(u)=a02+∑n=1N[anCn(u)+bnSn(u)]

    N is a positive integer harmonic number that bounds this sum; the symbol ∑ means “add for each harmonic number from 1 to N”. a0, an and bn are real, in watts. The Fourier series is the limit of these sums as N increases without bound. The symbol a0 names the constant coefficient, whose contribution is a0/2.

    Assumptions: q is real, periodic with period 1 in u and square integrable over a cycle. Convergence is then guaranteed in mean square: the integral of the square of the difference between q and q_N over the cycle tends to 0, in watts squared. This does not assert equality at every point. This guarantee uses the completeness of the trigonometric family: its finite combinations approximate any function in this setting in mean square. Orthogonality alone would not suffice. In our example, q is a finite sum of sinusoids and is therefore smooth; we will directly establish exact equality everywhere, without invoking general pointwise convergence.

  3. Average over the cycle and isolate a mode

    For any integrable function g of the phase, ⟨g⟩ denotes its average over the cycle; g may be a signal or a product. Since the interval in u has length 1, this average is simply the integral. For g = q, the change of variable t = Tu also gives the time average of p.

    ⟨g⟩=∫01g(u)du;⟨q⟩=1T∫0Tp(t)dt

    The real dot product is ⟨gh⟩, for two square integrable functions g and h. Saying that modes are orthogonal means that this average of their product is 0. For positive n and m, ⟨Cn⟩ = ⟨Sn⟩ = 0, ⟨CnSm⟩ = 0. If n ≠ m, ⟨CnCm⟩ = ⟨SnSm⟩ = 0; if n = m, these averages are 1/2. The constant has a mean square of 1.

    Why these values? The product of cosines can be rewritten as a sum of cosines: CnCm = (Cn-m + Cn+m)/2, with C0 = 1. Cosine has the same value at opposite angles, so a negative harmonic number is treated like its positive opposite. A cosine with a nonzero integer harmonic number completes an integer number of turns, and its integral vanishes. If n = m, half the constant remains. The product identities for sines and mixed products give the other relations. The indices n − m and n + m are simply harmonic numbers, with no units.

    a0=2⟨q⟩;an=2⟨qCn⟩;bn=2⟨qSn⟩

    We divide each projection by the mean of the square of the mode: 1/2 for a sine or cosine, so we multiply by 2. The formula for a0 reflects the convention a0/2; it does not change the norm of the constant. No interchange of an infinite sum and an integral is needed for our finite calculation.

  4. Calculate the constant component

    Integrate the given power function term by term. Each sine and each nonconstant cosine has 0 mean over the whole cycle. This leaves ⟨q⟩ = 10 W, so a0 = 2 × 10 = 20 W.

    a02=202=10 W

    Unit check: integrating p in watts against dt in seconds gives watt-seconds. Dividing by T gives watts again. In phase u, the differential in u is dimensionless; ⟨q⟩ is already in watts.

  5. Calculate the fundamental contributions

    ⟨qC1⟩=4×1/2=2 W

    The constant term times C1 has 0 mean. So do the products S1C1 and C2C1. Only 4 times the square of C1 remains, with the mean of the square equal to 1/2. Thus a1 = 2 × 2 = 4 W.

    ⟨qS1⟩=2×1/2=1 W

    For the sine, the products with the constant and the cosines average to 0; only 2 times the square of S1 remains. Therefore b1 = 2 × 1 = 2 W. Multiplication by the mode isolates a contribution; it does not give the instantaneous power.

  6. Calculate the next harmonic and rule out the others

    ⟨qC2⟩=3×1/2=3/2 W

    The same reasoning gives a2 = 2 × 3/2 = 3 W. q contains no S2, and all its terms are orthogonal to it: b2 = 0 W. For each n ≥ 3, Cn and Sn are orthogonal to all the terms of q; an = bn = 0 W. This result follows from the complete power function provided, not from an arbitrary decision to truncate the series.

  7. Reconstruct, then check at specific instants

    q(u)=202+4C1(u)+2S1(u)+3C2(u)

    All the coefficients obtained reproduce the original power function exactly: the equality holds for every u. At t = 0 s, the cosines are 1 and the sine is 0. We obtain 10 + 4 + 3 = 17 W.

    At t = T/4 = 2 s, u = 1/4. The fundamental angle is 90°: C1 = 0, S1 = 1. The cosine of harmonic number 2 takes the angle 180°, so C2 = −1. The power is 10 + 2 − 3 = 9 W.

    These checks detect a missing constant or sign. Equality over the whole cycle rests on the projections and the fully known finite expression. Agreement at a few points would not suffice for an unknown curve.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the coefficient values. Why does the mean give a₀ divided by 2, while the projection onto C₁ must be multiplied by 2? Also explain why agreement at a few points does not prove that two signals are equal.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The mean is a₀, so I use 20 W as the baseline.”

    In the chosen convention, the constant in the series is a₀/2. The cycle average is 10 W; the coefficient a₀ is 20 W. Restore the division in the reconstruction.

    Reread the calculation of the constant component

  • “Harmonic number 2 means 2 Hz.”

    The harmonic number counts the cycles during T. Divide 2 by 8 s: you obtain 1/4 Hz. A dimensionless integer and a frequency do not represent the same quantity.

    Review the cycle and its components

  • “The sine is 0 at the check point, so its coefficient is 0.”

    A value at an instant and a projection over a cycle are distinct. Our S₁ vanishes at the origin, but b₁ is 2 W. Recalculate the mean of the product before removing a mode.

    Examine the check points and the hidden signal

Exercise 2 · Reconstruction and diagnosis

Check halfway through the cycle

Calculate p(T/2) in the same model. At phase u = 1/2, C₁ = −1, S₁ = 0 and C₂ = 1. A classmate wants to remove b₁ because the sine contributes nothing at this instant. Is that conclusion justified?

Hint 1

Add the signed contributions and the constant a₀/2.

Hint 2

A local 0 does not determine an integral over the whole cycle.

Compare with the solution

The time is T/2 = 4 s. The constant contributes 10 W; the fundamental cosine contributes −4 W; the sine contributes 0 W at this instant; the cosine of harmonic number 2 contributes 3 W. Thus p(T/2) = 10 − 4 + 3 = 9 W.

Here this result coincides with p(T/4), although these phases are different. We keep b1 = 2 W: the mean of qS1 remains 1 W. The check at a single point does not replace this projection.

Check your reasoning

  • Define the instant and retain the sign of each contribution.
  • Do not confuse a 0 value of the mode with a 0 coefficient.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same scene

An oscillation escapes the checks

The rig could follow q̃(u) = q(u) + 2 S₂(u), still in watts. We compare the power functions only at phases 0, 1/4, 1/2 and 1. Do they give the same power at these phases? Are they identical? Find an instant that distinguishes them and the coefficient b₂ of q̃.

Hint 1

At these phases, the angle of the added sine is a multiple of 180°.

Hint 2

At u = 1/8, the angle of S₂ is 90°: the sine reaches 1.

Hint 3

Project the added term onto S₂; the modes already present are orthogonal to it.

Compare with the solution

S2 is 0 at the proposed phases: the checks give the same values for both power functions. Yet at u = 1/8, that is, t = 1 s, S2 = 1. The difference q̃ − q is then exactly 2 W. There is no need to calculate the other terms: they cancel in the difference.

For q̃, the mean of the product with S2 is 2 × 1/2 = 1 W; therefore b2 = 2 × 1 = 2 W, instead of 0 W. The coefficients depend on the signal over an entire period. The few checks did not establish its uniqueness.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the period determine in a real Fourier series?

The frequency grid n/T for positive integer harmonic numbers. The time origin also determines how the signal is divided between sines and cosines.

Recall 2How do you recover aₙ and bₙ using our average over a cycle?

aₙ = 2⟨qCₙ⟩ and bₙ = 2⟨qSₙ⟩: different modes are orthogonal, and the mean of the square of each sine or cosine with a positive harmonic number is 1/2.

Recall 3What is the difference between a₀ and the mean?

With the convention a₀/2 + sum of the modes, the mean is a₀/2. Here it is 10 W, while a₀ is 20 W.

Recall 4What does reconstruction guarantee in the square integrable setting?

The Fourier sums converge to the function in mean square over a cycle. This does not guarantee equality at every point. In our trigonometric polynomial, the sum is finite and equality is exact everywhere.

Recall 5Why do checks at individual points fail to prove that two signals are equal?

An additional oscillation can vanish at the chosen points. A justification over the whole cycle is needed; our reconstruction rests on the integrals and the exact expression of the given power function.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without support: define T, u, Cₙ and Sₙ with their units; write the series convention and its integrals; explain the factor 2; recover the constant and the modes of the rig. Finish by stating the sense of convergence and a limitation of checks at individual points. Reading or an answer obtained with a hint remains preparatory work, never an attained level.
