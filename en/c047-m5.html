Level 2 · C047·M5

Stability of an equilibrium

Your goalDistinguish staying near an equilibrium from returning towards it, then prove these properties in a thermal model with a constant rate.

Before you start
c045-m5 : introductory quadrature · c046-m5 : approximation error · c033-m5 : affine approximation · c047-m1 : direction field · c047-m2 : existence and uniqueness · c047-m3 : separation · c047-m4 : linear equation

✿01 See the idea

A small thermal enclosure is surrounded by air kept at 20 °C. It is left to evolve without heating. If its interior is slightly warmer, it cools down; if it is slightly colder, it warms up. The outside air acts as a reference towards which deviations shrink.

At exactly 20 °C, nothing changes in our model: this is an equilibrium. But knowing this stationary position does not yet tell us what happens after a small perturbation. We need to imagine a nearby starting temperature on either side and follow its entire future evolution.

An equilibrium is stable if a starting point that is close enough stays as close as required at all future times. It is attracting if starting points that are sufficiently nearby approach it without limit over time. Stable and attracting is called asymptotically stable. Staying near and returning are therefore separate requirements.

The scene and all its values are fictional. We assume a uniform interior temperature, a constant outside temperature and no new heat input. The restoring coefficient will be constant. We study the consequences of this ideal law without claiming to have measured a real enclosure. The starting temperatures 24 °C and 16 °C help us understand the mechanism; the proof will also cover arbitrarily small deviations.

The rule to remember

Find the stationary state, then study the deviation from that state: its sign tells you which side you start on; its size tells you whether you stay near and whether you return.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A stationary reference, starting temperatures that return

The reference temperature is T★ = 20 °C. The restoring coefficient is k = 0.50 min⁻¹. Start from T(0) = 24 °C, then check the symmetric starting temperature 16 °C. We will find the equilibrium, the rates of change and an exact return towards it before proving stability.

  1. Write the law and state its domain

    t is elapsed time in minutes, with t ≥ 0. T(t) is the interior temperature in °C; T′(t) = dT/dt is its instantaneous rate of change in °C/min. T★ is the constant outside temperature. The star marks the reference and does not indicate multiplication. The law is autonomous: at a given temperature, it does not depend explicitly on the time.

    T′(t)=−k(T(t)−T★)

    The coefficient k is constant and strictly positive. Its unit min⁻¹ makes the product k(T − T★) dimensionally consistent with a rate of change in °C/min. The minus sign turns an excess temperature into cooling. Mathematically, we allow any real starting temperature for this affine law; its physical validity far from the reference has not been established. The affine law gives a unique solution for all future times.

  2. Find the equilibrium before deciding whether it is stable

    An equilibrium is a constant solution. Its derivative must therefore be 0: −k(T − T★) = 0. Since k ≠ 0, divide by −k to obtain T = T★ = 20 °C. Substitution into the law does give -0.50 × (20 − 20) = 0 °C/min.

    This check concerns a starting temperature exactly at the reference. It is not enough to classify nearby starting temperatures: a stationary temperature could also repel perturbations.

  3. Move from temperature to deviation

    Set u(t) = T(t) − T★, in °C. Since T★ is constant, u′ = T′ = −ku. The quantity |u| is the distance from the equilibrium in °C; u also retains the information about which side we are on. For the warm start, u(0) = 24 − 20 = 4 °C, so u′(0) = -0.50 × 4 = -2 °C/min.

    For the cold start, u(0) = 16 − 20 = -4 °C and u′(0) = -0.50 × (-4) = 2 °C/min. A positive temperature can therefore have a negative rate of change. Here the signs bring the temperature back towards the reference from both sides. These initial rates are not constant changes to add minute after minute.

  4. Recover the solution and check it

    The linear equation u′ = −ku gives the following solution. exp is the exponential function and its argument −kt is dimensionless. We do not divide by u: the formula also includes a start exactly at the equilibrium.

    u(t)=u(0)exp(−kt)
    T(t)=T★+(T(0)−T★)exp(−kt)

    Initial check: exp(0) = 1, so we recover T(0). Check of the law: differentiating the expression for u gives −k × u(0) × exp(−kt) = −ku(t). The expression therefore satisfies the equation and the initial condition. For positive k, the exponential factor is positive and at most 1: the sign of the deviation does not change.

  5. Calculate an exact halving of the deviation

    Choose h, the time needed to divide the deviation by 2. ln is the natural logarithm, the inverse of exp. The equality exp(−kh) = 1/2 leads to h = ln(2)/k. The duration τ = 1/k is 2 min, so h = 2 ln(2) min. h is kept in exact form; it is not 2 min.

    exp(−kh)=exp(−ln(2))=12

    Reduce the deviation, then add the reference back: u(h) = 4 × 1/2 = 2 °C; T(h) = 20 + 2 = 22 °C. For the cold start: u(h) = (-4) × 1/2 = -2 °C, then T(h) = 20 + (-2) = 18 °C.

    Check using another expression: after one halving time, the temperature is the average of the starting temperature and the reference. (24 + 20)/2 = 22 °C and (16 + 20)/2 = 18 °C. Each result stays between its starting temperature and the reference.

    After 2h, apply the same factor to the deviation again: 2 × 1/2 = 1 °C and (-2) × 1/2 = -1 °C. The temperatures become 21 °C and 19 °C. The direct check uses exp(−k × 2h) = (1/2) to the power 2: two intervals together produce the same return as a single interval of their combined duration.

  6. Prove stability at all times

    Fix a strictly positive tolerance ε in °C: ε is the maximum distance requested. Look for an initial margin δ, also in °C, such that every start at a distance strictly less than δ stays at a distance strictly less than ε for all t ≥ 0. δ may depend on ε, but not on the time at which we check.

    |u(t)|=|u(0)|exp(−kt)≤|u(0)|

    Here it is enough to choose δ = ε. If |u(0)| < δ, this inequality gives |u(t)| < ε for all future times: this is stability. We have checked more than just the times in the diagram.

    Moreover, exp(−kt) tends to 0 as t increases without limit. The distance for any fixed initial deviation therefore tends to 0: this is attraction. The equilibrium is asymptotically stable; this holds for every starting temperature in the mathematical model. Yet a nonzero initial deviation is never exactly 0 at a finite time, since exp(−kt) remains strictly positive.

  7. See what changing the sign would remove

    Within the same family of laws, now take k = 0 min⁻¹. Then T′ = 0 and every initial temperature stays constant. The reference 20 °C is stable: a start that is close enough stays close. It does not attract distinct starting temperatures. Every point is then an equilibrium; we can no longer divide by k to claim there is only one.

    If the coefficient becomes negative, k = -0.50 min⁻¹, the law amplifies the deviation. This is a mathematical variant, rather than the passive cooling described at the start. The same solution now contains exp(−kt) with −k = 0.50 min⁻¹: every nonzero deviation eventually exceeds a fixed tolerance, even if it is tiny at the start. The reference is unstable. The sign of the rate of change around the point, rather than just its vanishing at the point, makes the difference.

  8. Recognise the local slope test

    Call f(T) the rate of change prescribed at temperature T. Here f(T) = −k(T − T★), in °C/min, and f′(T★) = −k = -0.50 min⁻¹. Keep the derivatives distinct: T′ describes evolution over time; f′ describes how this rate changes when the temperature is shifted.

    For a scalar autonomous equation T′ = f(T), assume f is differentiable and its derivative is continuous on an open interval around an equilibrium T★, with f(T★) = 0. The affine approximation gives f(T★ + u) ≈ f′(T★)u for small u. If f′(T★) < 0, continuity preserves a restoring effect on both sides near the point: the equilibrium is locally asymptotically stable. If f′(T★) > 0, the directions point outwards and the equilibrium is unstable.

    For a general law, this test gives a conclusion only within a neighbourhood; our affine law allowed a proof for all its starting temperatures. If f′(T★) = 0, the affine approximation is inconclusive: the law must be studied beyond its slope. We solved the particular case k = 0 exactly; this does not classify all laws with 0 slope.

Hide the solution. Recover the equation for the deviation, explain why δ = ε works at every time and add the argument that establishes attraction. Finish with the case of a 0 coefficient: what remains true, and what disappears?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The derivative is 0 at 20 °C, so the equilibrium is stable.”

    The vanishing derivative proves only that the exact starting temperature stays stationary. Move the starting temperature to either side and study the deviation over time. With the sign reversed, the same reference remains an equilibrium but repels every nonzero perturbation.

    Review the proof and the sign change

  • “After one halving time, I divide the temperature by 2.”

    It is the distance from 20 °C that is divided. Subtract the reference, multiply the deviation by 1/2, then add the reference: 20 + (24 − 20) × 1/2 = 22 °C. The result must stay between the starting temperature and the reference.

    Redo the halving calculation and check it using the average

  • “Stable means that the return is complete after a long enough time.”

    Stable means staying near. A 0 coefficient gives an example without a return. With a positive coefficient, the return is asymptotic: the deviation tends to 0, but does not vanish in finite time if it was initially nonzero.

    Distinguish the cases in the last exercise

Exercise 3 · Transfer · distinguish the properties

Stop the return, then reverse its sign

Start again from 20.50 °C. Compare k = 0 min⁻¹ and k = -0.50 min⁻¹ in the same law T′ = −k(T − T★). For each case, classify the reference 20 °C: is it an equilibrium, stable, attracting? In the negative case, calculate the temperatures at h and 2h, where h keeps the duration defined in the example. Finally, explain why instability also concerns starting temperatures that are even closer.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

With a 0 coefficient, the deviation stays constant; with a negative coefficient, it is multiplied by 2 over each interval h.

Hint 2

A single trajectory is not enough to rule out every initial margin. Ask yourself what happens to an arbitrarily small positive deviation when you repeat the intervals h.

Compare with the solution

With k = 0, the reference is an equilibrium, and so is every other temperature. The deviation stays at 0.50 °C for this start. The reference is stable because every small initial distance stays small; it is not attracting because distinct starting temperatures do not approach it.

With k = -0.50 min⁻¹, the reference remains an equilibrium: a 0 deviation stays 0. At the proposed start, the rate of change is +0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25 °C/min. At h, the deviation becomes 0.50 × 2 = 1 °C, so T(h) = 21 °C. At 2h, it becomes 1 × 2 = 2 °C, so T(2h) = 22 °C. The requirement of a distance strictly less than 1 °C is already violated at h.

To prove instability, fix this same tolerance 1 °C and propose any margin δ > 0. We can choose a positive initial deviation smaller than both δ and the tolerance. Repeated multiplication by 2 will make it exceed 1 °C after a finite number of intervals h. No margin therefore guarantees staying within this tolerance at all times. The reference is unstable and is not attracting. This is mathematical amplification, with no claim to thermal realism.

Check your reasoning

  • You distinguish stability without attraction for a 0 coefficient from instability for a negative coefficient.
  • You recover 21 °C then 22 °C by amplifying the deviations.
  • You explain why starting even closer delays leaving the tolerance without preventing it.
  • You do not infer general instability from the single numerical start proposed.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you find an equilibrium, and why does this calculation not establish stability?

A constant solution must make the rate of change vanish: −k(T − T★) = 0. For nonzero k, this gives T★. This calculation follows only the exact starting temperature; stability also concerns all sufficiently nearby starting temperatures and all future times.

Recall 2What must you guarantee to establish stability?

For each positive tolerance ε, find a positive initial margin δ such that every start at a distance less than δ stays at a distance less than ε at all future times. In our model with positive k, |u(t)| ≤ |u(0)| allows δ = ε.

Recall 3What does attraction add to stability, and does a 0 coefficient demonstrate this distinction?

Attraction requires the distances of nearby starting temperatures to tend to 0. With a 0 coefficient, they stay constant: the reference is stable without being attracting. Stable and attracting means asymptotically stable.

Recall 4How do you reconstruct the temperature after one halving time?

Subtract T★ from the starting temperature, multiply the deviation by 1/2, then add T★. The duration is h = ln(2)/k for positive k. Check the result using the average of the starting temperature and the reference.

Recall 5What does the slope test establish, and what limits must you state?

For scalar autonomous T′ = f(T), with f differentiable and its derivative continuous near the equilibrium, a strictly negative slope f′ gives local asymptotic stability; a positive slope gives instability; a 0 slope is inconclusive. The exact return in our example additionally assumes a constant reference and coefficient, with no new input.
