Level 4 · C235·M1

Gaussian noise

Your goalDescribe Gaussian noise as a family of variables W(h) indexed by weights h, whose covariance is a dot product, then use it to calculate variances, a covariance and independence over three fictional time intervals.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A fictional measurement line is affected by random noise that we observe over three consecutive time intervals. The first lasts 1 s, the second 4 s and the third 1 s. These durations and all the weights below are invented for the course; they do not describe any real device.

In each interval k, the accumulated noise is an increment ΔBk. We assume that the three increments are independent, have mean 0 and are Gaussian, with a variance equal to the duration of the interval, expressed in s. A filter applies a dimensionless weight hk to each interval, then adds the results: W(h) = h1ΔB1 + h2ΔB2 + h3ΔB3.

The central question concerns how two filters compare, rather than the value of a single draw. Two sets of weights h and g produce two random variables W(h) and W(g). Their covariance can be read directly from the weights: it is a dot product weighted by the durations. Gaussian noise is thus a linear map from weights equipped with a dot product to Gaussian variables, turning this dot product into covariance.

This viewpoint is the starting point of Malliavin calculus: the geometry of the weight space governs the probability. This unit stops there. Tensor products, Wiener chaos, the derivative and the divergence belong to later units in the chapter. With three intervals, the weights are piecewise constant functions; the continuous case replaces the sum with an integral without changing the idea.

The rule to remember

W(h) is Gaussian with mean 0 and variance ⟨h, h⟩; the covariance of W(h) and W(g) is ⟨h, g⟩; W is linear in h. Orthogonal weights give independent variables because the pair is jointly Gaussian.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Three intervals of fictional noise

The intervals last τ₁ = 1 s, τ₂ = 4 s and τ₃ = 1 s. Two filters have dimensionless weights h = (2 ; 1 ; 1) and g = (1 ; 0 ; 2). A third filter m = (2 ; −1 ; 0) will be used to check orthogonality. We calculate the variances, the covariance, the variance of a sum, then the independence of W(h) and W(m). All these values are fictional.

  1. Define the quantities and assumptions

    The intervals are numbered by k, from 1 to 3. ΔBk is the noise increment over interval k. Assumptions: the ΔBk are independent; each follows a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance τk, expressed in s, using the normalization in which a duration gives a variance of the same value; the weights hk are deterministic, known and dimensionless.

    The weight space H is the set of triples of real numbers. We equip it with the following dot product, in s. It is a real Hilbert space of dimension 3, a discrete version of a space of square integrable functions over the full interval. The squared norm ‖h‖² = ⟨h, h⟩ is therefore also in s.

    ⟨h,g⟩=∑kτkhkgk

  2. Construct W(h) and find its distribution

    W(h) is the weighted sum of the increments.

    W(h)=∑khkΔBk

    It is a linear combination of independent Gaussian variables; by a property of the normal distribution taken as given here, it is Gaussian. Its mean is 0 because each ΔBk has mean 0. For the variance, we expand the square: the product of two increments from different intervals has mean 0, by independence and the 0 means, leaving the squared increments, whose means are τk.

    Var(W(h))=∑kτkhk2=⟨h,h⟩

    First calculate the duration-weighted weights τk hk: 2 s, 4 s and 1 s. The contributions are 2 × 2 = 4 s, 4 × 1 = 4 s and 1 × 1 = 1 s. Their sum gives Var W(h) = 8 + 1 = 9 s.

    Thus W(h) follows a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 9 s. Its standard deviation is 3 √s, since 3 × 3 = 9.

  3. Calculate the covariance of W(h) and W(g)

    The same product expansion gives the covariance: terms from different intervals vanish, and each interval retains the mean τk of its squared increment.

    Cov(W(h),W(g))=∑kτkhkgk=⟨h,g⟩

    Using the duration-weighted weights of h: 2 × 1 = 2 s, 4 × 0 = 0 s and 1 × 2 = 2 s. The second interval contributes nothing because g2 = 0. Total: 2 + 2 = 4 s.

    For the variance of W(g), we weight g by the durations: τk gk equals 1 s, 0 s and 2 s. The weighted squares are 1 s, 0 s and 4 s, so Var W(g) = 1 + 4 = 5 s.

  4. Check using linearity

    W is linear: W(h + g) = W(h) + W(g). The variance of a sum therefore contains two variances and twice the covariance.

    Var(W(h+g))=Var(W(h))+Var(W(g))+2Cov(W(h),W(g))

    Numerically: 9 + 5 = 14 s, then 2 × 4 = 8 s, giving 14 + 8 = 22 s.

    Check by another method: the weights of the sum are (3 ; 1 ; 3). Applying the formula interval by interval to these weights gives the same number, 22 s, which calculs.py checks separately. The two methods agree.

  5. Orthogonality and independence

    Calculate ⟨h, m⟩ with m = (2 ; −1 ; 0). First interval: 2 × 2 = 4 s. Second interval: 4 × 1 = 4 s, to be subtracted because of the minus sign on the weight. Third interval: no contribution because m3 = 0. Result: 4 − 4 = 0 s.

    The covariance of W(h) and W(m) is therefore 0. The pair (W(h), W(m)) consists of linear combinations of the same independent Gaussian increments: it is jointly Gaussian. For a jointly Gaussian pair, 0 covariance is equivalent to independence; this result is taken as given here. Thus W(h) and W(m) are independent. Without joint Gaussianity, the conclusion would not hold.

  6. Check the units and limitations

    The weights are dimensionless; the variances and covariance are in s, as are the products τk hk gk. A variance is positive, and 22 s is indeed positive. If two sets of weights have no interval on which both are nonzero, their covariance is 0 because every product hk gk vanishes.

    These results describe the chosen fictional model. They say nothing about the distribution of a real sensor or the Gaussianity of a pseudorandom generator.

Explain it in your own words

Why does the covariance of W(h) and W(g) reduce to a sum over intervals, without products between different intervals? What additional assumption turns 0 covariance into independence?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Two uncorrelated Gaussian variables are always independent.”

    The pair must be jointly Gaussian. If the increments took only the two values −√τₖ and +√τₖ, with the same variances, the covariance of W(h) and W(m) would remain 0, but these two variables would be dependent. Here, the conclusion follows from the construction using the same independent Gaussian variables.

    Review the orthogonality step

  • “The variance of W(h) is the sum of the squared weights.”

    The dot product in the weight space is weighted by the durations τₖ, in s. Without this weighting, the resulting number does not have the units of a variance and depends on how the time span is divided into intervals. Repeat the calculation interval by interval.

    Review the variance calculation

  • “A noise draw directly gives the variance or covariance.”

    Variance and covariance are properties of the model distribution. A draw, or a pseudorandom sequence, gives only an estimate of them and proves neither perfect Gaussianity nor independence. Reread what the cited code shows and what it does not show.

Compare with the solution

Covariance: Σ τk uk gk = 1 × 0 + 1 × 2 = 2 s. Only the third interval contributes because g2 = 0 and u1 = 0.

Exercise 2 · Transfer

Covariance with a sum

Without summing over the intervals again, calculate Cov(W(h + g), W(h)) from the results of the example. State the two properties that justify your method.

Hint 1

Write W(h + g) = W(h) + W(g), then expand the covariance.

Hint 2

Identify a variance and a covariance that have already been calculated.

Compare with the solution

By linearity of W, W(h + g) = W(h) + W(g). By bilinearity of covariance, Cov(W(h + g), W(h)) = Var W(h) + Cov(W(g), W(h)).

This leaves 9 + 4 = 13 s. Covariance is symmetric, so Cov(W(g), W(h)) does equal 4 s.

Check your reasoning

  • The linearity of W is named.
  • The bilinearity and symmetry of covariance are used.
  • The result is in s.

Exercise 3 · Reasoning

Make a residual independent

Find the coefficient c such that W(g) − c W(h) is uncorrelated with W(h). Calculate the variance of this residual and explain why it is independent of W(h). This coefficient is a fictional calculation tool with no physical interpretation.

Cov(W(g)−cW(h),W(h))=⟨g,h⟩−c⟨h,h⟩
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Set the right-hand side equal to 0 and solve for c.

Hint 2

For the variance, expand ‖g − c h‖², then use the value of c to simplify.

Compare with the solution

Setting the covariance to 0 gives c = ⟨g, h⟩ / ⟨h, h⟩ = 4 / 9 = 4/9, dimensionless. Check: c × 9 = 4, and 4 − 4 = 0 s.

Variance: ‖g‖² − 2 c ⟨g, h⟩ + c² ‖h‖². Since c ‖h‖² = ⟨g, h⟩, this simplifies to ‖g‖² − c⟨g, h⟩ = 5 − 16/9 = 29/9 s, or approximately 3.22 s.

W(g) − c W(h) and W(h) are linear combinations of the same independent Gaussian increments: the pair is jointly Gaussian. Since their covariance is 0, they are independent.

Check your reasoning

  • The condition of 0 covariance is written before solving.
  • The check c × ⟨h, h⟩ = ⟨g, h⟩ is performed.
  • Joint Gaussianity is invoked to conclude independence.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does W(h) represent in our example?

A Gaussian variable with mean 0: the sum of the independent noise increments weighted by h. Its variance is ⟨h, h⟩.

Recall 2Why does the covariance of W(h) and W(g) contain only terms within each interval?

The increments have mean 0 and are independent: the product of increments from two different intervals has mean 0. This leaves the sum of τₖ hₖ gₖ, namely ⟨h, g⟩.

Recall 3When are W(h) and W(m) independent?

When ⟨h, m⟩ = 0 and the pair is jointly Gaussian, which follows here from a construction using the same independent Gaussian increments.

Recall 4What units do the weights, variances and covariances have?

The weights are dimensionless. Variances and covariances are in s under this normalization, in which the variance of an increment equals the duration of its interval.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Specify the intervals, the independent Gaussian increments with mean 0, and the weights; define W(h) and the dot product weighted by the durations; obtain variance and covariance by removing the cross terms; check using linearity; conclude independence only with orthogonality and joint Gaussianity; keep track of the units.
