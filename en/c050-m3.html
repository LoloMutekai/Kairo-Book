Level 2 · C050·M3

Pseudoinverse

Your goalConstruct the pseudoinverse using the SVD and distinguish the best fit from the choice of smallest norm.

Before you start
c048-m5 : positivity · c040-m5 : least squares · c050-m1 : Cholesky · c050-m2 : SVD

✿01 See the idea

Two pieces are placed end to end. We would like to know their individual lengths, but our sensors see only the total length. Sensor A reports 6 cm; sensor B reports 10 cm. These data are fictional. No division of the total between the pieces can make both readings exact at the same time.

There are two separate difficulties. First, find a total that deviates as little as possible from the readings. We measure this deviation by the sum of the squared errors and give the sensors equal weight. Then, choose how to divide the total: the same total can come from very different pieces. The data do not settle this second question.

The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse provides a precise answer: among the divisions that best fit the readings, it chooses the one with the smallest vector length, called the Euclidean norm. In our example, this will give equal pieces. This mathematical choice does not prove that they are actually equal.

Imagine making one piece longer and shortening the other by the same amount. The total does not change; the sensors do not see this change. The pseudoinverse discards this invisible component instead of claiming to have measured it. The second diagram first shows how the readings are fitted; the division will be justified later.

The rule to remember

Fit the data by least squares, then choose the smallest norm among the solutions that attain this minimum. An invisible direction remains physically unknown.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Find a total, then choose how to divide it

The fictional readings are b = (6, 10)ᵀ cm. Each sensor is modeled by p + q, where p and q are the unknown lengths in centimeters. We seek a real solution with no additional constraint, using the usual Euclidean norms. The components being compared have the same unit and the sensors have equal weight.

  1. Write what the sensors see

    The column vector x contains p and q. The matrix A transforms these lengths into predicted readings Ax. Its coefficients are dimensionless. The symbol T denotes the transpose, which exchanges rows and columns; the vector b contains the observations. The residual is r = Ax − b, in centimeters. Its squared norm is the sum of the squares of its components, in square centimeters.

    A=(1111),x=(pq),b=(610)

    The identical columns mean that A cannot distinguish the pieces. The vector (1, −1)ᵀ belongs to the null space: multiplying it by A gives the 0 vector. The rank, the number of independent directions transmitted by A, is 1. A therefore has no ordinary inverse. We do not require r to be 0: the readings contradict each other.

  2. Reverse only the transmitted directions

    For any real matrix A of size m × n, the singular value decomposition, or SVD, is written A = UΣVᵀ. The integers m and n count the observations and unknowns. U and V are orthogonal matrices, of sizes m × m and n × n: their columns are mutually perpendicular directions of unit norm. Σ has size m × n, and its only potentially nonzero entries are the singular values σ on its diagonal.

    Construct Σ⁺, of size n × m: replace each strictly positive singular value by its reciprocal 1/σ, and leave each 0 value at 0. Then put the directions back in reverse order. The + sign labels the pseudoinverse; it does not denote addition.

    A+=VΣ+UT,x⋆=A+b

    The symbol x⋆ denotes the chosen solution. It first minimizes ‖Ax − b‖²; among all minimizers of this quantity, it minimizes ‖x‖². These minimizations are successive: we do not add their objectives. The construction also exists for a rectangular matrix or one with deficient rank. If A is square and invertible, A⁺ coincides with its inverse.

  3. Construct the pseudoinverse of our matrix

    In our example, the only nonzero singular value is σ = 2. The corresponding singular vectors are u = v = (1, 1)ᵀ/√2. They have unit norm because the sum of their squared components is (1 + 1)/2 = 1. The perpendicular direction, proportional to (1, −1)ᵀ, has a 0 singular value.

    A=σuvT,u=v=12(11)

    We therefore only need to replace the factor σ by its reciprocal. The product vuᵀ has coefficients 1/2; multiplying them by 1/2 gives 1/4, or 0.25, dimensionless. The zeros have never been used as divisors.

    A+=1σvuT=14(1111)

    This expression for A⁺ is dimensionless here because A is dimensionless. The square roots serve only to normalize the directions and cancel out of the rational result.

  4. Apply the matrix to the observations

    Each row of A⁺ performs the same calculation: add the readings, then multiply by 1/4. The sum is 6 + 10 = 16 cm. Each chosen component is therefore 1/4 × 16 = 4 cm.

    x⋆=14(1111)(610)=(44) cm

    The prediction is Ax⋆ = (8, 8)ᵀ cm, since 4 + 4 = 8 cm. The residuals, prediction minus observation, are 2 cm and -2 cm. The cost is 4 + 4 = 8 cm². The result fits the sensors without reproducing their readings exactly.

  5. Prove that the residual is minimal

    Write s = p + q for the total length in centimeters. All divisions with the same s produce the same prediction. Completing the square gives the following identity for the cost J(s), in square centimeters:

    J(s)=(s−6)2+(s−10)2=2(s−8)2+8

    In this numerical formula, s is the value expressed in centimeters. A square is nonnegative. The minimum is therefore 8 cm², attained exactly at s = 8 cm. This is the same total as the mean of the readings: 16/2 = 8 cm.

    Another check: Aᵀr is the 0 vector because each component is 2 + (-2) = 0 cm. The residual is therefore perpendicular to the columns of A, as expected for least squares. This checks the fit, but does not yet check the choice of a division among all those with total 8 cm.

  6. Prove that the chosen norm is minimal

    All residual minimizers can be written x = (4 + h, 4 − h)ᵀ, where h is a signed displacement in centimeters. The added vector is in the null space of A: it does not change the total. The squared Euclidean norm is then:

    ‖x‖2=(4+h)2+(4−h)2=32+2h2

    Here again, the numerical values of h are in centimeters and the squared norm is in square centimeters. It is minimal only when h = 0, hence for x⋆ = (4, 4)ᵀ cm. Minimizing the norm or its square is equivalent because norms are nonnegative.

    For example, h = 2 cm gives (6, 2)ᵀ cm, the same total and the same cost 8 cm², but a squared norm of 36 + 4 = 40 cm², greater than 32 cm². The pseudoinverse chooses a unique solution; the data alone still do not identify the pieces.

  7. Check the pseudoinverse and limit the conclusion

    The Moore–Penrose definition can also be checked using the identities below. They characterize a unique matrix A⁺. The last two say that the indicated products are symmetric, meaning equal to their transpose.

    AA+A=A,A+AA+=A+
    (AA+)T=AA+,(A+A)T=A+A

    In our particular case, AA⁺ and A⁺A are both equal to 1/2 times the matrix A: each entry of either product is 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2. This product is symmetric and equals its square: it projects onto the vectors with equal components. It is not the identity matrix. Checking the identities above prevents confusion between the pseudoinverse and an ordinary inverse.

    We have established an optimum for a given model, units, and weights. We have not established the actual lengths, the quality of the sensors, or an experimental gain. The numerical rule that decides whether a singular value is negligible will require an analysis of conditioning; here we have worked in exact arithmetic with an exact 0.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Inverting AᵀA is enough in every case.”

    The formula inverse of (AᵀA) times Aᵀ assumes independent columns. Here they are identical and AᵀA is singular. Start again from the SVD: invert the strictly positive singular values and keep the 0 values at 0.

    Revisit the construction using singular directions

  • “A⁺b always solves Ax = b exactly.”

    Our sensors require incompatible totals. The pseudoinverse produces the residual of smallest norm, here with cost 8 cm². An exact solution exists only if b belongs to the range of A, meaning the readings that A can actually produce.

    Review the point outside the line of predictions

  • “The unique solution proves that the pieces are equal.”

    Uniqueness comes from the choice of smallest norm after fitting. Any change (h, −h) preserves the total: no reading observes it. A new measurement that separates the pieces would be needed to settle the physical question.

    Test the claim with another division

Compare with the solution

The sum of the readings is 6 + 14 = 20 cm. Each row of A⁺ gives 20/4 = 5 cm. Thus x⋆ = (5, 5)ᵀ cm and Ax⋆ = (10, 10)ᵀ cm.

Exercise 2 · Written justification

Another division fits just as well

Return to the initial readings. Compare x⋆ = (4, 4)ᵀ cm and y = (6, 2)ᵀ cm. Does y minimize the residual? Why does the pseudoinverse not choose it? Then prove the uniqueness of the choice of smallest norm for any real displacement h.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare the totals before comparing the norms of the vectors.

Hint 2

Expand (4 + h)² + (4 − h)²: the terms linear in h cancel.

Compare with the solution

Both totals are 8 cm. The predictions and residuals are therefore identical, and their minimum cost is 8 cm². Thus y is indeed a least-squares solution.

But ‖y‖² = 36 + 4 = 40 cm², compared with 32 cm² for x⋆. For any h, the squared norm is 32 + 2h²; it attains its minimum only when h = 0. This general reasoning establishes uniqueness, whereas simply comparing the two candidates would not be enough.

Check your reasoning

  • Recognize that y is also a residual minimizer.
  • Compare 40 cm² and 32 cm².
  • Justify uniqueness for any h, rather than only for these two candidates.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

The sensors agree: is the division known?

The same sensors now both report 8 cm. Someone concludes: “The pseudoinverse returns (4, 4)ᵀ cm, so both pieces really measure 4 cm.” Answer by constructing another compatible division with positive lengths. What additional observation would distinguish the two divisions?

Hint 1

The sensors agree on the total. Try moving 1 cm from one piece to the other.

Hint 2

Compare the vectors (4, 4) and (4 − 1, 4 + 1). Which measurement would differ between them?

Compare with the solution

The division (3, 5)ᵀ cm has total 8 cm. It therefore reproduces both readings exactly, just like the equal division. Its residual is 0. Its squared norm is 9 + 25 = 34 cm², greater than 32 cm². The division of smallest norm therefore remains the one returned by the pseudoinverse.

The smallest norm is a mathematical selection convention; it does not reveal the true division. Measuring the first piece separately would distinguish these two possibilities. Here, agreement between the sensors removes the inconsistency in the data but preserves the invisible direction of the model.

  • Distinguish a 0 residual from identification of the two lengths.
  • Propose a measurement that actually differs between the two divisions.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What exactly does A⁺b select when the system may be inconsistent or have several solutions?

It minimizes the norm of the residual Ax − b, then the norm of x among all minimizers of that residual. The second selection makes the choice unique without making the parameters physically identifiable.

Recall 2How do you construct A⁺ from A = UΣVᵀ, and what happens to a 0 singular value?

Construct Σ⁺ by exchanging its dimensions, replacing each strictly positive singular value σ by 1/σ, and keeping the 0 values at 0. Then A⁺ = VΣ⁺Uᵀ. There is never any division by 0.

Recall 3Under what condition is A⁺b an exact solution of Ax = b?

When b is in the range of A, meaning that there is a vector x for which Ax reproduces b exactly. Otherwise AA⁺b is its orthogonal projection onto this range and a residual remains.

Recall 4Why does the pseudoinverse not reconstruct a difference between the pieces?

The change (h, −h) is in the null space of A: it leaves the predictions unchanged. The solution of smallest norm discards this invisible direction; this does not prove that the actual difference is 0.

Recall 5What does Aᵀ(Ax − b) = 0 check, and what must still be established?

It checks the optimality of the least-squares fit. If the null space is nontrivial, several solutions may satisfy this check. The smallest norm among them must still be established to identify A⁺b.
