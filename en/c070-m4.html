Level 2 · C070·M4

Dijkstra

Your goalCalculate a shortest route with nonnegative weights, justify each settled distance and reconstruct the path that achieves it.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You need to carry a part from depot S to workshop T. You can go through archway A, over bridge B and through courtyard C. The lines on the map are the permitted links; their labels give walking times in minutes. The map and all the times are fictional. The positions shown are not to scale.

From S, reaching the archway appears to cost 8 min. But going to the bridge takes 2 min, then reaching the archway takes 3 min: that makes 5 min. The first time discovered for a place can therefore be improved. Imagine writing these times in pencil before circling some of them in ink.

Dijkstra organizes this move from tentative to final. It selects the place that is still tentative and whose best known route from the depot has the lowest cost. It settles that time, then checks what this place can improve for its neighbours. It compares complete routes from the depot, rather than just the times of the final links.

We assume the times are known, constant during the calculation, the same in both directions and additive. There is no extra waiting time and no link with a negative time. The method permits a time of 0. We are looking for a route to the workshop; we are not trying to connect all the places at the lowest total cost.

The rule to remember

Keep discovered distances tentative; settle the smallest remaining distance; offer neighbours the cost of the route through this vertex; record where each improvement comes from.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Deliver the part and justify each choice

The network is exactly the one on the map. Its link times are S–A: 8 min; S–B: 2 min; B–A: 3 min; B–C: 6 min; A–C: 1 min; A–T: 9 min; C–T: 2 min. Let us find a minimum time from S to T, then a route that achieves it.

  1. Name what we keep in pencil

    The finite graph G = (V, E) contains the vertices V, our places, and the edges E, our links. The letters s and t denote source S and destination T; u and v denote arbitrary vertices. Place names and sets have no units. For a link, w(u, v) is its time in minutes. Here, w(u, v) = w(v, u) and w(u, v) ≥ 0 min.

    d(v) is the cost of the best route discovered from s to v, in minutes. It can exceed the true minimum while v is unsettled. The symbol ∞ means that no route to v has yet been discovered; it is not a very large measured time. The set F contains the settled vertices. The predecessor p(v) is the place just before v in the recorded route: it is a vertex and has no units.

    Initially, F is empty, d(S) = 0 min and d(A) = d(B) = d(C) = d(T) = ∞. There are no predecessors yet. Staying at the depot costs nothing.

  2. The loop and why settling is valid

    At each iteration, select u outside F with the smallest finite value of d(u). If there is a tie, we choose the name that comes first alphabetically; the minimum times would stay the same with another choice. Settle u by adding it to F. For each neighbour v still outside F, compare its label with the candidate d(u) + w(u, v). This comparison is called a relaxation.

    d(v)←min(d(v),d(u)+w(u,v))

    The arrow means “replace with” and min selects the smaller value. A finite candidate improves ∞. If the candidate is strictly better, also replace p(v) with u; in a tie, keep the existing predecessor here. All the quantities added are in minutes.

    Why can we settle the minimum? The depot is correct: no route costs less than 0 min. To justify settling another vertex, consider F before adding u and assume its vertices are already correct. If a route to u were cheaper than d(u), take the first vertex y on that route still outside F, immediately after a settled vertex x. Going through x has already offered y a time no greater than the cost of the prefix of that route. The rest of the route is nonnegative, so d(y) would be less than d(u). This contradicts the selection of u as the minimum. This property, preserved at each iteration, is the correctness invariant.

    For our destination, we may stop when T is selected and settled, before examining its neighbours. Discovering it as a neighbour is not enough. If no finite label remains before that point, the remaining vertices are unreachable from S. The same mechanism works for a directed graph, examining only outgoing links.

  3. Settle the depot and open up the possibilities

    S is the only candidate with a finite time: settle d(S) = 0 min. For A, offer 0 + 8 = 8 min and record p(A) = S. For B, offer 0 + 2 = 2 min and record p(B) = S. These candidates replace ∞ because they describe existing routes.

    After this iteration: S is settled; A has 8 min, B has 2 min, and C and T have ∞. A and B remain tentative. The next choice must compare 2 and 8, rather than follow the order of the letters in the drawing.

  4. Settle the bridge and revise the archway

    B has the smallest tentative label, 2 min: settle B. The link back to S is ignored because S is already settled. For A, calculate 2 + 3 = 5 min. This is less than the 8 min of the direct route: replace d(A) and replace p(A) with B.

    For C, calculate 2 + 6 = 8 min. Replace ∞ with this time and record p(C) = B. The settled vertices are S and B; the tentative ones are A at 5 min, C at 8 min and T at ∞. A discovered time has therefore not been treated as final.

  5. Settle the archway and discover the workshop

    Select A: 5 min is less than C's 8 min. A becomes settled. Its neighbours S and B are already settled. For C, the new candidate is 5 + 1 = 6 min, better than 8 min: replace d(C) and record p(C) = A.

    For T, offer 5 + 9 = 14 min and record p(T) = A. T has now been discovered, but do not announce it as the solution yet. The remaining candidates are C at 6 min and T at 14 min.

  6. Go through the courtyard, then settle the workshop

    Select C at 6 min. Its neighbours A and B are already settled. The link to T gives 6 + 2 = 8 min, less than the recorded 14 min. Update d(T) and replace its predecessor with C.

    T is then the only remaining vertex; select it at 8 min and settle it. We can stop. The settling order is S, B, A, C, T and the corresponding final distances are 0, 2, 5, 6, 8 min. The justification of the minimum comes from the selection rule and the nonnegative weights, rather than from reaching T for the first time.

  7. Recover the route and check its cost

    Follow the predecessors backwards: p(T) = C, p(C) = A, p(A) = B and p(B) = S. This goes from the destination to the start; reverse it to obtain S–B–A–C–T. The sum is 2 + 3 + 1 + 2 = 8 min, matching T's final label. The distance and the route provide a check on each other.

    This route uses 4 links. S–A–T uses only 2, but costs 8 + 9 = 17 min. A breadth-first search minimizes the number of links; with different times, this does not necessarily minimize the minutes. The route S–B–C–T also costs more: 2 + 6 + 2 = 10 min. Comparing these alternatives is useful, but does not by itself prove that no other route does better.

  8. A check of the minimum independent of the iterations

    Take the final labels again. For each link, check that the absolute value of the difference between its labels does not exceed its time. The absolute value, written using bars, gives the size of the difference without its sign.

    |d(v)−d(u)|≤w(u,v)

    All the following comparisons are in minutes: S–A, 5 ≤ 8; S–B, 2 ≤ 2; B–A, 3 ≤ 3; B–C, 4 ≤ 6; A–C, 1 ≤ 1; A–T, 3 ≤ 9; C–T, 2 ≤ 2. The check covers every link in the network, in both directions thanks to the absolute value.

    On any route from S to T, each link therefore costs at least the increase in the label that it allows, even if this increase is negative. When these increases are added, the intermediate labels cancel: what remains is d(T) − d(S) = 8 − 0 = 8 min. No route can cost less. Our route attains this bound, so it is indeed minimal. This check gives a proof; a simple list of competing routes did not yet provide one.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculation iterations. Why can A change from 8 to 5 min, while B, settled at 2 min, will no longer be revised? Then explain why seeing T among A's neighbours does not yet allow us to stop.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I have discovered the workshop: its distance is final.”

    After A, T has 14 min; after C, it changes to 8 min. Circle a distance only when its vertex is selected as the tentative minimum. Also change the predecessor when a better route appears.

    Review the steps through the archway and the courtyard

  • “The route with the fewest links will be the fastest.”

    S–A–T uses 2 links and costs 17 min; S–B–A–C–T uses 4 and costs 8 min. State what you are minimizing. Counting links and adding times answer different questions.

    Review the check of the reconstructed route

  • “Settling would remain guaranteed with any weights.”

    The proof uses the fact that adding the rest of a route does not reduce its cost. A link with a weight less than 0 would destroy this argument. The walking times in our scene are not negative; for other costs, check this assumption before using Dijkstra and choose a suitable method if it fails.

Exercise 1 · Local application

A label and its predecessor

In the original network, A has just been settled at 5 min. C still has 8 min with p(C) = B; A–C costs 1 min. Write the candidate, decide whether it improves C and give its new predecessor. Can C be settled solely because of this improvement?

Hint 1

Add the distance from the depot to A and the time for A–C.

Hint 2

An improvement changes the recorded route. Settling then requires a choice among all the remaining candidates.

Compare with the solution

The candidate is 5 + 1 = 6 min, less than 8 min. We therefore keep d(C) = 6 min and p(C) = A. Keeping p(C) = B would describe a route whose cost no longer matches the label.

Relaxation leaves C tentative. In this network, C will indeed be the next minimum, ahead of T at 14 min; this comparison will allow it to be settled at the next iteration.

Check your reasoning

  • The candidate starts from 5 min, rather than the time for a single link.
  • You compare 6 with 8 min and also replace the predecessor.
  • You distinguish improving the label from selecting the next vertex.

Exercise 2 · Recalculation on paper

The link from the courtyard to the workshop closes

Use the same map, removing only C–T in both directions. All other times remain unchanged. Run Dijkstra again from S, give the settling order up to T, the minimum time and the route. Explain why T remains reachable.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The closure does not change any candidate before C is settled.

Hint 2

When C is settled, can it still improve the label for T provided by A? The link A–T still exists.

Compare with the solution

S gives A at 8 min and B at 2 min. B improves A to 5 min and gives C at 8 min. A improves C to 6 min and gives T at 14 min. The same comparisons therefore lead to settling S, B, A, C.

C no longer has a link to T; its other neighbours are already settled. T's label remains 14 min and T is then settled. Following the predecessors backwards gives T, A, B, S, hence the route S–B–A–T.

Its cost is 2 + 3 + 9 = 14 min and it uses 3 links. The closure of C–T rules out the old optimal route, but not every route: A–T still lets us reach the workshop. The direct alternative S–A–T would still cost 17 min.

Check your reasoning

  • You remove C–T without inventing another time or another link.
  • You settle C before T, because 6 min is less than 14 min.
  • You reconstruct S–B–A–T and check the sum of 14 min.
  • You justify reachability with a route present in the modified network.

Exercise 3 · Audit and justification

A correct time with an incorrect route

Return to the original network. A note states “minimum: 8 min; route: S–A–C–T”. It gives the final labels S: 0, B: 2, A: 5, C: 6, T: 8 min. Check whether the claim is consistent, repair the route and prove the minimum using the labels and links. Simply saying “Dijkstra found this value” is not enough.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start by adding the times of the route actually written down.

Hint 2

Check on every link that the absolute difference between the labels is less than or equal to the time. Then add the label increases along an arbitrary route.

Compare with the solution

S–A–C–T costs 8 + 1 = 9 min to reach C, then 9 + 2 = 11 min to reach T. This route therefore does not achieve the stated time. The note mixes a correct value with an incorrect reconstruction.

The repaired route is S–B–A–C–T, with cost 2 + 3 + 1 + 2 = 8 min. To establish that this is a minimum, we still need to rule out all cheaper routes, rather than just the one that was written incorrectly.

The absolute differences, in minutes, all pass the check: S–A, 5 ≤ 8; S–B, 2 ≤ 2; B–A, 3 ≤ 3; B–C, 4 ≤ 6; A–C, 1 ≤ 1; A–T, 3 ≤ 9; C–T, 2 ≤ 2.

Each link cost is therefore greater than or equal to the label increase in the direction travelled. On any S–T route, the increases cancel at intermediate vertices and total 8 − 0 = 8 min. Every route costs at least 8 min; the repaired route achieves this value. The value and the proof are now consistent.

Check your reasoning

  • You calculate 11 min for the written route and refuse to assign it 8 min.
  • You provide a feasible route with cost 8 min.
  • You check every link and explain how the intermediate labels cancel.
  • You distinguish proving that a cost can be achieved from proving that no lower cost can be achieved.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does d(v) mean while v is unsettled?

The cost of the best route discovered from the source. The true minimum may still be smaller. ∞ marks the absence of a discovered route, rather than a measured time.

Recall 2What quantity does Dijkstra compare to select the next vertex?

The finite tentative distances from the source, among all unsettled vertices. It does not simply select the cheapest link from the last vertex visited.

Recall 3What should you do when going through u improves v?

Replace d(v) with d(u) + w(u, v) and p(v) with u. The new distance then matches the newly recorded route. A tie preserves the existing predecessor here.

Recall 4Where are nonnegative weights used in the proof for settling a vertex?

The rest of a route cannot reduce its cost. A route that would improve on the selected minimum would therefore already have a first unsettled vertex with a smaller label, a contradiction. Negative weights remove this guarantee.

Recall 5When can we stop for the workshop, and how do we recover the route?

When T is selected as the smallest tentative distance and settled. Follow its predecessors backwards to S, then reverse the list and add the weights. Discovering T as a neighbour is not enough.

Reconstruct the reasoning

What you understand
A route can be minimal in number of links without being minimal in minutes. Dijkstra handles a sum of fixed nonnegative times here. A score attached to a link is not automatically a cost with these properties.
