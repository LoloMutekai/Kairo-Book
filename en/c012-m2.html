Sets

Your goalGroup the boxes on a table, construct an intersection, a union and a complement, then check the members before counting them.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You are preparing the lunch boxes on a table. A is closed and unlabeled; B is closed and labeled; C is open and labeled; D is open and unlabeled. You want to be able to ask for the closed boxes, the labeled boxes, or the boxes that meet both conditions.

A set is a collection defined by its members: the objects that belong to it. We will call the group of closed boxes F and the group of labeled boxes E. B belongs to both F and E. You can list it in each group without making a new box: it is still B.

The order in which you name A and B does not change the members of the group. Repeating the name B does not create an extra member either. A set keeps neither the order of presentation nor the number of repetitions of a member.

The table defines our universe, called U: all the boxes we can select from. The 4 boxes, their names and their properties are fictional data chosen for learning. They remain unchanged throughout the example. We are not looking for any boxes in another room.

The rule to remember

State the universe, test membership, write the members without repetition, then count. The connectives and, inclusive or and not give the selection rules.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Grouping boxes without duplicating them

Using the same boxes and properties, let us construct F∩E, F∪E and the complement of F in U. Each result will first be a list of distinct members, then a number of boxes.

  1. Defining the sets and notation

    Curly braces { } enclose the members of a set, separated by commas. The sign = says that the members on the left and right are exactly the same. U is the universe of boxes on the table; F groups its closed boxes; E groups its labeled boxes.

    U={A,B,C,D}
    F={A,B};E={B,C}

    The letter x will stand for any box in U. The sign ∈ is read “belongs to”; ∉ is read “does not belong to”. Thus B∈F is true and D∉F is true. These signs express membership, not physical movement.

    For a finite set S, |S| denotes its cardinality: the number of distinct members. Here the bars ask us to count, not to measure a length. Cardinalities are whole numbers with no physical unit; we specify “boxes” to say what is being counted. We read |U| = 4 boxes, |F| = 2 boxes and |E| = 2 boxes.

  2. Distinguishing membership from inclusion

    F⊆U is read “F is a subset of U”: every member of F belongs to U. This is true because A and B are on the table. The sign ⊆ compares sets; here ∈ links a box to a set. B∈F refers to the box B, whereas {B}⊆F refers to the group that contains only B.

    Inclusion allows equality: U⊆U is true. To compare {A,B} and {B,A}, we check only the members; these expressions therefore denote the same set. The group E is not a subset of F, because C belongs to E but not to F.

  3. Combining the conditions: the intersection

    F∩E, the intersection, contains exactly the boxes that belong to F and to E. For a box x in U, x∈F∩E therefore means “x∈F and x∈E”. Both conditions must be met.

    A is closed but unlabeled: we exclude it. B meets both conditions: we keep it. C is not closed and D meets neither condition: we exclude them. The result is a set whose only member is B.

    F∩E={B};|F∩E|=1 boîte

    We check against the original lists: B appears in each; no other letter has this property.

  4. Bringing the members together: the union

    F∪E, the union, contains exactly the boxes that belong to F or to E. This or is inclusive: a box that belongs to both groups is also kept. We keep A because it is in F, B because it is in F and E, and C because it is in E; we exclude D.

    F∪E={A,B,C}

    Adding |F| and |E| gives 2 + 2 = 4 counts in total. B is then counted in F and again in E. To count the distinct members, we must remove its extra count. There is 1 shared box whose extra count must be subtracted, without removing B from the union.

    |F∪E|=|F|+|E|−|F∩E|
    |F∪E|=2+2−1=3 boîtes

    This counting rule holds for finite sets: shared members appear in each initial count, so we subtract their extra count. A direct check gives the same 3 members: A, B and C.

  5. Finding what remains in the stated universe

    The complement of F in U is written U∖F. The sign ∖ indicates that we keep the members of the first set that do not belong to the second. For a box x, x∈U∖F means “x∈U and x∉F”. We therefore start with the whole table, then remove A and B from the selected group.

    U∖F={C,D}

    Since F is a subset of U and these sets are finite, the remaining count is the total for U minus the count for F.

    |U∖F|=|U|−|F|=4−2=2 boîtes

    Check: C and D are exactly the open boxes on this table. E is not the complement of F: it contains B, which is closed, and leaves out D, which is open. The words “not in F” do not mean “in E”.

  6. Checking the count without repeating the same addition

    Let us classify each box only once according to its properties. A is in F only: 1 box. B is in F and E: 1 box. C is in E only: 1 box. D is outside the union: 1 box. These groups do not overlap and cover the whole table.

    For the union, the groups containing A and B give 1 + 1 = 2 boxes; adding the group containing C gives 2 + 1 = 3 boxes. We obtain the result again without subtracting any overlap, because no group is counted twice.

    For the universe, the union and D give 3 + 1 = 4 boxes, as in U. For the complement of F, C and D give 1 + 1 = 2 boxes. The lists and the counts agree. A correct count alone would not guarantee that the right members had been selected.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the final lists and redraw the table. Find the members of the intersection, the union and the complement of F. Explain why we remove an extra count of B for the union while keeping B among its members.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If I write B before A, or if I repeat B, I have changed the set.”

    Check which members are present. {A,B}, {B,A} and {A,B,B} denote the same set of 2 distinct boxes. The notation changes; the members remain A and B. To distinguish a box from a group, compare B∈F and {B}⊆F.

    Return to membership and inclusion

  • “The union contains 4 boxes, since each group contains 2.”

    Write the names: A and B for F, B and C for E. The name B appears in each count, but refers to only 1 box. Remove its extra count: 4 − 1 = 3 boxes. Removing B from the selection would be another error.

    Review the union and its check

  • “The complement of F must be E.”

    Start again from U and look for the boxes that are not closed. You get C and D, whereas E contains B and C. Specifying the universe also prevents you from looking for boxes beyond those on the table.

    Place D back inside the rectangle representing the universe

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · reading notation

Same group, different notation

Someone writes {B,A,B} for F. Have they changed the set? Give its cardinality, then explain the difference between the statements B∈F and {B}⊆F.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write the distinct names, checking off each box that is present.

Hint 2

B∈F refers to a box; {B}⊆F compares a group with F.

Compare with the solution

The set is still F={A,B}. Repeating B and changing the order of notation do not change the members. Its cardinality is 2 boxes, since A and B are the only distinct members.

B∈F is true: the box B is a member of F. {B}⊆F is also true: every member of the group {B}, namely B, belongs to F. The signs do not ask the same question.

Check your reasoning

  • You keep A and B and obtain 2 distinct boxes.
  • You connect ∈ with a box being a member and ⊆ with inclusion between sets.
  • You justify the statements using the members, without referring to their position in the notation.

Exercise 2 · Application · calculation and checking

Correcting a proposed union

With F={A,B} and E={B,C}, someone states F∩E={B} and F∪E={A,B,B,C}, then counts 4 boxes in the union. Write the correct members and cardinality of each result; explain exactly what is wrong with their count.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The intersection requires both conditions to be met; the union accepts either condition or both.

Hint 2

Distinguish the set written with a repetition from the number of distinct members it contains.

Compare with the solution

The stated intersection is correct: B is shared by the groups, so F∩E={B} and |F∩E|=1 box.

The notation {A,B,B,C} does denote the same set as {A,B,C}: repeating B does not create a member. However, the count of 4 boxes is wrong. The union has 3 distinct members: A, B and C.

The formula confirms the direct count: 2 + 2 − 1 = 3 boxes. We remove the extra count of B; we do not remove B from the union. D is excluded because it belongs to neither F nor E.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain {B} for the intersection and {A,B,C} for the union.
  • You distinguish the redundant notation, which denotes the correct set, from the incorrect cardinality.
  • You explain why B stays in the union and check the cardinality with a direct count.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · changing the reference universe

The same table without D

D is removed from the table and the new universe U′={A,B,C} is stated. The prime ′ simply indicates this modified universe. F={A,B} and E={B,C} remain unchanged. Someone claims: “The complement of F is still {C,D}, since F has not changed.” Answer with the members and a cardinality calculation. Does the union F∪E change?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The complement must be a subset of the universe that has just been stated.

Hint 2

Look in U′ for the members that are not in F; for the union, reread only the members of F and E.

Compare with the solution

D no longer belongs to U′, so it cannot belong to the complement of F in U′. The only remaining member is C, giving U′∖F={C}.

The new universe contains 4 − 1 = 3 boxes. F remains a subset of U′ and contains 2 boxes; the complement contains 3 − 2 = 1 box. In U, it contained 2 boxes. The same F can therefore have different complements depending on the universe.

The union remains F∪E={A,B,C}, with a cardinality of 3 boxes. It depends on the members of F and E, which have not changed. No box removed from the table belonged to this union.

Check your reasoning

  • You state U′ and give {C}, without keeping D in the complement.
  • You justify 1 box using 3 − 2 and the list of members.
  • You explain why the complement changes while the union stays the same.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why do {A,B}, {B,A} and {A,B,B} denote the same set?

The distinct members are always A and B. A set takes no account of order or repetitions; its cardinality is 2 boxes.

Recall 2What do B∈F and {B}⊆F say? Does inclusion allow equality?

B∈F says that the box B belongs to F. {B}⊆F says that every member of the group {B} belongs to F. Inclusion allows equality: U⊆U is true.

Recall 3How can you reconstruct the intersection and union from membership?

The intersection applies and: F∩E={B}. The union applies inclusive or: F∪E={A,B,C}. B is kept in the union because belonging to both groups also satisfies this or.

Recall 4Why subtract |F∩E| from |F|+|E|, and exactly what is removed?

Shared members appear in each initial count. We remove their extra count without removing these members from the union: 2 + 2 − 1 = 3 boxes.

Recall 5What must you state before finding the complement of F? What happens to it when D leaves the table?

You must state the universe. In U={A,B,C,D}, U∖F={C,D}. In U′={A,B,C}, U′∖F={C}. We keep only the members of the stated universe that do not belong to F.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the table and name the universe. Explain what ∈ and ⊆ compare, then translate and into an intersection, inclusive or into a union and not into a complement relative to the universe. Finish by finding the members and cardinalities, with an explanation of the extra count of B.
