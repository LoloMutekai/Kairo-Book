Level 2 · C127·M1

Parametric curves

Your goalDescribe a path using a parameter, calculate its position and tangent, then distinguish the rail, the direction of travel, and the speed along it.

Before you start
c058-m5 : change of variables · c057-m5 : Hessian · c027-m5 : complex numbers

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a small trolley on a rail laid flat. The rail curves to the left, then back to the right, while the trolley moves toward the top of the plane. A photograph gives its position; a sequence of photographs with timestamps also gives the order of travel. The scene and all its values are fictional.

To locate the trolley, we give its x-coordinate and y-coordinate at the same instant t. The rule r assigns the pair r(t) = (x(t), y(t)) to each t. This is a parametric curve: the parameter is an input, and the pair of coordinates is the output. Here the parameter is time in seconds; positions are in metres.

The trace is the set of positions visited, in other words the rail described by this rule over its interval. The parametrization keeps additional information: which point corresponds to which parameter value. The orientation is the direction of travel as this parameter increases. The same trace can therefore be followed in different directions or at different speeds.

The diagram shows D and F at the same x-coordinate 1 m, but at different y-coordinates. A single value y = f(x) cannot describe this entire rail: at this x-coordinate, it would have to give both 0 m and 2 m. The parameter distinguishes the visits without difficulty.

The derivative r′ indicates how the position changes instantaneously. With our time in seconds, it is a velocity vector in metres per second. Its direction gives a tangent when this vector is nonzero. If the parameter were simply a reference variable without a unit of time, its derivative would not be a physical velocity in metres per second.

The rule to remember

Always give the rule and its interval. To find the tangent, differentiate the coordinates with respect to the same parameter; to compare two ways of travelling along the rail, examine the trace, the direction of travel, and the norm of the derivative separately.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Pass the leftmost point of the rail, then change the control rule

In a fixed orthonormal coordinate system, x(t) is b times the square of (t − τ), and y(t) = ct, from t = 0 s to t = 2 s. The fictional coefficients are τ = 1 s, b = 1 m/s² and c = 1 m/s. Let us calculate the passage through A at t = τ, then compare a short interval and a faster control rule. The model describes ideal positions; it models neither a motor nor friction.

  1. Read the rule with its units and domain

    The symbol r denotes the position vector; x and y are its coordinates in metres. The set I is the interval of allowed times, t is time in seconds, and τ is a reference instant. The coefficient b converts a squared duration into a length; c converts a duration into a length. Subtracting τ before squaring locates the leftmost point of the rail.

    I=[0;2] s
    r(t)=(b(t−τ)2;ct)

    We assume the coordinates are continuous and continuously differentiable: their derivatives exist and vary continuously. Here they are polynomials, so this assumption is satisfied. Orthonormal means that the axes are perpendicular and use the same unit of length; the Euclidean norm will then give the speed.

  2. Find the point and check the rail

    At t = τ, the duration t − τ is 1 − 1 = 0 s. Its square is 0 s². Thus x(τ) = 1 × 0 = 0 m and y(τ) = 1 × 1 = 1 m. This gives A = (0 m ; 1 m).

    At the endpoints, the shifted durations are -1 s and 1 s; their squares are 1 s² and 1 s². D = (1 m ; 0 m) and F = (1 m ; 2 m). Since c is strictly positive, y increases with t: the path does follow D → A → F.

    Independent check: since y = ct and c is nonzero, we recover t = y/c. On the trace, x is therefore b times the square of (y/c − τ), with y between 0 m and 2 m. The positions D, A and F satisfy this relation. Without this interval, the equation would describe more than our section of rail.

  3. Differentiate the coordinates and check regularity

    v(t)=r′(t)=(2b(t−τ);c)

    The prime ′ means “derivative with respect to the parameter”. The square gives the factor 2; the derivative of t − τ with respect to t is 1, because τ is fixed. The derivative of ct is c. Each component of v has the unit m/s. The factor 2b is 2 m/s².

    A parametrization is regular wherever its derivative differs from the vector (0 ; 0). Here the vertical component is always c = 1 m/s: the vector is never 0 on I. A 0 derivative would not, by itself, be enough to decide whether the trace has a tangent; it would make this method of finding it fail.

  4. Calculate the velocity and tangent line at A

    The horizontal component is 2 × 0 = 0 m/s; the vertical component is 1 m/s. The vector v(τ) = (0 ; 1) m/s therefore points vertically upward. The norm, written with double bars, is the length of this vector in the orthonormal coordinate system.

    ‖v(τ)‖2=(0 m/s)2+(1 m/s)2=1 m²/s²

    The squares of the components are 0 and 1 m²/s². The speed is the positive square root of their sum: here it is 1 m/s. Check: squaring it gives 1 m²/s² again.

    ℓ(λ)=r(τ)+λr′(τ)

    ℓ denotes the tangent line, and λ is a free real parameter in seconds. Adding λ times a velocity to A does produce a position in metres. Here ℓ(λ) = (0 m ; 1 m + cλ): the line it traces is x = 0 m. λ can take any real value; this line does not predict the future position of the trolley on the rail.

  5. Distinguish a displacement from an instantaneous velocity

    Take h = 1/2 s, or 0.5 s. The next time is τ + h = 1.5 s. The offset from τ is 0.5 s, its square is 0.25 s², and the next position is (0.25 m ; 1.5 m).

    Subtracting A gives the displacement: (0.25 m ; 0.5 m). Dividing each component by h gives the average velocity over this interval: (0.5 ; 1) m/s. Its horizontal component differs from that of v(τ). The line joining the positions at the two times is a secant.

    r(τ+h)−r(τ)h=(bh;c)→(0;c)

    This alternative approach checks the derivative: near τ, the horizontal displacement is b times the square of h, and the vertical displacement is ch. For nonzero h, dividing by h gives (bh ; c). As h tends to 0 while staying within the domain, we recover v(τ). An interval being short does not make this average exactly equal to the instantaneous velocity.

  6. Change the parameter without changing the rail

    Now use the control rule q(s) = r(ks), with dimensionless k = 2 and s in seconds. q is the new position rule. To stay within I, s must vary from 0 s to 1 s. The correspondence t = ks reaches each original time once and in the same order, because k is positive: the trace and orientation are preserved.

    q′(s)=kr′(ks)

    This is the chain rule for differentiating a composition: a small change in s produces a change in t that is k times as large. The passage through A occurs at s = τ/k = 0.5 s. The velocity at A becomes (0 ; 2) m/s. Its norm is 2 m/s, instead of 1 m/s; the complete journey takes 1 s instead of 2 s.

    More generally, for q(s) = r(φ(s)), the function φ converts the new parameter to the old one: q′(s) = r′(φ(s))φ′(s). If φ covers all of I, is continuously differentiable, and has an everywhere positive derivative, the direction of travel is preserved; with an everywhere negative derivative, it is reversed. A derivative of φ that becomes 0 can cause regularity to be lost. Here s is also time in seconds: q describes a different physical control rule on the same rail.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Reconstruct A, its velocity and its tangent. Explain why a vertical tangent is still possible, why the secant gives a different horizontal component, and why the new control rule preserves the rail while changing the passage through A.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The parameter t must be the x-coordinate.”

    For the rail, t is the input to a rule that calculates x and y. D and F share x = 1 m, but their times and y-coordinates differ. First identify the input and the output coordinates; a parameter need not even be time.

    Review D and F in the rail diagram

  • “The tangent gives the exact next position.”

    The tangent is a local line constructed using the derivative. At τ + 0.5 s, the rail gives x = 0.25 m, whereas the tangent at A stays at x = 0 m. Work through the difference of positions and its division by the duration again to separate displacement, secant and limit.

    Work through the short interval around A again

  • “The same curve means the same speed and direction of travel.”

    The trace keeps only the positions. q(s) = r(ks) visits the same points faster; the last exercise visits them in reverse order. Check the domain of the new rule and the sign of the derivative of the parameter change.

    Test the return control rule on the rail

Exercise 1 · Application · coordinates and units

Before the leftmost point of the rail

Using the original rule r, calculate the position at t = 0.5 s. Is the trolley to the left or right of A? Is its x-coordinate already increasing? Justify the horizontal direction using the derivative, rather than just the sign of x.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate t − τ. Its square and its sign do not give the same information.

Hint 2

The horizontal position uses the square; the horizontal velocity uses 2b(t − τ).

Compare with the solution

The offset is 0.5 − 1 = -0.5 s, and its square is 0.25 s². The position is therefore (0.25 m ; 0.5 m). Its x-coordinate exceeds 0 m: the trolley is to the right of A.

The horizontal velocity is 2 × (-0.5) = -1 m/s; the vertical velocity is 1 m/s. The trolley therefore moves to the left and upward. A positive x-coordinate does not mean that the x-coordinate is increasing.

Check your reasoning

  • Write the signed offset, then its square with the appropriate unit.
  • Obtain the coordinates (0.25 m ; 0.5 m).
  • Distinguish being to the right of A from moving to the left.

Exercise 2 · Comparative calculation · secant and tangent

Is an average velocity already the derivative?

Between t = 0.5 s and t = 1 s, calculate the displacement and then the average velocity. Compare it with the velocity vector at t = 0.5 s. Is the secant through the two positions the tangent at the start of this interval?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Subtract the initial position from the final position, in that order, then divide each component by h = 0.5 s.

Hint 2

The vertical component is identical in this comparison. Compare the horizontal components; having the same direction would require the vectors to be proportional.

Compare with the solution

The displacement is (0 − 0.25 ; 1 − 0.5) m, or (-0.25 m ; 0.5 m). Dividing by 0.5 s gives the average velocity (-0.5 ; 1) m/s.

At the start of the interval, the derivative is (-1 ; 1) m/s. The equal nonzero vertical components would require a proportionality factor equal to 1, but the horizontal components differ. The directions are therefore different: the secant is not this tangent.

Calculation check: multiplying the average velocity by 0.5 s recovers the displacement. This checks an average over a finite duration, without making it a derivative. To calculate the derivative by this method, we would need to take a limit as the duration tends to 0.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the negative sign of the horizontal displacement.
  • Give a displacement in metres and an average velocity in metres per second.
  • Compare the directions using both components and justify why they do not coincide.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · change of parameter

Return along the same rail

A return control rule is p(s) = r(T − s), with T = 2 s and s between 0 s and T. Determine the start and end points, the passage through A, and the velocity there. Are the trace, orientation and speed at A preserved?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Substitute the endpoints of the interval for s before differentiating. The original time t = T − s decreases.

Hint 2

The derivative of T − s is −1. Apply the chain rule to r, then take the norm to find the speed.

Compare with the solution

p is the new position and s is its time; T is the fixed duration of the original journey. For s = 0 s, the original time is T: the start is F = (1 m ; 2 m). For s = T, it is 0 s: the end is D = (1 m ; 0 m). The entire interval of original times is covered; the trace is preserved, but the orientation is reversed.

Passing through A requires T − s = τ, so s = 2 − 1 = 1 s. The derivative is p′(s) = −r′(T − s). At A, it is (0 ; -1) m/s: the velocity vector points downward. Its norm remains 1 m/s, because changing the sign of a vector does not change its length.

The geometric tangent remains the vertical line x = 0 m. The direction of travel and the velocity vector have changed; this line and the trace of the rail are preserved. Unlike the fast control rule q, this return does not scale the speed at A.

Check your reasoning

  • Check the domain and the exchange of endpoints D and F.
  • Include the negative sign in the derivative and give its unit.
  • Distinguish the preserved trace, reversed orientation and preserved norm at A.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What information defines a parametric curve, and what is lost if we keep only its trace?

A rule r(t) giving the coordinates and a domain I for the parameter. The trace keeps only the points reached; it loses the correspondence with the parameter, and therefore the order of visits and, if the parameter is time, their timing.

Recall 2Under what condition does r′ give a tangent, and which line do we construct?

At the point being considered, the coordinates must be differentiable and r′ must be nonzero. The line is r(t) + λr′(t), with λ free and with a suitable unit. When time is in seconds, λ is in seconds. A 0 derivative vector does not allow a conclusion using this construction.

Recall 3When is the norm of r′ a physical speed in metres per second?

When r describes a position in metres, the parameter is time in seconds, and the norm is taken in an orthonormal coordinate system. With another parameter, the derivative describes a change per unit of that parameter.

Recall 4Why does dividing a displacement by a short duration not necessarily give the instantaneous velocity?

This quotient gives an average velocity. The derivative is its limit as the duration tends to 0. At the leftmost point of our rail, the average is (bh ; c), while the derivative is (0 ; c).

Recall 5What should we examine for q(s) = r(φ(s))?

The domain and image of φ to preserve the trace, then q′(s) = r′(φ(s))φ′(s). A positive derivative of φ preserves the direction of travel, and a negative one reverses it; its absolute value multiplies the norm at the corresponding point. A 0 derivative can cause regularity to be lost.
