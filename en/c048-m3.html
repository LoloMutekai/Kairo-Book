Level 1 · C048·M3

Symmetric matrices

Your goalRecognize a real symmetric matrix and construct its diagonalization using orthogonal eigendirections.

Before you start
c040-m5 : least squares · c048-m1 : eigenvalues · c048-m2 : diagonalization

✿01 See the idea

A fictional control panel adjusts 2 lighting zones, left and right. Adjusting the left control produces a direct response with coefficient 3 on the left and a cross response with coefficient 1 on the right. Adjusting the right control reverses the roles: 1 on the left and 3 on the right. The cross effects match exactly.

Arrange the responses in rows and the control inputs in columns, with left first and right second. The array in the first diagram stays the same when its rows and columns are exchanged. This property is called matrix symmetry. Its mirror line is the diagonal from the top left to the bottom right; this does not mean flipping the array from left to right.

Next, try moving the controls together, then in opposite directions. In our array, these ways of adjusting the controls stay along their own directions after the transformation. They are perpendicular in the plane of the control settings. Symmetry allows us to choose perpendicular eigendirections, which a general diagonalization does not guarantee.

All the data are fictional. We represent deviations from a reference lighting level, divided by a common scale: inputs, outputs, and coefficients are dimensionless. A negative deviation means a decrease relative to this reference. The model is real, linear, and has no saturation within the range studied; its symmetry is an assumption of the exercise, not a universal law of lighting.

The rule to remember

Compare each coefficient with the one obtained by exchanging its row and column. For a real symmetric matrix, then choose an orthonormal eigenbasis: the transpose of the change of basis matrix is then its inverse.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Separating the inputs of the same control panel

The control panel receives the input x = (2, 0)ᵀ. Let us calculate its output y = Ax and recover it by separating an input that moves the controls together from an input that moves them in opposition. All numerical quantities in this example are dimensionless.

  1. Read the array, then its transpose

    x is the column of control inputs; y is the column of responses. L and R denote left and right. The coefficient aᵢⱼ of A gives the effect of control input j on response i, where i and j identify a row and a column. The symbol ᵀ denotes transposition: rows become columns, with no change of signs.

    A=[3113]=Aᵀ

    A real square matrix is symmetric if Aᵀ = A, that is, if aᵢⱼ = aⱼᵢ for every pair of indices. Diagonal entries correspond to themselves: they do not have to equal one another. A nonsquare array cannot satisfy this equality of matrices.

  2. Check the proposed eigendirections

    Let p+ denote the column (1, 1)ᵀ and p- the column (1, -1)ᵀ. The + sign names the input that moves the controls together, and the − sign names the input that moves them in opposition. These are nonzero vectors. An eigenvalue λ is a real factor such that Ap = λp: the output stays on the line through the eigenvector p.

    Ap+=[3+11+3]=4p+

    For p-, the first component is 3 × 1 + 1 × (-1) = 2. The second is 1 × 1 + 3 × (-1) = 1 + (-3) = -2. Thus Ap- = 2p-. The eigenvalues are λ+ = 4 and λ- = 2. We have checked them by multiplication, without inferring them from the diagram.

  3. Explain perpendicularity

    The dot product p+·p- is 1 × 1 + 1 × (-1) = 1 + (-1) = 0. The directions are therefore orthogonal. The norm of each vector is √2, because the sum of the squares of its components is 2.

    Here is why symmetry matters. For any real vectors u and v, (Au)·v = u·(Aᵀv). This identity comes from the same sums of products, grouped by rows or by columns. If Aᵀ = A, Au = λu, and Av = μv, with λ and μ the associated eigenvalues, we obtain:

    λ(u·v)=μ(u·v)⇒(λ−μ)(u·v)=0

    If λ and μ are distinct, their difference is nonzero and u·v = 0. If they are equal, this reasoning imposes no condition on an arbitrarily chosen pair. The eigenspace is the set of vectors that A multiplies by the eigenvalue under consideration, including the 0 vector. We can choose an orthonormal basis within this space. The real spectral theorem states that every real symmetric matrix has a real orthonormal eigenbasis. The general existence result is accepted here; our calculation constructs such a basis for this array.

  4. Normalize before replacing the inverse with the transpose

    An orthonormal basis combines perpendicularity with norm 1. Set q+ = p+/√2 and q- = p-/√2. The matrix Q has these vectors as its columns. Let R be the matrix whose columns are the unnormalized p vectors; then Q = R/√2.

    Q=12[111-1]

    The dot products of the columns give RᵀR = 2I, where I is the identity matrix: its diagonal entries are 1 and its other entries are 0. The common factor appears in both matrices of the product, so QᵀQ = RᵀR/2 = I. Q is called orthogonal; its inverse is Qᵀ. R does not have this property: its transpose alone is not enough to reverse the change of basis. All these vectors, norms, and factors are dimensionless.

  5. Calculate the matrix in the new basis

    The entry in row + and column − of RᵀAR is p+·(Ap-). Since Ap- = λ-p- and p+·p- = 0, this entry is 0. The other off-diagonal entry vanishes in the same way. The +,+ entry is λ+ times the square of the norm of p+: 4 × 2 = 8. The −,− entry is 2 × 2 = 4.

    QᵀAQ=12[8004]=[4002]=D

    D denotes the diagonal matrix of eigenvalues, in the same order as the columns of Q. The factor 1/2 comes from normalization, not from the lighting system. We obtain A = QDQᵀ: changing basis, multiplying each coordinate by its eigenvalue, then returning to the original basis reproduces A. The standalone letter D denotes this matrix; the right side of the control panel is labeled R.

  6. Check using the control panel input

    Direct calculation: y_L = 3 × 2 + 1 × 0 = 6; y_R = 1 × 2 + 3 × 0 = 2. Let us check using the eigendirections without rounding the square roots.

    Write x = c+p+ + c-p-. The coefficients c+ and c- are the amplitudes in this unnormalized orthogonal basis; they are not the coordinates Qᵀx. Adding and then subtracting the components gives c+ = (x_L + x_R)/2 and c- = (x_L − x_R)/2. Here the numerators are 2 and 2, respectively, so c+ = 1 and c- = 1.

    The output is therefore 4p+ + 2p-, or (4 + 2, 4 − 2)ᵀ = (6, 2)ᵀ. It agrees with the direct calculation: the same left component and the same right component. This check establishes the result for the given input; the matrix equality in the previous step covers all inputs in the model.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why equal off-diagonal entries allow an orthonormal eigenbasis to be chosen, then why normalization is needed to use the transpose as the inverse. Finally, recover the output by both routes.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A symmetric matrix must have the same value everywhere on its diagonal.”

    Symmetry compares the entry in row i, column j with the entry in row j, column i. A diagonal entry is compared with itself. The first exercise preserves symmetry while changing a direct response.

    Reread the roles of rows and columns

  • “Perpendicular eigenvectors are enough to replace the inverse with the transpose.”

    Their norms must also equal 1. The columns of R are perpendicular, but RᵀR = 2I. Divide each by √2 to obtain Q and check that QᵀQ = I.

    Review normalization and the common factor

  • “Any two eigenvectors of a symmetric matrix are always perpendicular.”

    Automatic orthogonality applies to distinct eigenvalues. For a repeated eigenvalue, we choose an orthonormal basis in its eigenspace; an arbitrary pair has no such guarantee. Read the condition that λ differs from μ in the proof.

    Find the condition in the dot product

Exercise 2 · Application and checking

Adjusting only the right control

Return to A, the original array. Now take x = (0, 2)ᵀ. Calculate c₊ and c₋ in the basis p₊, p₋, then the output. Check it by direct multiplication. All these values are dimensionless.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the components to find c₊; subtract the right component from the left for c₋. Divide each result by 2.

Hint 2

A negative coefficient multiplies the whole column p₋, including its component that is already negative.

Compare with the solution

c+ = (0 + 2)/2 = 1; c- = (0 − 2)/2 = -1. The input is therefore p+ − p-. The negative sign of the second coefficient is needed to cancel the left component.

Ap+ = 4p+ and Ap- = 2p-. The output is 4p+ − 2p-: its left component is 4 − 2 = 2, and its right component is 4 + 2 = 6.

Direct check: 3 × 0 + 1 × 2 = 2 on the left; 1 × 0 + 3 × 2 = 6 on the right. We recover (2, 6)ᵀ. These are indeed amplitudes in the p basis; using coordinates in the q basis would require its normalization.

Check your reasoning

  • Find c₊ = 1 and c₋ = -1, justifying the signs.
  • Apply the eigenvalues to the corresponding amplitudes.
  • Obtain (2, 6)ᵀ by two distinct calculations.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and diagnosis

Changing a cross effect

A designer chooses to increase the effect of the right control input on the left output to 2; the reciprocal effect stays at 1 and the direct responses stay at 3. Call this array B. Is it symmetric? Check whether p₊ is still an eigenvector. The designer then asks for both reciprocal effects to equal 2: write C and calculate its eigenvalues for p₊ and p₋.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The effect of right on left is in the top right entry, not the bottom left.

Hint 2

For p₊ to be an eigenvector, the components of Bp₊ must be equal.

Hint 3

After choosing to make the array symmetric, add and then subtract the coefficients in its first row to check p₊ and p₋.

Compare with the solution
B=[3213]

B is not symmetric: 2 differs from 1. Bp+ = (5, 4)ᵀ; its components differ. It therefore cannot be a multiple of p+. This does not mean that B has no eigendirections: only this candidate is rejected.

C=[3223]

C is the new design choice. Cp+ = 5p+, because 3 + 2 = 5 in each component. Cp- = 1p-, because 3 − 2 = 1 in the top component and its negative in the bottom component. The directions remain orthogonal, and the same normalization gives an orthonormal basis. Replacing array B with C is an explicit decision here; a real reading could not be changed in this way to declare it symmetric.

Check your reasoning

  • Construct B with the off-diagonal entries in the correct positions.
  • Reject p₊ using its image and keep the conclusion limited to this candidate.
  • Justify the eigenvalues 5 and 1 of C by multiplication.
  • Identify the change of model rather than presenting it as a proof about B.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do you check to declare a real matrix symmetric?

It must be square and equal to its transpose: each coefficient in row i and column j equals the coefficient in row j and column i. The diagonal coefficients may differ.

Recall 2What does the real spectral theorem allow for a symmetric matrix?

Choosing a real orthonormal eigenbasis. With these columns in Q and the corresponding eigenvalues in D, we have QᵀAQ = D and A = QDQᵀ.

Recall 3What condition makes two eigenvectors automatically orthogonal?

For a real symmetric matrix, distinct eigenvalues. With the same eigenvalue, an arbitrary pair need not be orthogonal; we can choose an orthonormal basis within that eigenspace.

Recall 4Why can we not use Rᵀ directly as the inverse in our example?

The columns of R are orthogonal, but their norm is √2. RᵀR = 2I. After dividing each column by √2, QᵀQ = I: the transpose of Q is then its inverse.

Recall 5How can you check the control panel output without repeating the same calculation?

Compare direct multiplication Ax with the decomposition of x along the eigendirections, followed by multiplication of their amplitudes by the eigenvalues. Both output components must agree.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the sequence: identify rows and columns; check Aᵀ = A; multiply the candidate directions; explain their orthogonality using the condition on the eigenvalues; normalize; obtain QᵀAQ = D; check an output by another route.
