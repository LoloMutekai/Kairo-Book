Level 3 · C102·M5

Checking with finite differences

Your goalCheck the components of a gradient using loss evaluations, diagnose a missing factor, and define the limits of the result.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A numerical model represents the adjustment of a lamp. A setpoint x enters a model; a gain w and an offset b produce a predicted intensity s. The target y states what we want to obtain. All these signals are expressed as ratios to fixed references: the numbers in the model are dimensionless. The values are fictional, with no hardware readings.

The loss L is the square of the difference between prediction and target. A backpropagation calculation gives a value for how it changes when the gain is adjusted. To check it, we can keep the setpoint, offset, and target fixed, move the gain slightly to either side of its setting, and recalculate the loss. This check uses only the forward computation, without reusing the differentiation rules we want to test.

A slope is a change in loss divided by a change in setting. Between w − h and w + h, the width is 2h, even though each point is at distance h from the center. This secant slope approximates the derivative at the center. A finite difference thus replaces a limit with a quotient using a nonzero step size; it provides a local check, with assumptions that need to be verified.

The rule to remember

Perturb just one parameter, recalculate the same loss on both sides, divide by the total distance, and compare with the gradient at the central point.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Check the gradient of the lamp model

Fix x = 2, y = 4, w = 1, and b = 1. We choose the step size h = 0.10 and an absolute tolerance τ = 0.001, all dimensionless. The tolerance is a choice for this exercise to compare derivatives at this scale, with no claim of universal applicability. Let us calculate the component associated with w by differentiation, then independently by finite differences.

  1. Define the loss and hold the required quantities fixed

    x is the fixed setpoint; w, the adjustable gain; b, the adjustable offset; s, the predicted intensity; y, the fixed target; r, the signed residual; L, the scalar loss. The gradient collects the partial derivatives with respect to w and b: each measures the effect of one setting while the other stays fixed. Here, loss and parameters are dimensionless, so their derivatives are too.

    s=wx+b;r=s−y;L(w,b)=r2

    The same setpoint and target are used for every evaluation. No setting is updated during the check. The model is deterministic and differentiable around the point; the perturbed points remain in its domain. If a program involves random draws or mutable state, the same evaluation conditions must be ensured. If it switches branches at a point where it is not differentiable, the comparison with a unique derivative no longer applies as stated.

  2. Obtain a candidate value using the chain rule

    At the center: w × x = 1 × 2 = 2, then s = 2 + 1 = 3. The residual r = 3 − 4 = -1 gives L = (-1) × (-1) = 1. The loss is positive, but its derivative can be negative: increasing the gain slightly brings the prediction closer to the target here.

    Let g_w denote the candidate derivative with respect to w and g_b the one with respect to b. The symbol ∂ denotes a partial derivative. The derivative of the loss with respect to r is 2r; that of r with respect to w is x, while that with respect to b is 1. The chain rule gives:

    gw=∂L∂w=2rx=2×(-1)×2=-4

    Similarly, g_b = 2 × (-1) × 1 = -2. These are the values to test; copying them into the checker would not provide another calculation route.

  3. Construct the points for the check

    h is a positive change in the parameter being checked. For w, prepare two copies of the central point: w − h = 1 − 0.10 = 0.90 and w + h = 1 + 0.10 = 1.10. In both copies, b stays at 1. Starting again from the center prevents a perturbation from accumulating with the previous one.

    D_w(h) denotes the centered slope with respect to w at the point (w, b). The denominator measures the full distance between the points: (w + h) − (w − h) = 2h = 0.20.

    Dw(h)=L(w+h,b)−L(w−h,b)2h

  4. Recalculate the entire forward computation

    Minus side: 0.90 × 2 = 1.80; adding b gives 2.80; subtracting y gives -1.20; squaring this residual gives L(w − h, b) = 36/25 = 1.44. Each operation is the one used in the original loss, with only the gain changed.

    Plus side: 1.10 × 2 = 2.20; adding b gives 3.20; subtracting y gives -0.80; squaring gives L(w + h, b) = 16/25 = 0.64. Do not keep any intermediate value from the old gain: an unchanged residual would produce a false derivative of 0.

    Dw(h)=0.64−1.440.20=-0.800.20=-4

    Sign check: the gain increases and the loss decreases, so the numerator and slope are negative. We obtained -4 without using g_w in the perturbed evaluations.

  5. Compare and understand the exact agreement

    The chosen criterion is |D_w(h) − g_w| ≤ τ; the bars denote absolute value. Here, -4 − (-4) = 0, so the absolute difference is 0 and meets the tolerance 0.001. This local check does not replace checks of other parameters and other points.

    A second check explains why the agreement is exact. Let δ denote any perturbation of the gain. The residual becomes r + xδ. Expanding its square:

    L(w+δ,b)=r2+2rxδ+x2δ2

    Subtracting the expressions for δ = h and δ = −h cancels the constant and squared terms. This leaves 4rxh; dividing by 2h gives 2rx again. The loss is quadratic in w: the centered formula is exact here in exact arithmetic for any nonzero h. This particular property does not extend to an arbitrary loss.

  6. Choose the step size and limit the scope of the verdict

    In general, if the loss has a continuous third derivative in the neighborhood considered, the approximation error of the centered difference is of the order of h squared: its size is bounded by a local constant multiplied by that square for sufficiently small step sizes. In floating point arithmetic, however, making h ever smaller can make the result worse: the losses become almost equal, their subtraction loses significant digits, and division by h amplifies evaluation errors.

    Compare several step sizes around the initial choice, for example h then h/2 = 0.05, and look for a stable range. An absolute tolerance such as our τ is suitable only at the stated scale; at very different scales, an appropriate comparison rule must also be stated. Agreement at a single step size is not a general proof of correctness.

    For the complete gradient of this model, b still needs to be perturbed on its own. With 2 parameters, the centered formula requires 2 evaluations per parameter, giving 4 loss evaluations per point, excluding calculation of the candidate gradient. This is suitable for a check on a small case. The verdict concerns a mathematical sensitivity; it checks neither how faithfully the model represents the lamp nor any real improvement in lighting.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I moved by h, so I divide by h.”

    The centered quotient connects the points w − h and w + h. Their distance is 2h. Dividing their loss difference by h would double the derivative. Draw the points before choosing the denominator.

    Reread the diagram of the total width

  • “I move all the settings together to check the gradient.”

    Moving w and b together checks a combined direction, rather than each component separately. Start again from the central point for every trial and keep the other setting fixed. Component errors can cancel each other out in a single direction.

    Check the offset on its own in the exercises

  • “The smallest step size must give the best check.”

    A smaller step size improves the approximation only while evaluation errors do not dominate. Compare several step sizes, keep the same data, and examine points where the function is not differentiable. The exact agreement of our parabola gives no universal rule for choosing the step size.

    Revisit the choice of step size and the scope of the verdict

Exercise 1 · Application · one-sided quotient

Why the central point changes the quotient

Keep the model at the initial point. Now calculate [L(w + h, b) − L(w, b)]/h with h = 0.10, using the losses already obtained. Compare this result with g_w = -4. Explain why the denominator h is correct in this quotient, then why a disagreement is not enough to blame backpropagation.

Hint 1

This time, the points are w and w + h. Their distance is h, and the loss at the center is 1.

Hint 2

The loss at the plus point is 0.64. Curvature is no longer canceled by a symmetric subtraction.

Compare with the solution

The change in loss is 0.64 − 1 = -0.36. Dividing by the distance 0.10 gives -18/5 = -3.60. The signed difference from g_w is -3.60 − (-4) = 0.40. The derivatives and this difference are dimensionless in the model.

The quotient is consistent with its points, but it approximates the derivative from just one side. In the expansion from the example, setting δ = h and then subtracting the central loss leaves an additional term equal to the square of x multiplied by h. Here it is 0.40. Backpropagation can therefore be correct despite this approximation difference. The centered quotient removes this term for this quadratic loss.

Check your reasoning

  • I obtain -3.60 with a denominator h justified by the points used.
  • I distinguish an approximation error from a differentiation error.
  • I recover the difference 0.40 without rounding the losses before the calculation.

Exercise 2 · Independent application · another component

Check the offset without changing the gain

Return to w = 1 and b = 1, with the same x and y. Perturb b alone with h = 0.10. Recalculate the predictions, residuals, and losses, then form D_b(h). Compare with g_b = -2 using the tolerance 0.001. Explain why you do not reuse the losses obtained by perturbing w.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The offsets are 0.90 and 1.10, but the product w × x stays at 2.

Hint 2

Subtract y from each new prediction before squaring. The denominator remains 2h.

Compare with the solution

D_b(h) is the centered slope obtained by varying only b. Minus side: b − h = 0.90, so s = 2 + 0.90 = 2.90, then r = 2.90 − 4 = -1.10 and L = 121/100 = 1.21.

Plus side: b + h = 1.10, s = 2 + 1.10 = 3.10, r = 3.10 − 4 = -0.90, then L = 81/100 = 0.81. The loss does not change as much as when the gain is perturbed: the setpoint x multiplies w, but does not multiply b.

The numerator is 0.81 − 1.21 = -0.40. The quotient -0.40/0.20 gives D_b(h) = -2, equal to g_b. The absolute difference is 0, below τ = 0.001. At the tested point, the checked components therefore form the pair (-4, -2), in the order gain then offset.

Check your reasoning

  • I keep w fixed and recalculate the intermediate values associated with the new b.
  • I find the losses 1.21 and 0.81, then D_b(h) = -2.
  • I compare each component with its own candidate derivative.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis · justification and procedure

A missing factor and an overly broad validation

A fictional program returns the value -2 for the gain. Its formula is 2r: the factor x is missing. It then announces: “The forward computation works; the entire gradient is validated.” Use the centered difference from the example to accept or reject this component with τ = 0.001. Then propose a stronger procedure on the same model, and state what it would still not prove.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare -2 with D_w(h), not with D_b(h). The sign alone is not enough.

Hint 2

Think about separate components, step sizes, settings, and the difference between a numerical model and hardware.

Compare with the solution

The absolute value of -2 − (-4) is 2, above 0.001. This component is rejected. Applying the chain rule again restores the factor x = 2 and gives -4. The incorrect value nevertheless had the correct sign; that sign was not enough to check its magnitude.

On the same model, check w and b separately, starting again from the center, repeat for h and h/2, then for other settings where the residual is nonzero. Keep exactly the same loss and signals for each comparison. Document the differences for each component and the tolerance rule. A stationary point where the residual is 0 would do too little to distinguish this defect, since the missing factor would already multiply 0 there.

The agreements obtained would support the local gradient calculation for the cases examined. They would constitute neither a proof for every point, nor hardware validation, nor an observation of a program operating in practice. ✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you reconstruct a centered difference with respect to a parameter?

Keep the other parameters fixed, evaluate the same loss at the positions minus h and plus h, subtract the minus-side loss from the plus-side loss, and divide by 2h. Compare with the gradient at the central point.

Recall 2What independence does this check provide for backpropagation?

It reevaluates the forward computation without reusing the differentiation rules being checked. It still shares the programmed loss: an error in its definition may remain common to both routes.

Recall 3Why try several step sizes instead of the smallest possible one?

Reducing the step size can reduce the approximation error, but brings the losses closer together and amplifies numerical errors after division. Look for stable agreement while keeping evaluation conditions fixed.

Recall 4Why is the centered difference exact in our model?

The loss is quadratic in each setting separately. The constant and squared terms cancel in the symmetric subtraction; the linear term gives the derivative after division. This assumes exact arithmetic here.

Recall 5What can you conclude from agreement between the two calculations?

It supports the correctness of the components and points tested, for the stated loss, step sizes, and tolerances. It proves neither the entire program nor the suitability of the model for a real lamp.
