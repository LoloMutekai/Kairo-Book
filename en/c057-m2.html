Level 1 · C057·M2

Differential

Your goalBuild the differential from partial derivatives, apply it to simultaneous changes, and check the difference from the exact change.

Before you start
c033-m5 : affine approximation · c039-m5 : changes of basis · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c057-m1 : partial derivatives

✿01 See the idea

You are preparing a rectangular piece of canvas measuring 4 m by 3 m. Before cutting it, you want to extend the horizontal side by 0.10 m and the vertical side by 0.20 m. How much extra canvas area should you allow for? All dimensions in this scene are fictional; we work with an ideal rectangle, with no hem or offcuts.

If only the horizontal side changes, a strip appears with the same height as the original rectangle. If only the vertical side changes, a strip appears with the same width as the original rectangle. Adding these strips gives an initial estimate when the changes are small.

When the sides change together, a small corner also appears. It depends on the product of the changes. The differential includes the contribution of each change using the starting dimensions: it leaves this corner in the remainder that must be checked.

Before using formulas, point out the parts of the diagram that come from the horizontal change, the vertical change, and their overlap. The differential will be a rule that turns a pair of changes into an approximate change in area; it will not be the final area.

The rule to remember

At the fixed starting point, multiply each increment by the corresponding partial derivative, then add the results. To approximate the final value, add this change to the initial value and examine the remainder.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Predict the added area before recutting the canvas

The area function is A(x,y) = xy. Start from a = 4 m and b = 3 m, then take h = 1/10 m = 0.10 m and k = 1/5 m = 0.20 m. We will calculate the linear change, the approximate area, and then the exact difference.

  1. Fix the point, changes, and units

    x and y are the variable side lengths, in metres; A is their area, in square metres. p = (a,b) denotes the fixed starting point. The pair u = (h,k) contains the signed increments: the new horizontal length is a + h, and the vertical length is b + k. A negative increment shortens a side; the final lengths must remain positive.

    The symbol Δ denotes an actual difference between the final and initial values. Here ΔA = A(a+h,b+k) − A(a,b). This is a change in area, in square metres. We keep the same point p throughout the estimate.

    A(p)=ab=4×3=12 m²

  2. Turn the partial slopes into a linear rule

    The partial derivative with respect to x measures sensitivity to x while y stays fixed; for xy, it equals y. At point p, it therefore equals b. Similarly, the partial derivative with respect to y equals a. Each of these coefficients is expressed in square metres per metre, hence in metres.

    ∂A∂x(p)=b;∂A∂y(p)=a

    The notation dA at point p denotes a map: it takes increments and returns a linear change. The notation dAp(h,k) denotes its value for our pair. The d is neither a number to multiply by nor an instruction to replace a and b with the final lengths.

    dAp(h,k)=bh+ak

    The coefficients a and b stay fixed. This rule is linear in (h,k): it adds the contributions, and multiplying all the increments by the same factor multiplies its output by that factor. The product hk does not follow this scaling rule.

    The shorthand dA = b dx + a dy at point p uses dx and dy for the increments h and k to which the rule is applied. Here they are in metres. This notation does not make the remainder vanish.

  3. State when this rule is a differential

    For a function f of two variables defined at all points sufficiently close to p, being differentiable at p means that a linear map dfp approximates its change with a remainder that is negligible compared with the size of the displacement. We denote this remainder by r(u). The symbol ∥u∥ denotes the length of the vector of increments; here both components are in metres, so ∥u∥ is also in metres.

    f(p+u)=f(p)+dfp(u)+r(u)
    ∥u∥=h2+k2;|r(u)|∥u∥→0 quand ∥u∥→0

    The bars | | denote absolute value. The quotient is taken for nonzero displacements and must tend to 0 regardless of how p is approached. In our example, r is expressed in square metres; the quotient is expressed in metres, using the fixed units. We will check this condition directly for A.

    If f is differentiable, the coefficients of dfp are its partial derivatives at p:

    dfp(h,k)=∂f∂x(p)h+∂f∂y(p)k

    Each coefficient has the unit of the output of f divided by that of the corresponding input. Merely having partial derivatives at a point does not guarantee this approximation: the remainder must also be checked for simultaneous displacements. Continuous partial derivatives in a neighbourhood of p are a sufficient condition. For our product, the explicit check below avoids relying on that criterion alone.

  4. Calculate each contribution using its starting coefficient

    The strip created by the horizontal displacement keeps height b. Its area is bh = 3 × 0.10 = 0.30 m². Multiplying metres by metres gives square metres, as required.

    The strip created by the vertical displacement keeps width a. Its area is ak = 4 × 0.20 = 0.80 m². The contributions have the same unit, so we can add them.

    dAp(h,k)=3/10+4/5=11/10 m²

    The exact fraction 11/10 m² is written as 1.10 m² in decimal form. This is the estimated change. To obtain an approximate final area, we must still add the initial 12 m²: 12 + 1.10 = 13.10 m². The rule that adds this starting value is an affine approximation.

  5. Recalculate the exact area using the final sides

    The new lengths are a + h = 4.10 m and b + k = 3.20 m. Their product gives 328/25 m², or 13.12 m². Here we use the final sides to check the estimate, not to change its coefficients.

    The exact change is therefore 13.12 − 12 = 1.12 m². It exceeds the value of the differential by 1.12 − 1.10 = 0.02 m². Where does this difference come from? Expand the product:

    (a+h)(b+k)−ab=bh+ak+hk

    The remainder is exactly hk = 0.10 × 0.20 = 0.02 m²: the area of corner D. Check by splitting the rectangle: initial area + strip B + strip C + corner D = 12 + 0.30 + 0.80 + 0.02 = 13.12 m². This partition gives the same result as the product of the final sides.

  6. Justify why the corner becomes negligible at a small scale

    A numerical equality for a single displacement is not enough to establish differentiability. For all increments h and k, their product satisfies the following inequality. It follows from (|h| − |k|) squared, which is nonnegative.

    |hk|≤h2+k22;|hk|∥u∥≤∥u∥2→0

    The second inequality holds for nonzero u. Its right side tends to 0 as both increments tend to 0 together. The remainder hk is therefore negligible compared with the length of the displacement: our linear map is indeed the differential.

    In practical terms, dividing h and k by 2 divides bh + ak by 2, but divides hk by 4. The corner shrinks faster than the strips along this path. This does not justify claiming a universal relative accuracy: contributions can cancel, as the last exercise will show.

  7. State what the calculation allows us to report

    For these dimensions, the differential applied to the changes is 1.10 m²; the approximate final area is 13.10 m². The exact geometric model gives 13.12 m², with a known difference of 0.02 m². We can report these results without deciding for the person cutting the canvas whether that difference is acceptable.

    Closeness is assessed against the need. Here the remainder can be calculated exactly; for another function, an estimate of that remainder would be needed before promising a tolerance. The formula for the differential alone does not provide a numerical error bound for a given displacement.

Explain it in your own words

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The differential gives the final area exactly.”

    dAₚ is a rule for changes. Its value for (h,k) is 1.10 m²; adding it to the initial area gives 13.10 m², still different from the exact area of 13.12 m². Split the rectangle again to recover the remainder hk.

    Distinguish the change, the approximation, and the exact area

  • “I evaluate the derivatives at the final lengths to get a more accurate result.”

    The differential at point p keeps a and b fixed. Replacing b with b+k and a with a+h adds 2 times the product hk to the linear change, whereas the exact change adds it only once. Start again from the strips attached to the initial sides.

    Identify the dimensions of each strip

  • “The partial derivatives exist, so their sum must give a good approximation.”

    Partial slopes only describe separate changes. To obtain a differential, the remainder divided by the size of the displacement must tend to 0 in every direction. For the canvas, the bound on |hk| establishes this property; a calculation along the axes alone would not establish it in general.

    Review the definition and the bound on the remainder

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Only one length changes

Hint 2

Compare with the solution

The vertical contribution is a × k = 4 × 0 = 0 m². The horizontal contribution is 0.30 m². The differential is therefore 0.30 m², and the approximate area is 12 + 0.30 = 12.30 m².

Check using the final rectangle: 4.10 × 3 = 12.30 m². The exact change is 12.30 − 12 = 0.30 m². Here hk is 0: the differential gives the change exactly because A is affine when only one side varies and the other stays fixed, even if h is not infinitesimal, provided the lengths remain positive.

Check your reasoning

  • I kept the coefficients at the initial point and distinguished the change from the total area.
  • I justify the equality by the 0 remainder, not merely by numerical closeness.
  • My changes in area and my areas are in square metres.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and scaling check

Reduce the changes together

Still at the same point p, divide the initial increments by 2: h′ = 0.05 m and k′ = 0.10 m. The prime marks this new trial. First predict how the differential and the remainder change, then calculate them along with the exact change.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The differential is linear in the pair of increments; the remainder multiplies its two components.

Hint 2

Calculate bh′, ak′, and h′k′ separately, without rounding before adding.

Compare with the solution

The coefficients a and b do not change. The contributions become 3 × 0.05 = 0.15 m² and 4 × 0.10 = 0.40 m². Their sum is 11/20 m², or 0.55 m²: the differential is divided by 2.

The remainder becomes 1/20 × 1/10 = 1/200 m², or 0.005 m². It is divided by 4, because both factors have each been divided by 2.

The exact change is 11/20 + 1/200 = 111/200 m², or 0.555 m². An independent check is to calculate (4 + 1/20) × (3 + 1/10) − 12: this gives 111/200 m² again. The total change is not exactly half the previous change; its linear term and its remainder do not change at the same rate.

Check your reasoning

  • I state the scaling factors before calculating.
  • I keep the same starting point, with the same partial derivatives.
  • I distinguish the factor applied to the strips from the factor applied to the corner.
  • I check the change using the product of the new lengths.

Exercise 3 · Critical reasoning

The differential vanishes: is the area preserved?

At the usual starting point, extend the horizontal side again by h = 0.10 m, but shorten the other side by 0.075 m: k = −3/40 m. Calculate dAₚ(h,k), then check the exact area. Can a 0 differential tell us that the canvas preserves its area exactly?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Shortening is a negative increment. Compare bh and ak, keeping their signs.

Hint 2

Even if the linear contributions cancel, the product hk can remain nonzero.

Hint 3

Check using the lengths 4.100 m and 2.925 m.

Compare with the solution

The contribution bh is 0.300 m²; ak is -3/10 m², or −0.300 m². Their sum is 0 m². The affine approximation therefore predicts an unchanged area of 12 m².

The remainder is hk = 1/10 × (-3/40) = -3/400 m², or -0.0075 m². The exact change is -3/400 m²: it is negative, despite a 0 differential.

Direct check: 4.100 × 2.925 = 11.9925 m²; subtracting 12 m² gives -0.0075 m². The linear contributions cancel only to first order. A small absolute remainder does not guarantee a small error relative to an exact change that may itself be very small. The definition compares the remainder with the size of the displacement, not with the value of the differential.

Check your reasoning

  • I gave k the sign corresponding to shortening.
  • I distinguish the cancellation of the linear term from exact preservation of the area.
  • The product of the final sides confirms the negative sign of the change.
  • I do not divide by the 0 differential to assess the error.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you distinguish dAₚ from dAₚ(h,k)?

dAₚ is the linear map associated with the fixed point p. dAₚ(h,k) is its value for a given pair of increments. For A(x,y) = xy and p = (a,b), this value is bh + ak.

Recall 2At which point are the partial derivatives evaluated, and what units do they have for the canvas?

At the starting point p = (a,b), which stays fixed: the coefficients are b and a. They are in square metres per metre; multiplying them by h and k, in metres, gives contributions in square metres.

Recall 3How do you separate the initial value, the linear change, and the final value?

The initial area is ab. The linear change is bh + ak. The approximate final area is ab + bh + ak; the exact value also includes the remainder hk. A differential gives a rule for changes, not a final area directly.

Recall 4What check on the remainder distinguishes a differential from a simple sum of partial slopes?

The remainder divided by the length of the nonzero displacement must tend to 0 for all displacements tending to 0. Partial derivatives alone do not guarantee this. For the product, |hk| is at most (h squared + k squared)/2, which provides this check.

Recall 5Does a 0 differential prove that the exact value does not change?

No. It predicts a 0 linear change; the remainder can be nonzero. In the shortening trial, the strips cancel but hk stays negative. This remainder must be checked before concluding that the area is preserved exactly.
