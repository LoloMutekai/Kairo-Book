Trees

Your goalRecognize a tree, justify its number of edges, and reconstruct parents and depths after choosing a root.

Before you start
c025-m5 : O, Omega and Theta · c028-m5 : choice trees · c069-m5 : congruences and Euclid · c070-m1 : graphs · c070-m2 : traversals

✿01 See the idea

A small workshop wants to connect its workstations A, B, C, D, E and F. The cables allow travel in both directions. Here is a fictional layout: AB, AC, BD, BE and EF. Start at C and reach F without visiting a workstation again. You pass through A, B and E; no detour lets you avoid any of these steps.

All the workstations can communicate, but no cable provides a backup loop. This structure is called a tree: a connected network with no cycle. Connected means that every pair of workstations can be joined by a path. A cycle returns to its starting point without repeating any other vertex or reusing an edge.

Imagine adding CD. The new cable joins workstations already connected by C–A–B–D: it closes a loop. Instead, cut BE in the original layout: E and F become unreachable from A. In a tree, every edge is needed to keep all the vertices connected.

The positions in the drawing help you read the connections; they do not represent any physical distance. Choosing A as the root simply means reading the branches from A. It does not turn the cables into one-way routes or change the network.

The rule to remember

Check that all the vertices are connected and that there is no cycle. The edge count confirms the reasoning; on its own, it does not identify a tree.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Verify the layout, then read its branches

The fictional layout keeps exactly the workstations A, B, C, D, E, F and the cables AB, AC, BD, BE, EF. We will prove that it forms a tree, recover its edge count in two ways, and choose A as the root. No cost, cable length or data rate is given.

  1. Define the objects and conventions

    Write T = (V, E) for the graph. V is the set of vertices, here the workstations; E is the set of edges, here the cables. The workstation name E and the letter E for the set of edges are distinguished by context. AB abbreviates the unordered pair {A, B}: AB and BA denote the same cable. The graph is finite, undirected and simple: no cable connects a workstation to itself, and there are no parallel cables between the same pair.

    n = |V| counts the vertices; m = |E| counts the edges. The bars | | here denote the number of elements, not a length. n is 6 vertices. These counts have no physical dimension; we specify “vertices” or “edges” to distinguish the objects being counted. A simple path repeats no vertex; its length counts the edges traversed.

  2. Establish connectivity and rule out cycles

    From A, we reach B and C, then D and E from B, then F from E. All the workstations are reached: the graph is connected. To check that there is no cycle without relying on the drawing, reconstruct the layout: start with A alone, add B via AB, C via AC, D via BD, E via BE and F via EF.

    Each addition attaches a new vertex to the part already built with just 1 edge. It preserves connectivity and cannot close a cycle: the new vertex had no other path to that part. The final layout is therefore connected and has no cycle. It is a tree. This justification concerns the complete list of edges, not just those selected during a traversal.

  3. Understand the unique path

    Connectivity ensures at least one simple path between every pair of distinct vertices. If there were several, follow them to their first separation, then to their next meeting: their different portions would form a cycle. In a tree, the simple path is therefore unique. Conversely, having a unique simple path between every pair guarantees connectivity and rules out cycles: a cycle would offer distinct simple routes between its vertices.

    From C to F, the path is C–A–B–E–F. Count CA first, then AB, BE and EF: 1 edge to reach A, then 3 to F, giving 4 edges. The route contains 5 vertices. Allowing travel back and forth would give several possible walks; uniqueness applies specifically to simple paths.

  4. Recover the edge count

    Initially, A alone gives 1 vertex and 0 edges. Each of the remaining 6 − 1 vertices was added with one edge. The expected count is therefore:

    m=n−1=6−1=5 arêtes

    Why does this rule hold for every finite nonempty tree? In a tree with several vertices, take a simple path that is as long as possible. An endpoint cannot have a neighbor outside the path, since that would extend it; it cannot join any vertex on the path other than its immediate neighbor, since that would create a cycle. Its degree is therefore 1: it is a leaf. Removing this leaf and its edge leaves a tree: no cycle appears, and a path between the remaining vertices did not need the leaf as an intermediate vertex.

    Repeat until only the isolated vertex remains. Each removal takes away 1 vertex together with 1 edge; the difference between their counts remains 1. This is also a proof by induction of m = n − 1. The case with just a single vertex is indeed a tree, despite its 0 edges.

  5. Check independently using degrees

    The degree d(v) of a vertex v is its number of incident cables, meaning cables that touch it. v denotes any vertex in V. From the cable list: A touches AB and AC, so d(A) = 2; B touches AB, BD and BE, so d(B) = 3. The other degrees are d(C) = 1, d(D) = 1, d(E) = 2 and d(F) = 1.

    Add them: 2 + 3 = 5; 1 + 1 = 2; 2 + 1 = 3. Then 5 + 2 = 7, and 7 + 3 = 10 incidences. Each cable has 2 endpoints; this sum therefore counts every edge 2 times.

    m=∑v∈Vd(v)2=102=5 arêtes

    The symbol ∑ here means to add d(v) for all vertices v in V. This recovers the direct count AB, AC, BD, BE, EF and the result n − 1. The sum of the degrees is 2m for every simple undirected graph; it checks the count without proving on its own that the graph is a tree.

  6. Choose a root without changing the cables

    The root r is the vertex chosen as the starting point; here r = A. For every other vertex v, its parent is the neighbor that precedes it on the unique path from r. Its children are the vertices for which it is the parent. Thus A is the parent of B and C; B is the parent of D and E; E is the parent of F. The root has no parent. A leaf of the rooted tree has no children: here C, D and F, giving 3 leaves.

    The depth h(v) counts the edges on the path from r to v; the height H is the greatest depth. h(A) = 0. Then h(B) = h(C) = 1; h(D) = 1 + 1 = 2 and h(E) = 1 + 1 = 2. Finally h(F) = 2 + 1 = 3, so H = 3 edges. All these numbers count edges, not drawn levels or meters.

    Check: every child has a depth 1 greater than its parent. In an unrooted tree with several vertices, “leaf” means degree 1; after rooting, however, a root with degree 1 has a child. For a tree with just a single vertex, the root is also a leaf in the sense of “having no children”.

  7. Test an addition and a cut

    Add only CD to the original layout. The count goes from 5 to 6 edges. The existing path C–A–B–D, followed by DC, closes the cycle C–A–B–D–C. In any tree, adding an edge between distinct vertices that are not already neighbors creates exactly one cycle, because their previous simple path was unique.

    Start again from the original layout and remove only BE. The count becomes 5 − 1 = 4 edges. The groups {A, B, C, D} and {E, F} no longer have a connection. If a detour from B to E still existed, it would have formed a cycle with BE before the cut. An edge of a tree is therefore a bridge: removing it increases the number of connected components, meaning maximal groups of connected vertices.

    After the cut, there is still no cycle: the result is a forest, a graph in which every component is a tree. If c counts its components, each component contributes its number of vertices minus 1 edges; adding gives m = n − c. Here c = 2, so 6 − 2 = 4 edges, matching the direct count. Isolated vertices also count as components.

  8. State the conclusion and its conditions

    The original layout is a tree because it is connected and has no cycle. It has 6 vertices and 5 edges, a result confirmed by the degrees. For a finite, simple, undirected and nonempty graph, connectivity together with m = n − 1 is also sufficient: we can reach the vertices from a starting point by keeping one discovery edge for each new vertex; these edges alone already give n − 1, so no additional edge can close a cycle.

    The count alone leaves a question open: can all the vertices communicate? The last exercise will use the same workstations to construct a graph that satisfies the count but fails this condition. We have assigned neither costs to cables nor directions to edges: finding a minimum cost or weighted paths will require the following units.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the steps. Using only the list AB, AC, BD, BE, EF, justify “tree”, recover the edge count using degrees, then explain to someone why BE is essential and why moving the root does not replace any cable.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I have n − 1 edges, so it must be a tree.”

    You still need connectivity of all vertices, or another justified equivalent condition. Removing BE and then adding CD keeps the correct count, but leaves {E, F} separated from a group containing a cycle. The last exercise asks you to check both problems.

    Construct the counterexample with the same workstations

  • “The path C–A–B–E–F has 5 edges.”

    You counted the vertices visited. Write the cables CA, AB, BE and EF: 4 edges for 5 vertices. Depth follows the same convention; the depth of the root is 0.

    Recount the path, then the depths

  • “My traversal ignores vertices already seen, so the network has no cycle.”

    A traversal can choose just one discovery edge per vertex and produce a tree from a graph with cycles. Consider the addition of CD again: the cycle exists in the graph even if a traversal does not keep all its edges. Check the complete cable list.

    Distinguish the source graph from the discoveries made during traversal

Exercise 1 · Recognize and count

A route and a branch

Keep the original layout AB, AC, BD, BE, EF. Write the simple path from C to F, give its length, and explain why D does not appear on it. Then give the leaves in the unrooted sense.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

From C, the only cable leads to A; from F, the only cable leads to E.

Hint 2

Count the moves between successive vertices. A detour through D would force you to return to B.

Compare with the solution

The path is C–A–B–E–F: 4 edges and 5 vertices. It is unique because the layout is a tree. Going through D would require B–D–B: B would be repeated, so this would no longer be a simple path.

C, D and F are the 3 leaves: each has degree 1. A and E have degree 2, and B has degree 3. “Leaf” describes the connections; a position at the edge of the drawing is not enough.

Check your reasoning

  • Write all the vertices of the path in order and count 4 edges.
  • Justify excluding D by the repetition of B.
  • Identify C, D and F by their degree, without relying on their position.

Exercise 2 · Reconstruct on paper

B becomes the root

Without changing any cable in the original layout, choose B as the root. Give the parent of every other workstation, then all the depths and the height. Do the number of edges and the simple path from C to F change? Justify your answer.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The direct neighbors of B are A, D and E. Place them one edge away from B.

Hint 2

A leads to C and E leads to F. The height is the greatest depth, not the number of rows.

Compare with the solution

Parents: A has B as its parent; D has B; E has B; C has A; F has E. B has no parent. Depths: B is 0; A, D and E are 1; C and F are 2. The height is therefore 2 edges, compared with 3 when A is the root.

Check: every parent–child relation uses an existing cable and increases the depth by 1. The 5 edges are preserved; C–A–B–E–F remains the path with length 4. The root changes the hierarchical view, not the connections in the graph.

Check your reasoning

  • Give exactly one parent to each workstation other than B and none to B.
  • Obtain the height 2 by counting edges.
  • Explain why the cables and path are preserved despite the change in parents.

Exercise 3 · Refute and repair

The correct count, the wrong certificate

Someone removes BE from the original layout, then adds CD. They announce: “There are still 6 vertices and 5 edges, so it is a tree.” Write the new edge list, find a cycle and the connected components. Repair the layout with one removal and one addition, without changing the workstations. Justify that your repair gives a tree.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Removing BE separates {E, F} from the rest. Adding CD touches neither E nor F.

Hint 2

Look for the loop through A, B, D and C. A repair must both open this loop and reconnect the groups.

Compare with the solution

The new list is AB, AC, BD, CD, EF. The count is 4 + 1 = 5 = 6 − 1 edges. Yet A–B–D–C–A is a cycle, and the components are {A, B, C, D} and {E, F}, giving 2 groups. The count therefore does not prove connectivity; the graph is not even a forest, since it contains a cycle.

One possible repair removes CD and restores BE. This gives AB, AC, BD, BE, EF again: all the vertices can communicate and no cycle remains, as the construction in the example showed. Other repairs are possible: after cutting an edge of the cycle, connect the two components with a single new edge.

Check this last method: removing an edge of the cycle leaves its group connected through the rest of the loop. The resulting groups are trees; joining them with a single edge creates no cycle, since no path connected them before. The final certificate concerns connectivity and the absence of cycles, not just 5 edges.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep all the workstations, including those in the group {E, F}.
  • Show the cycle A–B–D–C–A and the 2 components.
  • Propose a removal on the cycle and an addition between the groups, then justify connectivity and the absence of cycles.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What conditions define a tree on this page, and what do they imply about simple paths?

It is a finite nonempty, simple and undirected graph that is connected and has no cycle. There is a unique simple path between every pair of distinct vertices: connectivity ensures existence, and the absence of cycles ensures uniqueness.

Recall 2Why does a tree have n − 1 edges? What is missing if you know only this count?

Removing a leaf takes away an edge together with a vertex until only the isolated vertex remains, which has 0 edges. The count is therefore n − 1. To conclude that a graph is a tree from this count, you must also establish connectivity; AB, AC, BD, CD, EF gives the counterexample with the same workstations.

Recall 3What changes when you choose a root and set the depth convention?

The root determines parents and children. Depth counts edges from the root and starts at 0; height is the maximum depth. The choice of root can change these values, but preserves the edges and simple paths of the graph.

Recall 4Why does cutting BE in the original layout separate the network? What would adding only CD do?

BE is the only connection between {A, B, C, D} and {E, F}. A detour from B to E would have created a cycle with BE. Cutting it produces 2 components. Adding only CD instead closes a cycle with the existing path C–A–B–D.

Recall 5Why does a traversal without revisits fail to certify that the source graph is a tree?

The edges that discover a new vertex can form a tree from the root, while other edges in the graph close cycles. You must distinguish the selection made by the traversal from the complete edge list and check that all expected vertices are connected.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, draw AB, AC, BD, BE, EF again. Reconstruct the definition of a tree, the argument for the unique simple path, and the two checks giving 5 edges. Root the tree at A, then at B, and justify the heights 3 and 2. Finish with the cut of BE and the counterexample that replaces it with CD. An answer produced after a hint remains an assisted answer; reading and immediate success do not establish an acquired level.
