Level 4 · C228·M2

Levy-Khintchine

Your goalRead the Levy–Khintchine triplet, calculate the exponent of a volume delivered in batches, and explain why its drift depends on the compensation convention.

Before you start
c227-m5 : Ito-Tanaka · c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c173-m5 : completeness · c228-m1 : characteristic function

✿01 See the idea

A workshop receives liquid in deliveries of 0.50 L. The cumulative volume counter stays still between arrivals, then rises by a step. We choose a fictional model with an intensity of 4 arrivals per hour: this describes a mean, without requiring regularly spaced appointments.

How can we describe all possible values of the counter, rather than a single path? The previous unit used the characteristic function: it assigns each frequency a complex number that describes the distribution. Levy–Khintchine organises its exponent into a drift, a Gaussian contribution, and a jump contribution.

In our workshop, only deliveries cause a physical change. Yet the coefficient called drift in the formula may be nonzero. The reason is a convention for writing the expression: we subtract a linear part from small jumps, then add it back in the drift. The sum still describes the same counter.

We will follow this single model of cumulative volume, with no loss or continuous flow. The data and the drawn path are fictional. We are seeking neither a financial price nor a simulation method; those questions require other units.

The rule to remember

Fix the small-jump convention before reading the triplet. The full exponent determines the distribution; the drift coefficient on its own can change with the convention.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From a batch counter to its triplet

The batches have size a = 0.50 L, the rate is λ = 4 per hour, and the time horizon is t = 1.50 h. We will calculate the triplet at the threshold r = 1 L, recover the characteristic function, then check the mean and dispersion using the number of deliveries.

  1. Define the model and exponent precisely

    Nt denotes the number of arrivals between the start and time t, a nonnegative duration in hours. We model it with a homogeneous Poisson process: the numbers of arrivals in disjoint intervals are independent; their distribution depends only on the duration. Over a duration t, this is a Poisson distribution with parameter m = λt, a number with no unit. For an integer k ≥ 0, it gives P(Nt = k) = exp(−m)mᵏ/k!. Here P denotes a probability; k counts arrivals. The symbol k! denotes the factorial, the product of the integers up to k, with the convention 0! = 1.

    The cumulative volume is Xt = aNt, with X initially equal to 0 L. A real-valued Levy process starts at 0, has independent and stationary increments, and is continuous in probability: over a duration tending to 0, the increment tends to 0 in probability. We use its version with right-continuous paths and left limits. Our Poisson counter, multiplied by a, satisfies these properties.

    u is a real frequency in inverse litres; i is the imaginary unit, defined by i squared equal to −1. The product uXt is dimensionless. E denotes expectation. For a Levy process, the characteristic functions can be written using the same exponent ψ, in inverse hours:

    φt(u)=E[exp(iuXt)]=exp(tψ(u))

    This notation avoids an arbitrary choice of branch of the complex logarithm. The exponent is continuous in u and equals 0 at the origin. The increments also split any duration into any number of independent parts with the same distribution: this is infinite divisibility.

  2. Read Levy–Khintchine with a stated convention

    Fix a strictly positive threshold r, in litres. Our convention will compensate exactly those jumps for which |x| < r, using a strict inequality. The Levy–Khintchine theorem then gives the following form; its general proof is beyond this unit. We will verify it fully for our counter.

    ψ(u)=ibru−cu22+∫ℝ∖{0}[exp(iux)−1−iux1{|x|<r}]ν(dx)

    The triplet is (bᵣ, c, ν). bᵣ is a real coefficient in L/h. c ≥ 0 is the variance rate of the continuous Gaussian part, in litres squared per hour. ν is a positive measure of jump sizes: for a set A of sizes bounded away from 0, ν(A) is the mean number of jumps of those sizes per hour. x denotes a size, in litres; ν(dx) indicates integration with respect to this measure, without assuming a density.

    ℝ is the real line. The symbol 1 with the set {|x| < r} as a subscript is an indicator: it equals 1 if the condition is true, and 0 otherwise. The measure assigns no mass to 0 and must satisfy the following condition, where min selects the smaller argument:

    ν({0})=0;∫min(1,(xr)2)ν(dx)<∞

    Near 0, exp(iux) starts with 1 + iux. The subtraction removes this linear term: the remainder is of the order of x squared, which the integrability condition controls. The collection of small jumps can therefore have infinite intensity; ν is not a probability distribution. Large jumps have finite intensity, without necessarily having a finite mean or variance.

    Conversely, any triplet satisfying these conditions defines the distributions of a Levy process. The triplet is unique once the convention is fixed. The threshold r is a choice of notation, rather than a minimum jump size in the model.

  3. Construct the workshop measure and triplet

    All arrivals have size a = 0.50 L: ν = λδa, here 4δa per hour. δa is the Dirac measure, with mass 1 at the point a; integrating a function f with respect to ν gives λf(a). Thus ν has total mass 4 per hour, rather than a dimensionless 1. It satisfies the integrability condition because its only size is nonzero and its intensity is finite.

    There is no continuous Gaussian noise: c = 0 litres squared per hour. At the threshold r = 1 L, a is strictly smaller than r. The integral therefore adds λ[exp(iua) − 1 − iua]. The subtracted contribution is i u λa. To recover the counter with no continuous flow, we must add it back in i bᵣ u:

    br=λa=4×0.50=2 L/h

    With this convention, the triplet is therefore (2 L/h, 0 litres squared per hour, 4δa per hour). The physical drift between jumps remains bᵣ − λa = 0 L/h. In this finite-intensity case, the difference can be read directly; we do not assume that such a subtraction is integrable for every Levy process.

  4. Calculate the exponent and use the correct duration

    In the numerical formulas that follow, u, t, and X are their values in the units inverse litre, hour, and litre. The Gaussian rate is 0, and the linear terms cancel:

    ψ(u)=i2u+4[exp(iu0.50)−1−iu0.50]

    The remaining linear coefficient is 2 − 4 × 0.50 = 0 L/h. Hence ψ(u) = 4[exp(iu × 0.50) − 1] per hour. For the time horizon t = 1.50 h, we must multiply the whole of ψ by t: the mean number of arrivals is m = λt = 4 × 1.50 = 6.

    φt(u)=exp{6[exp(iu0.50)−1]}

    Check compensation at this time horizon: bᵣt = 3 L and λta = 3 L. Their difference is 0 L. Forgetting t would describe a different duration; removing bᵣ without removing the compensation would describe another process.

  5. Recover the same function from the number of deliveries

    Let us calculate without using the triplet. If Nt = k, the volume is ak. The characteristic function is therefore the sum of the exp(iuak) terms, weighted by their Poisson probabilities:

    φt(u)=∑k=0∞exp(−m)mkk!exp(iuak)

    The exponential series gives exp(−m) × exp(m exp(iua)), or exp(m[exp(iua) − 1]). With m = 6 and a = 0.50 L, we recover exactly the previous result. This approach checks both the sign of the compensation and the duration factor.

    At u = 0, the exponent equals 0 and the characteristic function equals 1, as does the sum of the probabilities. Also, the real part of ψ is λ[cos(ua) − 1] ≤ 0, so the modulus of φ does not exceed 1. cos is the cosine function; this bound checks the form of the exponent, without proving on its own that an arbitrary function is a characteristic function.

  6. Check the mean and what c does not measure

    For the Poisson distribution used here, E(Nt) = m and Var(Nt) = m. We can recover them by summing k times the probability, then k(k − 1) times that probability: the sums are m and m squared. The volume multiplies the count by a, so its mean is multiplied by a and its variance by a squared.

    Mean: a × m = 0.50 × 6 = 3 L. Variance: a squared × m = 0.25 × 6 = 1.50 litres squared. E and Var denote the mean and variance under the model, respectively; they are not an observed volume and a measured error.

    We can also recover the mean by differentiating the exponent at the origin: ψ′(0) = iλa, and E(Xt) = tψ′(0)/i = 3 L. Here the prime indicates the derivative with respect to u. All moments of our counter exist. This differentiation check must not be applied in the general case without checking that the moments exist.

    The Gaussian rate c = 0 therefore does not eliminate the dispersion due to jumps. The drawn path ends at 1.50 L; its difference from the mean 3 L is compatible with a random model. It is not enough to validate or refute the chosen rate.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The counter only rises at arrivals, so bᵣ must be 0.”

    At the threshold r = 1 L, the integral subtracts i u λa. We need bᵣ = λa = 2 L/h to describe the same counter. bᵣ depends on the convention; the physical flow between jumps remains 0 L/h. Work through the cancellation again before changing a coefficient.

    Revisit the triplet and the cancellation of linear terms

  • “ν is the probability distribution of the size of a delivery, so its mass must equal 1.”

    The size distribution is δₐ, but the Levy measure is λδₐ. It indicates an intensity per hour: its mass here is 4 per hour. Separate the arrival frequency from the size distribution; in other models, the total mass of ν can even be infinite.

    Review the arrivals and their frequency in the scene

  • “With c equal to 0, the variance is 0.”

    c describes only the continuous Gaussian contribution. The number of deliveries remains random: the volume variance here is 1.50 litres squared. Calculate it from Nₜ to recover the dispersion contributed by jumps.

Exercise 1 · Reading the model

State what each component measures

Without copying the example, give the workshop triplet at the threshold r = 1 L. What are ν({a}) and ν({x : 0 < x < a})? Is the volume at the time horizon t = 1.50 h deterministic because c is 0? Justify your answer using its variance.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

All deliveries have exactly size a. A Dirac mass assigns nothing to the sizes in between.

Hint 2

The Gaussian rate is distinct from the variance of the Poisson count. Start again from Xₜ = aNₜ.

Compare with the solution

The triplet is (2 L/h, 0 litres squared per hour, 4δa per hour), with a = 0.50 L. ν({a}) = 4 per hour, whereas the open interval between 0 and a has 0 mass: no jump has a size in that interval.

The variance of Xt is a squared × λt = 0.25 × 6 = 1.50 litres squared, which is strictly positive. The absence of a Gaussian contribution therefore does not mean the absence of randomness.

Check your reasoning

  • State the threshold and justify the compensation in bᵣ.
  • Give an intensity with its unit, without normalising ν into a probability distribution.
  • Relate the positive variance to the randomness of the number of deliveries.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and independent check

Consider only half the duration

The delivery model stays the same, but the time horizon becomes τ = t/2. Calculate τ, the mean number of deliveries, the exponent τψ(u), and the mean and variance of the volume. Explain why φₜ(u) = [φτ(u)] squared using the increments.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

ψ is a rate per hour: changing the duration does not change the triplet.

Hint 2

The volumes delivered during the successive halves are independent and have the same distribution. The characteristic function of their sum is the product.

Compare with the solution

τ = 1.50/2 = 0.75 h. The mean count is λτ = 4 × 0.75 = 3. The exponent is therefore τψ(u) = 3[exp(iu × 0.50) − 1], for u expressed in inverse litres.

The mean is aλτ = 1.50 L. The variance is a squared × λτ = 0.75 litres squared. Halving the duration divides these quantities by 2, but leaves the size of a batch and the intensity per hour unchanged.

Xt is the sum of the volume delivered during the first half and the volume delivered during the second. The increments are independent and stationary, so their characteristic functions multiply: φt = φτ × φτ. The exponents add, and 3 + 3 = 6 recovers the exponent for the full duration.

Check your reasoning

  • Multiply ψ by the new duration and retain the triplet per hour.
  • Present the mean volume and variance separately, with their units.
  • Justify the product using the independence and identical distributions of the increments.

Exercise 3 · Changing the convention

The same counter with another threshold

Now choose r′ = a = 0.50 L, still with the strict condition |x| < r′. Should the workshop batches be compensated? Find the new b and check that ψ stays the same. Then explain which process would be described by setting b = 0 while keeping the old threshold r = 1 L.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A jump exactly at the threshold does not satisfy a strict inequality.

Hint 2

Write the integral at the new threshold, then require the full exponent to equal that of the original counter.

Compare with the solution

The batches have |a| = r′: they do not lie in |x| < r′. The compensation becomes 0 L/h. With b′ = 0 L/h and the same c and ν, ψ(u) = λ[exp(iua) − 1], exactly as before. Here b′ denotes the new drift, rather than a derivative. Only the representation has changed.

However, b = 0 with the old threshold would retain the term −iuλa without counterbalancing it. The exponent would become λ[exp(iua) − 1] − iuλa. It describes Yt = a(Nt − λt), the delivered volume minus its mean volume. This centred process is no longer the cumulative volume counter: between jumps, it decreases at the rate λa = 2 L/h.

The triplet is unique for a fixed convention. Changing the threshold with the adjusted drift preserves the distribution; changing only the drift under the old convention changes the distribution.

Check your reasoning

  • Handle equality with the threshold correctly.
  • Check that the full exponents are equal under the different conventions.
  • Distinguish a change in notation from an actual subtraction of the mean volume.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What relation connects the characteristic function to the rate ψ and the duration?

φₜ(u) = exp(tψ(u)). ψ is a rate in inverse time; tψ(u) is dimensionless. Independent and stationary increments make the characteristic functions multiply and their exponents add.

Recall 2What do bᵣ, c, and ν describe once the compensation threshold is fixed?

bᵣ is the linear drift coefficient under the convention; c is the continuous Gaussian variance rate; ν gives the intensities of jump sizes. In the workshop, bᵣ = 2 L/h, c = 0, and ν = 4δₐ per hour for r = 1 L and a = 0.50 L.

Recall 3Why subtract iux from small jumps, then adjust the drift?

The subtraction removes the linear term near the origin, leaving a remainder controlled by the size squared. The drift coefficient must account for this convention to preserve the exponent. Compensation is a way of writing the expression, without removing deliveries.

Recall 4What condition replaces probability normalization for the measure ν?

ν is positive, assigns no mass to 0, and satisfies the integrability of min(1, (x/r) squared). Its total mass is not required to equal 1; it can be infinite near the origin. Away from the origin, it describes a finite intensity of jumps.

Recall 5How can we check the workshop triplet without using Levy–Khintchine again?

Condition on the count Nₜ, which has a Poisson distribution with m = λt, and sum exp(iuak) times its probability. The series gives exp(m[exp(iua) − 1]). Then recovering E(Xₜ) = am and Var(Xₜ) = a squared × m checks the mean and dispersion despite c being 0.
