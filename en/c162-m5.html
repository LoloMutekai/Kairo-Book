Level 2 · C162·M5

Contemplative interpretations

Your goalRead an account of a practice while distinguishing the reported experience, its contemplative interpretation and what would support a claim of effectiveness.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

During a seated break, Leah notices the sentence “I am going to mess up my presentation.” She rephrases it silently: “I am having the thought that I am going to mess it up.” Later, this sentence is no longer in the foreground. After the break, she decides to reread her notes. The entire scene, her words and its timings are fictional.

Saying “this thought has passed” describes a change in the account. Saying “I can view it as a mental event” proposes a way of relating to this change. Saying “this practice brings a lasting improvement in attention” claims an effect that needs further evidence. Moving from one sentence to the next adds a claim; we need to show what supports it.

Here we will use contemplation to mean careful examination of an experience and the meaning given to it. A contemplative interpretation is this reading of the experience: for example, noticing its changing nature or loosening identification with a thought. These definitions set the vocabulary for our analysis; they do not summarize all contemplative traditions.

The testimony tells us about Leah’s reported experience. It deserves a precise description. It does not directly reveal her brain activity, her future performance or a general truth about the self. We will keep the same scene to learn to limit each conclusion to what supports it.

The rule to remember

For each sentence, ask: what is reported, what meaning is proposed, and what further evidence would the conclusion require?

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Read the change without claiming more than it supports

The fictional break lasts 3 min. Leah reports a continuous episode: the thought is noticed at 60 s and is no longer in the foreground at 84 s. She then writes: “It changed; I did not have to believe it straight away.” Let us describe what these details support.

  1. Set out the data and assumptions

    T denotes the total duration of the break; a, the time when Leah notices the episode; b, the time when she reports that it leaves the foreground. T, a and b are in seconds, denoted s, measured from the same starting point. A minute, denoted min, contains 60 s. We assume 0 ≤ a ≤ b ≤ T.

    By convention, the scenario assigns a continuous episode to the interval between a and b. The timings are exact for the calculation in this exercise. In a real observation, a retrospective account would not give this accuracy; isolated timings would not establish continuity of experience either.

    The method for reading the account is simple: first retain the words and time markers, then propose a local meaning, then examine any broader conclusion separately. This method analyzes claims; it is not a model of the brain.

  2. Express time in the same unit

    To compare an interval in seconds with the whole break, let us convert the total duration. The multiplication uses the number of seconds in each minute.

    T=3 min×60 s/min=180 s

    We therefore have T = 180 s, a = 60 s and b = 84 s. Subtracting minutes directly from seconds would make no sense.

  3. Calculate the interval, then limit its meaning

    d denotes the interval between the time markers, in seconds. Subtracting the start time from the end time removes the time already elapsed before the thought was noticed.

    d=b−a=84−60=24 s

    p denotes the proportion of the break occupied by this interval defined by convention. It is a ratio of durations: the units s cancel, so p has no unit.

    p=dT=24180

    The exact fraction is 2/15, or approximately 0.13 with no unit. It describes the time occupied by this episode in the account. It measures neither contemplative success nor the total time spent distracted. A thought can be observed while it remains present.

  4. Check the calculation another way

    After b, T − b = 180 − 84 = 96 s remain. Let us reconstruct the break in parts: 60 s before the thought was noticed + 24 s for the interval + 96 s afterwards = 180 s. We recover the total without simply repeating the subtraction.

    Outside the interval, there are T − d = 180 − 24 = 156 s. This result is a duration outside the episode described. Nothing allows us to rename these 156 s “perfect attention.”

    Checking the bounds gives 0 ≤ 24 ≤ 180 and 0 ≤ 2/15 ≤ 1. Consistent calculations check our description of the timing; they do not automatically validate the interpretation.

  5. Give the experience a local contemplative meaning

    Here impermanence means the changing nature of what is experienced. The local reading is: the place of this thought in the experience changed during the reported break. This is an example that allows us to examine the idea; it does not demonstrate that all reality has the same nature.

    Here decentering means considering a thought as a mental event rather than immediately confusing it with a fact or with one’s identity. “I am having the thought that I am going to mess up” describes this change in relationship. The content may nevertheless point to a real difficulty: preparation for the presentation still needs to be checked.

    Accepting this thought without condemning oneself for its presence does not require endorsing its content, suppressing it or giving up action. In the scene, Leah can acknowledge it and then reread her notes. The account does not say that the thought will never return.

  6. Identify the leap in the conclusion

    “This episode left the foreground” stays close to the account. “I see it as an illustration of change and decentering” states a reading. “My self does not exist” introduces a general philosophical thesis: our interval does not settle that thesis. The absence of evidence provided here does not refute it.

    “The break brings a lasting improvement in my attention” requires something further: defining the aspect of attention being considered, choosing a task that provides information about it and comparing conditions similar enough to examine the effect of the break. Rest, expectations or familiarity with the task are competing explanations to consider.

    No performance before or after the break is provided in this scene. The defensible conclusion therefore remains precise: Leah reports a change and gives it a contemplative meaning. This account does not establish effectiveness, how long any effect lasts or how broadly it applies.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking, recount the same episode as a reported experience, then as a local interpretation. Add a broader claim and explain exactly what evidence it lacks. Also recall why the 156 s are not an attention score.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The thought has gone: I have demonstrated a truth about the self.”

    The account concerns the place of a thought during this break. A thesis about the nature of the self goes beyond that scope. First state the local observation, then present the thesis as an interpretation that would require its own arguments.

    Revisit the scope of the conclusions

  • “Not judging the thought means having to believe it or remain passive.”

    Separate the presence of the thought, the assessment of its content and the chosen action. Leah can avoid condemning herself, check her notes and take preparation seriously. Decentering does not determine whether her presentation will succeed.

    Return to Leah’s scene and her next action

  • “All the time outside the episode is time spent paying attention successfully.”

    The difference 180 − 24 = 156 s removes only the interval described. The account does not tell us about all the other moments. Keep the name “outside the episode,” then look for a suitable measure if the question becomes one of attention.

    Test the trap of interpreting the remaining time in the exercises

Exercise 2 · Calculation and interpretation

A time marker changes: does the scope of the result change too?

In a variation of the same account, the thought leaves the foreground at 90 s, with the same noticing time of 60 s and the same break of 180 s. Recalculate d and p. Can we rate this variation as worse because the interval is longer? What do we know about the time outside the interval?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Keep the same unit and the same denominator; only the end marker changes.

Hint 2

How long the thought is present and how someone relates to it answer different questions.

Compare with the solution

The new interval is 90 − 60 = 30 s. Its proportion is 30/180 = 1/6, or approximately 0.17 with no unit. The inverse check gives 60 + 30 = 90 s.

It is longer by 30 − 24 = 6 s. This change in duration does not rate decentering: Leah could observe a thought for a long time without taking it as a fact. The rest of the break remains outside the episode described, without automatically being time spent paying attention.

The variation checks that we have not hidden a scale of “spiritual quality” in a subtraction. To assess an objective, we would need to define it separately and have evidence that provides information about it.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 30 s and the exact fraction 1/6 before rounding.
  • Check the subtraction with an addition.
  • Reject inferring the quality of decentering from duration alone.
  • Keep what happened at the other moments unknown.

Exercise 3 · Critical examination and transfer

Rewrite an overly broad conclusion

Leah sums up: “Since this thought has passed, I am never my thoughts and my method brings a lasting improvement in attention.” Rewrite her conclusion, keeping what the scene supports. Then explain what would allow the claim about attention to be examined without asking the account to demonstrate it.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Separate the reported change, the meaning she gives it and the promise of effectiveness.

Hint 2

Propose an observable outcome related to the presentation, then a comparison that helps examine other explanations.

Compare with the solution

Possible rewrite: “I reported an episode whose place changed during my break. Considering it as a mental event offers me a way of avoiding immediate identification with its content. This account does not settle a general thesis about the self and does not demonstrate a lasting effect on attention.”

To examine attention in this context, we could define in advance a task involving rereading notes to spot omissions. Comparing a contemplative break with a rest break of the same duration, using texts of comparable difficulty and a balanced task order, would help examine the effect specific to the practice. A balanced order means avoiding systematically assigning the most familiar task to the same condition.

Such a project would still need to be carried out and produce results; it is not carried out here. Even a favorable result on this task would demonstrate neither a doctrine about the self nor a lasting benefit during all presentations. Observations made after a delay would also be needed to establish how long the effect lasts.

Check your reasoning

  • Retain the reported experience and state the status of the interpretation.
  • Remove generalizations that the scene does not support.
  • Connect the proposed indicator to a precise question about attention.
  • Name a comparison and a competing explanation.
  • Keep the proposed verification distinct from a result obtained.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can we distinguish reported experience, contemplative interpretation and a claim of effectiveness in Leah’s account?

The experience states what she reports having noticed. The interpretation proposes a meaning, such as the changing nature of the thought. Effectiveness asserts an effect that requires a defined outcome and a suitable comparison.

Recall 2Why does “I am having the thought that I am going to mess up” not mean that the thought is false?

The rephrasing changes the relationship to the thought: it is considered as a mental event. It does not settle its content. Leah can then check her preparation and act.

Recall 3What do 24 s and 2/15 represent in our calculation, and what do they not represent?

The interval between the time markers in the account is 24 s; its proportion of the break is 2/15, with no unit. These numbers measure neither all distraction, nor contemplative attainment, nor a gain in attention.

Recall 4How far does our example allow us to speak of impermanence?

We can read the change in the place of this thought as a local illustration of impermanence. This episode does not settle a universal thesis about reality or the self.

Recall 5What should we ask for before turning the account into a claim of a lasting effect on attention?

A defined attention objective, a measure that provides information about it, a suitable comparison and observations made after a delay to establish how long the effect lasts. A measurement proposal and testimony alone do not provide these results.
