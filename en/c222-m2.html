Repeated measures

Your goalRecognize measurements from the same person, calculate a paired change, and judge what its precision allows us to conclude.

Before you start
c074-m5 : identification assumptions · c145-m5 : policy evaluation · c073-m5 : interaction and repeated measures · c081-m5 : experimental ethics · c222-m1 : protocol

✿01 See the idea

A report compares response speed on the same task before and after a break. People A, B, C, and D each return for the next session. A already responded faster than D at the start: comparing only their final times would mix this initial difference with whatever changed during the break.

A repeated measure is a new observation of the same unit, here the same person. Pairing is the link that connects their before record to their after record. In the diagram, read each row horizontally: the question becomes “how much has this person changed?”

The file contains 8 records from 4 people observed in 2 sessions. These records are not 8 independent people. Observations of the same person may resemble each other because they share that person’s lasting characteristics. Repetition allows a comparison within a person; it does not create a new participant.

All the times below are fictional, in milliseconds, abbreviated ms. Each cell is a summary time for the session, calculated using a rule set before the study; individual responses to the task are not added to the sample size. A shorter duration here means a faster response, without indicating whether it was correct. No real measurement or real improvement is claimed.

The rule to remember

Keep track of each person’s identity, calculate their change, then summarize these changes. Count records and independent people separately; then examine what the protocol allows you to attribute to the break.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Read a before–after comparison while keeping track of people

All 4 people perform the task before the break, then after it. No group without a break is observed. The task, equipment, and summary rule are kept the same. We want the mean change across people, with equal weight for each person, and the dispersion of those changes. The data are those in the diagram.

  1. State the comparison and its assumptions

    i identifies a person among A, B, C, and D; t denotes the session, before or after. yᵢt is that person’s summary response time, in ms. n = 4 counts people with a complete pair. dᵢ is their change, in ms; the bar in d̄ will indicate the mean of these changes. We choose after minus before and keep this direction throughout.

    di=yi,après−yi,avant

    The descriptive calculation primarily requires correct identities and units. To then estimate the variance of a mean by dividing by n, we will assume that people’s changes are independent and drawn from the same distribution with finite variance. One person does not influence another’s result, and no unaccounted common event links their errors. These assumptions require examination of the protocol; the table does not prove them.

  2. Understand what subtraction can remove

    A simple additive model shows the value of repetition: yᵢt = aᵢ + bt + eᵢt. aᵢ is the stable component specific to the person; bt is the component shared within the session; eᵢt is what remains. All these quantities are in ms. This model is an explanatory assumption, not a law demonstrated by the records.

    di=(baprès−bavant)+(ei,après−ei,avant)

    The same component aᵢ cancels through subtraction. A stable difference in speed between A and D therefore no longer directly increases the dispersion of changes. However, familiarization with the task between sessions remains part of the change: subtraction does not distinguish it from the break.

  3. Calculate the changes, then their mean

    A: 480 − 500 = -20 ms. B: 580 − 600 = -20 ms. C: 680 − 700 = -20 ms. D: 740 − 800 = -60 ms. Subtract within each person before adding across people.

    The changes for A and B total -40 ms; those for C and D total -80 ms. Their sum is -120 ms. Dividing by the number of people gives each person equal weight. In the formulas, ∑ means adding across all people i.

    d¯=∑din=-1204=-30 ms

    The mean time decreased in these fictional pairs. The sign and unit state exactly this result; they do not yet tell us about its stability in a population or its cause.

  4. Check the result using the columns

    Before: (500 + 600) + (700 + 800) = 1100 + 1500 = 2600 ms; the mean is 2600/4 = 650 ms. After: (480 + 580) + (680 + 740) = 1060 + 1420 = 2480 ms; the mean is 2480/4 = 620 ms.

    The check gives 620 − 650 = -30 ms, matching the mean of the changes. This equality follows from addition and holds here because the same people appear in each column, with the same weight. By itself, it does not verify that identities have been paired correctly: the permutation exercise will test this limitation.

  5. Measure the dispersion across changes

    The corrected sample variance of the changes is written as s with subscript d and a squared exponent in the formula. It is expressed in squared milliseconds. To calculate it, measure each deviation dᵢ − d̄ from the mean, then square it to prevent deviations with opposite signs from canceling. A, B, and C each give -20 − (-30) = 10 ms; D gives -60 − (-30) = -30 ms.

    The squares are 100 squared milliseconds for each of the first changes and 900 squared milliseconds for the last. Their sum is 100 + 100 + 100 + 900 = 1200 squared milliseconds. We divide by n − 1 because the deviations are calculated around a mean estimated from these same people: their sum is constrained to 0, leaving 3 degrees of freedom.

    sd2=∑(di−d¯)2n−1=12003=400 ms2

    Check: 10 + 10 + 10 + (-30) = 0 ms. The squares are nonnegative, and so is the variance. This dispersion describes changes across people; it does not describe the raw session times.

  6. Move from dispersion to uncertainty in the mean

    Under the assumptions that the changes are independent and share a common distribution, we estimate the variance of d̄ by dividing the variance across changes by n. The symbol Var denotes the variance under hypothetical repetitions of sampling; its hat indicates that we estimate it from this table. It is neither the variance of the raw times nor the actual error made by this particular mean.

    Var^(d¯)=sd2n=4004=100 ms2

    The denominator remains 4 independent people, rather than 8 records. The correction using n − 1 concerns the variance across changes; the subsequent division by n concerns the variance of their mean. Under the stated assumptions, these variance identities do not require normality. They are not enough to construct a reliable test or interval without examining other conditions.

  7. See why pairing changes the dispersion

    The following identity explains the role of the link between sessions. In the formula, s with a squared exponent and a subscript for after or before denotes the corrected sample variance of that column, in squared milliseconds. c is their corrected sample covariance: the sum of the products (before time − before mean) × (after time − after mean), divided by n − 1, keeping the same person in each product. It is also in squared milliseconds.

    sd2=saprès2+savant2−2c

    This algebraic equality can be obtained by expanding the square of the difference between the centered deviations. A positive covariance reduces the variance of the change relative to the sum of the column variances; a negative covariance increases it. Pairing therefore does not always guarantee greater precision. Mixing up identities preserves column means but changes the products that define c.

  8. Write a conclusion with the appropriate scope

    Descriptive conclusion: in the 4 fictional pairs, the mean change, after minus before, is -30 ms, and the variance across changes is 400 squared milliseconds. Under the stated assumptions, the estimated variance of the mean is 100 squared milliseconds. The small sample size does not make these calculations wrong; it makes generalization uncertain and does not check any assumption for us.

    Causal conclusion: everyone followed the same order. Familiarization with the task, the passage of time, or an unnoticed change in the equipment could also contribute to the change. This comparison therefore does not isolate the break. A control condition and an order assigned according to an appropriate protocol would help distinguish these explanations; neither exists in our example. Here we test neither an interaction between groups nor the consequences of missing data.

Explain it in your own words

Without the table, explain what subtraction within a person removes, why the denominator for the variance across changes differs from the denominator for the variance of their mean, and why a correct mean does not certify correct pairing.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The file has 8 rows, so the study has 8 independent people.”

    Reconstruct the identities: each person provides a before record and an after record. The number of people remains 4. Additional records may refine an individual measurement, but their number does not replace the number of people in the formula presented here.

    Recount the people and their records in the diagram

  • “The difference between the means is correct, so my pairs are correct.”

    The order of a column does not change its mean. Check the identifiers before calculating individual differences: a permutation can preserve d̄ and completely change the variance across changes. The permutation exercise provides a numerical example.

    Try swapping the records for A and D

  • “Everyone is their own control, so the break caused the change.”

    Being compared with oneself removes a stable initial component under the stated model. It does not separate the break from other changes between sessions. Reread the common order and the absence of a control condition before claiming a cause.

    Review the model and the conclusion from the calculation

Exercise 3 · Evaluate a conclusion

Copied rows and added certainty

The author copies the entire export a second time and obtains 16 records. The report now claims 8 independent people and attributes the mean decrease to the break. Correct this conclusion while keeping what the original data actually allow us to calculate. State which protocol documents to request to go further.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Look for new identities and new observations, rather than just new rows.

Hint 2

Separate the change between sessions from its attribution to the break.

Compare with the solution

An exact copy of the export adds no person or information: the 16 rows still describe 4 people and the same 8 original records. Remove the duplicates while keeping a record of the rule applied, then return to the 4 pairs.

The descriptive result remains d̄ = -30 ms, with a variance across changes of 400 squared milliseconds. Under the stated assumptions of independence between people and a common distribution, the estimated variance of d̄ remains 100 squared milliseconds. Duplication gives no reason to lower it; dividing by an artificially increased number of people would invalidate this calculation.

The decrease is observed between the fictional sessions, but it cannot be attributed to the break alone. Request the comparison plan with a control condition, the method for assigning the order of conditions, the pairing identifiers, and evidence that measurement remained consistent. A new order must also account for the lasting effects of one condition on the next. These documents would allow an assessment of the protocol; merely requesting them establishes no effect.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the actual number of people and reject precision created through duplication.
  • Report the descriptive results with their assumptions and units.
  • State a causal limitation and request specific protocol details.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is repeated in this study, and what does n count?

The same person is observed before and after. n counts people with a complete pair, rather than records or individual responses to the task. Independence between people remains an assumption to examine.

Recall 2How do you construct a person’s change, and what can this subtraction remove?

Subtract their own before time from their own after time. In the additive model on this page, the stable component specific to the person cancels. Learning between sessions or another change over time does not thereby cancel.

Recall 3Why divide by n − 1, then by n?

The sum of squared centered changes divided by n − 1 gives the corrected sample variance across changes. Under independence and a common distribution with finite variance, dividing this variance by n estimates the variance of their mean. These variances are in squared milliseconds and are not an actually observed error.

Recall 4Why is recovering the difference between the means not enough to verify the pairs?

Permuting the values in a column preserves its mean. Yet the individual differences and the covariance between the columns change. You must check the identifiers; the mean alone can stay the same with an incorrect variance.

Recall 5Why does being one’s own control not isolate the effect of the break here?

Everyone followed the same order. The break is mixed with familiarization, time, and other changes between sessions. Pairing removes a stable initial component, not these competing explanations.
