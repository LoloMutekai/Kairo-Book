Root finding

Your goalFind the side length of a panel by bisection, state an error bound in length and compare this guarantee with a Newton step.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A workshop is preparing a square panel for a stage set. Its area must be 2 m². With a side length of 1 m, the square is too small; with 2 m, it is too large. We are looking for the right length between these trials, without a square root button. The dimensions and tolerance are fictional, chosen for calculation on paper.

At each trial, the useful question is “too small or too large?” We split the interval of possible lengths into equal halves, test its midpoint and keep the half that still contains the solution. This search is called bisection. It maintains a bracket around the root, rather than just a number that seems plausible.

Here, the difference between the area obtained and the required area is called the residual. A negative residual means “area too small”; a positive residual means “area too large”. A root is a length that makes this difference 0. The root itself need not be 0: it is the function that must equal 0.

We assume an ideal square, exact measurements for the exercise and an area that varies without jumps as the side length changes. The resulting guarantee concerns this geometric model. It certifies neither an actual cut nor the precision of an instrument.

The rule to remember

Keep a root between the endpoints; use the signs to choose the half to retain; return a midpoint with half the width as its error bound.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

An approximate side length with a stated error bound

For the panel with area A = 2 m², find a positive length in [1 ; 2] m. We want an absolute error in the side length of at most ε = 1/16 m, or 0.0625 m. This first tolerance is deliberately coarse. Fractions remain exact until they are displayed.

  1. Turn the required area into an equation

    x denotes the trial side length, in metres; A denotes the target area, in square metres. The function f takes a length and returns the area residual f(x) = x × x − A, in square metres. r denotes the exact side length sought, in metres: f(r) = 0 m². We seek only positive r, because a panel side length is positive.

    f(x)=x2−A;f(r)=0

    f is continuous: it does not skip any value as x varies. It is strictly increasing for positive x: increasing the side length strictly increases the area. Continuity will guarantee a root between endpoints with opposite signs; strict increase will guarantee that the root there is unique.

  2. Check the bracket before bisecting

    Call the lower endpoint a and the upper endpoint b, in metres. Initially, f(a) = 1 × 1 − 2 = -1 m² and f(b) = 2 × 2 − 2 = 2 m². The signs are opposite: continuity requires a root between the endpoints.

    In this increasing model, we maintain the invariant f(a) < 0 < f(b): a property that remains true after each bisection. If an endpoint or midpoint gives exactly 0 m², we have found a root and can stop. For a general continuous function, a change of sign guarantees existence without guaranteeing uniqueness; without continuity, it is no longer sufficient.

  3. Test the first midpoint

    The midpoint m is a length: m = (a + b)/2. Here, (1 + 2)/2 = 3/2 m = 1.500 m. Its square is 9/4 m²; its residual is 9/4 − 2 = 1/4 m², which is positive. The panel would be too large: the midpoint becomes the new upper endpoint.

    We keep [1 ; 1.500] m. The lower endpoint retains its negative residual, and the upper endpoint has a positive residual. Comparing signs directly avoids forming their product, which could overflow or round to 0 in a floating-point calculation.

    m=a+b2

  4. Continue bisecting while keeping the root

    Second test: (1 + 3/2)/2 = 5/4 m = 1.250 m. Its square is 25/16 m²; f(m) = 25/16 − 2 = -7/16 m². This negative sign means that the midpoint replaces the lower endpoint: we keep [1.250 ; 1.500] m.

    Third test: (5/4 + 3/2)/2 = 11/8 m = 1.375 m. Its square is 121/64 m²; f(m) = 121/64 − 2 = -7/64 m². The sign is negative again: we keep [1.375 ; 1.500] m.

    Each time, the decision depends on the sign of the new residual compared with that of the endpoint to replace. For a continuous function with no assumption of increase, the general rule is to replace the endpoint with the same sign as f(m), without assuming that negative always means “on the left”.

  5. Stop using an error bound in length

    The remaining width is 3/2 − 11/8 = 1/8 m = 0.125 m. We return its midpoint, denoted x̂, in metres: (11/8 + 3/2)/2 = 23/16 m = 1.4375 m. The hat indicates an approximation.

    Since r lies in the interval, its distance from the midpoint is at most half the width: 1/8/2 = 1/16 m = 0.0625 m. This bound equals ε: the criterion is satisfied. The last point tested was 1.375 m; the returned result is the midpoint of the final interval, rather than that last test point.

    |x^−r|≤b−a2≤ε

    Independent check: the initial width is 1 m. After n bisections, it equals the initial width divided by 2 to the power n; n is the number of bisections and has no unit. With n = 3, we recover 1/8 m. The squares of the endpoints, 121/64 m² and 9/4 m², do bracket 2 m².

  6. Distinguish residual, error and numerical stopping

    Evaluating the returned result gives x̂ × x̂ = 529/256 m² and f(x̂) = 17/256 m², or 0.066406 m². This measures an area difference. The error |x̂ − r| is a length, unknown but bounded by 0.0625 m. We do not compare the area residual directly with ε in metres.

    The identity f(x) = (x − r)(x + r) explains their relationship here: the length error is the absolute value of the residual divided by x + r, giving a result in metres. A small output therefore does not imply a small input error without information about how the function varies.

    A floating-point program must also limit the number of iterations, reject non-finite evaluations and detect a midpoint that rounds to an endpoint. Such a stop signals a numerical limit if the tolerance has not been met. A guarantee about the root of the model requires reliable signs; the fractions on this page avoid this rounding problem.

  7. See what a Newton step adds and what it does not guarantee

    The derivative f′(x) is the local slope: the change in area per change in side length, in square metres per metre, hence in metres. Here f′(x) = 2 × x. A tangent is the straight line that follows this slope at the trial point. Newton takes the point where this line predicts a 0 residual.

    xnouveau=x−f(x)f′(x)

    The quotient is in metres. Starting from the side length 1.500 m already tested, the slope is 3 m and the displacement to subtract is 1/4/3 = 1/12 m. The proposed value becomes 3/2 − 1/12 = 17/12 m, or approximately 1.416667 m. The final exercise will check its sign and the bracket it provides.

    This step requires a derivative that is defined and nonzero. For a general function, Newton can leave the interval, fail or converge to another root. Near a root with a nonzero derivative, with a sufficiently regular function and a starting point close enough, it can speed up the search. Maintaining the bracket and testing the proposed value preserves a guarantee; a small displacement alone does not provide one.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations. Explain which endpoint each sign replaces, then why half the width bounds the error of the returned result. Justify the unit of the residual and the unit of the Newton displacement separately.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The endpoints have the same sign: there is no root.”

    Matching signs remove the guarantee from a change of sign; they do not prove a general absence of roots. For our panel, whose area increases with side length, we can draw a conclusion about an interval by also using that increase. State the assumptions that allow this conclusion.

    Reread the bracket and its assumptions

  • “The residual is small: my side length is accurate to within that number.”

    The residual is in square metres and the side length error is in metres. Give the interval and half its width; use information about how the function varies if you want to convert a residual into an error bound.

    Distinguish residual from length error

  • “Newton gives many decimal places: its answer is certified.”

    The decimal places displayed do not prove any accuracy. Check the domain, slope, residual and a maintained bracket. The final exercise shows how a value proposed by Newton can narrow the interval.

A poorly chosen interval

Compare with the solution

The residuals are -1 m² and -7/16 m², both negative. The opposite-sign criterion is not met. Furthermore, f is increasing: up to the upper endpoint 1.250 m, the area remains too small. There is therefore no root in this particular interval.

This does not rule out a root elsewhere: [1 ; 2] m has the signs -1 m² and 2 m² and brackets the unique positive length sought. For a general continuous function with no known increase, matching signs at the endpoints would not even suffice to rule out roots between them.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate or retrieve the residuals at both endpoints.
  • Explicitly use increase to draw a conclusion about the proposed interval.
  • Do not turn a poor choice of interval into a claim that there is no solution anywhere.

Hint 1

Hint 2

Its residual is positive. Keep the lower endpoint whose residual is negative, then take the midpoint of the new interval.

Compare with the solution

Initially, f(x) = 1/4 m² and f′(x) = 3 m. We subtract 1/12 m from 3/2 m and propose 17/12 m. Its square is 289/144 m²; its residual is 1/144 m², which is strictly positive.

The proposed value lies in the initial interval and therefore replaces its upper endpoint. The lower endpoint 11/8 m retains its negative residual -7/64 m². We have [11/8 ; 17/12] m, with width 1/24 m, or approximately 0.041667 m.

The midpoint is 67/48 m, or approximately 1.395833 m. Its distance from the root is at most 1/48 m, or approximately 0.020833 m; the exact fraction is the bound to retain, because the decimal display is rounded. Check: 67/48 − 1/48 = 11/8 and 67/48 + 1/48 = 17/12, in metres.

Here, the proposed value narrowed the interval more than the bisection in the previous exercise, whose bound was 1/32 m. This compares mathematical results for this panel; it is not a measurement of program speed. If Newton left the interval or its derivative were unusable, we could resume bisection. The certification comes from the signs, continuity and remaining width.

  • Check the sign and membership of the interval before replacing an endpoint.
  • Return a midpoint and an exact bound in metres.
  • Explain why this case does not demonstrate that Newton is generally superior.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What equation must the panel side length satisfy, and what is the unit of the residual?

The length r, in metres, satisfies f(r) = 0 m² with f(x) = x × x − A. The residual is an area difference in square metres; the root is an input, rather than an output.

Recall 2What do opposite signs guarantee, and under what conditions?

For a continuous function, opposite signs at the endpoints guarantee at least one root between them. The strict increase of our function over positive lengths also gives uniqueness. Reliable signs are necessary for numerical calculation.

Recall 3Which half should you keep after testing a midpoint that is not a root?

Replace the endpoint whose residual has the same sign as the midpoint residual. Opposite signs are preserved. For our increasing function, a negative residual raises the lower endpoint; a positive residual lowers the upper endpoint.

Recall 4What certificate accompanies the returned midpoint?

If a root remains in [a ; b], the midpoint x̂ satisfies |x̂ − r| ≤ (b − a)/2. The bound is expressed in the unit of x. A small residual cannot replace it without an additional assumption about the function.

Recall 5What does Newton calculate, and what must still be checked?

It proposes x new = x − f(x)/f′(x), using a nonzero slope. Check the domain, derivative and bracket: a small displacement or many decimal places do not establish the error relative to the root.
