Posterior distribution

Your goalConstruct the full posterior distribution of a hidden type, justify its normalization, and distinguish a new observation from a repeated observation.

Before you start
c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c059-m6 : deterministic discrete Bellman · c061-m1 : likelihood · c061-m2 : MLE · c061-m3 : MAP

✿01 See the idea

On a shelf, 4 boxes are type A and 8 are type B. They look identical from the outside. You choose one at random without seeing its type. An A box contains 3 blue marbles out of 4 marbles; a B box contains 1 out of 4. All the other marbles are ochre. The scene and all its quantities are fictional.

You draw a marble at random from the chosen box: it is blue. This color favors A, which produces blue more often. But B is more common on the shelf and can also produce blue. The color alone therefore cannot reveal the type with certainty.

Before the draw, the probabilities of the types form the prior distribution. After seeing the color, they form the posterior distribution: the allocation of probabilities among the types that are still possible, given this observation. The box does not change type; our information changes.

Imagine keeping only the pairs “chosen box, drawn marble” that would have produced blue. The proportion coming from A in this set is exactly what we want to find. The second diagram shows this check; the calculation will explain why it works.

The rule to remember

For each type, multiply its initial probability by the probability of the observation under that type; then divide all the weights by their sum, if it is strictly positive.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Infer the box type after a blue marble

The box remains hidden. We know the composition of the shelf and of each type. The only observation used is “the marble drawn is blue”. Let us calculate the posterior probabilities of A and B, then check them by another method.

  1. Define what is unknown and what is observed

    H denotes the unknown type of the selected box; its 2 possible values are A and B. The letter h denotes one of these types in a formula; a subscript h, A, or B specifies the type concerned. E denotes the event “the marble drawn is blue”. P denotes probability in our model; the vertical bar means “given that”. Thus P(E | H = A) assumes type A is known, whereas P(H = A | E) assumes the blue color is known.

    The types are mutually exclusive and cover all possible boxes. Each box has the same chance of being chosen; within each box, each marble has the same chance of being drawn. We know the compositions exactly, and the color is read without error. For this single observation, no assumption of independence between draws is needed.

    Counts are expressed in boxes or marbles; probabilities, likelihoods, and their products have no units. A probability lies in the interval from 0 to 1.

  2. Write the prior distribution and the likelihood

    The symbol π is read as “pi”. We define π_A = P(H = A) and π_B = P(H = B); the underscore indicates the type subscript, placed below the symbol in the formulas. The inventory gives 4 + 8 = 12 boxes: π_A = 4/12 = 1/3 and π_B = 8/12 = 2/3.

    L_A = P(E | H = A) and L_B = P(E | H = B) are the likelihoods of the fixed observation. They come from the composition of the boxes: L_A = 3/4 = 3/4, L_B = 1/4 = 1/4. They evaluate blue under each type; they are not yet the probabilities of the types after blue.

  3. Multiply to obtain the joint weights

    We write w_A = P(H = A and E) and w_B = P(H = B and E). To produce a type A pair with a blue marble, we must choose A and then obtain blue under A: these steps justify the conditional product. The same reasoning applies to B.

    wh=πh×Lh

    A: (1/3) × (3/4) = 1/4. B: (2/3) × (1/4) = 1/6. These weights still count the blue observation among all outcomes, and their sum does not necessarily equal 1.

  4. Construct the common reference

    Z denotes P(E), the probability of seeing blue before the draw. Since A and B cover all types without overlapping, we add their contributions: Z = w_A + w_B = 1/4 + 1/6 = 5/12.

    Dividing by Z means taking only the blue outcomes as the new reference set. Z must be strictly greater than 0. If the model assigned 0 probability to the observation actually received, this division would be impossible: we would need to revise the assumptions or the description of the observation.

  5. Normalize the full distribution, then interpret it

    We write π_h^E = P(H = h | E) for the posterior probability of type h; the ^ sign places E as a superscript in the formula to recall the information used. By the definition of conditional probability, it is the joint probability divided by P(E). This gives Bayes’ rule:

    πhE=whZ=πhLhπALA+πBLB

    For A: (1/4) ÷ (5/12) = 3/5. For B: (1/6) ÷ (5/12) = 2/5. The full distribution therefore assigns 0.60 to A and 0.40 to B, with no units. The fractions are exact; the decimals here require no rounding.

    Normalization check: 3/5 + 2/5 = 1, with nonnegative terms. A becomes the most probable type, but B retains probability 2/5. The maximum a posteriori estimate, studied in the previous unit, would select A; giving only this type would lose the distribution of uncertainty.

    This calculation concerns a discrete distribution, meaning probabilities assigned to types A and B. It does not claim to establish that the assumed compositions represent a real situation.

  6. Check by counting the possibilities

    Distinguish each box and each marble position. There are 12 × 4 = 48 equally likely pairs, since all boxes contain the same number of marbles and the choices are uniform.

    Among the blue pairs, A contributes 4 × 3 = 12; B contributes 8 × 1 = 8. This leaves 12 + 8 = 20 possibilities. The proportion of A is 12/20 = 3/5 and that of B is 8/20 = 2/5: we recover the calculated distribution.

    Another cross-check: 20/48 = 5/12 gives P(E) again. This counting argument is a proof within the finite model; it does not simulate any draw or measure any actual frequency.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the result. Explain why multiplication is necessary before division, what Z counts, and why choosing A as the most probable type does not describe the full distribution. Reconstruct the check using box–marble pairs.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Blue has probability 3/4 under A; therefore A has probability 3/4 after blue.”

    Name the known group after the bar: in P(E | H = A), the type is known; in P(H = A | E), the color is known. To reverse the question, bring back the initial frequency of the types and divide the weights by the total probability of blue.

    Review the symbols, then normalization

  • “The weights 1/4 and 1/6 already form my posterior distribution.”

    They describe the joint events “this type and blue”. Their sum is 5/12; it is not 1. Divide each weight by the same sum to describe the distribution of types among only the blue outcomes.

    Review the change of reference set

  • “Reading the same color again strengthens the conclusion further.”

    Reading the same draw a second time adds no observation. Applying the likelihood again would count it again. To multiply once more, we must describe a new draw and its relationship to the previous one; replacing the marble will allow conditional independence given the type here.

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · interpret

Which question does each number answer?

After the blue marble in the example, someone states that the probability of B is 1/4. What does this number measure? Recover the posterior probability of B from that of A, then explain why B remains possible.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

1/4 comes from the composition of a B box, before any calculation about the population of types.

Hint 2

The posterior probabilities of A and B must add up to 1.

Compare with the solution

1/4 is P(E | H = B): the chance of drawing blue if the box is type B. The question asks for P(H = B | E), where blue is known.

The full distribution gives P(H = B | E) = 1 − 3/5 = 2/5. B remains possible because its initial probability and its likelihood of blue are both strictly positive. We can also check (1/6) ÷ (5/12) = 2/5.

Check your reasoning

  • Name what is known in each conditional probability.
  • Obtain 2/5 and use the model to justify why it is nonzero.

Exercise 2 · Application · reconstruct

If the observed marble had been ochre

Start again from the same initial inventory. This time, the only marble drawn is ochre. This case replaces the blue observation in the example. Calculate the likelihoods, the weights, the total probability of ochre, and the full posterior distribution. First state which type ochre should favor.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Count the ochre marbles by subtracting from 4 marbles per box.

Hint 2

Keep the initial probabilities 1/3 and 2/3; only the observed color changes.

Hint 3

Add the weights before dividing each one by their sum.

Compare with the solution

Ochre favors B: its composition produces this color more often. A contains 4 − 3 = 1 ochre marble; B contains 4 − 1 = 3. The likelihoods are 1/4 under A and 3/4 under B.

Weight for A: (1/3) × (1/4) = 1/12. For B: (2/3) × (3/4) = 1/2. The total probability of ochre is 1/12 + 1/2 = 7/12.

After normalization: A receives (1/12) ÷ (7/12) = 1/7; B receives (1/2) ÷ (7/12) = 6/7. Their sum is 1. The predicted direction is confirmed: B is more probable than before the draw.

Additional check: blue and ochre cover the possible colors; 5/12 + 7/12 = 1. This check concerns the probabilities of the observations, whereas the previous sum concerns the types after ochre.

Check your reasoning

  • Start again from the initial inventory, without keeping the distribution obtained after blue.
  • Give all the weights and the common denominator.
  • Check the sum of the type probabilities and the sum of the color probabilities separately.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · distinguish information

A photo or a new draw?

After the blue marble in the example, you are shown its photo again. Should the distribution change? Then the marble is actually put back into the same box, the contents are mixed, and a new uniform draw is made: blue again. Keeping the same box and composition, calculate the new distribution. State precisely which independence justifies your calculation.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The photo repeats the same fact; the new draw produces another observation.

Hint 2

For a new draw after replacement, the probability of blue under each type remains 3/4 or 1/4.

Hint 3

The distribution after the first blue becomes the initial distribution for this new update.

Compare with the solution

The photo does not change the available information: the distribution remains A: 3/5; B: 2/5. An additional update based only on this photo would count the same marble several times.

After replacement and mixing, the result of the new draw is independent of the previous one conditional on type H: if H is fixed, knowing the first color does not change the probability of the next. We do not assume that the colors are independent when H remains unknown.

For the new blue: weight A = (3/5) × (3/4) = 9/20; weight B = (2/5) × (1/4) = 1/10. Their sum is 11/20: this is the probability of the new blue given the first blue.

The distribution becomes A: (9/20) ÷ (11/20) = 9/11 and B: (1/10) ÷ (11/20) = 2/11. Sum: 1. This strengthening is justified by the new draw. Obtaining the same result from the photo alone would create artificial certainty.

To check, start again from the initial distribution and multiply each probability by its squared likelihood of blue: with replacement, the probability of the sequence “blue then blue” under a type is the product of its likelihoods. After normalization, you recover 9/11 and 2/11. Without replacement, you would need to recalculate the probability of the second blue using the remaining composition.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the distribution unchanged for the photo alone.
  • Calculate the new weights from 3/5 and 2/5.
  • Specify “independent conditional on the type” and explain the role of replacement.
  • Normalize the full distribution without claiming certainty about A.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What changes between the prior distribution and the posterior distribution in the box scene?

The information used to allocate probabilities between A and B. After seeing the color, the actual type of the box stays the same; our conditional distribution changes.

Recall 2Why is the probability of blue under A not enough to give the probability of A after blue?

The known group switches sides in the conditional probability. We also need the initial probability of each type and the total probability of the observation.

Recall 3How do you reconstruct the full distribution from the likelihoods and the initial distribution?

Multiply each initial probability by its likelihood, add the weights, check that the sum is strictly positive, then divide each weight by that same sum. The resulting probabilities are nonnegative and total 1.

Recall 4What is lost by giving only A as the most probable type after blue?

The distribution of uncertainty: A receives 3/5, and B retains 2/5. A type that maximizes the posterior probability does not replace the full distribution and is not a certainty.

Recall 5Why do looking at a photo again and making a new draw with replacement not justify the same update?

The photo repeats the same fact. The new draw adds an observation whose likelihood is known under each type; replacement and mixing make the draws independent conditional on the type. Reusing the photo would create a strengthening without new information.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the example, reconstruct this sequence: name the possible types and the observation; write the initial probabilities and the likelihoods; form the joint weights; find their sum and explain its meaning; normalize the full distribution; check by counting. Finish by explaining what a maximum and a repeated observation do not allow you to conclude.

What you understand
A posterior distribution retains a probability for each hypothesis after taking an observation into account. Its maximum summarizes a choice; the distribution also retains the competing possibilities. The box scene gives an explicit finite model for understanding this difference.
