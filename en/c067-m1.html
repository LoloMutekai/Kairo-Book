Level 2 · C067·M1

Empirical risk

Your goalCalculate a mean loss over given observations, choose its denominator, and limit the conclusion to the batch and loss used.

Before you start
c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c017-m5 : reproducibility protocol

✿01 See the idea

A delivery service announces a duration before each departure. For deliveries a, b, and c, rule A predicts 10, 20, and 22 min. The observed durations are 12, 18, and 25 min. All these data are fictional. How can we summarize the differences without letting an error disappear through cancellation by an error of the opposite sign?

First, we choose a loss: a penalty assigned to each prediction after comparison with the observed duration. Here, we use the square of the difference. A duration that is too short and one that is too long by the same amount receive the same positive penalty. This mathematical cost does not represent an amount of money.

Empirical risk is the mean of these losses over the observed batch. Here, empirical means “calculated from the available observations”. The diagrams show the durations for each delivery separately, followed by the addition and division that produce this mean.

We keep the prediction rule fixed throughout the calculation. Each delivery counts equally, predictions are paired with the correct observations, and no duration is missing. The batch is nonempty and all its values are finite. These conditions are sufficient for the calculation; the deliveries do not need to be independent to calculate this mean.

To make claims about future deliveries, we would also need to justify the connection between this batch and future situations. The result primarily describes the records in front of us. Learning the rule and separating the data will be studied in the following units.

The rule to remember

Fix the batch, rule, and loss; calculate a loss for each observation; add the losses, then divide by the number of observations.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Evaluate the durations announced by rule A

For deliveries a, b, and c, the (prediction; observation) pairs, in minutes, are (10 ; 12), (20 ; 18), and (22 ; 25). We want the empirical risk of rule A with squared loss and an equal weight for each delivery.

  1. Name what is fixed

    D denotes the list of observed records; n = 3 is their number, a count with no unit. The index i identifies a record, from 1 to n; the letters a, b, and c name them in the diagram. xᵢ is the input record: the route and conditions known at departure. This composite record has no single unit. yᵢ is the observed duration, in minutes.

    f is rule A, which turns a record xᵢ into a predicted duration. We write this duration as ŷᵢ = f(xᵢ), in minutes; the hat distinguishes the prediction from the observation. Here, we have its outputs, without investigating how f was built. We change neither f nor the records during this evaluation.

  2. Define the loss and the mean

    ℓ, read “ell”, denotes the loss. For a predicted duration u and an observed duration v, both in minutes, we choose ℓ(u, v) = (u − v) squared. Each loss therefore has the unit min², the square minute. The penalty is 0 exactly when the durations match.

    ℓ(u,v)=(u−v)2

    R̂_D(f) denotes the empirical risk of f on D: the hat indicates a calculation on an observed batch, and the subscript D recalls which batch was used. The symbol ∑ tells us to add the losses for all records, once each. The factor 1/n turns this sum into a mean.

    R^D(f)=1n∑i=1nℓ(f(xi),yi)

    This is a descriptive mean: we divide by n. A denominator correction used for some variance estimates answers a different question. Here, neither a normal distribution nor an assumption of noise with 0 mean is required.

  3. Calculate the differences while keeping their direction

    Let eᵢ = ŷᵢ − yᵢ be the signed difference in minutes. For a: 10 − 12 = -2 min, a duration that is too short. For b: 20 − 18 = 2 min, a duration that is too long. For c: 22 − 25 = -3 min, a duration that is too short.

    The first differences cancel in a signed sum: -2 + 2 = 0 min. Yet neither of these announcements was accurate. Over the whole batch, the signed mean is -1 min; it indicates the average direction of the differences without giving the mean penalty we want.

  4. Apply the penalty before adding

    For a, the loss is (-2) × (-2) = 4 min². For b: 2 × 2 = 4 min². For c: (-3) × (-3) = 9 min². Each product uses the difference for a single delivery.

    Squaring makes the losses positive or 0 and penalizes larger differences more heavily. Squaring only after adding the differences would already allow cancellation to occur; it would be a different quantity.

  5. Add the losses, then normalize

    First, add the losses for a and b: 4 + 4 = 8 min². Add c: 8 + 9 = 17 min². There are 3 losses, so we divide by 3.

    R^D(f)=4+4+93=173≈5.67 min²

    The fraction 17/3 min² is exact; 5.67 min² is its rounded representation. This result is neither a mean error duration in minutes nor a probability. Its unit comes from the chosen loss.

  6. Check the count, scale, and units

    A mean with equal weights stays between the smallest and largest of its losses: 4 ≤ 17/3 ≤ 9, in min². Multiplying the exact fraction by the count gives back 3 × (17/3) = 17 min². These checks can reveal, in particular, an incorrect denominator or confusion between a sum and a mean.

    Dividing by n − 1 would give 17/2 = 17/2 min², which would no longer be the mean of the 3 losses. If all announced durations matched the observations, each loss would be 0 min², and so would their mean.

  7. State exactly what the result measures

    We have evaluated rule A on D, using squared loss. To compare another rule on these records, keep the same pairing, weights, and loss. Choosing a different loss can change the ranking, as the last exercise will show.

    The expected risk would be the theoretical mean of this loss over the distribution of the deliveries we want to predict. This distribution describes which situations and durations can occur, and how frequently. These records alone do not provide it. A low empirical risk therefore does not, by itself, demonstrate good predictions for new deliveries.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Durations that are too short and too long cancel out, so the predictions are good.”

    Cancellation concerns the signed differences. Apply the loss to each difference before summing: for a and b, the signed sum is 0 min, whereas the losses total 8 min². Redo the products, with their units.

    Revisit the differences, then the losses

  • “A mean of squared errors is always divided by n minus one.”

    First, name the quantity requested: here, the descriptive mean of the losses. There are 3 losses, so the denominator is 3. Count the rows again and check that the risk multiplied by this count gives back their sum.

    Review normalization and its checks

  • “A small empirical risk proves that the rule will be good tomorrow.”

    State the batch and loss alongside the result. The available records do not, by themselves, describe all future situations. Distinguish the calculation that can be checked on these records from the additional evidence needed about the situations of interest.

    Return to the scope of the evidence

Compare with the solution

For c, the new difference is 25 − 25 = 0 min; its loss is 0 min². The other losses still total 8 min². The new sum is 8 + 0 = 8 min².

There are still 3 observations: the risk becomes 8/3 = 8/3 min², or approximately 2.67 min². Eliminating the loss for c reduces the sum without reducing the number of losses. This describes the modified rule on these records.

Hint 1

Each loss now appears 2 times. Apply the same factor to the sum and the count.

Hint 2

Distinguish the number of rows written from the number of distinct observations made.

Compare with the solution

The sum becomes 2 × 17 = 34 min² and the count becomes 2 × 3 = 6. The risk is 34/6 = 17/3 min²: the mean is unchanged because all records have been repeated in the same proportion.

Copying adds no new observation. Equal means do not justify claiming more independent evidence. Copying only some records would change their relative weights; our equality relies on repeating the whole batch.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · defend a ranking

A better rule under which loss?

For the same deliveries, rule B predicts 12, 18, and 20 min; the observations remain 12, 18, and 25 min. Compare A and B using squared loss. Repeat using absolute loss |u − v|: the distance between the predicted duration u and the observed duration v, in minutes. Does the ranking stay the same?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write the differences for B, then evaluate both rules with the same loss before comparing them.

Hint 2

For absolute loss, remove the sign of each difference without squaring it. The count remains 3.

Compare with the solution

The differences for B are 0, 0, and -5 min. The squared losses are 0, 0, and 25 min²; their sum is 25 min². The risk for B is 25/3 min², approximately 8.33 min². The risk for A, 17/3 min², is lower: A is preferable on this batch with this loss.

For absolute loss, A gives the distances 2, 2, and 3 min. Their sum is 7 min; the mean is 7/3 min, approximately 2.33 min. B gives 0, 0, and 5 min, so its risk under absolute loss is 5/3 min, approximately 1.67 min. B is preferable under this second criterion.

Squared loss penalizes the single large error of B more heavily. Absolute loss leads to the reverse ranking here. Choose the penalty according to the need before interpreting the ranking; do not directly compare a value in minutes with a value in square minutes. Neither ranking demonstrates superiority on new deliveries.

  • You compare the rules using the same loss and give the corresponding units.
  • You justify the reversal of the ranking and limit the conclusion to the records evaluated.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be fixed for “empirical risk” to refer to a precise quantity?

The batch of observations, the prediction rule, the loss, and the weights. Here, the weights are equal: add a loss for each observation and divide by the number of observations.

Recall 2Why apply the loss before summing the differences?

A signed sum can cancel opposite differences. Squaring each difference penalizes each inaccurate announcement separately; the square of their sum does not measure the same thing.

Recall 3What denominator and unit does our risk under squared loss have?

The denominator is the number of deliveries evaluated, with no correction. The difference is in minutes, its square is in square minutes, and the mean remains in square minutes.

Recall 4Does a ranking obtained with squares remain valid with absolute distances?

Not necessarily. The penalties change: in our batch, A is preferable with squares and B with absolute distances. A ranking must state its loss and batch.

Recall 5What does a low risk calculated on the available records fail to demonstrate?

It does not, by itself, demonstrate a low loss in future situations. Additional evidence is needed about the connection between the observed batch and the situations we want to predict.
