Level 1 · C034·M3

Fundamental theorem of calculus

Your goalConnect flow rate with accumulated volume, then calculate a definite integral by evaluating an antiderivative at the limits.

Before you start
c033-m5 : linear approximation · c034-m1 : Riemann sums · c034-m2 : integral

✿01 See the idea

A pump fills a tank. Reading the flow rate at the end does not tell you how many litres have entered: the pump may have run more slowly earlier. To find the added volume, you need to accumulate what entered throughout the interval.

The flow rate is a rate of filling, in litres per minute. The added volume is in litres. On a graph of flow rate against time, a small time width multiplied by a flow rate height gives a small volume; the integral combines these contributions. This is the meaning of the Riemann sums and the integral covered in the prerequisites.

Now consider the volume added since a fixed starting time. Over a very short period, its increase comes from the flow rate at that time. If the flow rate varies continuously, the slope of this accumulation gives exactly the flow rate. This connection will also let us calculate the entire area by taking a difference at the endpoints.

The tank and all the values are fictional. The model assumes a continuous flow rate, no leaks or outflow, and sufficient capacity. We follow the same tank from a = 1 min to b = 4 min; it already contains 5 L at time a. The area represents only the volume added after a.

The rule to remember

For a continuous flow rate, differentiating the accumulation gives the flow rate; to calculate the accumulation between a and b, take an antiderivative F and subtract F(a) from F(b).

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

How many litres has the pump added?

On the interval [a, b] = [1, 4] min, the flow rate follows q(t) = α + βt, with α = 2 L/min and β = 1 L/(min·min). At time a, the tank contains V(a) = 5 L. Let us find the added volume, then the volume present at b.

  1. Name what varies and what accumulates

    t denotes the time in minutes; a and b are the fixed starting and ending times, with a < b. α is the constant term of the flow rate and β is its increase per minute. Each term of q(t) is therefore in L/min. The function q is affine, so it is continuous: it has no jumps on this interval.

    A(t) denotes the volume added between a and t, in litres. The letter u is a time variable used only inside the integral; the differential notation indicates integration with respect to this time variable. The upper limit t varies, while the lower limit a stays fixed. V(t) is the total volume present in the tank.

    A(t)=∫atq(u) du;V(t)=V(a)+A(t)

    At the start, the accumulation interval is empty: A(a) = 0 L. This does not require V(a) = 0 L: the volume already present is 5 L.

  2. Understand why the derivative gives the flow rate

    h is a small, nonzero change in time, in minutes, such that t and t + h stay in the interval. The difference A(t + h) − A(t) keeps only the volume added over this small interval. Dividing it by h gives the average flow rate over this interval, in L/min.

    A(t+h)−A(t)h=1h∫tt+hq(u) du

    Because q is continuous, on a sufficiently short interval all its flow rates stay as close as desired to q(t). Their average therefore tends to q(t) as h tends to 0 min. For negative h, the orientation of the integral and the sign of h cancel each other out. We obtain A′(t) = q(t) in the interior of [a, b]; the prime denotes the derivative with respect to time. At the endpoints, we consider the derivative from within the interval.

    This is the first connection in the fundamental theorem of calculus: the accumulation of a continuous function is an antiderivative of that function. By definition, an antiderivative is a function whose derivative gives the original function.

  3. Go from the antiderivative to the difference at the limits

    Let F be an antiderivative of q: F is continuous on [a, b], differentiable in the interior, and F′ = q. It is expressed in litres. Since A and F have the same derivative, their difference has 0 slope and stays constant on this interval. The condition A(a) = 0 fixes this constant and gives A(t) = F(t) − F(a).

    ∫abq(t) dt=F(b)−F(a)

    This is the second connection: to obtain the accumulation, evaluate the same antiderivative at the end and at the start, then subtract in that order. A definite integral is a number with a unit; an antiderivative is a function.

  4. Construct the antiderivative and check it by differentiating

    For q(t) = α + βt, try F(t) = αt + (β/2)t squared. Differentiating the linear term gives α; differentiating the squared term multiplies its coefficient by 2 and leaves t. The coefficient β/2 is chosen precisely to recover βt.

    F(t)=αt+β2t2
    F′(t)=α+β2×2t=α+βt=q(t)

    The coefficient β/2 is 1/2 L/(min·min), or 0.5 L/(min·min). Multiplied by t squared, it does give litres, just like αt. We could add a constant C in litres to F: its derivative would be 0 L/min, and C would cancel in the difference at the limits.

  5. Evaluate at the required times

    At the end, b squared is 4 × 4 = 16 min·min. The linear contribution is 2 × 4 = 8 L; the quadratic contribution is (1/2) × 16 = 8 L. Thus F(b) = 8 + 8 = 16 L.

    At the start, a squared is 1 min·min. We find αa = 2 L and (β/2)a squared = 1/2 L. Thus F(a) = 2 + 1/2 = 5/2 L, or 2.5 L. This number is a value of our chosen antiderivative; it is not the initial volume in the tank.

    Subtract the starting value to keep only the interval under study: F(b) − F(a) = 16 − (5/2) = 27/2 L, or 13.5 L added. Then add the volume already present: V(b) = 5 + (27/2) = 37/2 L, or 18.5 L.

    ∫14(2+1t) dt=16−2.5=13.5 L

  6. Check using the diagram and the units

    The starting flow rate is q(a) = 2 + 1 × 1 = 3 L/min; the ending flow rate is q(b) = 2 + 1 × 4 = 6 L/min. The duration is b − a = 4 − 1 = 3 min. Calculate the trapezoid area by adding its parallel heights, multiplying by its width, then dividing by 2.

    (3+6)×32=272=13.5 L

    This geometric check gives 13.5 L again. It is exact here because the flow rate is affine: the boundary of the area is a straight line segment. For another continuous flow rate, a trapezoid would generally give an approximation, whereas F(b) − F(a) would remain exact if F′ = q.

    Check the order of magnitude: the flow rate increases from 3 to 6 L/min, so the added volume lies between 9 and 18 L. The 13.5 L satisfy these bounds. Finally, L/min multiplied by min gives L: 6 L/min cannot be the final volume.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The final flow rate is enough to determine the added volume.”

    6 L/min describes a rate at the end. Multiplying this flow rate by the entire duration would give 18 L, as if the final flow rate had applied throughout the interval. Here the flow rate was lower earlier; we need to accumulate q(t), which gives 13.5 L.

    Compare the final height with the area under the flow rate graph

  • “The integral from a to b equals F(b).”

    F(b) also includes the reference value carried by F(a). Subtract F(a) using the same antiderivative: 16 − (5/2) = 27/2 L. F(b) alone works only if the chosen antiderivative is 0 at the start.

    Review the step from the antiderivative to the limits

  • “The 13.5 L from the integral are everything in the tank.”

    A measures the volume added since a and satisfies A(a) = 0 L. The volume present follows V(t) = V(a) + A(t). Here 5 L were already present: the volume at the end is 18.5 L. The arbitrary value F(a) does not replace this initial condition.

    Distinguish added volume from volume present in the exercises

Hint 1

A is the accumulation of a continuous flow rate. Which function do you recover when you differentiate A?

Hint 2

Evaluate q(t) = α + βt at the required time, then read the units.

Compare with the solution

The theorem gives A′(t) = q(t). At time 2 min, βt = 1 × 2 = 2 L/min; therefore A′(2) = 2 + 2 = 4 L/min.

The slope describes the instantaneous rate of filling. A very short duration multiplied by this slope approximates the volume added over that duration. The volume accumulated since a must be calculated in litres using an integral; the derivative alone does not give it.

  • Find 4 L/min and describe an instantaneous rate.

Exercise 2 · Independent calculation on paper

Stop the pump earlier

The tank still contains 5 L at a = 1 min, but the pump is stopped at t = 2 min. With the same q, calculate the volume added since a and the volume present when the pump stops. Write the antiderivative, its evaluations, and their difference.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The ending limit changes; the starting time a stays the same. You can keep an antiderivative that you have already checked.

Hint 2

Subtract F(a) from F(2), then add the initial volume only to find the volume present.

Compare with the solution

Use F(t) = αt + (β/2)t squared again; its derivative gives q. At t = 2 min, the square is 4 min·min; the contributions are 4 L and 2 L. Thus F(2) = 6 L.

We must subtract F(a) = 5/2 L: A(2) = 6 − (5/2) = 7/2 L, or 3.5 L. The volume present is 5 + (7/2) = 17/2 L, or 8.5 L.

These results are consistent with an earlier stop and a positive flow rate: the added volume is less than 13.5 L and the volume present is less than 18.5 L. The check uses the same flow rate function and the same starting volume.

Check your reasoning

  • Check F′ = q before evaluating the antiderivative.
  • Subtract F(a) from the new ending value.
  • Give 3.5 L added and 8.5 L present, explaining the difference.

Exercise 3 · Answer an objection

Does changing the antiderivative change the added volume?

Return to the interval [1, 4] min. Someone chooses G(t) = F(t) + 7 L and claims that the added volume increases by 7 L. Calculate G(a), G(b), then the integral using G. What happens to this integral if its limits are swapped? Explain the sign without inventing any draining of the tank.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The added constant appears at both the end and the start. Write the parentheses in the subtraction.

Hint 2

When the limits are swapped, the order of the difference also changes. Mathematical orientation does not change the events in the scene.

Compare with the solution

G′ = F′, because the derivative of the constant is 0 L/min. G is therefore also an antiderivative of q. The values at the limits are G(a) = 5/2 + 7 = 19/2 L and G(b) = 16 + 7 = 23 L.

The difference is G(b) − G(a) = 23 − (19/2) = 27/2 L, or 13.5 L. Algebraically, [F(b) + C] − [F(a) + C] = F(b) − F(a). The reference constant cancels: the added volume does not change.

With the limits swapped, the integral is F(a) − F(b) = -27/2 L, or -13.5 L. The sign comes from the chosen orientation of the integral. The pump did add 13.5 L between a and b in chronological order; writing the limits in the other order does not cause any draining.

Neither F(a) nor G(a) is automatically the actual initial volume. That remains the given value V(a) = 5 L. Only an antiderivative adjusted to this condition could represent V directly.

Check your reasoning

  • Use its derivative to justify that G is an antiderivative.
  • Show that the same constant cancels at the limits and recover 13.5 L.
  • Obtain -13.5 L for the reverse order and distinguish orientation from physical draining.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the derivative of A(t) = integral from a to t of q(u) with respect to u give, and under which assumption used here?

If q is continuous on the interval, A′(t) = q(t) in the interior. The average flow rate on a shrinking interval tends to the instantaneous flow rate. A is in litres and A′ is in litres per minute.

Recall 2How do you calculate the integral of a continuous flow rate between a and b using an antiderivative F?

Check F′ = q, then calculate F(b) − F(a), end minus start using the same antiderivative. The difference gives the added volume; F(b) alone does not remove the starting reference.

Recall 3Why does adding a constant to an antiderivative leave the definite integral unchanged?

The derivative of the constant is 0, so the function remains an antiderivative. The same constant appears in the starting and ending values, then cancels when they are subtracted.

Recall 4How do you distinguish A(t), F(t), and V(t) for the tank?

A is the volume added since a, with A(a) = 0 L. F is any antiderivative of the flow rate: A(t) = F(t) − F(a). V is the volume present: V(t) = V(a) + A(t), with the initial volume provided separately.

Recall 5Why does the trapezoid check our calculation exactly, and when does this check lose its exactness?

Our flow rate is affine, so its graph is a line segment and the area under that segment is a trapezoid. For a continuous flow rate with a curved graph, this trapezoid generally gives only an approximation; the antiderivative formula remains exact under the assumptions of the theorem.
