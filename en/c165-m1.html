Level 3 · C165·M1

Lp spaces

Your goalChoose a measure, calculate an Lᵖ norm and distinguish a function from its equivalence class under equality almost everywhere.

Before you start
c164-m5 : Fubini-Tonelli · c040-m5 : least squares · c142-m5 : convergence criteria

✿01 See the idea

A probe monitors the temperature deviation from a setpoint for 60 s. In our fictional record, the deviation stays at -1 °C for 30 s, then at 7 °C for the same length of time. The sign indicates which side of the setpoint the temperature is on. To measure the size of the deviation, should we add the signed values, their distances from the setpoint or their squares?

A signed average lets cold offset heat. An average of absolute values preserves each distance. An average of squares gives greater weight to large distances; taking its square root then returns the result to degrees. An essential bound describes something else again: the smallest bound that holds outside a set of duration 0.

Duration matters as much as height. We give each portion of time a weight equal to its duration divided by the total duration. Half of the record therefore has weight 0.50, with no unit. Lᵖ spaces organize the functions for which the quantity calculated in this way remains finite; p is the exponent that selects this quantity.

An isolated point in time has duration 0. Changing only the value at that instant does not change any integral with respect to duration. This is why Lᵖ groups functions that are equal almost everywhere: different expressions can represent the same element. This rule depends on the chosen measure; it does not allow us to remove a value arbitrarily from a table of readings.

We use measurable functions, the Lebesgue integral and sets of measure 0 from the prerequisite on measure. This page establishes their role in the record and introduces equivalence classes and their norms. Completeness, Hilbert spaces, operators and duality belong to the following units.

The rule to remember

Fix the domain and the measure; integrate a power of the absolute value, then return to the original scale; identify functions that differ only on a set of measure 0.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Measure the size of the record while accounting for duration

The function f gives the fictional deviation from the setpoint, in °C: -1 up to 30 s, then 7 up to 60 s. We choose the normalized duration measure and calculate the norms for p = 1, p = 2 and the essential bound.

  1. Fix the domain, measure and units

    The domain X is the time interval [0, T], with T = 60 s. The symbol t denotes an instant, in seconds. The letter 𝒜 denotes the collection of measurable subsets of X. For A in 𝒜, λ(A) is its Lebesgue duration, in seconds, and μ(A) = λ(A)/T is its weight with no unit. Thus (X, 𝒜, μ) is the measure space in which we work, and μ(X) = 1.

    f(t)={-1 °C0≤t<30 s7 °C30 s≤t≤T

    The function is measurable: it is constant on measurable intervals. The first plateau has weight 30/60 = 1/2 = 0.50; the second has weight 1 − 1/2 = 1/2. Check: 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. Integrating a function that is constant on each plateau means multiplying its value by the weight of the plateau, then adding.

    Normalization is part of the problem. With the unnormalized duration λ, the integral would have an additional time factor. Here dμ = dt/T: dt represents the duration element and dμ the weight element. The numerical temperature values are expressed in the fixed unit °C; p and μ have no unit.

  2. Define equivalence classes before discussing distance 0

    Two measurable functions u and v are equal μ-almost everywhere if the set of points where they differ has μ-measure 0. The symbol ∼ denotes this relation; [u] is the class of all functions equivalent to u. A representative is a particular function chosen from this class.

    u∼v⇔μ({t∈X:u(t)≠v(t)})=0

    Consider g again, equal to f everywhere except at t = 15 s, where g equals 100 °C. At the modified point, g − f = 100 − (-1) = 101 °C. But μ({15 s}) = 0: a point has duration 0. Therefore [f] = [g], although the functions are not equal at every instant.

    For every finite positive p, |g − f| raised to the power p differs from 0 only at this point. Its integral is 0. In particular, the integrated absolute distance is 0 °C; the integral of the squared difference is 0 degrees squared. Grouping representatives prevents an element distinct from the 0 element from having distance 0 from it.

  3. Define Lᵖ and its norm

    On a fixed measure space, u is a real-valued measurable function and p is a real exponent with 1 ≤ p < ∞. We define Ip(u) as the integral of the pth power of its absolute value. Lᵖ(X, μ) is the set of classes [u] for which Ip(u) is finite. The symbol ∈ means “belongs to”. We often write the norm of f instead of the norm of [f].

    Ip(u)=∫X|u(t)|pdμ(t)
    [u]∈Lp(X,μ)⇔Ip(u)<∞
    ‖[u]‖p=(Ip(u))1p

    The power 1/p reverses the change of scale caused by the power p. The integral Ip has unit °C raised to the power p; the norm returns to °C. The integrand is nonnegative: we require a finite integral of the absolute value raised to the power p, rather than cancellation between signs.

    For p = ∞, we use a separate definition. L∞ contains the essentially bounded classes: there is a finite bound M such that |u| ≤ M almost everywhere. Its norm is the essential supremum, meaning the smallest bound of this kind. The infimum below denotes the greatest lower bound of the admissible values of M; if no finite M works, the value is ∞.

    ‖[u]‖∞=inf{M≥0:μ({t∈X:|u(t)|>M})=0}

  4. Calculate the absolute-value norm without cancellation

    Take p = 1. The absolute value of the first deviation is |-1| = 1 °C; that of the second is 7 °C. Each value is multiplied by the fraction of time for which it is present.

    ‖f‖1=12×1+12×7=4 °C

    The exact contributions are 1/2 °C and 7/2 °C; their sum gives 4 °C. In decimal form: 0.50 °C + 3.50 °C = 4.00 °C. With p = 1, the final power changes nothing.

    An independent check using durations: (30 s × 1 °C + 30 s × 7 °C)/60 s = 4 °C. The signed average would be (-1 + 7)/2 = 3 °C: it answers a different question and cannot replace this norm.

  5. Calculate the root-mean-square norm and restore the unit

    Take p = 2. The first square is 1 and the second 49, in degrees squared. Their weighted contributions are 1/2 and 49/2 degrees squared. We add them before taking the square root: the square root of a sum is not the sum of the square roots.

    I2(f)=12(-1)2+1272=25 °C2
    ‖f‖2=I2(f)=25 °C=5 °C

    The nonnegative square root is exactly 5, because 5 × 5 = 25 and 25 = 25. The number 25 alone is the average of squares, in degrees squared; it is not the norm.

    Another check: the square of the average of absolute values is 16. The difference 25 − 16 = 9 equals ((7 − 1)/2) raised to the power 2. This is the algebraic identity relating the average of squares to the square of the average for these equal weights. It confirms the radicand without repeating the same sum.

  6. Find the essential bound and check membership

    The absolute value of f never exceeds 7 °C. Any bound M smaller than 7 fails on the second plateau, whose measure is 1/2 > 0. Therefore the essential supremum norm of f is 7 °C. The function g has a pointwise maximum of 100 °C, but its essential supremum norm is also 7 °C: the single added point has measure 0.

    The integral of every finite power of f is finite, since the record is bounded and the domain has finite total measure. More explicitly, for 1 ≤ p < ∞:

    Ip(f)=121p+127p<∞

    This last formula concerns numerical values in the unit °C. Thus [f] and [g] belong to all the Lᵖ spaces considered here, including L∞. This does not mean that every measurable function on the same domain belongs to them; an unbounded function can make the integral of some powers diverge.

  7. Connect these calculations to a norm and specify their scope

    Within the same Lᵖ space, classes combine by [u] + [v] = [u + v] and a[u] = [au], where a is a real scalar with no unit. These operations are well defined: changing u and v on sets of measure 0 changes their sum only within the union of those sets, which still has measure 0. For a fixed p at least equal to 1, these operations preserve membership in Lᵖ.

    The norm is nonnegative and equals 0 exactly for the class of the 0 function. For finite p, a 0 integral of a nonnegative function here forces |u| raised to the power p to be 0 almost everywhere. For p = ∞, a 0 norm also forces u to be 0 almost everywhere. We have homogeneity ‖au‖p = |a| ‖u‖p and the triangle inequality ‖u + v‖p ≤ ‖u‖p + ‖v‖p.

    This last inequality, called Minkowski’s inequality, is stated here for general p; its proof is not needed for the calculations on this page. It says that the size of a sum does not exceed the sum of the sizes. The distance between classes is dp([u], [v]) = ‖u − v‖p; it is 0 exactly when the classes are equal. The symbol dp denotes this distance, in the unit of u and v.

    Scale check: if calibration multiplies all deviations by a = 2, each norm must double. Keeping Ip without taking the final power would multiply Ip by |a| raised to the power p, which would not respect the homogeneity of a norm when p > 1.

    For this measure of total mass 1, the values obtained agree with ‖f‖1 ≤ ‖f‖2 ≤ ‖f‖∞: 4 °C ≤ 5 °C ≤ 7 °C. On a space of total measure 1, this comparison holds for norms with increasing exponents when they are finite. With another measure, mass factors enter the comparison; on a domain of infinite measure, we cannot reuse this chain without additional assumptions. We stay within the range p ≥ 1 where the expressions define norms.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A 0 norm means that the function is 0 at every point.”

    In Lᵖ, a 0 norm means that the function is 0 almost everywhere for the chosen measure. The difference g − f equals 101 °C at one point, but represents the 0 element. We must distinguish the function from its class.

    Review the definition of classes and the modified point

  • “The average of squares is already the root-mean-square norm.”

    The average of squares is 25 degrees squared. Taking the square root gives 5 °C. Check homogeneity: a deviation multiplied by 2 must have a norm multiplied by 2, rather than by the square of this factor.

    Test the change in calibration in the first exercise

  • “I can ignore a point in any dataset.”

    A point has measure 0 for Lebesgue duration, but positive mass in an average of a few observations. We must state the measure before using “almost everywhere”. An anomaly of positive duration does not disappear from the integral either.

    Compare duration and observations in the second exercise

Exercise 1 · Application and scale check

Calibration multiplies the deviations

The same probe now multiplies each deviation of f by a = 2, without changing the durations or μ. Find the absolute-value, root-mean-square and essential supremum norms of af. Calculate at least the absolute-value norm directly, then explain why keeping the average of squares as a norm would be inconsistent.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The plateaus become -2 °C and 14 °C; their weights remain 1/2 and 1/2.

Hint 2

Use ‖af‖ₚ = |a| ‖f‖ₚ, then check the absolute-value norm using the new plateaus.

Compare with the solution

The absolute value of each plateau is multiplied by 2. Direct calculation gives (2 × 1 + 14)/2 = 8 °C. Homogeneity gives the same result: 2 × 4 = 8 °C.

For the root-mean-square norm: ‖af‖2 = 2 × 5 = 10 °C. For the essential bound: ‖af‖∞ = 2 × 7 = 14 °C. The durations have not changed; no new normalization by duration is needed.

The average of squares is multiplied by a raised to the power 2. The square root brings the factor back to |a|. Without it, we would have a quantity in degrees squared and an incorrect scaling rule for a norm.

Check your reasoning

  • Give 8 °C, 10 °C and 14 °C, with the correct names.
  • Keep the weights and check a norm by direct calculation.
  • Justify the square root using the unit and homogeneity, rather than just reciting the result.

Exercise 2 · Reasoning about the measure

An isolated instant becomes an observation

The function g equals 100 °C at 15 s and remains equal to f elsewhere. For the duration measure μ, give ‖g‖∞ and decide whether [f] = [g]. Then we change the problem: we keep only the observations at instants 15 s and 45 s. The new measure ν assigns each a weight of 1/2. Calculate the absolute-value norm of g for ν and explain the difference.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For μ, the set where f and g differ has measure 0. For ν, this set contains an observation with weight 1/2.

Hint 2

The values of g at the observed instants are 100 °C and 7 °C. The requested average uses their absolute values.

Compare with the solution

For μ, [f] = [g] and ‖g‖∞ = 7 °C. Yet the pointwise maximum is 100 °C. The essential bound ignores only sets of measure 0, rather than every large deviation.

For ν, the sum of absolute values is 100 + 7 = 107 °C. We assign the same weight to each: ‖g‖1,ν = 107/2 = 107/2 °C, or 53.50 °C. The comma followed by ν specifies the measure used.

The measure ν gives the modified point a weight of 1/2 > 0: f and g are no longer equal ν-almost everywhere. The norm of f for ν remains 4 °C, whereas that of g changes. There is no contradiction: μ describes a continuous distribution of duration and ν a distribution among selected observations.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish the pointwise maximum from the essential bound for μ.
  • Obtain 53.50 °C for ν and identify the weight of the modified point.
  • Never use “almost everywhere” without specifying the relevant measure.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and boundary of integrability

An ideal model becomes unbounded at startup

To test the definition in the same setting, we replace the plateau record with a fictional mathematical model h(t) = √(T/t) °C for 0 < t ≤ T, and h(0) = 0 °C. This unbounded model is not a proposed physical law for the probe. Keep μ and set s = t/T, a time variable with no unit. By cutting off the integral at ε, with 0 < ε < 1, decide whether [h] belongs to L1, L2 and L∞.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The change of variable gives dμ = ds. The numerical value of h becomes s raised to the power −1/2; its square becomes s raised to the power −1.

Hint 2

Between ε and 1, the numerical integrals are 2 × (1 − √ε) and −ln ε. Here ln is the natural logarithm. Study their limits as ε approaches 0 through positive values.

Compare with the solution

Since t = Ts, we have dt/T = ds. The symbol ε is a strictly positive cutoff with no unit; we let it approach 0. The integrands are nonnegative. Their truncated integrals increase towards the full integral, which may be infinite, as in the passage to the limit studied in the prerequisite on the Lebesgue integral.

∫ε11sds=2(1−ε)→2

An antiderivative of 1/√s is 2√s: its derivative is indeed 1/√s, since 2 × 1/2 = 1. The limit is finite; the absolute-value norm of h is 2 °C. The class [h] therefore belongs to L1.

∫ε11sds=−lnε→+∞

The symbol ln denotes the natural logarithm. An antiderivative of 1/s is ln s and ln 1 = 0. The integral of the square diverges: [h] does not belong to L2. Assigning a finite value at the initial instant does not repair this divergence, which comes from an entire neighborhood of that instant.

The class does not belong to L∞ either. For any finite bound M > 1 °C, the values of h exceed M on an interval of positive duration near startup. To see this, let m be the number with no unit such that M = m °C:

0<s<1m2⇒h(Ts)>M

The interval described in this way has strictly positive length. A smaller bound also fails. This is therefore not an exception confined to a single point.

Check your reasoning

  • Change to s = t/T and keep a measure with no unit.
  • Show a finite limit for the absolute-value integral and an infinite limit for the integral of the square.
  • Conclude yes for L1, no for L2 and L∞, without confusing the value at the initial point with the behavior nearby.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What object belongs to Lᵖ, and what does a 0 norm mean?

An element is a class of measurable functions equal almost everywhere for the fixed measure. A 0 norm means that the class is that of the 0 function; a representative may remain nonzero on a set of measure 0.

Recall 2Reconstruct the norm for a finite exponent p: membership condition, operations and units.

For 1 ≤ p < ∞, integrate |u| raised to the power p and require a finite integral, then take the power 1/p. The absolute value prevents cancellation; the final power restores the unit and homogeneity. The measure must be stated.

Recall 3Why does the isolated spike of g not determine its essential supremum norm for μ?

The point with value 100 °C has duration 0. The bound 7 °C holds μ-almost everywhere, and any smaller bound fails on the second plateau of positive measure. With a measure giving the point positive weight, this conclusion would change.

Recall 4Is being integrable enough for the square to be integrable on our domain of finite measure?

No. The model h(t) = √(T/t) is integrable for normalized duration, with absolute-value norm 2 °C, but the integral of its square diverges. Changing p can change membership, even on the same domain.

Recall 5What information must be fixed before comparing two calculated norms?

The domain, the measure and its normalization, the exponent and the unit of the signal. An average over observations and an average over duration use different measures. For μ(X) = 1, the record gives 4 °C, 5 °C and 7 °C for the absolute-value, root-mean-square and essential supremum norms.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct this sequence: domain and measure → measurable function → integrability of |u| raised to the power p → equivalence class under equality almost everywhere → norm after taking the power 1/p. Reconstruct the weights and norms of the record; explain the exception of measure 0 and the separate definition for ∞. Finish with a function whose square is not integrable. What you understand
An average of absolute values and a square root of an average of squares are norms for a fixed uniform measure on a nonempty finite set. The concept of Lᵖ lets us specify what is measured, what is identified and when the quantity is finite.
