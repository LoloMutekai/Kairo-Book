Level 2 · C142·M1

Real numbers and bounds

Your goalDetermine the bounds of a set of real-valued lengths, prove that they are optimal, and check whether they are attained.

Before you start
c014-m5 : invariants · c031-m5 : asymptotes · c046-m5 : approximation error · c069-m5 : congruences and Euclid

✿01 See the idea

A cutting stop allows cardboard strips at least 12 cm long and strictly less than 18 cm long. The length 12 cm is allowed; 18 cm is excluded. Can we still say that 18 cm is the supremum of the allowed lengths?

Imagine a barrier placed to the right of all possible lengths. 20 cm works, but leaves some room. 18 cm also works. If you move this barrier to 17 cm, a strip of 17.5 cm goes beyond it while still following the rule. The aim is to find the lowest barrier that still bounds the whole set from above.

The scene and its values are fictional. We idealize the setting: it can take any real-valued length in the interval, with no minimum increment or measurement error. A real machine would have a resolution that would need to be specified; we infer no manufacturing capability here.

ℝ denotes the real numbers, represented by all the points on a number line. They include the rational numbers, which are quotients of integers with a nonzero denominator, and the irrational numbers, which do not have this form. A finite decimal reading therefore does not describe the whole line. In this unit, we use its order and the fact that the midpoint of two distinct real numbers lies strictly between them.

The rule to remember

The supremum is the least upper bound. It is a maximum only if it belongs to the set. Likewise, the infimum is a minimum only if it is attained.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The optimal bound is not a final strip

Let us determine all upper and lower bounds of the allowed lengths, then their optimal bounds and any minimum and maximum. All length coordinates are expressed in centimeters.

  1. Write the set under study precisely

    x is the numerical value of a length in centimeters; a = 12 and b = 18 are the endpoint values in the same unit. A denotes the set of these allowed values. The symbol ∈ means “belongs to”; the vertical bar inside the braces is read as “such that”.

    A={x∈ℝ|a≤x<b}=[12;18[

    In the interval notation used here, the inward-facing bracket on the left includes a; the outward-facing bracket on the right excludes b. The width is b − a = 18 − 12 = 6 cm, which is strictly positive. A is nonempty because a belongs to it. All the inequalities that follow compare coordinates in the same unit.

  2. Give the barriers a precise meaning

    A real number M is an upper bound of A if every element of A is less than or equal to M. A real number m is a lower bound if every element is greater than or equal to m. Here M and m represent lengths in centimeters; they do not have to belong to A. ∀ is read as “for every”.

    M majorant :∀x∈A,x≤M
    m minorant :∀x∈A,m≤x

    Thus 20 cm is an upper bound and 10 cm is a lower bound. A is called bounded because it has both a real upper bound and a real lower bound. Checking just a few strips is not enough for the quantifier “for every”.

  3. Define the optimal bounds and extrema

    The supremum, written sup A, is the least of all upper bounds: s = sup A means that s is an upper bound and s ≤ M for every upper bound M of A. The infimum, written inf A, is the greatest of all lower bounds: i = inf A means that i is a lower bound and m ≤ i for every lower bound m. Again, s and i are coordinates in centimeters.

    A maximum is an element of A that is an upper bound of A. A minimum is an element of A that is a lower bound of A. Membership is therefore an additional condition. If they exist, the maximum equals sup A and the minimum equals inf A: for example, every upper bound must be at least as large as the maximum because it must also bound that element from above.

    These definitions alone do not prove that optimal bounds exist for an arbitrary set. Here we will exhibit and justify ours directly; general completeness will be studied in the unit devoted to it.

  4. Prove the left bound and propose the right one

    For every x ∈ A, a ≤ x < b. Thus a is a lower bound of A and b is an upper bound of A, because x < b implies x ≤ b. Since a ∈ A, a is also the minimum. Every lower bound m satisfies m ≤ a when its definition is applied to the element a. No lower bound greater than a is possible: inf A = min A = 12 cm.

    On the right, we have only shown that b works as an upper bound. To establish sup A = b, we still need to rule out every candidate strictly less than b. The fact that b is excluded from A does not settle this question.

  5. Calculate a witness that refutes a candidate

    Let r be the proposed upper bound. Try r = b − 1 = 17 cm. To find a length that exceeds it without reaching b, take their midpoint t: adding the coordinates and then dividing by 2 splits the gap into equal halves. The divisor has no unit.

    t=r+b2=17+182=352=17.5 cm

    Membership check: 12 ≤ 17.5 < 18, so t ∈ A. Refutation check: t − r = 17.5 − 17 = 0.5 cm > 0 cm. A single element exceeding r is enough to prove that r is not an upper bound of A.

    Another calculation: the gap b − r is 1 cm; half of it is 0.5 cm. Subtracting this half from b again gives 18 − 0.5 = 17.5 cm. The remaining distance b − t is indeed 0.5 cm, equal to the amount by which t exceeds r.

  6. Move from the numerical candidate to all candidates

    Now fix any real number r strictly less than b. If it is less than a, the element a ∈ A already exceeds it. Otherwise a ≤ r < b: take t = (r + b)/2 again. The gaps on either side of the midpoint are:

    t−r=b−t=b−r2>0

    Since b − r is strictly positive, these equalities give r < t < b. Since a ≤ r, we also have a ≤ t. Thus t belongs to A and exceeds r: r is not an upper bound. This covers all r < b, including those to the left of the interval.

    Thus b is an upper bound, and no smaller real number is one. This is exactly the definition of sup A: sup A = 18 cm. The argument applies to an arbitrary candidate; adding more numerical trials would not replace this proof.

  7. Check whether the right bound is attained

    b ∉ A: the symbol ∉ means “does not belong to”. The supremum is therefore not a maximum. More directly, take any length x ∈ A. Applying the previous construction with r = x gives another allowed length that is strictly greater. A has no greatest element.

    There is no “last decimal value just before 18”. Our model still allows the midpoint between any allowed length and b. This claim concerns the real numbers in the interval; a finite list of settings would have a different set of possible values.

  8. Collect the results and check their scope

    The upper bounds of A are exactly the real numbers M ≥ b: they all work, and the construction has ruled out all the others. The lower bounds are exactly the real numbers m ≤ a: they all work, and the element a rules out those that exceed it. In centimeters: inf A = min A = 12; sup A = 18; no maximum.

    Final check: the rule must allow a and reject b; the gaps in the calculation must be positive and in centimeters. Having bounds does not show that a machine attains them. For this ideal set, only the left bound is attained.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If 20 cm exceeds everything, it is the supremum.”

    This only proves that 20 cm is an upper bound. 18 cm is a smaller one. To establish a supremum, you must also refute all candidates smaller than it, using an allowed witness when needed.

    Revisit the definition, then the proof for an arbitrary candidate

  • “Since 18 cm is excluded, there is no supremum.”

    A supremum does not have to belong to the set. Membership is what distinguishes a maximum. Here 18 cm is the least upper bound, while every allowed element can still be exceeded.

    Review the empty endpoint and the witness placed before it

  • “I have checked many lengths, so my bound is proved.”

    A finite series of trials does not cover all allowed real-valued lengths. The rule a ≤ x < b establishes an upper bound for every x; the midpoint construction rules out every candidate r < b. Keep these quantifiers in your proof.

Exercise 1 · Application · read the definitions

Classify the barriers

For the same cutting stop, classify 10, 12, 18 and 20 cm as lower or upper bounds of A. Then give inf A, sup A, the minimum, and the maximum if it exists. Also justify why 12 cm is not an upper bound.

Hint 1

A barrier can lie outside A. Ask whether it is on the correct side of all its elements.

Hint 2

To refute the proposed upper bound 12 cm, look for a strictly greater allowed length.

Compare with the solution

10 and 12 cm are lower bounds because all elements are at least 12 cm. 18 and 20 cm are upper bounds because all elements are strictly less than 18 cm. 12 cm is not an upper bound: for example, 17 cm belongs to A and exceeds it.

inf A = 12 cm, which is also the minimum because it is allowed. sup A = 18 cm, with no maximum because this endpoint is excluded and no element can be the greatest. 10 and 20 cm are looser bounds than the optimal bounds.

Exercise 2 · Exact calculation · construct a counterexample

The new candidate also fails

The person accepts the calculation but now claims that 17.50 cm is the maximum of A. Construct an allowed length that exceeds it by taking the midpoint with 18 cm. Give the exact result and then its decimal form, and calculate the amount by which it exceeds the candidate and the remaining distance to 18 cm.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The new candidate is an exact fraction: 35/2. Add it to 18, then divide by 2.

Hint 2

To check the construction, the gaps on either side of the midpoint must be equal and strictly positive.

Compare with the solution

The sum of the coordinates is 35/2 + 18 = 71/2 cm. The midpoint is therefore (71/2)/2 = 71/4 cm = 17.75 cm. Keep the fractions until this final decimal form.

Membership: 12 ≤ 17.75 < 18. Increase over the candidate: 17.75 − 17.50 = 1/4 cm = 0.25 cm. Remaining distance: 18 − 17.75 = 0.25 cm. The equal, positive gaps confirm the midpoint and refute the claimed maximum.

This provides a sufficient counterexample to this claim. The absence of any maximum rests on the construction for an arbitrary element x of A.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain 71/4 cm and check both inequalities for membership.
  • You show a strictly positive increase of 0.25 cm without rounding during the calculation.
  • You distinguish refuting a candidate from the general proof that there is no maximum.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · write a proof

The stop also excludes the lower length

Change only the admission rule: lengths must now be strictly between 12 and 18 cm. Call this new set B. Determine inf B, sup B, and then whether a minimum and a maximum exist. To prove the result on the left, start with an arbitrary length x ∈ B and construct a smaller allowed length. Illustrate this with the midpoint of the endpoints.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The construction on the right still works. On the left, try the midpoint between a and x.

Hint 2

To refute a proposed lower bound m > a, handle m ≥ b and a < m < b separately; in the latter case, take the midpoint of a and m.

Compare with the solution

B = ]12 ; 18[: a and b remain a lower bound and an upper bound respectively. To rule out every lower bound m > a, if m ≥ b, any element of B is less than m; for example c = (a + b)/2 = 30/2 = 15 cm. If a < m < b, the midpoint (a + m)/2 is in B and strictly less than m. Thus inf B = a = 12 cm.

For every x ∈ B, the midpoint v = (a + x)/2 satisfies a < v < x < b. It is a smaller element: there is no minimum. Illustration with x = c = 15 cm: v = (12 + 15)/2 = 27/2 = 13.5 cm. The gaps v − a and x − v are each 1.5 cm; the second is also 1.5 cm by direct subtraction.

On the right, for every candidate r < b, if r ≤ a, the midpoint c exceeds it; otherwise (r + b)/2 belongs to B and exceeds it. Thus sup B = b = 18 cm. For every x ∈ B, (x + b)/2 is allowed and greater: there is no maximum. Opening the lower endpoint has removed the minimum while preserving the infimum.

Check your reasoning

  • You prove that the bounds are optimal, rather than just naming the endpoints.
  • You construct a strictly smaller element for every x ∈ B, and a strictly greater element.
  • You explain why the same bounds can go with different membership conditions.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must you check for M to be an upper bound of A? And for a lower bound m?

For every x ∈ A, x ≤ M. For a lower bound m, for every x ∈ A, m ≤ x. These bounds do not have to belong to A. The comparisons use the same unit.

Recall 2What requirements distinguish a supremum from an upper bound?

s must be an upper bound of A, and then be less than or equal to every other upper bound. You can prove this second part by refuting every candidate strictly less than s with an element of A that exceeds it. The infimum is defined by reversing the inequalities.

Recall 3Why does our interval have a supremum but no maximum?

18 cm is its least upper bound, but this length is excluded. Every allowed length x can be exceeded by the midpoint between x and 18 cm, which is still allowed. On the left, 12 cm belongs to A and is therefore both the minimum and the infimum.

Recall 4How can you refute all candidates r < b for A = [a ; b[ without limiting yourself to a few trials?

Fix an arbitrary r. If r < a, a belongs to A and exceeds r. If a ≤ r < b, t = (r + b)/2 belongs to A and exceeds r because t − r = b − t = (b − r)/2 > 0. A witness element may depend on the candidate.

Recall 5What assumption about the cutting stop makes the midpoint construction possible every time?

The model allows all real numbers in the interval, with no minimum increment. A finite list of settings does not represent this same set: it may have a final allowed value. The ideal proof does not establish a device’s resolution.
