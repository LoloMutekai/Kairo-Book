Level 2 · C042·M4

Embeddings as an external service

Your goalUse a vector representation supplied by a service, check its validity and explain why changing the model can make an index incompatible.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You search for “adjust the brake” in a collection of bicycle instruction guides. The Brake guide explains how to adjust the cable; the Wheel guide explains how to remove the wheel. The collection stores the texts and their stable identifiers. But who turns these sentences into comparable vectors?

An embedding is an ordered sequence of numbers produced by a trained model. Here, the application delegates this work to an embedding service: it gives the service a text and receives a vector. It remains responsible for checking the returned vector, linking it to the correct guide and searching its index.

“External” describes the division of responsibilities. The computation can be provided by a library in the same process, by another local program or by a remote server. You need to know the actual provider: sending a text remotely involves a transfer that simply comparing vectors locally does not require.

The service is not a table of words to translate by hand. Its coordinates depend on a trained model; they are not necessarily axes named “brake” and “wheel”. We will not reconstruct its training. We will learn to use its output according to an explicit contract.

The collection, the Iris service and all the coordinates below are fictional. The vectors are chosen for calculation with pencil and paper; they do not come from any model that was run. The resulting score describes their geometric proximity, without proving the actual relevance of the guides.

The rule to remember

Have the vectors produced under a compatible contract, validate each returned vector, then compare. A correct dimension guarantees neither the right representation space nor the quality of the result.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Find the Brake guide without inventing its coordinates

The fictional Iris alpha contract specifies the model, its revision, the text preparation and a raw output of dimension d = 2. The client normalizes the vectors before comparison. The supplied reply for the question is q = (3 ; 4), for Brake it is a = (4 ; 3), and for Wheel it is b = (0 ; 5). These numbers are exercise data, not deductions from the words.

  1. Specify what must remain compatible

    Let e(t) denote the vector returned for text t. The letter e denotes the supplied transformation; t is the text, with no physical unit. The dimension d counts the components. q, a and b denote the raw vectors of the question, Brake and Wheel respectively. Every component, norm and score is dimensionless.

    Here, Iris alpha uses the same encoding for guides and questions. The contract includes a specific model revision and text preparation, such as whitespace processing. The chosen rule produces the same vector for the same text. Some services provide different but compatible preparation for documents and questions: their compatibility must then be stated rather than guessed.

    With each vector, the application records the guide identifier, the version of its text and the contract used. The name “Iris” alone is not enough if the provider changes the parameters under that name. We assume that this information is reliable and that the texts remain unchanged during the calculation.

  2. Reject unusable returned vectors

    Each returned vector must have exactly 2 components, all finite: no infinite or undefined values. For a vector v = (vx ; vᵧ), the letters vx and vᵧ denote its components; the subscripts x and y only identify their positions. Its Euclidean norm ‖v‖ is the square root of the sum of the squares of its components. It must be strictly positive to allow division.

    ‖q‖2=32+42=9+16=25

    For Brake: 16 + 9 = 25. For Wheel: 0 + 25 = 25. Each norm therefore equals n = 5, because 5 × 5 = 25 and the square root is positive. The letter n abbreviates this common norm, a feature of the chosen data.

    If the service is unavailable, returns the wrong length or produces (0 ; 0), the client reports an embedding failure. It produces neither a 0 score nor the conclusion “no relevant guide”. If only the question fails, the previously validated guide vectors remain stored and are not used to invent a result.

  3. Normalize without changing direction

    Let uᵥ = v/‖v‖ denote the normalized vector associated with a nonzero vector v: divide each of its components by its norm. The coordinates change, but the direction stays the same. The norm of uᵥ is 1.

    Thus u_q = (3/5 ; 4/5), u_a = (4/5 ; 3/5), u_b = (0 ; 1). In French decimal notation: u_q = (0.60 ; 0.80), u_a = (0.80 ; 0.60), u_b = (0.00 ; 1.00), still dimensionless.

    For example, the squared norm of the normalized question vector equals (9 + 16)/25 = 1. If the service already promises normalized vectors, this check verifies that promise within a stated numerical tolerance; our exercise uses exact fractions, with no tolerance or intermediate rounding.

  4. Calculate the Brake score

    The score s_a is the cosine similarity between the question and Brake. The dot · denotes the dot product: it multiplies components in matching positions and then adds the products. For nonzero vectors, the cosine divides this dot product by the product of their norms.

    sa=q·a‖q‖‖a‖=3×4+4×35×5=2425=0.96

    The products are 12 and 12. Their sum is 24; the denominator is 25. Division gives the exact fraction 24/25, or 0.96, dimensionless. Keeping this fraction avoids accumulating rounding errors.

  5. Compare Wheel and check by another method

    For Wheel, q · b = 3 × 0 + 4 × 5 = 0 + 20 = 20. The score s_b equals 20/25 = 4/5 = 0.80, dimensionless. Brake ranks ahead of Wheel because 0.96 is greater than 0.80.

    Independent check using the normalized vectors: u_q · u_a = (3/5) × (4/5) + (4/5) × (3/5) = 24/25. Similarly, u_q · u_b = (3/5) × (0) + (4/5) × (1) = 4/5. The dot product is sufficient here because the norms equal 1; it is not sufficient for arbitrary raw outputs.

    The scores do lie between −1 and 1, the bounds of the cosine. The result establishes the proximity ordering of these vectors in Iris alpha. It does not turn 0.96 into a probability of a correct answer: you still need to read the guide to check that it meets the need. Measuring search quality will be the subject of the next unit.

  6. Keep a result whose provenance is known

    The returned result links the Brake identifier to the score 0.96 and the Iris alpha contract. The retrieved text remains a document distinct from its vector: its content and version can be checked. This association gives an interpretable result without claiming to explain how the model learned its coordinates.

    A correctly formatted reply from another contract remains unusable with this index until compatibility is established. If an incompatible change occurs, prepare a new set of vectors for the guides and questions using the new contract, then check that set before replacing the old one. Keeping the same identifiers does not make the coordinates compatible.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The service replied, so I can definitely calculate a score.”

    First check the length, finite components and positive norm. An empty, 0 or malformed reply is an embedding failure, not a search score. Also reread the contract it belongs to.

    Review the checks before normalization

  • “Same dimension, so I can mix versions.”

    The dimension counts the coordinates; it does not say what they represent. Recalculate the coordinate change in the last exercise, then prepare a consistent index using the new contract.

    Test the change of contract

  • “A high cosine proves that the guide is the right one.”

    The cosine measures geometric proximity under the chosen model. It does not directly measure the relevance of the text. Return to the Brake result and separate its ranking from checking its content.

Exercise 1 · Diagnose a returned vector

Reject without concluding that there is no result

For the same question, the Iris alpha service first returns (0 ; 0), then, on another attempt, (3 ; 4 ; 0). The contract expects 2 components. For each returned vector, state what fails and what the application can report. Can it fill in a score of 0?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A correct length does not guarantee that normalization is possible.

Hint 2

Distinguish a completed comparison from a comparison prevented by an invalid returned vector.

Compare with the solution

The vector (0 ; 0) has the correct length, but its norm is 0: the division required for the cosine is impossible. The returned vector (3 ; 4 ; 0) has 3 components instead of 2; it fails the dimension check, even though its norm would be positive.

In both cases, the application reports an invalid embedding output and produces no semantic score. A score of 0 could represent a true 0 cosine between valid vectors: using it to hide a failure would confuse these situations. The stored guides do not become absent as a result.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify the 0 norm and the incorrect length separately.
  • Do not divide by a 0 norm or silently truncate the returned vector.
  • Report that the search was not computed, without inventing a score or a verdict about the guides.

The new vector is identical to the Brake vector; also use this fact to check your calculation.

Compare with the solution

The squared norm of q′ equals 16 + 9 = 25; its norm is 5. With Brake: q′ · a = 25, then 25/25 = 1. Check: the cosine of a nonzero vector with itself equals 1.

With Wheel: 4 × 0 + 3 × 5 = 0 + 15 = 15. The cosine equals 3/5 = 0.60, dimensionless. Brake remains the first candidate.

Even the maximum score 1 proves only that the representations are aligned. The model may give identical representations to different texts. You need to read the guide before asserting that it contains the information sought.

Exercise 3 · Reason about a migration

The same number of coordinates, a different coordinate frame

Return to the initial question. To isolate the problem, a fictional Iris beta contract replaces every alpha vector (x ; y) with (y ; −x). It therefore returns qᵦ = (4 ; -3), aᵦ = (3 ; -4), bᵦ = (5 ; 0). The subscript β denotes this contract. First calculate the disallowed cosine between qᵦ and the old a; then recalculate it between qᵦ and aᵦ. Why is normalization not enough to repair the mixture? What needs to be prepared for the whole collection?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The norms remain 5. Compare the numerator of the mixed calculation with the one where both vectors have changed.

Hint 2

The rule from (x ; y) to (y ; −x) is a common coordinate change that preserves dot products if applied to both vectors.

Compare with the solution

The mixed calculation gives 4 × 4 + (-3) × 3 = 16 + (-9) = 7. Dividing it by 25 produces 7/25 = 0.28. The length and norm are valid, but the calculation mixes contracts.

Also using the new guide vector: 4 × 3 + (-3) × (-4) = 12 + 12 = 24. The cosine returns to 24/25 = 0.96. The rule preserves the sum of squares and the dot product when it transforms both vectors; this checks the result through geometry.

Normalizing each vector changes its length, not the coordinate frame its components come from. You need to produce vectors for all the guides under beta, keep the link to their identifiers and texts, then check the new index and the questions under that same contract before switching over.

The coordinate change rule is a teaching construction. Two real trained models are not necessarily related by such a rotation, and their rankings can differ even after a consistent migration. The calculation demonstrates the need for compatibility; it does not guarantee the equivalence of two models.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain both scores with their respective provenance.
  • Explain why normalization does not repair a change of coordinate frame.
  • Prepare all the guides under the new contract and do not promise unchanged quality.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do you delegate to the service, and what do you keep in the application?

The service transforms a text into an embedding. The application checks the returned vector, its provenance and its association with the document, then maintains the index and search. The provider can be local or remote.

Recall 2Why is knowing the dimension not enough to compare vectors?

The length does not guarantee a shared representation space. A compatible contract for the model, revision, text preparation and normalization is needed for the guides and questions.

Recall 3What should you do with a 0 or malformed output?

Refuse to compare it and report the embedding failure. Check the length, finite components and positive norm. A failure becomes neither a 0 score nor proof that no relevant guide exists.

Recall 4When is the dot product sufficient, and what does its result prove?

For vectors of unit norm in a compatible space, the dot product equals the cosine. This score establishes geometric proximity under the chosen model, without certifying the relevance of the text.

Recall 5What should you prepare before using an embedding contract incompatible with the old one?

A new, consistent set of guide vectors, linked to the texts and identifiers, and questions encoded with the compatible contract. Check the new index before switching over; quality must be evaluated separately.
