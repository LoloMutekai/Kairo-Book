Level 4 · C260·M4

Likelihood

Your goalBuild the likelihood of a Hawkes event record by accounting for the events and all the observed time, then compare two parameter settings without confusing preference with proof.

Before you start
c226-m5 : integration with jumps · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c259-m5 : simulation and limitations · c090-m5 : stationarity · c260-m1 : conditional intensity · c260-m2 : kernel · c260-m3 : branching ratio

✿01 See the idea

A workshop alert counter records the exact time of each alert. In our fictional record, it sounds at minutes 1, 2 and 5, then stays silent until minute 6. We want to evaluate a model in which an alert temporarily increases the rate of subsequent alerts.

This model must explain the points on the timeline as well as the gaps between them. A parameter setting that predicts alerts almost everywhere may fit the observed alerts well while explaining all the silent periods poorly. The likelihood brings both requirements into the same calculation.

The intensity is a conditional rate, in events per minute: it depends on the history available just before the time being considered. It is not directly a probability. The area under this rate helps determine the weight of periods without events.

We use the same counter, event times and window throughout the lesson. The values and the assumed mechanism are fictional; no real workshop measurements or causal relationships between alerts have been established. The exercises change only the end of observation or the parameter setting of the same model.

The rule to remember

Evaluate the rate just before each alert; multiply these rates; apply the silence factor calculated over the whole window.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Assign a weight to the complete counter record

Observation starts at minute 0 with an empty history and ends at T = 6 min. All alerts are observed: tₐ = 1 min, tᵦ = 2 min and t𝒸 = 5 min, giving n = 3 events. The background rate is μ = 0.50 per minute; each alert adds a = 0.25 per minute for d = 2 min. Let us evaluate this parameter setting without looking for an optimum yet.

  1. Specify the model and what we condition on

    The parameter vector θ contains μ, a and d. Time t and event times tⱼ are in minutes; j indexes the events. The kernel h(s) equals a for 0 < s ≤ d and 0 elsewhere; s is the time elapsed since an alert. The indicator symbol 𝟙 equals 1 if its condition is true and 0 otherwise.

    λθ(t)=μ+a∑tj<t𝟙t−tj≤d

    The sum includes only alerts strictly earlier than t. The notation λθ(tᵢ⁻) denotes the rate before alert i arrives: this alert does not contribute to its own intensity. Limits at expiration times follow the kernel convention; none of the observed event times coincides with an expiration here.

    We assume a simple process, meaning no simultaneous events, exact event times, no missing alerts, strictly positive μ, nonnegative a and strictly positive d. The empty history is a chosen initial condition, rather than an observation of a stationary regime. The branching ratio is ad = 1/2, less than 1: this parameter setting also satisfies the usual stability condition for a linear Hawkes process, without making this initialization stationary.

  2. Include silent periods in the density

    Over an interval without a new alert, the known history determines how λθ evolves. If S(t) is the conditional probability of remaining without an alert since the start of this interval, its derivative satisfies S′(t) = −λθ(t)S(t), with S equal to 1 at the start. The solution is the exponential of the negative area under the rate. The function exp is the natural exponential; ln is its inverse logarithm.

    The density of the next event time is the rate at that time multiplied by the probability of having waited until then. Multiplying these factors for successive alerts, followed by the factor for no alert from the last alert until T, gives the density of the whole record. Waiting times are not assumed to be independent: each factor is conditional on the history already observed.

    Λθ(T)=∫0Tλθ(t)dt
    L(θ)=[∏i=1nλθ(ti−)]exp(−Λθ(T))

    Λθ(T), the integrated intensity or compensator at T, has no units: events per minute multiplied by minutes. The product ∏ multiplies the n intensities. L is a density of the ordered event times, conditional on the initial history, in a window with a fixed end; we do not condition on the final number of alerts. L is neither the probability that these exact event times occur nor a probability of θ. In continuous time, each exact event time has probability 0.

  3. Read the intensities before the alerts

    Before A, the history is empty: λθ(ta⁻) = μ = 1/2 per minute. Before B, A is still active: λθ(tᵦ⁻) = μ + a = 3/4 per minute. Before C, A and B have expired at minutes 3 and 4: λθ(t𝒸⁻) = μ = 1/2 per minute.

    We multiply these values because they are the density factors associated with successive event times. The partial product is 1/2 × 3/4 = 3/8; then 3/8 × 1/2 = 3/16. Its unit is the inverse of a minute raised to the power 3. The peak of 1 per minute just after B must not replace the intensity of B before its arrival.

  4. Integrate the background and each observed contribution

    The background remains present throughout the window: μT = 1/2 × 6 = 3, with no units. Alert A contributes for 2 min, and B for 2 min. C would last until 7 min, but T = 6 min: its observed duration is T − t𝒸 = 1 min.

    Λθ(T)=μT+a∑j=1nmin(d,T−tj)

    The function min selects the shorter duration: the memory d or the time actually observed after the alert. The durations sum to 2 + 2 + 1 = 5 min. Excitation therefore adds 1/4 × 5 = 5/4, with no units. Finally, Λθ(T) = 3 + 5/4 = 17/4, or 4.25, with no units.

    This number is calculated from the realized history. It must not be identified with the number actually observed, 3, or automatically with the unconditional expected final count: before observation, the compensator itself depends on the random events.

  5. Assemble the result and its logarithmic form

    L(θ)=34×4exp(−17/4) min−3

    The coefficient is indeed 3/16 and the exponent is −17/4. This expression is the exact result; no approximation of the exponential is needed. The silence factor is strictly between 0 and 1: it reduces the weight that the product of intensities alone would give.

    To take a logarithm of a quantity with no units, fix a reference rate λᵣ = 1 per minute, shared by all parameter settings being compared. We define ℓ(θ) = ln(L(θ)/λᵣⁿ). Dividing the intensities by this reference does not change their ranking for the same record.

    ℓ(θ)=∑i=1nln(λθ(ti−)λr)−Λθ(T)

    Here, ℓ(θ) = ln(3/16) − 17/4. The logarithm turns products into sums and preserves order because it is increasing. It lets us add scores rather than multiply very small factors. Optimization would then seek the parameters giving the largest ℓ, subject to their constraints; we are only evaluating given parameter settings.

  6. Check the area using a different partition

    Now partition time rather than the contributions from alerts. Between minutes 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6, the intervals each last 1 min. Their rates are respectively 1/2, 3/4, 1, 3/4, 1/2 and 3/4 per minute.

    Their total area is therefore (1/2 + 3/4 + 1 + 3/4 + 1/2 + 3/4) × 1 = 17/4, with no units. This check gives exactly the same result as adding the background and contributions. It also verifies that the final silence, from 5 to 6 min, has been included.

    Model check: if a = 0, all intensities return to μ and the compensator becomes μT = 3. The formula then reduces to that of a homogeneous Poisson process: a constant rate for the events and a silence factor over the whole window.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, explain why the final silence changes the likelihood even though it adds no factor to the product of intensities. Then show where counting the alert itself would distort the result.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I take the rate just after the alert: it is the rate visible on the step.”

    The alert must be evaluated from its preceding history. For B, use 3/4 per minute, rather than the peak of 1 per minute created after its arrival. Reconstruct the list of strictly earlier alerts before calculating any sum.

    Review the intensities before the alerts

  • “The product of intensities is enough; empty periods add nothing.”

    The whole window is part of the data. Multiply by exp(−Λθ(T)), including the silence after the last alert. In the exercise extending the window, the event times stay the same, yet this factor changes.

    Test the extension without an alert

  • “I always count d minutes after each alert, even outside the window.”

    Unobserved time must not enter this likelihood. For C, only 1 min is observed, whereas d = 2 min. Replace each duration with min(d, T − tⱼ).

    Review the integration of contributions

Exercise 1 · Identify, then calculate

Evaluate B without making it explain its own arrival

Using the same record, reconstruct λθ(tᵦ⁻), then the rate just after B. Explain the difference and state which rate enters the likelihood.

Hint 1

At minute 2, A is still active. B is not yet part of the history before its arrival.

Hint 2

First write μ + a for the rate before, then add the new contribution for the rate after.

Compare with the solution

Before B, only A adds a contribution to the background: 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4 per minute. Just after B, both A and B contribute: 3/4 + 1/4 = 1 per minute.

The difference is a = 1/4 per minute. The likelihood uses 3/4 per minute because it evaluates the arrival from the preceding information. Using 1 would let B increase the weight of its own arrival.

Check your reasoning

  • Name A as the only earlier contribution.
  • Give the rates 3/4 and 1 with their unit.
  • Choose the rate before B and justify this choice using the available history.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and boundary check

Observe for longer without a new alert

The counter is now observed until T′ = 8 min, with no new alert after C. Keep all parameters. Calculate the new compensator and the ratio L′/L of the record densities up to their respective horizons. Which factor explains the difference?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

C finishes contributing at 7 min. In the extended window, each alert therefore contributes for the full duration d.

Hint 2

The product at the observed event times is unchanged. Calculate only the additional area to obtain the ratio.

Compare with the solution

The background gives 1/2 × 8 = 4, with no units. The 3 contributions each last 2 min, giving 6 min in total; their area is 1/4 × 6 = 3/2. The new compensator is 11/2, or 5.50, with no units.

The product remains 3/16 in inverse minutes raised to the power 3. Thus L′/L = exp(−(11/2 − 17/4)) = exp(−5/4), strictly less than 1. Here this ratio is the conditional probability of having no additional alert between the horizons, given the record already observed.

Direct check: the background adds μ × 2 min = 1, with no units; the last contribution adds a × 1 min = 1/4, with no units, before expiring. Their sum is 5/4. The final wait does provide information.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 11/2 by truncating the contributions at the new horizon.
  • Keep the intensities at the earlier events unchanged.
  • Recover the added area 5/4 using only the newly observed time.
  • Interpret the ratio as an additional conditional silence, without confusing L with a probability of exact event times.

Exercise 3 · Comparison and interpretation

Is the closeness of A and B enough to prefer excitation?

Return to T = 6 min. Compare the parameter setting studied with the same background μ = 1/2 per minute and a = 0. Call the likelihood of this setting without excitation L_fond. Calculate L/L_fond, then determine how it compares with 1. You may use ln(r) < r − 1 when r > 1. What can you conclude about this record, and what remains unproven?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Without excitation, the 3 intensities all equal μ and the compensator is μT.

Hint 2

Separate the ratio of products, 3/2, from the excess area, 5/4. Then compare their logarithmic contributions.

Compare with the solution

Without excitation, the product is 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8, and the compensator is 3. Thus L_fond = (1/8) exp(−3), in the same density unit as L.

The ratio of products is 3/16 ÷ 1/8 = 3/2. The excess area is 5/4, with no units. The complete ratio is therefore L/L_fond = (3/2) exp(−5/4), and its logarithm is ln(3/2) − 5/4.

Using the given inequality, ln(3/2) < 3/2 − 1 = 1/2. The logarithm of the ratio is therefore less than 1/2 − 5/4 = -3/4, which is itself less than 0. Thus L/L_fond < 1: for these parameter settings and this window, the model without excitation gives the greater likelihood.

Why does the inequality hold? For r > 1, ln(r) is the area under 1/u between 1 and r, where u is a variable with no units. This height is less than 1 inside the interval, so the area is less than r − 1.

The product alone rewarded the closeness of A–B, but time without an alert penalizes this setting with excitation more strongly. This comparison between fixed parameter settings does not prove that every Hawkes model is worse, that the alerts are independent, or that causality is absent. Estimating other parameters and evaluating their uncertainty would require a separate analysis.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate 1/8 and 3 before forming the ratio.
  • Include the excess area in the likelihood ratio.
  • Justify the sign without a numerical approximation of the exponential.
  • Limit the conclusion to the record, window and parameter settings compared.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which factors must be combined to evaluate a complete record up to T?

The product of the intensities just before the events and exp(−Λθ(T)), where Λθ(T) is the integral of the intensity over the whole window, including the final silence. The factors are conditional on the successive histories.

Recall 2Why do we use the intensity before an alert arrives?

The arrival is evaluated from the available history. An alert cannot contribute to its own arrival rate; its contribution acts only afterwards.

Recall 3What duration of the rectangular kernel enters the integral after an alert tⱼ?

min(d, T − tⱼ), for an alert within the window and an empty initial history. We count neither a contribution after T nor a contribution before the alert.

Recall 4What does likelihood mean here, and why is it not a probability of parameters?

It is the density of the observed event times, viewed as a function of the parameters, for a fixed window and initial condition. No distribution over the parameters is given. Exact event times have probability 0 in continuous time, even when their density is positive.

Recall 5Why is the closeness of A and B not enough to prefer the setting with excitation?

It increases the product of the intensities at the alerts, but excitation also increases the area and penalizes silent periods. Both terms must be compared using identical data, time units and windows; a preference proves neither causality nor general model adequacy.
