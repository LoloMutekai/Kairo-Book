Level 2 · C049·M2

Oscillations

Your goalReconstruct the free motion of a cart attached to a spring, calculate its period, and distinguish oscillation without dissipation from damped oscillation.

Before you start
c047-m5 : stability of an equilibrium · c048-m5 : positivity · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c046-m5 : approximation error · c049-m1 : higher order

✿01 See the idea

You pull a small cart to the right, then release it without pushing it. A horizontal spring connects it to a wall. The spring brings the cart back towards its equilibrium position, but the cart already has a velocity when it gets there: it crosses this position and compresses the spring on the other side. The restoring force and inertia therefore produce a back-and-forth motion.

Equilibrium is the position where the spring exerts no horizontal force. It does not make the cart stop: passing through this position with a nonzero velocity carries the cart beyond it. At the ends of the motion, the opposite happens: the velocity is momentarily 0, but the spring exerts its strongest force.

We study the same cart in a fictional model: mass 2 kg, spring constant 8 N/m, initial displacement 3 cm to the right. At first, there is no friction and no push after release. Only later will friction proportional to velocity dissipate the motion. These values are not measurements of a real device.

Here, an oscillation is a motion that alternates between the two sides of equilibrium. Without friction, the same state, meaning position and velocity, returns after a period. With friction, the back-and-forth motion can continue while decreasing: the motion is then no longer exactly periodic.

The rule to remember

The restoring force opposes displacement; inertia carries the cart past equilibrium. The spring constant and mass set the rate of oscillation, while the initial conditions determine the particular motion.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From release to the first crossing of equilibrium

The cart is released at x(0) = 3 cm, with v(0) = 0 m/s. For m = 2 kg and k = 8 N/m, let us find the equation of motion, the period, and the state a quarter of the way through the cycle. We will then add friction with c = 4 N·s/m to the same model.

  1. Write down what accelerates the cart

    t is the time since release, in seconds; x(t) is the signed displacement from equilibrium, in metres. v = x′ is the velocity in m/s and a = x″ is the acceleration in metres per second squared. A prime denotes a derivative with respect to time. The mass m is constant and positive, in kg; the spring constant k is constant and positive, in N/m. A newton, written N, is the unit of force: a kilogram metre per second squared.

    The track is horizontal; the weight and the support force balance vertically. The spring is assumed to have negligible mass and to be linear: its horizontal force F = −kx is proportional to displacement and points towards equilibrium. There are no impacts, no friction, and no additional applied force after release. The law m a = F then gives:

    mx″+kx=0

    Convert before multiplying: A = 3/100 m = 0.03 m is the positive initial displacement, and therefore the amplitude for this release from rest. The initial force is −8 × 0.03 = -0.24 N; the acceleration is -0.24/2 = -0.12 metres per second squared. It points to the left, even though the initial velocity is 0.

  2. Find the angular frequency with the correct unit

    Dividing the equation by m isolates the acceleration. We define the positive natural angular frequency ω as the square root of k/m. It measures the rate at which the angle of the cosine advances; its usual unit is the radian per second, written rad/s. The radian is dimensionless in unit calculations.

    x″+ω2x=0

    Here k/m = 8/2 = 4 per second squared. Its positive square root is ω = 2 rad/s, because 2 × 2 = 4. The ratio k/m is therefore not the angular frequency itself.

  3. Use the release conditions to select the solution

    The real solutions are combinations of cosine and sine: x(t) = C cos(ωt) + D sin(ωt). C and D are constants in metres to be determined. Angles are in radians; θ = ωt will denote this dimensionless angle. Differentiating gives: v(t) = −Cω sin(ωt) + Dω cos(ωt).

    Initially, cos(0) = 1 and sin(0) = 0. Therefore C = x(0) = A and Dω = v(0). Since ω is positive, D = 0/2 = 0 m. The initial velocity eliminates the sine term; the equation alone did not do this.

    x(t)=Acos(ωt),v(t)=−Aωsin(ωt)

    Numerically, x(t) = 0.03 cos(2 t) m and v(t) = −0.06 sin(2 t) m/s, when t is the numerical value of time in seconds. The symbolic expression ωt keeps the unit of the coefficient visible.

  4. Convert angular frequency into a period

    The period T, in seconds, is the shortest positive time after which position and velocity repeat. Cosine and sine return together to their original phase after an angle of 2π radians; π is the circle constant. The frequency f counts cycles per second and is expressed in hertz, written Hz.

    ωT=2π⇒T=2πω,f=1T

    With ω = 2 rad/s, T = (2/2)π s = π s and f = 1/π Hz. The value 1 s is T/π, not T. We keep π exact: no rounding of this constant is involved.

    A quarter of the cycle occurs at t = T/4 = 1/4 π s; half the period is T/2 = 1/2 π s. Changing only A does not change T in this linear model.

  5. Calculate the state as the cart crosses equilibrium

    A quarter of the way through the cycle, θ = ωT/4 = π/2. The cosine is 0 and the sine is 1. Position and velocity must be evaluated separately:

    x(T4)=0 m,v(T4)=−Aω=-0.06 m/s

    The cart crosses equilibrium towards the left. Its acceleration a = −(k/m)x is 0 metres per second squared at this instant; yet its velocity has its greatest absolute value, 0.06 m/s. 0 acceleration does not erase the velocity already acquired.

    After T/2, the angle is π: the cosine is −1 and the sine is 0. We find x = -0.03 m, v = 0 m/s and a = 0.12 metres per second squared. The cart stops momentarily on the left, then starts moving to the right. After T, x and v both return to their initial values.

  6. Check using the equation and energy

    Differentiating again gives the acceleration, which we then substitute back into the equation:

    x″=−Aω2cos(ωt),mx″+kx=A(k−mω2)cos(ωt)

    The product of the mass and the square of the angular frequency is 2 × 4 = 8 N/m = k: the residual is identically 0. The initial conditions have also been checked. This check applies at every instant, not just at the points in the diagram.

    Another approach: the mechanical energy E is the sum of the kinetic energy of the cart and the elastic energy stored in the spring. It is expressed in joules, written J, with J = N·m. In this model:

    E=mv22+kx22,E′=v(mx″+kx)=0

    Initially, only the elastic part remains: E = 8 × 3/100 × 3/100 / 2 = 9/2500 J = 0.0036 J. A quarter of the way through the cycle, only the kinetic part remains: 2 × (-3/50) × (-3/50) / 2 = 9/2500 J = 0.0036 J. The difference is 0 J. Recovering |v| = √(k/m) A from this energy balance also checks the maximum speed without reusing the derivative of the cosine.

  7. Add friction to the same cart

    We change one assumption: a force −cv now opposes the velocity. The coefficient c is constant and nonnegative, in N·s/m. The equation becomes m x″ + c x′ + kx = 0. The spring and release conditions stay the same. Taking the energy derivative again gives E′ = −cv × v: the energy decreases when c is positive and the cart is moving.

    Let γ = c/(2m), in inverse seconds, be the damping coefficient. The same ω = √(k/m) still denotes the natural angular frequency without friction. To find a solution of the form e^(rt), where r is also in inverse seconds, the equation requires the following characteristic polynomial. Completing the square lets us read off its roots:

    r2+2γr+ω2=0⇒(r+γ)2=γ2−ω2

    If 0 ≤ γ < ω, the roots are r = −γ ± iω_d, where i is the imaginary unit such that i × i = −1. We define the damped angular frequency ω_d, in rad/s, by:

    ωd=ω2−γ2

    The real displacement then combines an exponential factor with sinusoids; this factor decreases when γ is positive:

    x(t)=e−γt[Ccos(ωdt)+Dsin(ωdt)]

    e denotes the base of the exponential; C and D are new constants in metres to be fitted to the same release conditions. γt and ω_d t are dimensionless. If γ = ω, the root is repeated; if γ > ω, the roots are real and distinct. In these latter cases, the free return no longer involves repeated back-and-forth motion. We do not develop their solutions or forced motion here.

  8. Decide whether back-and-forth motion continues

    With c = 4 N·s/m, γ = 4/(2 × 2) = 1 per second and its square is 1 per second squared. The difference between the square of ω and the square of γ is 4 − 1 = 3 per second squared: this radicand is positive. The roots have a real part of -1 per second and an imaginary part of magnitude √3 per second. The cart therefore continues to oscillate with ω_d = √3 rad/s, under a decreasing factor e^(−γt).

    The time between identical phases of the sinusoids is 2π/√3 s; this is called the pseudo-period. It is not an exact period of the motion, since the exponential factor has decreased between these instants.

    To impose x(0) = A and v(0) = 0, we need C = A and −γA + ω_d D = 0. Therefore D = γA/ω_d. The displacement is e^(−γt)[A cos(ω_d t) + (γA/ω_d) sin(ω_d t)]. The sine coefficient becomes (0.03/√3) m, rather than 0 m. Friction requires us to revisit the initial conditions, even for release from rest.

    This result comes from the stated linear model. It establishes neither the value of real friction nor the validity of the spring model outside its linear range.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “At equilibrium, the cart must stop.”

    At equilibrium, force and acceleration are 0, but velocity need not be. At the first crossing, x = 0 m but v = -0.06 m/s. The cart would stop only if its velocity were also 0.

    Revisit the calculation of the state a quarter of the way through the cycle

  • “An angular frequency of 2 rad/s means 2 oscillations per second.”

    A cycle covers 2π radians. Divide the angular frequency by 2π to obtain the frequency in Hz: here f = 1/π Hz. Taking the reciprocal of ω alone gives T/(2π), not the period.

    Recover the period from a full angular turn

  • “I add e^(−γt) to the cosine and the release conditions automatically remain correct.”

    Differentiating a product adds the term −γA at the start. The sine must compensate for this velocity if the cart is released without a push. A formula that resembles the diagram must also satisfy the initial conditions.

Still without friction, use conservation of E to recover the velocity at the first crossing of x = 0. Explain why the energy calculation alone does not give its sign.

Hint 1

At release, v is 0. At equilibrium, x is 0: in each state, only one of the parts of E remains.

Hint 2

Equate the initial elastic energy to the kinetic energy at the crossing, then take the positive square root to obtain the absolute value.

Compare with the solution

The initial energy is k × A × A / 2 = 9/2500 J = 0.0036 J. At x = 0, it is m × v × v / 2. It follows that the square of v is (k/m) × A × A = 4 × 9/10000 = 9/2500, in square metres per second squared.

The positive square root is |v| = 3/50 m/s = 0.06 m/s, since the square of 3/50 is 9/2500. The first crossing is towards the left: v = -0.06 m/s. Energy involves a square; the sign comes from the history of the motion.

Substituting the velocity back in gives: m × v × v / 2 = 9/2500 J. This recovers the initial energy without having used the derivative of the cosine to calculate the velocity.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · diagnose and repair

The right curve, the wrong release conditions

With c = 4 N·s/m, someone proposes x(t) = A e^(−γt) cos(ω_d t), where γ = 1 per second and ω_d = √3 rad/s. Check whether the regime is oscillatory, then test the release from rest. Repair the formula by adding D sin(ω_d t) inside the brackets multiplied by the exponential factor.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare the squares of γ and ω. To check the release, differentiate the exponential too.

Hint 2

With C = A, the initial velocity of the general motion is −γA + ω_d D.

Compare with the solution

The radicand is 4 − 1 = 3 per second squared, which is positive: the sinusoidal part exists. The proposed solution satisfies x(0) = A, but its derivative gives v(0) = −γA = -0.03 m/s. It describes a cart already moving to the left.

We therefore need D = γA/ω_d = (0.03/√3) m and x(t) = e^(−γt)[A cos(ω_d t) + D sin(ω_d t)]. At the start, the sine term does not change the position. Its derivative contributes ω_d D = 0.03 m/s, which exactly offsets -0.03 m/s: the total is 0 m/s.

The equation is satisfied because the exponential and sinusoidal terms come from the roots −γ ± iω_d of the characteristic polynomial; the repair now selects the correct initial state. The motion oscillates with decreasing amplitude and is not exactly periodic.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify the regime by comparing the squares of ω and γ, with units.
  • Show the velocity term omitted at the start.
  • Check position and velocity separately after the repair.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why does 0 force at equilibrium not stop the cart?

0 force makes the acceleration 0 at that instant. The cart retains its velocity and crosses equilibrium; the spring then reverses the direction of its force.

Recall 2How can you reconstruct the period from k and m?

Calculate the positive angular frequency ω = √(k/m), then impose ωT = 2π for a cycle: T = 2π/ω. The frequency in Hz is f = 1/T.

Recall 3Why does release from rest select a cosine in the case without friction?

In C cos(ωt) + D sin(ωt), the initial position fixes C and the initial velocity is Dω. 0 velocity requires D = 0, since ω is positive.

Recall 4What does the energy balance check, and what can it not select?

Without friction, the sum of kinetic and elastic energies is conserved. At states where x is 0, this balance gives the absolute value of the velocity. Its sign requires knowing the direction of motion.

Recall 5When does friction allow back-and-forth motion, and why do we speak of a pseudo-period?

With γ = c/(2m), positive friction allows back-and-forth motion if γ < ω. The damped angular frequency is the square root of the difference between the square of ω and the square of γ. The exponential decreases between identical phases: the motion does not repeat exactly.
