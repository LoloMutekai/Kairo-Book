Martingale

Your goalVerify a martingale by calculating, for each known history, the conditional mean of the next capital value.

Before you start
c168-m5 : Radon-Nikodym · c129-m5 : backward induction · c130-m5 : financing conditions · c169-m1 : filtration

✿01 See the idea

At a fictional gaming table, a counter starts at 10 points. Each time a sign is drawn, + adds 2 points and − removes 2. The signs are independent; each has probability 1/2. We observe 2 draws, with no fees or changes to the rules. These numbers are exercise data, not actual gaming results.

The first sign is +: you now have 12 points. The next draw can take you to 14 or 10 points. These continuations remain equally likely. Their mean is 12 points: your current capital is exactly the mean of what can come next, given what you already know.

This is the idea of a martingale. The capital can change; its change has no predictable average direction given the available information. This test must be repeated after every possible history. Mixing all the histories together at the start would lose this local requirement.

Information matters as much as the values: knowing only that the first sign is + leaves uncertainty; also knowing the next sign removes it. A martingale is therefore defined relative to a probability law and a filtration, meaning a sequence of accumulating information.

The rule to remember

At each time, check that the variable is known from the current information, that the expectation of its absolute value is finite, and that its next conditional mean equals its current value.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Check the counter at every node

The counter M starts at 10 points and receives an increment of +2 or -2 points at each of the 2 independent draws with equally likely signs. Let us check the property using only information from the signs already drawn.

  1. Specify the law and what is visible

    Ω is the set of 4 histories ++, +−, −+, −−. The signs are written in the order of the draws. The law ℙ assigns each history probability 1/4 = 1/2 × 1/2. All subsets of Ω are allowed as events; their collection is denoted by 𝒜. A probability has no unit.

    n is the number of completed draws, so it equals 0, 1 or 2. ℱn is the information available at that time: initially, ℱ is {∅, Ω}, where ∅ is the impossible event; after the first draw, it distinguishes histories starting with + from those starting with −; after the last draw, it distinguishes every history and equals 𝒜. No future information is provided in advance.

    c = 10 points is the initial capital and a = 2 points is the magnitude of an increment. ξk denotes the increment at draw k: +a or −a, each with probability 1/2, independently of earlier signs. k is a draw index. Mn is the capital after n draws: the sequence (Mn) is our random process.

    M0=c;Mn+1=Mn+ξn+1

  2. Give the complete definition

    A martingale relative to (ℱn) under ℙ is an adapted, integrable real process that satisfies the following equality at every available step. Adapted means that Mn is measurable with respect to ℱn: its value can be determined using the information at that time. Integrable means that the expectation of its absolute value is finite.

    𝔼ℙ[|Mn|]<∞;𝔼ℙ[Mn+1|ℱn]=Mn

    𝔼 under ℙ is the expectation calculated using the law ℙ; |Mn| is the absolute value of the capital and ∞ means infinity. The bar before ℱn means “conditioning on the known information”. This conditional expectation is itself a variable: it gives a different mean depending on the observed history. Its unit is still the point.

    The equality is required almost surely, meaning except on an event of probability 0. Here, every history has strictly positive probability: none can be discarded. In our tree, n equals 0 or 1 for this equality; at the last draw, there is no next step to test.

  3. Check visibility and integrability

    The counter can be calculated using the signs already observed: it is adapted. It equals 12 = 10 + 2 points after +, or 8 = 10 − 2 points after −. Its final values are 14, 10, 10, 6 points, in the order of the histories in the diagram.

    All values lie between 6 and 14 points. Thus |Mn| is at most 14 points at each time, so its expectation is finite. This check is sufficient here because the horizon and the values are bounded; the general definition does not require every martingale to be bounded.

  4. Condition on the first sign being +

    The event “first sign +” consists of ++ and +−. Its probability is 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2. Once this event is known, the probability of each of its leaves becomes 1/4 ÷ 1/2 = 1/2. We renormalize the weights: their sum is 1.

    The possible capital values are 14 = 12 + 2 and 10 = 12 − 2 points. We weight them by their conditional probabilities:

    12×14+12×10=14+102=12 points

    The result equals the current 12 points. Using 1/4 for the weights after conditioning would overlook the fact that the other histories are now excluded.

  5. Check the other node and the starting point

    After −, the leaves −+ and −− give 10 and 6 points, each with conditional probability 1/2. Their sum is 16 points; dividing by 2 gives 8 points, exactly the capital 8 at this node. The 2 histories of the first draw have now been checked.

    Initially, no sign is known. The mean capital after the first draw is (12 + 8) ÷ 2 = 20 ÷ 2 = 10 points, which is the initial capital 10. All steps of the tree now satisfy the definition.

  6. Check using the increments and the leaves

    Another approach: the next increment remains independent of the current information and equals +2 or -2 points with weights 1/2. Its conditional mean is 1 + (-1) = 0 point. Since Mn is already known, it can be taken outside the conditional mean:

    𝔼ℙ[Mn+1−Mn|ℱn]=0 point

    This equality is equivalent to the equality in the definition for an adapted, integrable process. Independence of the increments is a sufficient assumption in our example; the definition only requires their conditional mean to be 0, without requiring this independence.

    Finally, the mean of all the leaves is (14 + 10 + 10 + 6) ÷ 4 = 40 ÷ 4 = 10 points. It confirms the calculation from the starting point. On its own, this overall mean would not check the intermediate nodes.

  7. Read exactly what has been proved

    We have proved a property of (Mn) under ℙ with the filtration of past signs. Yet the path ++ passes through 10, 12, then 14 points: being a martingale does not mean remaining constant or returning to the starting value in every realization.

    The property does not promise an individual profit. Nor is it sufficient to justify a rule for choosing when to stop: that question and its assumptions belong to the following units. We have restricted ourselves to fixed times in this finite tree.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Martingale means that my capital does not move.”

    The path ++ changes at every draw. What equals the current capital is the mean of the continuations compatible with the current information. Recalculate the mean after + without replacing the possible continuations with the single continuation that actually occurred.

    Reread the tree and group the continuations

  • “The overall mean stays the same, so it is a martingale.”

    Opposite deviations can cancel across histories that are already distinguishable. The mean must be checked at every node. The counter reset to its initial capital in the diagnostic exercise keeps its overall mean and fails this test.

    Test the return to the initial capital

  • “The same values form a martingale regardless of the information.”

    If the next sign is revealed, the continuations can no longer be averaged as before. Always state the filtration and the law. Also reread the conditions of adaptedness and integrability: the equality alone is not the complete definition.

    Review the definition and the available information

Exercise 2 · Diagnostic

A misleading constant mean

The same table, signs and filtration. Another counter Y starts at 10 points, follows M after the first sign, then is reset to 10 points after the last draw, whatever the sign. Check its overall means at each time, then decide whether Y is a martingale.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At the last draw, Y is constant; its conditional mean equals that constant at every node.

Hint 2

Compare this mean with the 12 points after +, then with the 8 points after −.

Compare with the solution

The overall means are 10 points initially, (12 + 8) ÷ 2 = 10 points after the first sign, then 10 points after the reset. They are indeed constant. Y is adapted: the counter can be read at the current time; it is integrable because its values are bounded.

After +, the conditional mean of the final capital Y is 10 points and should equal 12 points: it does not. The conditional mean increment is 10 − 12 = -2 points. After −, it is 10 − 8 = 2 points. Thus Y is not a martingale.

The conditional deviations cancel in the overall mean: (-2 + 2) ÷ 2 = 0 point. This cancellation explains why the overall check missed the failure.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the overall means and check adaptedness and integrability separately.
  • Give a node of positive probability where the equality fails.
  • Explain how the deviations cancel, without confusing the overall mean with the conditional mean.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

Reveal the next sign

Return to the original counter M. The new filtration 𝒢 keeps the initial information at the start, but reveals the entire history as soon as the first draw is complete: initially, 𝒢 equals the initial ℱ; at subsequent times, 𝒢 equals ℱ at the final time. Under the same law ℙ, is M still a martingale for 𝒢? Analyse the history ++ in particular.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A value already determined by the known information is its own conditional expectation.

Hint 2

On ++, the final capital is already known after the first draw. Compare it with the current capital.

Compare with the solution

M remains adapted to 𝒢, which contains more information, and its integrability is unchanged. But at the end of the first draw, the final M is already known: its conditional mean given 𝒢 is therefore the final M itself.

On ++, which has probability 1/4, this mean is 14 points and the current capital is 12 points. Their difference is 2 points. The equality fails on an event of positive probability: M is not a martingale for 𝒢.

The law of the signs and the counter values have not changed. The property has disappeared because the information used to predict has changed. A sequence of values therefore cannot be called a “martingale” without specifying its filtration.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify why the new information already determines the final capital.
  • Retain the checks of adaptedness and integrability.
  • Compare 14 and 12 points on a history of probability 1/4; name the filtration that makes the test fail.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which conditions must be checked to call a process a martingale?

Specify a law ℙ and a filtration (ℱₙ). The process is adapted and integrable; at each step, 𝔼 under ℙ of the next capital conditional on ℱₙ equals the current capital, almost surely. Adapted: the value can be determined using the current information; integrable: the expectation of the absolute value is finite.

Recall 2Why do the weights of the leaves ++ and +− change after the first + is announced?

The other histories are excluded. Each initial weight 1/4 is divided by the probability 1/2 of the announcement, giving 1/2. Their weighted average is 12 points, equal to the capital after +.

Recall 3Which equivalent formulation uses the next increment? Is independence required?

For an adapted, integrable process, the conditional mean of the next increment must be 0. Independence in our example is sufficient together with a 0 mean; it is not part of the general definition. The 0 mean must be conditional on the current information.

Recall 4Is a constant overall expectation sufficient? Reconstruct the counterexample using counter Y.

No. Y follows the first sign and then always returns to 10 points. Its overall mean remains 10, but after + its future mean is 10 instead of 12 points. The conditional equality fails.

Recall 5What happens to the test if the entire history is revealed after the first sign?

The final capital is already known, so its conditional mean is its final value. On ++, 14 points differ from the current 12 points. The original martingale loses its property for this enlarged filtration.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the verification in this order: law and information; adaptedness; integrability; conditional weights of each continuation; mean compared with the current capital; check using the increment. Then explain why a path can vary and why a constant overall mean is insufficient.
