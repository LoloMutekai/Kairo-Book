Level 4 · C227·M4

General integral

Your goalDefine an integral of a predictable process with respect to a semimartingale, calculate its martingale and finite variation parts, and choose the correct coefficient at jumps.

Before you start
c226-m5 : integration with jumps · c165-m5 : duality · c171-m5 : multidimensional case · c227-m1 : finite variation · c227-m2 : Doob-Meyer · c227-m3 : general quadratic variation

✿01 See the idea

On a fictional test rig, a pressure rises slowly and sometimes receives a shock. A controller multiplies each pressure change by the current setting. Its accumulator adds these weighted changes. It does not add the displayed pressures, and it does not just count elapsed time.

Suppose a shock arrives while the setting is 2. The controller can reduce its setting after observing this shock. That decision affects subsequent changes; it does not rewrite the weighting of the shock already received. This is the idea of predictability: deciding without using the new information carried by the increment being weighted.

The semimartingale provides the signal to integrate: it combines a martingale part, which here represents the shocks with mean 0, and a finite variation part, which represents the drift. The general integral adds their contributions under the same decision convention. A Brownian integral alone does not describe all possible jumps.

All the values and rules of the test rig are fictional. Pressure is expressed in kilopascals, written kPa; time is in seconds, written s; the setting is dimensionless. The accumulator is therefore in kPa. This is a mathematical model, with no validation of a physical device.

The rule to remember

Choose an admissible predictable integrand; weight increments with the setting already available; add the martingale and finite variation contributions separately to check the total.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Accumulate drift and shocks without anticipating them

The pressure starts at 0 kPa. It drifts at rate a = 1 kPa/s and receives shocks of size +2 or -2 kPa at times 1 s and 2 s. The initial setting is 2; after the first shock, it becomes 1 if the shock was positive, 3 if it was negative. Let us calculate the accumulator on the path where the first shock is positive and the next is negative.

  1. Set out the framework and units

    We work on a filtered probability space (Ω, ℱ, (ℱt), ℙ). Ω collects the possible outcomes; ℙ assigns their probabilities; ℱ is the sigma-algebra of events; ℱt describes the information available at time t. The filtration is increasing, complete and right-continuous, the usual conditions for this framework. These objects have no physical unit.

    X is a real-valued adapted semimartingale, right-continuous with left limits, which is what cadlag means. On each finite horizon, we write X = X initially + M + A, where M is a cadlag local martingale and A an adapted cadlag process of finite variation; M and A start at 0. A local martingale becomes a martingale after suitable stopping at times tending to infinity. Finite variation means that the sum of the absolute values of changes is bounded on each compact interval. M and A are in kPa.

    H denotes the setting, which is dimensionless. Y = H · X denotes the integral process; the dot is integration notation, rather than the instantaneous product HtXt. We integrate over the interval (0, t] and set Y initially equal to 0 kPa: no initial mass is added. The symbol Δ denotes a jump: ΔXt = Xt − Xt-, where Xt- is the limit just before t.

  2. First define the setting as a step process

    An elementary setting H is constant on each step interval: it equals hᵢ on the interval that starts just after Tᵢ and ends at the next stopping time, including the right endpoint. In the formula, the symbol Σ adds a finite number of terms and i indexes them. The Tᵢ are increasing stopping times: at the current time, we can tell whether each has already occurred. The bounded coefficient hᵢ is measurable with respect to ℱ at time Tᵢ: it is chosen using the information at that time. Times are in s; hᵢ is dimensionless.

    Yt=∑ihi(Xt∧Ti+1−Xt∧Ti)

    The sign ∧ takes the minimum of the times: it stops the increment at t if the step interval has not ended. The multiplication therefore applies to a pressure change in kPa. The interval, open on the left and closed on the right, assigns the final jump to the coefficient chosen at the start of the interval. Changing the coefficient after that jump affects the next interval.

  3. Extend the integral under explicit assumptions

    The general framework allows H to be predictable and locally bounded. Predictable means measurable for the sigma-algebra generated by left-continuous adapted processes; “known before the change” gives the intuition, without replacing this definition. Locally bounded means that H can be stopped at times tending to infinity so that each stopped process has a finite deterministic bound. This is a sufficient condition; it does not describe all integrable integrands.

    (H·X)t=∫0tHsdMs+∫0tHsdAs

    The part with respect to A is a Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral on each path: it is enough that ∫|H| d|A| is finite on the horizon studied. |A| denotes the accumulated total variation of A, in kPa. The part with respect to M is a stochastic integral that extends the elementary sums; it does not require the total variation of M to be finite. The resulting process Y is cadlag and satisfies ΔYt = Ht ΔXt. The definition does not depend on the admissible decomposition chosen for X, even when that decomposition is not unique.

    To understand the extension, take an adapted H that is left-continuous with right limits. On increasingly fine deterministic partitions, keep its value at the left endpoint on each step interval. The integrals of these step processes converge to H · X uniformly on every compact time horizon in probability: for every finite T and positive ε, the probability that the supremum over t ≤ T of |Y⁽ⁿ⁾t − Yt| exceeds ε tends to 0. n indexes the approximation; T is in s; ε and the Y processes are in kPa. This mode of convergence, often abbreviated ucp, does not promise uniform convergence for every path. The theorem then extends the construction to predictable locally bounded H; here we state its scope without proving the entire extension.

    A useful check in the more restricted case of a square-integrable martingale M starting at 0 is the isometry below. E denotes expectation under ℙ and [M] the quadratic variation of M, in kPa squared. Here we assume E∫H squared d[M] is finite. Both sides are in kPa squared. For a general unbounded H, this condition is a sufficient criterion in this setting; predictability alone does not guarantee the existence of every integral.

    E[(H·M)T2]=E[∫0THs2d[M]s]

  4. Specify a probability law before selecting a path

    For the test rig, At = a t, with a = 1 kPa/s. At times 1 s and 2 s, M receives jumps J and K. Each equals +2 or -2 kPa, with independent, equally likely signs. The filtration reveals J at the first time, then K at the next, without announcing them beforehand. Between these times, M remains constant; it is stopped after the last one. The jumps have conditional expectation 0: here M is therefore a square-integrable martingale, and X = A + M is a semimartingale.

    The setting is 2 on (0, 1] s. On (1, 2] s, it is 1 if J is positive, 3 if J is negative. This latter choice uses J once it is revealed; it does not use K. After the horizon, we can set H to 0. The process defined this way is predictable and bounded. On the displayed time intervals, the numbers are expressed in s.

    Now select J = +2 kPa, K = -2 kPa. Then A at the first time is 1 kPa and at the end 2 kPa; M is respectively 2 kPa and 0 kPa. The observed levels are therefore X(1 s) = 1 + 2 = 3 kPa, then X(2 s) = 2 + 0 = 2 kPa. The law and rule are defined before this selection: knowing the path in order to describe it does not allow the controller to anticipate it.

  5. Weight the entire first increment

    On the first step interval, H remains equal to 2 up to and including the shock. The full increment is X(1 s) − X(0 s) = 3 − 0 = 3 kPa. We multiply by the setting that applies throughout this interval.

    Y1 s=2×(3−0)=6 kPa

    This result includes 2 kPa from drift and 4 kPa from the jump. Just before the shock, Y was only 2 kPa. Its jump is ΔY = 2 × 2 = 4 kPa; this differs from the full increment 6 kPa.

  6. Apply the new setting to the next step interval

    The positive shock led to the setting 1 for what follows. The next change is X(2 s) − X(1 s) = 2 − 3 = -1 kPa. Its sign is negative: the negative shock exceeds the positive drift on this interval.

    The contribution is 1 × (-1) = -1 kPa. The total becomes Y(2 s) = 6 + (-1) = 5 kPa. At each operation, H is dimensionless and the increment is in kPa; the sum keeps this unit. A negative total would also have been admissible: Y is a signed sum of changes, rather than a physical pressure that must be bounded.

  7. Check using the decomposition and the jumps

    Let us recalculate without subtracting the levels of X. The martingale part gives 2 × 2 + 1 × (-2) = 4 + (-2) = 2 kPa. It is nonzero on this path; it is the expectation of the martingale integral that is 0 in our bounded model.

    For A, the first duration is 1 s and the next 2 − 1 = 1 s. The drift changes are 1 and 1 kPa. Their weighted integral is 2 × 1 + 1 × 1 = 2 + 1 = 3 kPa. The sum 2 + 3 = 5 kPa gives exactly 5 kPa again.

    At the first jump, H is still 2, so ΔY = 4 kPa. The setting immediately afterwards is 1: applying it retroactively would multiply the same shock by the wrong coefficient. At the last jump, the formula ΔY = H ΔX gives -2 kPa. These checks separate continuous changes, jumps and decisions.

  8. Move to feedback without prescribed steps

    On the same test rig, we can replace the previous rule by Gs = Xs-/b, where b = 1 kPa is a reference pressure. G is dimensionless. The process X-, defined by the left limits and by the initial value at the start, is adapted and left-continuous with right limits, hence predictable. On our horizon, X is bounded for all outcomes: G is admissible. It now varies continuously between shocks; the transfer exercise calculates its integral.

    For our M with finitely many jumps and At = a t, the decomposition becomes very concrete: the integral with respect to M is the sum of G at each shock time × shock size; the integral with respect to A is the ordinary integral of Gs a with respect to time. This simplification comes from the finite model of the test rig. A general martingale can have infinitely many small variations; its stochastic part does not reduce to an ordinary integral with respect to time. The continuation on Ito-Tanaka will cover other transformations; it is not needed for these calculations.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, explain what H multiplies, why its setting at the shock differs from the one decided after the shock, and how the decomposition checks the result. Then distinguish the stated existence theorem, the calculation on our path and the behaviour of a real controller.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I can weight the shock with the setting calculated when I see it.”

    Under our rule, H at the first shock is still 2; the value 1 starts only immediately afterwards. The coefficient for the shock belongs to the interval that is ending. Using a value that depends on the current jump changes the mechanism and can lose predictability.

    Review the step process definition and the jump check

  • “A martingale has mean 0, so its contribution is always 0.”

    On the calculated path, the martingale contribution is 2 kPa. Having mean 0 concerns expectation, under the integrability assumptions. Moreover, X contains A: the general integral is not necessarily a martingale.

    Review the separate contributions in the second diagram

  • “Every adapted function can be integrated as soon as I write down sums.”

    Adapted means using current information; it does not guarantee predictability at jumps. Even if predictable, an unbounded process needs an integrability check. Here, predictable and locally bounded is the stated sufficient condition. Arbitrary pointwise convergence does not replace the mode of stochastic extension.

Exercise 1 · Direct application

A jump and a step interval are different changes

On the path in the example, give Y just before 1 s, its jump at that time, then Y just afterwards. Explain why replacing H by 1 for this jump would be incorrect. Give each unit.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Before the first shock, only the drift has acted for 1 s.

Hint 2

The jump of the integral equals the setting at that time multiplied by the jump of X.

Compare with the solution

Before the shock, X is 1 kPa. The constant setting 2 gives Y just before = 2 × 1 = 2 kPa.

The jump is 2 × 2 = 4 kPa. After this jump, Y is 2 + 4 = 6 kPa. The drift is continuous, so it adds no mass at the jump.

The setting 1 depends on the observed shock and starts after it. It does not replace H at the right endpoint of the first step interval. The sum over this interval includes the drift and the jump; its value 6 kPa must not be used as the size of the jump alone.

Check your reasoning

  • Separate the value before, the jump and the value after, all in kPa.
  • Justify the coefficient using the interval convention and the timing of information.
  • Do not confuse the increment over the step interval with its final jump.

Exercise 2 · Application with a conditional decision

Same final pressure, different accumulation

Keep the law, the drift and the controller rule, but consider J = -2 kPa then K = +2 kPa. Calculate X at the first time, X at the end and the integral Y at the end. Is the final total of X enough to recover Y?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The first negative shock leads to the setting 3 on the next step interval.

Hint 2

Write the changes in X over the step intervals before multiplying them.

Compare with the solution

At the first time, X = 1 + (-2) = -1 kPa. The shocks cancel by the end, so final X = 2 = 2 kPa, as in the example.

The first step interval contributes 2 × (-1 − 0) = -2 kPa. On the next one, the change is 2 − (-1) = 3 kPa; it is weighted by 3, giving 9 kPa. Final Y = -2 + 9 = 7 kPa.

We obtain 7 kPa instead of 5 kPa with the same final pressure 2 kPa. The rule uses the first shock: the path and the settings matter. Checking by decomposition remains possible by separating the drift and the jump within each change.

Check your reasoning

  • Choose the setting from the first shock, without using the second.
  • Keep the sign of each increment.
  • Explain why the endpoints of X are no longer sufficient when the setting varies.

Exercise 3 · Transfer to a variable integrand

Set the controller using the pressure just before the change

Return to the path J = +2 kPa, K = -2 kPa. Replace H by Gₛ = Xₛ₋/b with b = 1 kPa. Justify admissibility, then calculate ∫G dX by separating the continuous portions and the jumps. Do not assume that G remains constant between shocks.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Between shocks, X increases linearly. The integral of an affine function over a duration is the average of its endpoint values, multiplied by that duration.

Hint 2

The levels just before the shocks are 1 kPa and 4 kPa. Divide each by b to obtain the coefficient for the jump.

Hint 3

For an independent check on this piecewise affine path, expand the square of X at each jump and use the antiderivative of the product X dX on the continuous portions.

Compare with the solution

X- is adapted and left-continuous with right limits, hence predictable. Here, the jumps are bounded and finite in number on the horizon: G is bounded, which suffices. G is dimensionless and the integral is in kPa.

On the first continuous portion, G goes from 0 to 1, with a = 1 kPa/s over 1 s. Its average value gives 1/2 kPa = 0.50 kPa for the integral. At the first jump, G = 1; its contribution is 1 × 2 = 2 kPa.

On the next continuous portion, X goes from 3 to 4 kPa. The sum of the endpoint values of G is 7, so their average is 7/2. Since a = 1 kPa/s and the duration is 1 s, the contribution is 7/2 kPa = 3.50 kPa. At the last shock, G = 4; we add 4 × (-2) = -8 kPa.

Continuous part: 1/2 + 7/2 = 4 kPa. Jump part: 2 + (-8) = -6 kPa. The total is -2 kPa. A positive final pressure does not require a positive accumulator.

Check by another method: on a continuous portion, the integral X dX is half the change in X squared. At a jump of size δ from level x, the identity (x + δ) squared − x squared = 2 x δ + δ squared shows that δ squared must be subtracted to recover 2 X- ΔX. Here x and δ are pressures in kPa. Adding along our path, the squares of the intermediate levels cancel.

∫02 sGdX=X2 s2−X02−∑s(ΔXs)22b

The sum includes only the shock times on this horizon. The squared jumps are each 4 kPa squared, so their sum is 8 kPa squared. The final square is 4 kPa squared; the initial square is 0. The numerator is -4 kPa squared. After division by 2 b, we recover -2 kPa. This check uses the affine behaviour between shocks on our test rig; for a process with a continuous martingale part, the squared jumps alone would not describe all the quadratic variation.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify predictability and boundedness before calculating.
  • Integrate the variation of G over the continuous portions and use its left limits at shocks.
  • Recover the result using the identity for squares with the correct units.
  • Do not apply the check based only on jumps to a general continuous martingale.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1On a step interval open on the left and closed on the right, which coefficient multiplies the final jump?

The coefficient chosen at the start of the interval with the information available then. A decision made after the jump applies to the next step interval. The integral adds this coefficient multiplied by the increment of X.

Recall 2How does the integral with respect to X = X initially + M + A combine its parts?

It equals the stochastic integral of H with respect to M plus the Lebesgue–Stieltjes integral of H with respect to A, under the same predictable convention. On this page, the initial term of X creates no mass in the integral.

Recall 3Which sufficient class of integrands guarantees existence here, and what does adaptedness alone fail to guarantee?

A predictable locally bounded H can be integrated with respect to every semimartingale in the stated framework. Adaptedness does not guarantee predictability at a jump; predictability alone does not remove the need for integrability conditions for a general unbounded H. The sufficient class given does not exhaust all integrable cases.

Recall 4What does convergence of the integral processes uniformly on compact intervals in probability mean?

On every finite horizon, the probability that the largest difference over time exceeds a positive tolerance tends to 0. This does not guarantee uniform convergence on each path. Arbitrary pointwise convergence of the integrands is not a universal criterion for passing to the integral.

Recall 5What is the size of the jump of Y = H · X, and why is X₋ useful in feedback?

ΔYₜ = Hₜ ΔXₜ. For adapted cadlag X, X₋ is predictable: its value at the shock uses the level just before. The setting G = X₋/b in the exercise therefore weights the shock without using the level it reaches.
