Level 1 · C048·M5

Positivity

Your goalDecide whether a quadratic penalty is always nonnegative, strictly positive for every nonzero offset, or negative in some direction.

Before you start
c040-m5 : least squares · c048-m1 : eigenvalues · c048-m2 : diagonalization · c048-m3 : symmetric matrices · c048-m4 : quadratic forms

✿01 See the idea

A plotter head is offset from its target position. We record its horizontal and vertical offsets, then assign a penalty to them. A consistent penalty should not reward a displacement by becoming negative. But can it remain 0 while the head is still offset? This is the distinction to understand.

Our settings and penalty rule are fictional. With offsets x = 2 mm and y = -1 mm, the initial rule will give 6 mm². This number is neither a measured energy nor the ordinary distance to the target. We are examining only the sign of a proposed mathematical rule.

The rule contains squares of the offsets and a term that couples them. The coefficient c controls this coupling. Along the cross section where the offsets are opposites, changing c can turn a bowl into a flat floor, then into a slope toward negative values. The other directions must also be checked: a cross section or a few points do not prove a sign everywhere.

The rule to remember

Look for a proof covering all settings. Squares weighted by nonnegative coefficients give a nonnegative form; for strict positivity, also check that only the 0 setting can make the penalty 0.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Certify the plotter rule, then find its limit

The diagonal remains a = 2, which is dimensionless. The initial coupling is c = 1, which is dimensionless. The setting u has coordinates x = 2 mm and y = -1 mm. Let us calculate its penalty, then decide what the rule guarantees for every real setting.

  1. Name the form and its assumptions

    u is the column of offsets x and y, both in millimeters. The symbol ᵀ transposes a column into a row. A_c is a real symmetric matrix of size 2 × 2, whose coefficients a and c are dimensionless; the subscript c indicates the chosen coupling, not a time. We fix a = 2 and let c vary only when stated.

    u=[xy];Ac=[acca]
    qc(u)=uᵀAcu=ax2+2cxy+ay2

    q_c is the penalty, in mm². The factor 2 in the cross term comes from the symmetric entries: each contributes c × x × y. We test all real offsets, with no additional constraint on the plotter. This assumption is necessary: testing a restricted domain would be a different question.

  2. State exactly which sign is required

    Here a real symmetric matrix is called nonnegative, or positive semidefinite, when its form is at least 0 for every vector u. It is positive definite when its form is strictly greater than 0 for every nonzero u. “Nonzero” means that x and y are not both 0.

    Semi-définie positive :∀u,qc(u)≥0
    Définie positive :∀u≠0,qc(u)>0

    To avoid any ambiguity in terminology: the nonstrict condition allows a 0 penalty for a nonzero displacement; the strict condition rules this out. At the 0 setting, every quadratic form equals 0. An indefinite matrix produces positive values in some directions and negative values in others.

  3. Calculate the penalty for the given setting

    Start with the products: x × y = 2 × (-1) = -2 mm²; x squared = 4 mm² and y squared = 1 mm². The squares are positive, but the product x × y is negative: its sign must be kept.

    The diagonal contributions are 2 × 4 = 8 mm² and 2 × 1 = 2 mm². Their sum is 10 mm². The cross coefficient is 2 × 1 = 2; its contribution is 2 × (-2) = -4 mm².

    q1(u)=8+(-4)+2=6 mm²

    This result checks the given setting. It does not yet prove anything for all the other settings. To certify the rule, x and y must remain arbitrary.

  4. Move from the tested point to a global proof

    With c = 1, group the terms to reveal squares. Expanding the square of x + y gives exactly the cross term:

    q1(u)=x2+y2+(x+y)2

    Each square is nonnegative. If u is nonzero, at least one of the squares of x and y is strictly positive; their sum, and then the penalty, is therefore strictly positive. This proves that A with coupling 1 is positive definite. We have explained the sign for all vectors without listing them.

    Check numerically using a different expression from the expanded calculation: x + y = 1 mm, its square is 1 mm², and the sum of the separate squares is 5 mm². Thus 5 + 1 = 6 mm², as expected.

  5. Recover the verdict using eigenvalues

    The directions v+ = (1 ; 1)ᵀ and v- = (1 ; -1)ᵀ are dimensionless direction vectors. They are orthogonal and form a basis. Multiplying A_c by these columns gives (a + c)v+ and (a − c)v- respectively. The eigenvalues λ+ = a + c and λ- = a − c are dimensionless; the signs + and − name them without guaranteeing their numerical signs.

    For a real symmetric matrix, diagonalization in an orthonormal basis expresses the form as a sum of squares weighted by its eigenvalues. Having all eigenvalues nonnegative is therefore equivalent to positive semidefiniteness; having all eigenvalues strictly positive is equivalent to positive definiteness. A negative eigenvalue provides a direction that disproves positive semidefiniteness.

    Here we can check this criterion using the following identity, by expanding both squares. The directions v+ and v- each have a squared norm equal to 2; this explains the denominator, which must not be forgotten.

    qc(u)=(a+c)(x+y)2+(a−c)(x−y)22

    Initially, the eigenvalues are 3 and 1, both strictly positive. For our setting, x − y = 3 mm, so its square is 9 mm². The new approach gives (3 × 1 + 1 × 9)/2 = (3 + 9)/2 = 12/2 = 6 mm².

  6. Recognize a flat floor, then a negative direction

    Keep the plotter and the same diagonal, but take c = 2. The eigenvalues become 4 and 0. The form reduces to 2 times the square of x + y: it remains nonnegative everywhere, but every setting (t ; −t) makes it 0. With t = 1 mm, the setting (1 ; -1) mm is nonzero and gives 0 mm². The rule is positive semidefinite without being positive definite.

    With c = 3, the eigenvalues are 5 and -1. At the setting (1 ; -1) mm, the penalty is -2 mm²; at the setting (1 ; 1) mm, it is 10 mm². It therefore takes both signs: the matrix is indefinite. Yet its diagonal coefficients remain equal to 2. Checking the diagonals alone does not check coupled displacements.

  7. Check scaling and limit the conclusion

    A quadratic form satisfies q_c(su) = s squared × q_c(u), for a dimensionless real factor s: each term contains two coordinates. Reducing all coordinates by the factor 1/2 therefore multiplies the penalty by 1/4, not by 1/2. The first exercise asks you to check this property using another calculation.

    The proven verdict concerns this symmetric rule with exact coefficients and all real offsets. It validates neither a real plotter setting nor an optimization method. With approximate coefficients, deciding whether an eigenvalue close to 0 is negative requires a discussion of numerical errors; this page assigns no arbitrary threshold to this problem.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I found a positive penalty, so the matrix is positive semidefinite.”

    A test concerns a single vector. The definition requires all vectors. To certify the rule, find a sum of squares with nonnegative coefficients or check all eigenvalues under the symmetry assumption. A single negative result, however, is enough to disprove nonnegativity.

    Review the move from the tested point to the global proof

  • “The diagonals are positive, so the rest cannot change anything.”

    The diagonals test displacements along each axis separately. The coupling acts when the coordinates vary together. With c = 3, the setting (1 ; -1) mm gives -2 mm² despite the diagonals 2. Recalculate the cross term with its sign.

    Find the negative direction for the plotter again

  • “A 0 eigenvalue means that the penalty becomes negative.”

    A 0 eigenvalue creates a direction with 0 penalty. If the other eigenvalues are nonnegative, the form remains positive semidefinite. Strict positivity fails because a nonzero displacement can remain undetected by the penalty.

    Justify the boundary case in the second exercise

Exercise 1 · Numerical application

Reduce the plotter displacement

Return to c = 1. Divide the initial coordinates by 2: you obtain x = 1 mm and y = -1/2 mm. Calculate the new penalty directly, then check it using the scale factor. Keep exact fractions before expressing them as decimals.

Hint 1

Substitute x and y into a × x × x + 2 × c × x × y + a × y × y. The product x × y is negative.

Hint 2

The factor applied to the coordinates is 1/2. Each term of the penalty contains two coordinates.

Compare with the solution

The direct calculation is 2 × 1 × 1 + 2 × 1 × (-1/2) + 2 × (-1/2) × (-1/2). The contributions are 2, -1 and 1/2 mm²; their sum is 3/2 mm², or 1.50 mm².

Independent check: the factor 1/2 has square 1/4. The initial penalty 6 mm² therefore becomes 6 × 1/4 = 3/2 mm². Dividing the displacement by 2 does not divide the penalty by the same number.

  • Recover the result using the square of the scale factor.

Exercise 3 · Apply the reasoning to a parameter

Which couplings can be allowed?

The diagonal remains a = 2, but c can now be any real number. Determine all c that make A_c positive semidefinite, then those that make it positive definite. Finally, respond to the proposal c = 3 with a numerical counterexample and an explanation of the role of coupling.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The eigenvalues are a + c and a − c. Write the inequalities that impose the required sign on both at once.

Hint 2

At each boundary, one eigenvalue becomes 0. Beyond it, use the eigendirection carrying the negative value.

Compare with the solution

Positive semidefiniteness requires a + c ≥ 0 and a − c ≥ 0. The first imposes c ≥ −a; the second c ≤ a. With a = 2, we obtain -2 ≤ c ≤ 2. These conditions are also sufficient: the two squares in the spectral identity then have nonnegative coefficients.

For positive definiteness, both inequalities are strict: -2 < c < 2. The squares of x + y and x − y can both be 0 only when x and y are 0. At the boundaries, one of the eigendirections escapes the penalty.

The proposal c = 3 gives λ- = 2 − 3 = -1. The setting (1 ; -1) mm has a penalty 2 × 2 − 2 × 3 = -2 mm². The negative cross term outweighs the diagonal squares. In the other direction, (1 ; 1) mm gives 10 mm²: the matrix is indefinite, rather than merely flat.

Check your reasoning

  • Solve both inequalities simultaneously, including negative c.
  • Include the boundaries for positive semidefiniteness and exclude them for positive definiteness.
  • Calculate a negative penalty that refutes the proposal and explain where it comes from.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be checked to say that a real symmetric matrix is positive semidefinite?

Its form uᵀAu must be at least 0 for every real vector u. Isolated positive tests do not prove this universal condition; one negative test is enough to refute it.

Recall 2What does positive definiteness require beyond positive semidefiniteness?

The form must be strictly positive for every nonzero vector. A nonzero direction with 0 penalty rules out this property, even if the penalty never becomes negative.

Recall 3Which eigenvalue criterion allows a decision, and under which assumption?

For a real symmetric matrix: all eigenvalues nonnegative give positive semidefiniteness; all strictly positive give positive definiteness. Orthonormal diagonalization expresses the form as a sum of squares with these weights.

Recall 4Why does c = 2 allow a plotter displacement to go undetected?

The penalty becomes 2 times the square of x + y. All settings with opposite offsets (t ; −t) make it 0, even when t is nonzero. The rule is positive semidefinite but not positive definite.

Recall 5Why are positive diagonals insufficient? Reconstruct the plotter counterexample.

They check only the individual axes. With c = 3, the diagonals equal 2, but the setting (1 ; -1) mm gives -2 mm²: the cross term can outweigh the diagonal contributions.
