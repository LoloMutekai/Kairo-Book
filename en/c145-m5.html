Level 2 · C145·M5

Policy evaluation

Your goalEstimate the average effect of a programme on treated units using difference-in-differences, construct the counterfactual with a control group, then name the assumptions that allow this difference to be interpreted as an effect.

Before you start
c074-m5 : identification assumptions · c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c101-m5 : rolling-origin forecasting · c145-m1 : endogeneity · c145-m2 : instrumental variables · c145-m3 : fixed effects · c145-m4 : simultaneous equations

✿01 See the idea

A fictional region subsidizes evening opening at some municipal libraries. All the figures on this page are invented for the exercise: they describe no real region, programme or result. We follow a single measure, average opening hours per week, written h/wk here and h/sem in the formulas. It is calculated separately for subsidized municipalities and for municipalities receiving no subsidy, called controls, before and after the programme.

For subsidized municipalities, the average rises from 20 to 26 h/wk. Should the whole increase, 6 h/wk, be attributed to the programme? No: over the same period, control municipalities rise from 22 to 25 h/wk. A national budget, a seasonal change or a common rule could have pushed everyone upwards.

Comparing the groups after the programme is no better: 26 against 25 gives a lead of 1 h/wk, although subsidized municipalities started 2 h/wk lower. If they were chosen for that reason, their selection is a form of endogeneity (see c145-m1): the treated group is no random sample.

The idea behind difference-in-differences is to construct something never observed: the average that subsidized municipalities would have had without the programme. This is called the counterfactual. We assume that, without the programme, their average would have changed in the same way as that of control municipalities. The estimated effect is then the difference between what happened and this counterfactual.

This interpretation is an identification assumption (see c074-m5), not a result. A different but stable starting level is allowed: it acts as a group fixed effect, a concept from c145-m3. What the assumption rules out is the groups following diverging trends at the same time for some reason other than the programme. This unit uses neither instruments (c145-m2) nor a simultaneous system (c145-m4).

The rule to remember

Estimated effect = change in treated municipalities minus change in control municipalities; it can be interpreted as the average effect on the treated only if, without the programme, they would have changed in the same way as the controls, with no anticipation or shock specific to a group.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A subsidy for evening opening in fictional municipalities

Fictional data in h/wk. Subsidized municipalities: 20 before, 26 after. Control municipalities: 22 before, 25 after. An earlier period preceding the before period gives 18 for subsidized municipalities and 20 for controls. Let us estimate the average effect of the programme on subsidized municipalities, then check it.

  1. Name the quantities and assumptions

    Y denotes an average across municipalities, in h/wk. Its first subscript names the group: T for subsidized (treated) municipalities, C for controls. The second names the period: ant, av and ap mean earlier, before the programme and after the programme. The four observed averages are as follows.

    YT,av=20,YT,ap=26,YC,av=22,YC,ap=25 h/sem

    The counterfactual, written as Y with the superscript shown below, is the average that municipalities T would have had after the programme if it had never existed. The superscript means “without”. We do not observe this average. The target effect is τ = observed Y(T, ap) − counterfactual Y(T, ap), in h/wk: an average effect on subsidized municipalities.

    τ=YT,ap−YT,apsans

    Three assumptions support the reasoning. HP, parallel trends: without the programme, the change in municipalities T would have equalled that in municipalities C. HA, no anticipation: before the programme, municipalities T had not already changed their opening hours in expectation of the subsidy. HS, stability: the same municipalities appear in the periods being compared, and the programme did not affect municipalities C. If any assumption fails, the causal interpretation fails.

    YT,apsans−YT,av=YC,ap−YC,av (HP)

  2. Calculate the two simple comparisons

    Before and after for municipalities T: 26 − 20 = 6 h/wk. This interpretation attributes everything that changed to the programme, including what would have changed anyway.

    Levels after the programme: 26 − 25 = 1 h/wk. This interpretation ignores the fact that municipalities T started 2 h/wk lower, since 22 − 20 = 2.

    The two interpretations give 6 and 1 h/wk respectively: they cannot both be the effect, and nothing proves that either is the effect. We need a rule that removes the common trend and the initial difference.

  3. Construct the counterfactual using HP

    Under HP, the counterfactual is the starting level of municipalities T plus the change in municipalities C.

    YT,apsans=YT,av+(YC,ap−YC,av)

    Change in controls: 25 − 22 = 3 h/wk. Counterfactual: 20 + 3 = 23 h/wk. This number is not measured: it is what HP leads us to assume.

  4. Interpret the effect

    Estimated effect: observed value minus counterfactual, giving 26 − 23 = 3 h/wk. Substituting the formula for the counterfactual cancels the starting level of municipalities T and gives difference-in-differences.

    τ^=(YT,ap−YT,av)−(YC,ap−YC,av)=6−3=3 h/sem

    Under HP, HA and HS, the subsidy is associated with 3 additional opening h/wk on average in subsidized municipalities. This is a fictional value based on averages for 2 groups, with no measure of uncertainty.

  5. Check by other methods

    Using differences between groups: the difference T minus C was −2 before and 1 after, so it changed by 1 + 2 = 3 h/wk. The same result as 3.

    Shift in level: add 10 h/wk to both averages for municipalities T, giving 30 and 36. Their change remains 6, so difference-in-differences is still 6 − 3 = 3 h/wk. A stable difference in levels does not change the estimate, which explains why the initial difference is not an obstacle.

    These checks verify the arithmetic and the logic of the calculation; they do not verify HP.

  6. Look before the programme

    Over the earlier period preceding the before period, there was no programme. Change in municipalities T: 20 − 18 = 2 h/wk. Change in controls: 22 − 20 = 2 h/wk. Placebo difference-in-differences: 2 − 2 = 0 h/wk.

    A value close to 0 makes HP more credible; a clear departure from 0 would have cast doubt on it. This does not prove HP, since a trend can change at the exact time of the programme. This check examines the past, not the counterfactual itself.

  7. State what has not been established

    The calculation gives a point estimate based on fictional averages. It provides neither a standard error nor an interval; with few municipalities or municipalities linked to one another, these measures require the precautions discussed in c066-m5. The counterfactual remains a construction: it is supported by the assumptions and checks on the past, not by an observation.

Explain it in your own words

Explain in your own words why the initial difference of 2 h/wk does not bias difference-in-differences, then why diverging trends at the time of the programme would bias it.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The increase observed in subsidized municipalities is the effect of the programme.”

    This increase mixes the effect with what would have changed anyway. Subtract the change in controls, 3 h/wk here, as when constructing the counterfactual: the estimate goes from 6 to 3 h/wk.

    Revisit the construction of the counterfactual

  • “Comparing the groups after the programme is enough.”

    This difference of 1 h/wk includes the initial difference, since subsidized municipalities started 2 h/wk lower. Compare changes rather than levels, and check using the difference between groups, which changes by 3 h/wk.

    Reread the scene and the simple comparisons

  • “An earlier period with no difference in trends proves that the effect is causal.”

    It tests only the past: a trend may change at the time of the programme, or a shock may affect just one group. Difference-in-differences remains conditional on HP, HA and HS; repeat the diagnosis in the exercise about the earlier period.

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Another subsidy

Another fictional scene, in h/wk. Subsidized municipalities: 30 before, 35 after. Control municipalities: 28 before, 29 after. Calculate difference-in-differences, then check it by comparing the differences between groups before and after.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate each change as after minus before, then subtract the change in controls.

Hint 2

To check, calculate the difference between subsidized municipalities and controls before, then after, and compare these two differences.

Compare with the solution

Change in subsidized municipalities: 35 − 30 = 5 h/wk. Change in controls: 29 − 28 = 1 h/wk. Difference-in-differences: 5 − 1 = 4 h/wk.

Check: the difference is 30 − 28 = 2 before and 35 − 29 = 6 after; it has changed by 6 − 2 = 4 h/wk. The two methods agree.

For comparison, a before–after interpretation alone would give 5 h/wk, and comparing levels after would give 6 h/wk. Neither removes both the common trend and the initial difference.

Check your reasoning

  • Changes calculated as after minus before.
  • Change in controls subtracted from the change in the treated group.
  • Result expressed in h/wk.
  • Check using differences between groups agrees with the first calculation.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis

Were the groups already diverging?

Fictional scene in h/wk, with three periods. Subsidized municipalities: 10, 14, then 20. Control municipalities: 10, 11, then 14. The programme starts only between the last two periods. Calculate difference-in-differences between the first two periods, then between the last two. What can you say about the parallel trends assumption?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The first difference-in-differences calculation covers a period without the programme: it should not depart from 0 if HP is plausible.

Hint 2

Compare the two values obtained before drawing any conclusion about the effect.

Compare with the solution

Between the first two periods: 14 − 10 = 4 h/wk for subsidized municipalities and 11 − 10 = 1 h/wk for controls. Placebo difference-in-differences: 3 h/wk, with no programme at all.

Between the last two: 20 − 14 = 6 h/wk and 14 − 11 = 3 h/wk, giving 3 h/wk.

The same value, 3 h/wk, was already present before the programme: the groups were diverging before it. HP is in doubt, and the 3 h/wk cannot be interpreted as an effect. Adding the untested assumption that this divergence continues at the same rate would leave 0 h/wk, but this conclusion depends on that addition and is not established by the data.

Check your reasoning

  • Placebo difference-in-differences calculated over the period without the programme.
  • The two difference-in-differences estimates compared before any conclusion.
  • HP presented as being in doubt, without claiming that the effect is 0.
  • Additional assumption identified as untested.

Exercise 3 · Reverse reasoning

Recover the observed value

A fictional report uses difference-in-differences to announce an effect of 5 h/wk for subsidized municipalities that started at 40 h/wk. Control municipalities gained 2 h/wk over the period. Recover the average observed after the programme in subsidized municipalities, recalculate difference-in-differences as a check, and state which assumptions are needed to call this figure an effect.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The counterfactual adds the change in controls to the starting level.

Hint 2

The observed value is the counterfactual plus the reported effect.

Hint 3

To check, recalculate the change in subsidized municipalities, then subtract the change in controls.

Compare with the solution

Counterfactual: 40 + 2 = 42 h/wk. Observed value: 42 + 5 = 47 h/wk.

Check: 47 − 40 = 7 h/wk of change, then 7 − 2 = 5 h/wk. This recovers the reported effect.

This figure becomes an effect only under HP (parallel trends), HA (no anticipation) and HS (stable groups, unaffected controls). Without them, it is only a difference in changes between two groups.

Check your reasoning

  • Counterfactual formed from the starting level and the change in controls.
  • Observed value recovered, then checked by calculating forwards.
  • Three assumptions named.
  • No certainty about the real effect claimed.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does difference-in-differences calculate?

The change in treated municipalities minus the change in control municipalities between two periods; under the assumptions, it is the average effect on the treated.

Recall 2What is the counterfactual in the library scene?

The average that subsidized municipalities would have had afterwards without the programme; it is not observed, but constructed using the change in controls.

Recall 3Which assumptions are needed to interpret difference-in-differences as an effect?

Parallel trends without the programme, no anticipation, stable groups and controls unaffected by the programme.

Recall 4Why does a stable initial difference in levels not bias the estimate?

Each group is compared with itself before and after; a constant difference in levels, like a group fixed effect, disappears in the subtraction.

Recall 5What does difference-in-differences calculated over a period without the programme show?

It tests the plausibility of parallel trends in the past; a value close to 0 makes the assumption more credible without proving it.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Name the groups, periods, measure and target effect; construct the counterfactual using the change in controls; subtract that change from the change in the treated group; check using differences between groups and a period without the programme; state the assumptions and say that uncertainty is not calculated here.
