Level 4 · C232·M1

Laplace's method

Your goalApproximate the area under a narrow exponential curve using the parabola at its peak, then measure what this approximation leaves out.

Before you start
c231-m5 : geodesics · c228-m5 : incompleteness · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c174-m5 : conditions for validity

✿01 See the idea

An unnormalized probability density looks like a hill whose total area we want to find. This area does not always have a simple formula. But when the hill becomes very narrow, almost all the area lies around the peak, and near a peak a curve resembles a parabola. Laplace's method therefore uses the parabola at the peak to replace the hill with a curve whose area can be calculated exactly.

Our example is entirely fictional. A dimensionless parameter θ is observed through n = 12 fictional independent readings. The unnormalized density of θ is proportional to exp(−n h(θ)), where the dimensionless function h is:

h(θ)=θ44−θ

This toy model is chosen so that every calculation can be done by hand; it describes no real phenomenon. We want the mass Zn, meaning the total area under the density, for example to normalize it:

Zn=∫e−nh(θ)dθ

Why does the hill become narrower? Because n multiplies h in the exponent. Wherever h exceeds its minimum, even slightly, the factor n turns this difference into a rapid drop in density. As n increases, the hill becomes narrower, and the parabola at the peak describes it more accurately.

Three terms to establish. The peak θ̂ is the point where h is smallest, so the density is largest. The slope h′(θ̂) measures the inclination of h at the peak; it is 0 there. The curvature h″(θ̂) measures how sharply h rises on either side: the greater it is, the more pointed the hill. We use only these two derivatives, calculated step by step for a polynomial.

One fact is accepted here without proof: the area under a Gaussian curve with variance σ² centered at 0 is σ√(2π). Of the prerequisites in the catalogue, this example directly uses only the uncertainty from c061-m5, viewed as the width of a density. The other three prerequisites are not used in this example.

The result takes the form of a height multiplied by a width:

Zn≈e−nh(θ^)×2πnh″(θ^)

The symbol ≈ indicates an approximation, rather than an equality. The last part of the lesson measures what it leaves out.

The rule to remember

Find the peak θ̂ from h; read off the exponent −n h(θ̂) and the width σ² = the reciprocal of n h″(θ̂); write height times width; then check the neglected terms before trusting the result.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Approximate the mass Zₙ of a fictional density

Using the function h from the intuition section and n = 12 fictional readings, approximate Zₙ with Laplace's method, then measure what the approximation leaves out. All values are fictional and dimensionless.

  1. Define the objects and assumptions

    θ is the parameter; h is the function from the intuition section; n = 12 counts the fictional readings; Zn is the area under exp(−n h) over the entire real line. Everything is dimensionless.

    The method assumes four things: h is smooth; it has a unique global minimum θ̂ in the interior of the domain; its curvature h″(θ̂) is strictly positive; n is large enough for the mass to concentrate near θ̂. Here h tends to infinity on both sides, so the area is finite. We will check each assumption rather than take it for granted.

  2. Find the peak and check that it is unique

    Differentiating the term θ to the power 4, divided by 4, gives θ to the power 3, because the two fours cancel; differentiating −θ gives −1. The slope is therefore:

    h′(θ)=θ3−1

    Try θ̂ = 1: its cube is 1, so the slope is 1 − 1 = 0, and θ̂ = 1 does make the slope 0. To check uniqueness, factorize:

    θ3−1=(θ−1)(θ2+θ+1)

    The second factor is always strictly positive: for a θ² + b θ + c with (a, b, c) = (1, 1, 1), the quantity 4ac − b² is 4 − 1 = 3, which is positive with a > 0, so the polynomial never vanishes. The slope is negative to the left of θ̂ and positive to the right: θ̂ is the unique global minimum.

  3. Read off the height at the peak

    The height of the hill at θ̂ is exp(−n h(θ̂)). Calculate h at the peak, with θ̂ to the power 4 equal to 1:

    h(θ^)=14−1=−0.75

    In decimal form: 1/4 = 0.25, then 0.25 − 1 = −0.75, or the fraction −3/4. Read off the exponent using the sign in the formula:

    −nh(θ^)=12×0.75=9

    Check: h(θ̂) is negative, so −n h(θ̂) is positive, and the height is exp(9). An enormous absolute height causes no problem: only the shape of the hill around the peak matters.

  4. Measure the curvature and deduce the width

    The derivative of θ³ − 1 is:

    h″(θ)=3θ2

    At the peak: 3 × 1 = 3. The precision, meaning n h″(θ̂), is 12 × 3 = 36. It is strictly positive: the curvature assumption holds. The variance of the replacement Gaussian is the reciprocal of the precision:

    σ2=1nh″(θ^)=112×3=136

    Thus σ² = 1/36 ≈ 0.028, and σ = 1/6 ≈ 0.167. Check: 1/6 × 1/6 = 1/36, and 6 × 6 = 36. θ is dimensionless, so σ is too.

  5. Expand around the peak and keep only the parabola

    Set u = θ − θ̂. For a polynomial of degree four, h(θ̂ + u) = h(θ̂) + h′u + h″u²/2 + h‴u³/6 + h⁗u⁴/24 is exact (Taylor's formula, accepted here; you can also expand (θ̂ + u) to the power 4 by hand). The next derivatives of h are h‴(θ̂) = 6 × 1 = 6 and h⁗ = 6. The coefficients of u³ and u⁴ are h‴/6 = 1 and h⁗/24 = 0.25; the coefficient of u² is h″/2. Multiplying by n gives:

    nh(θ^+u)=nh(θ^)+18u2+12u3+3u4

    Check: 18 is indeed half the precision 36. A Gaussian with variance σ² has exponent −u²/(2σ²), or −18 u². Keeping only the term in u² therefore means replacing the hill with a Gaussian of variance σ². Its area is:

    ∫e−u22σ2du=σ2π

  6. Write Laplace's formula

    Combine the height exp(9) with the area under the Gaussian of variance 1/36:

    Zn≈e9×2π36

    We do not calculate a decimal value for Zn: this lesson uses exact arithmetic, with π and the exponential appearing only as symbols. Remember: a height factor multiplied by a width derived from the curvature.

  7. Measure the neglected terms one width from the peak

    Evaluate the three terms of the exponent at u = σ = 1/6. The quadratic term is 18 × 1/36 = 1/2. The cubic term is 12 × 1/216 = 1/18. The term in u⁴ is 3 × 1/1296 = 1/432. On the right, all terms add; on the left, the cubic term changes sign:

    1/2+1/18+1/432=241/432
    1/2−1/18+1/432=193/432

    Independent check: directly calculating n (h(θ̂ ± σ) − h(θ̂)), without an expansion, gives 241/432 and 193/432 again. Relative to the Gaussian, whose drop is 1/2, the deviation is 25/432 ≈ 0.058 on the right and 23/432 ≈ 0.053 on the left: small compared with 0.5, but nonzero. On the right the hill is lower than its parabolic approximation, and on the left higher, as in the first diagram.

  8. State what has been established

    Established: the peak, its height, its width, and the local deviations at ± σ, as exact fractions. Not established: the global error in Zn, because two points do not bound an area. Under regularity assumptions accepted here, the relative error of the formula decreases like 1/n as n increases.

    A separate numerical check in the unit's calculation file compares the actual integral and the formula, finding agreement within a few percent for n = 12 and n = 48. This checks the order of magnitude for this example; it is not a general proof.

Explain it in your own words

Why does keeping only the parabola become more reliable as n increases? Relate your answer to the width σ and the cubic and quartic terms.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The mass is the height at the peak: Zₙ ≈ exp(−n h(θ̂)).”

    An area is a height multiplied by a width. The height is exp(9), but the width √(2π σ²) comes from the curvature 3; without it, changing n would not change the scale of the answer even though the hill becomes narrower.

    Review the diagram of the hill and its parabola

  • “The parabola is exact, since Laplace's formula is a formula.”

    It is an approximation. At u = ± σ, the cubic term 1/18 and quartic term 1/432 show that the hill is not symmetric; these deviations decrease as n increases, as the cubic term in the first exercise shows.

    Measure the neglected terms again

  • “The method applies whenever a function has a minimum.”

    An interior global minimum is needed, unique or handled separately, with strictly positive curvature. For h_C, the curvature is 0: the variance σ² = the reciprocal of 0 does not exist, and the Gaussian formula no longer applies.

Exercise 1 · Explained application

A narrower hill

Keep the function h from the example and take n = 48 instead of 12. Calculate the precision, the variance σ², the standard deviation σ, and then the cubic term at u = σ. Compare it with the one in the example. The values remain fictional.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The curvature h″(θ̂) does not depend on n; only the precision n h″(θ̂) changes.

Hint 2

The cubic term is n × c × σ³, where c = 1 is the coefficient of u³ in h.

Compare with the solution

Precision: 48 × 3 = 144. Variance: the reciprocal of 144, giving 1/144. Standard deviation: 1/12, because 1/12 × 1/12 = 1/144; σ was divided by 2 while n was multiplied by 4.

Cubic term: n × c = 48, σ³ = 1/1728, product 1/36 ≈ 0.028, compared with 1/18 in the example. The ratio is 1/2: the cubic term is divided by 2 when n is multiplied by 4, so it decreases like the reciprocal of the square root of n. Check: 144 × 1/144 = 1, and 1/2 × 1/2 × 4 = 1.

Check your reasoning

  • The curvature remains 3; only the precision changes.
  • σ is divided by 2 when n is multiplied by 4.
  • The cubic term is compared with the one in the example, with ratio 1/2.

Another function, the same mechanism

Take n = 2 and the fictional function h_B in the formula, with c = 8. Its minimum is attained at a strictly positive integer. Find it by checking that the slope vanishes, then calculate the exponent n × (−h_B(θ̂)) and the variance σ².

hB(θ)=θ44−cθ
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The slope is h_B′(θ) = θ³ − c; look for an integer whose cube equals c.

Hint 2

Try θ̂ = 2, then calculate h_B(θ̂) and the curvature 3θ̂².

Compare with the solution

The cube of 2 is 8, so the slope 8 − 8 = 0 vanishes at θ̂ = 2. The second factor in the factorization remains positive, as in the example, so this peak is unique.

Height: θ̂ to the power 4 is 16, divided by 4 gives 4; c × θ̂ = 8 × 2 = 16; h_B(θ̂) = 4 − 16 = −12. Exponent: 2 × 12 = 24.

Curvature: 3 × θ̂² = 3 × 4 = 12. Precision: 2 × 12 = 24. Variance: σ² = 1/24. Check: the precision 24 times the variance 1/24 gives 1 again.

Check your reasoning

  • The 0 slope is checked by calculation, rather than guessed.
  • The exponent has the correct sign: n times the negative of h_B(θ̂).
  • The variance is the reciprocal of n times the curvature.

Exercise 3 · Diagnostic

When the parabola is flat

Consider the fictional function h_C in the formula, with no linear term. Its minimum is at θ̂ = 0. Calculate its curvature at the peak and the precision for n = 16. Can the Gaussian formula from the example be applied? Then find the scale ε such that n × ε to the power 4 equals 1, and compare it with the Gaussian scale such that n × ε² equals 1.

hC(θ)=θ44
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The curvature is h_C″(θ) = 3θ²; evaluate it at θ̂.

Hint 2

Look for ε as a simple fraction for n = 16: which fraction to the power 4 equals 1/16?

Hint 3

The substitution θ = ε s turns n h_C into s⁴/4 when ε is chosen this way.

Compare with the solution

Curvature: 3 × 0² = 0. Precision: 16 × 0 = 0. The variance σ² would be the reciprocal of 0: it does not exist, and the Gaussian formula does not apply. The parabola at the peak is flat; the fourth power term dominates.

Scale: with ε = 1/2, ε to the power 4 multiplied by 16 equals 1. Gaussian scale: ε = 1/4, ε² multiplied by 16 equals 1. The degenerate hill is wider (1/2 compared with 1/4): its mass decreases like n to the power minus one quarter, rather than n to the power minus one half.

After substitution, the mass is ε times the area under exp(−s⁴/4). This remaining area is not calculated here: the degenerate case requires a different treatment from the Gaussian formula.

Check your reasoning

  • The 0 precision is identified without dividing by 0.
  • The scale 1/2 is compared with the Gaussian scale 1/4.
  • The remaining area under exp(−s⁴/4) is explicitly stated to be uncalculated.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be checked before applying Laplace's method?

That h is smooth, that it has a unique interior global minimum θ̂ (0 slope), that its curvature h″(θ̂) is strictly positive, and that n is large enough.

Recall 2What is the variance of the replacement Gaussian?

σ² = the reciprocal of n h″(θ̂): the width is set by the curvature at the peak, multiplied by n.

Recall 3What form does the Laplace approximation take?

Zₙ ≈ exp(−n h(θ̂)) × √(2π / (n h″(θ̂))): a height at the peak multiplied by the width of a Gaussian.

Recall 4What does a check of the terms at ± σ leave unresolved?

It measures the local deviation from the Gaussian, but does not bound the error in the area; it also does not cover a peak with 0 curvature.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Find the peak by setting the slope to 0 and check that it is unique, interior, and has positive curvature; read off the exponent n × (−h(θ̂)); read off the width σ² = the reciprocal of n h″(θ̂); write exp(−n h(θ̂)) × √(2π σ²); measure the cubic and quartic terms at ± σ; state that this check does not bound the error or handle a peak with 0 curvature.

Milestone · distinguish Laplace smoothing from Laplace's method

What you understand
Laplace's method uses the parabola at the peak to approximate a narrow hill: the mass becomes a height multiplied by a width derived from the curvature. The name “Laplace” refers to other objects elsewhere; here it concerns the approximation of an integral.
