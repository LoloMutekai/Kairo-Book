Level 1 · C037·M4

Aggregations

Your goalGroup library desk visits by day, calculate the count, sum and mean, then combine the summaries while keeping their counts.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

At a library desk, the number of books borrowed is recorded for each visit. On Monday, visits A and B account for 2 and 4 books. On Tuesday, C, D and E account for 0, 6 and 9 books. These data are fictional and refer to the same week. A visit with no borrowing is still an observed visit.

To prepare the summary, imagine sorting record cards into folders labelled Monday and Tuesday. The day is the grouping key: it determines which folder each row goes into. Aggregating means replacing the rows in a folder with numbers that summarize them, such as their count and the sum of the books.

The summary no longer tells you everything the cards did. A total of 6 books can come from different distributions across visits. Keep the original record to recover the details; aggregation alone cannot reconstruct them.

In the diagrams, n denotes the number of visits, S the total number of books and m the mean number of books per visit. The letters will be defined precisely in the example. We calculate using all recorded visits, without trying to predict subsequent days.

The rule to remember

Choose what a row represents and the grouping key; count the visits, add up their books, then divide each sum by its corresponding count. Keep the sum and count to combine groups.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the library record to the summary

Using the fictional record, calculate the total and mean number of books per visit for each day, then for all visits together. The numbers of books are known for every row, including C.

  1. Set the unit of observation and the assumptions

    A row represents a visit to the desk, not a book or a distinct person. A person can return: we are not counting unique readers. Each visit appears only once, belongs to a single day and has a known, nonnegative whole number of books borrowed here.

    We keep the complete record for these days of the same week. No visit is removed, duplicated by a join or added during the calculation. The groups are disjoint and cover all the selected rows: each row contributes exactly 1 time to the summary.

  2. Define the summaries to calculate

    The letter g denotes a group, here Monday or Tuesday. G with subscript g, written G_g, is the set of identifiers of the visits in that group. i denotes such an identifier; x with subscript i, written xᵢ, is the number of books borrowed on visit i, measured in books. Thus, G_Lundi contains A and B; G_Mardi contains C, D and E.

    n_g is the group count, measured in visits; S_g is its sum, measured in books. The symbol Σ means “add up”, and i ∈ G_g means “for each visit i in group g”. When counting, each visit contributes 1, even if no books are borrowed.

    ng=∑i∈Gg1;Sg=∑i∈Ggxi

    m_g is the arithmetic mean number of books per visit: it divides the group total among its visits. It is calculated only if the count is strictly greater than 0. An empty group has no arithmetic mean; report “undefined”.

    mg=Sgng,ng>0

  3. Sort the rows, then count the visits

    The day places A and B in Monday, and C, D and E in Tuesday. Count the cards, not the number written in the Books column. Monday: 1 + 1 = 2 visits. Tuesday: 1 + 1 = 2, then 2 + 1 = 3 visits.

    Check the sorting: 2 + 3 = 5 visits, as in the original record. Visit C does count as 1 visit, with 0 books borrowed.

  4. Add up the books within each group

    This time, each card contributes the number of books it records. Monday: 2 + 4 = 6 books. Tuesday: 0 + 6 = 6 books, then 6 + 9 = 15 books.

    The total for the record becomes 6 + 15 = 21 books. Check by adding the values A to E directly, without using the folders: 2 + 4 + 0 + 6 + 9 = 21 books. Changing the order of addition does not change the total.

  5. Divide by the correct count and interpret the result

    On Monday, 6 books divided among 2 visits give 3 books per visit. On Tuesday, 15 books divided among 3 visits give 5 books per visit. These are means: no visit has to have exactly this value.

    mLundi=62=3 livres par passage
    mMardi=153=5 livres par passage

    Reverse check: 2 × 3 = 6 books and 3 × 5 = 15 books. This recovers the sums. The means also lie between the smallest and largest values in their groups: between 2 and 4 for Monday, and between 0 and 9 for Tuesday.

  6. Combine the days while keeping the same question

    For the mean across all visits, combine the books and the visits: 21 books divided by 5 visits give exactly 21/5 books per visit, or 4.2 books per visit. A decimal result is appropriate for a mean, even though each visit involves a whole number of books borrowed.

    Adding the daily means and then dividing by the number of days would give (3 + 5)/2 = 4.0 books per visit. This calculation gives each day equal weight; our question gives each visit equal weight. Tuesday contains more visits than Monday.

    If only the summary remains, each sum can be recovered by multiplying the exact mean by its count. This is why we keep the counts and avoid rounding the means before combining them.

    m=nLundimLundi+nMardimMardinLundi+nMardi

    Here, m without a subscript denotes the mean across all visits. Letters placed side by side indicate multiplication. Numerically, (2 × 3 + 3 × 5)/(2 + 3) = 21/5 = 4.2 books per visit. This is the same result as calculating directly from the record. This mean tells us neither about unique readers nor about a whole week.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the summary and explain what each denominator counts. Why does the visit with no borrowing increase the count without increasing the sum? Why must you keep the counts when you retain only the means?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “On Tuesday, there are 15 visits, since the total is 15.”

    The total 15 counts books. To count visits, each row contributes 1, regardless of its value: Tuesday has 3 visits. Check the headings and units before dividing.

    Review the counting and units

  • “An empty cell is the same as 0 books.”

    A value of 0 confirms a visit with no borrowing. An empty cell means here that the number of books is unknown. It counts as a recorded visit, but cannot be included as a known value in the sum or in the count used for the mean of observed values. You must state which mean you are calculating.

    Work through the incomplete record in the last exercise

  • “The overall mean must be the mean of the daily means.”

    To give each visit equal weight, combine the sums and counts. The simple mean of means is appropriate if the counts are equal, or if the question specifically calls for each group to have equal weight. Here, the counts differ and the question concerns visits.

    Review how to combine the days

Exercise 1 · Direct application

The visit with no borrowing

Hide the summary. Using the values 0, 6 and 9 books for visits C, D and E, recover the count, sum and mean for Tuesday. Explain the role of C in each operation.

Hint 1

Draw a card for each visit; for the count, count the cards.

Hint 2

The 0 value contributes 0 to the sum, but the visit contributes 1 to the count.

Compare with the solution

Visits C, D and E give 3 cards. The sum is 0 + 6 + 9 = 15 books. The mean is 15/3 = 5 books per visit.

C adds no books, but it belongs to the group and its number of books borrowed is known. Removing it from the denominator would change the question to “the mean among visits with borrowing”.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 3 visits, 15 books and 5 books per visit, with units.
  • Justify including C in the count and its 0 contribution to the sum.
  • Check that multiplying the count by the mean recovers the total.

Exercise 2 · Reconstruction from a summary

The details are no longer available

You have only the summaries: Monday, 2 visits at 3 books per visit; Tuesday, 3 visits at 5 books per visit. These means are exact. Recover the overall mean per visit, then examine the proposed value “4.0 books per visit” obtained by averaging the days.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Each sum can be recovered by multiplying the count by the exact mean.

Hint 2

The final denominator counts the visits in all the groups, not the groups.

Compare with the solution

Reconstruct 2 × 3 = 6 books and 3 × 5 = 15 books. The total is 21 books for 5 visits. Their quotient is 21/5 books per visit, or 4.2 books per visit.

The proposed value 4.0 comes from (3 + 5)/2. It gives Monday the same weight as Tuesday. It answers a question about equally weighted days, whereas we want equally weighted visits. The overall result 4.2 lies between the group means and is closer to the Tuesday mean, since Tuesday has the larger count.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover the sums 6 and 15 books before dividing.
  • Divide by 5 visits and obtain 4.2 books per visit.
  • Explain which question the simple mean of means answers, without claiming it is always wrong.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and limitation

The value for C is no longer known

A variation on the same record: the Books cell for C is now empty and means “unknown number”, instead of the known value 0 in the example. D and E still have 6 and 9 books. For Tuesday, give the number of recorded visits, the number of known values, their sum and their mean. Can you report the mean for all Tuesday visits?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Visit C still exists in the record. Distinguish the recorded rows from the known values.

Hint 2

The mean of the known values uses only visits with a recorded number of books.

Compare with the solution

Tuesday still has 3 recorded visits. There are 3 − 1 = 2 known values left: those for D and E. Their sum is 6 + 9 = 15 books, and their mean is 15/2 = 15/2 books per visit with a known value, or 7.5 books per visit with a known value.

The summary must specify “2 visits with known values out of 3 recorded visits”. The sum 15 is the sum of the known values; the mean 7.5 applies only to those visits. Neither the exact total nor the mean for all Tuesday visits is determined while the value for C is missing.

Replacing the empty cell with 0 would recover the mean 5 from the example, but would add information that was not observed. The result would no longer follow from the incomplete record. You must recover the value or explicitly retain this limitation.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish 3 recorded visits from 2 known values.
  • Report 15 known books and 7.5 books per visit with a known value, without extending this result to all visits.
  • Explain why replacing a missing value with a 0 value changes the data.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must you set before grouping the rows of a record?

What a row represents, the selected scope and the grouping key. Here, a row is a visit, and the day divides the visits into groups without duplicates or omissions.

Recall 2What is the difference between counting visits and summing books?

Each visit contributes 1 to the count; it contributes its number of books to the sum. A visit with 0 books borrowed therefore increases the count without increasing the sum.

Recall 3How do you combine groups to obtain the mean per visit?

Add their sums and their counts, then divide the total sum by the total count. If you have only the exact means, multiply each by its count to recover its sum. The simple mean of means gives the groups equal weight.

Recall 4Why must you distinguish a 0 value, an empty cell and an empty group?

A 0 value is known and is included in the count used for the mean. An empty cell means an unknown value here: report recorded visits and known values separately. With no known values, an arithmetic mean is undefined.

Recall 5How can you check a daily summary without simply repeating the same division?

Check that the group counts recover the number of rows, that their sums recover the direct total, and that each exact mean multiplied by its count gives its sum. These checks assume that the selected rows do represent the intended visits.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the steps from the cards to the summary: unit of observation, grouping key, count, sum, mean and checks. Then explain how to combine the means using their counts and what changes when a value becomes unknown. Finish by naming the information the summary no longer retains.

What you understand
An aggregate depends on the rows it summarizes. A sum and a count allow you to reconstruct a mean; that mean alone does not recover the individual values. The library record remains a fictional scene, with no claimed correspondence to the project data.
