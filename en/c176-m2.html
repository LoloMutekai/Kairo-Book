Level 3 · C176·M2

Monte Carlo error

Your goalEstimate a probability by simulation, quantify its standard error, choose the number of draws, and distinguish random variation from bias.

Before you start
c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c051-m5 : error diagnostics · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c017-m5 : reproduction protocol · c176-m1 : generators and seeds

✿01 See the idea

A fictional trajectory simulation returns an outcome Y for each draw: 1 if the trajectory ends above a fixed threshold, 0 otherwise. We want the probability of this event and replace it with the frequency observed over n draws. The trajectory advances in small time steps, as in the Euler–Maruyama scheme from C172·M5, and the generator seed, introduced in C176·M1, fixes the sequence of draws.

Running the simulation again with another seed would give another frequency. Monte Carlo error is primarily this variation from one run to another. It is measured by the standard error, which is the standard deviation of the frequency. It does not tell us whether the simulator targets the correct value.

Two mechanisms have different effects. Random variation decreases as n increases, slowly, because it follows the inverse of the square root of n. Bias comes from the simulator: if its time step is too coarse, repeating the same calculation more often concentrates the frequency around a value that remains offset.

All values in this scenario are fictional. We even invent the exact model value so that we can check the calculation. In a real project it is unknown, and bias must be investigated in other ways, for example by comparing simulator settings, an approach related to the error diagnostics in C051·M5. The assumptions behind these calculations relate to the topics in C060·M5 and C061·M5.

The rule to remember

Estimate the frequency, estimate the standard deviation of a draw, and divide by the square root of n to obtain the standard error; observe its slow decrease as n increases; always distinguish it from bias.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A simulation with 100 draws, from frequency to error

A fictional simulation produced 20 successes in 100 draws; these values are invented for the example and come from no real generator. Let us calculate the frequency, its standard error, and an interval, then separate random variation from a fictional bias.

  1. Define the symbols and assumptions

    n is the number of draws, here 100. Y is the outcome of a draw, 0 or 1, with no unit. k is the number of draws equal to 1, here 20. The frequency p̂ = k / n estimates the probability that the simulation assigns to the event; it has no unit and stays between 0 and 1.

    ps denotes the probability that the simulator, with its current settings, actually assigns to the event. pe denotes the probability in the exact model that the simulation aims to approximate. In a real project both values are unknown. Here we set them to 0.24 and 0.30, fictional values that allow us to check the final step.

    Assumptions: the draws are independent and identically distributed, with a single declared seed for the whole sequence; the standard deviation of a draw is estimated from the draws themselves; for the interval, the frequency is approximated by a normal distribution. Without these assumptions, the formulas below are no longer guaranteed to apply.

  2. Estimate using the frequency

    Since Y equals 0 or 1, the mean of the draws is exactly the proportion of successes.

    p^=kn=20100=0.20

    This value is the estimate from one run. Another seed would give another k, and therefore another frequency.

  3. Measure the variation of a single draw

    The error of the mean depends on the unknown standard deviation σ of a draw, which we estimate. Since Y equals 0 or 1, we have Y² = Y, so the mean of the Y² values is p̂. The empirical variance, the mean of the squares minus the square of the mean, is p̂ − p̂² = p̂ (1 − p̂).

    σ^2=p^(1−p^)=0.2×0.8=0.16
    σ^=0.16=0.4

    Check: 0.4 × 0.4 = 0.16, and the alternative expression gives 0.20 − 0.04 = 0.16. Both calculations agree. The variance has no unit, like Y. We divide by n; a correction using division by n − 1 exists, but is outside the scope of this unit.

  4. From a draw to the mean: standard error

    The frequency is the sum of the n draws divided by n. For independent draws, the variance of a sum is the sum of the variances: the cross terms have 0 mean. The sum therefore has variance n σ². Dividing by n multiplies the variance by (1 / n)², giving the result below. The standard error SE is its square root, in the same unit as Y (none here).

    Var(p^)=(1n)2×nσ2=σ2n
    SE=σ^n=0.4100=0.410=0.04

    Check: SE² = 0.16 / 100 = 0.0016, and 0.04 × 0.04 = 0.0016. SE describes the typical difference between the frequency from a run and its own mean ps; it does not yet consider pe.

  5. See the effect of the number of draws

    Increase the number of draws to 400, or 4 times as many, assuming that the standard deviation of a draw stays close to 0.4.

    SE=0.4400=0.420=0.02

    The standard error changes from 0.04 to 0.02: it is divided by 2. To divide it by 2, we must multiply the number of draws by 4. Computational cost scales with n, while the improvement scales with the square root of n.

  6. Report an interval with its assumptions

    Under the normal approximation, we report p̂ ± z × SE with z = 49 / 25 = 1.96, the coefficient for the nominal level of 95 %. Nominal means that, over many independent runs, approximately this proportion of intervals constructed this way would contain ps, if the approximation holds. It is not the probability that this particular interval contains the true value.

    p^±z×SE=0.20±1.96×0.04=0.20±0.0784

    The bounds are 0.1216 and 0.2784. Check: their midpoint is indeed 0.20, and each bound is 0.0784 away from it.

  7. Separate random variation and bias (fictional check values)

    The total difference between the estimate and the exact model splits into two parts: the random variation p̂ − ps, and the simulator bias ps − pe.

    p^−pe=(p^−ps)+(ps−pe)⇒-0.10=-0.04+(-0.06)

    The random component is -0.04: about one standard error in magnitude. The bias is -0.06, or 1.5 times SE in absolute value. The interval from the previous step contains ps = 0.24 but excludes pe = 0.30, since 0.2784 remains below 0.30.

    With 10000 draws, a hundred times as many, SE would fall to 0.004 but the bias would remain at 0.06, or 15 times SE. The interval would become narrower while retaining the same bias. Check: -0.04 + (-0.06) = -0.10.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “With enough draws, the result becomes exact.”

    More draws reduce only random variation. Simulator bias remains: with 10000 draws the standard error falls to 0.004, but the bias is still 0.06.

    Review the separation of variation and bias

  • “Standard error is the standard deviation of the draws.”

    The standard deviation of a draw, 0.4, describes the variation of an individual outcome. The standard error, 0.04, describes the variation of the frequency: it is divided by the square root of n. The code reviewed in the milestone uses the term standard error for a population standard deviation over a set of simulated errors, which is a different quantity.

    Reread the distinction in the milestone

  • “Doubling the number of draws halves the error.”

    The square root of 2 is not 2. To divide the error by 2, we need 4 times as many draws, and 16 times as many to divide it by 4, as in the sample size calculation.

    Repeat the sample size exercise

Exercise 1 · Worked application

A quarter as many draws

In the same fictional scenario, only 25 draws are made and 5 equal 1. Calculate the frequency, the standard deviation of a draw, and then the standard error, and compare it with the standard error in the example.

Hint 1

First calculate the frequency, then p̂ (1 − p̂) for the variance of a draw.

Hint 2

The square root of 25 is an integer.

Compare with the solution

Frequency: 5 / 25 = 0.20. Variance of a draw: 0.2 × 0.8 = 0.16, giving standard deviation 0.4. Square root of the number of draws: 5. Standard error: 0.4 / 5 = 0.08.

In the example it was 0.04: here it is multiplied by 2, because n has been divided by 4. This error belongs to the frequency and has no unit.

Check your reasoning

  • The frequency is calculated before the standard deviation.
  • The square root of n appears, rather than n.
  • The comparison states the factor 2 and its cause.

Exercise 2 · Sample size planning

Target a specified standard error

We want a standard error of 0.01, keeping the standard deviation of a draw at 0.4. How many draws are needed, and how many times as many as 100? What assumption is hidden in this calculation?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In SE = σ̂ / square root of n, first isolate the square root of n.

Hint 2

Then square it and divide by 100.

Compare with the solution

The square root of n is σ̂ / SE = 0.4 / 0.01 = 40. Therefore n = 40 × 40 = 1600 draws, or 16 times as many as 100.

Check: 0.04 divided by 4 equals 0.01, and 4 squared is 16. Hidden assumption: the standard deviation of a draw would stay close to 0.4; we estimate it again using the new draws. Computational cost is multiplied by 16.

Check your reasoning

  • The square root of n is isolated before squaring.
  • The cost factor is compared with 100.
  • The assumption about the standard deviation is stated.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis

A colleague wants 10000 draws

A colleague proposes 10000 draws to ensure that the interval contains the exact value. Using the fictional check values 0.24 for the simulator mean, 0.30 for the exact value, and a standard deviation of a draw of 0.4, calculate the new standard error and the half-width at 1.96 standard errors. What would the interval tell us about bias? What do you suggest?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The square root of 10000 is 100.

Hint 2

Compare the half-width with the absolute value of the bias, 0.06.

Compare with the solution

Standard error: 0.4 / 100 = 0.004. Half-width: 1.96 × 0.004 = 0.00784.

The absolute value of the bias is 0.06, or 15 times the standard error, far greater than the half-width: the interval, very narrow around ps, would exclude pe. More draws therefore do nothing to address this offset: we must reduce simulator bias, for example by refining its time step, then repeat the estimation with a declared seed and protocol (C017·M5).

Note: here pe is known because the scenario is fictional. In practice it is unknown: the narrow interval would give misleading confidence without revealing any bias, and this diagnosis requires another reference.

Check your reasoning

  • The standard error is recalculated using the square root of n.
  • Bias and half-width are compared on the same scale.
  • The remedy addresses the simulator, beyond simply changing n.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do we calculate the standard error of a frequency over n draws?

Divide the standard deviation of a draw by the square root of n, assuming independent and identically distributed draws; the standard deviation is estimated from the draws.

Recall 2How can we divide Monte Carlo error by 2?

Multiply the number of draws by 4, holding the standard deviation of a draw constant: cost scales with n, while the improvement scales with the square root of n.

Recall 3Why does increasing n fail to correct bias?

Because increasing n concentrates the frequency around the simulator mean; if that mean differs from the target value, the difference remains.

Recall 4What does an interval p̂ ± z × SE cover?

Under its assumptions (independence, normal approximation), it covers the simulator mean at a nominal level; it does not account for bias.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Estimate the frequency p̂; estimate the standard deviation of a draw; divide by the square root of n for the standard error under independence; state how many draws a target precision requires (4 times as many for 2 times less error); report the interval with its assumptions; investigate bias separately, because n does not reduce it.

What you understand
The error of a simulation estimate consists of random variation, measured by the standard error and divided by the square root of the number of draws, and bias that n does not reduce. You can identify which quantity a piece of code calls “error” before reading its value.
