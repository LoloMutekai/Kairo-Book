Level 0 · C027·M2

Trigonometric functions

Your goalRead sine, cosine and tangent as functions of an angle, then calculate the height of a point on a wheel.

Before you start
c005-m5 : areas and volumes · c006-m5 : composition · c027-m1 : unit circle

✿01 See the idea

A painted mark on a wheel rises, falls, then returns to its starting position. To describe its position, we can give the angle of the radius that passes through it. Cosine and sine translate this angle into horizontal and vertical positions. They let us track the same point throughout a full turn.

Our small ideal wheel stays in a vertical plane. Its radius is 5 dm; its centre O is 6 dm above horizontal ground. All these data are fictional. We study only the geometry: no rotation speed or measurements from a real machine are assumed.

From O, the horizontal displacement x is positive to the right and negative to the left. The vertical displacement y is positive upwards and negative downwards. The height h above the ground uses a different origin: being at the height of the centre means y = 0 dm, but h = 6 dm.

The angle θ, read as “theta”, starts from the horizontal radius pointing to the right. It increases as the wheel turns counterclockwise. Throughout this page, angles are expressed in degrees: start 0°, top 90°, left 180°, bottom 270°, return 360°.

A function assigns exactly one output to each allowed input. Here, the input is an angle; we can assign it a horizontal ratio, a vertical ratio or a height. Before reading the calculation, predict whether the height rises throughout the full turn. The second diagram lets you check this intuition.

The rule to remember

Cosine gives the horizontal displacement divided by the radius; sine gives the vertical displacement divided by the radius. To find a height above the ground, recover the vertical displacement, then add the height of the centre.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From a point on the wheel to its height

The mark P is at x = 3 dm and y = 4 dm relative to O. The radius is R = 5 dm and the centre is H = 6 dm above the ground. For this direction θ, let us find the trigonometric values, then the height h. We do not need to calculate the numerical measure of θ.

  1. Move from the wheel to the unit circle

    R is the strictly positive radius, in decimetres; x and y are the signed displacements from O, also in decimetres. Dividing each by R scales the circle to a radius equal to 1: its coordinates are then, by definition, the cosine and sine of θ. The units of length cancel; these functions give numbers without units.

    cos(θ)=xR;sin(θ)=yR

    Sine and cosine are defined for every real angle, including a negative angle or several full turns. With our convention in degrees, a negative θ indicates clockwise rotation. The argument in parentheses denotes the angle at which we want the value of the function; it is not a multiplication.

  2. Evaluate the functions for the given direction

    For P, the displacements are positive: the point is to the right and above the centre. We divide each by the same radius, because changing the size of the wheel must preserve the ratios associated with its direction.

    cos(θ)=35=0.60;sin(θ)=45=0.80

    These decimals are exact for the chosen data. Since each displacement lies between the negative of the radius and the radius, sine and cosine stay between -1 and 1, including the endpoints. Do not look for decimetres in 0.60 or 0.80.

  3. Define tangent and check its domain

    Tangent, written tan, is the quotient of sine divided by cosine when cosine is nonzero. In our coordinate system, it is also y divided by x: the vertical displacement per unit of horizontal displacement. It has no units.

    tan(θ)=sin(θ)cos(θ)=yx si x≠0

    Here: (4/5) ÷ (3/5) = 4/3, or approximately 1.33. A direct check gives 4 ÷ 3 = 4/3. Tangent can exceed 1; the bounds for sine and cosine do not apply to this quotient.

    At the top, x is 0 dm: cosine is 0 and tangent is undefined. The same applies at the bottom, then whenever the radius becomes vertical again. An invalid division does not produce a tangent of 0.

  4. Compose a height function

    Multiplying the ratios by R gives the displacements again. We recover x = 5 × 0.60 = 3 dm and y = 5 × 0.80 = 4 dm. This inverse operation checks the choice of denominator.

    H denotes the fixed height of the centre, in decimetres. The function h assigns the height of the point above the ground to the angle. First apply sine, then multiply by R, then add H: this is a composition of rules we already know.

    h(θ)=H+Rsin(θ)
    h(θ)=6+5×0.80=10 dm

    We therefore add 6 dm and 4 dm. Adding sine directly to H would mix a length with a ratio that has no units.

  5. Check using geometry and bounds

    The triangle in the diagram is right angled: its perpendicular sides have lengths 3 dm and 4 dm; its hypotenuse, the side opposite the right angle, is the radius. The Pythagorean theorem states that the sum of the squares of the perpendicular sides equals the square of the hypotenuse. Let us check that the data can describe our wheel.

    32+42=9+16=25=52 dm²

    The height cannot fall below H − R = 1 dm or exceed H + R = 11 dm. Our 10 dm is within these limits. Subtracting the height of the centre gives 10 − 6 = 4 dm again. Checking the bounds alone would not be enough: we must also recover the given displacement.

  6. Read the function over a full turn

    At 0°, the point is on the right: cos is 1 and sin is 0, so h is 6 dm. At 90°, it is at the top: cos is 0 and sin is 1, so h is 11 dm. At 180°, it is on the left: cos is -1 and sin is 0, so h returns to 6 dm. At 270°, it is at the bottom: cos is 0 and sin is -1, so h is 1 dm.

    Adding 360° to any angle returns the point to the same position. Sine and cosine are periodic: they repeat their values after a full turn, and therefore after any whole number of turns. The height of our wheel shares this repetition.

    sin(θ+360°)=sin(θ)
    cos(θ+360°)=cos(θ)

    The height curve rises and then falls; it is not increasing throughout the full turn. Distinct angles can give the same height. This is still a function: one input has exactly one output, but one output can correspond to several inputs.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations and explain why we divide by the radius to read the sine, then why we multiply by that radius to recover a height. Distinguish the angle, the ratio without units, the displacement from the centre and the height from the ground.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Sine gives the horizontal position.”

    With the angle measured from the right, cosine is horizontal and sine is vertical. Test the top: the horizontal displacement is 0 there, while the vertical displacement reaches the radius. Sine is therefore 1 there.

    Review the displacements on the circle

  • “The sine is 0.80, so the point is 0.80 dm above the ground.”

    Sine is a ratio without units. Multiply it by R to recover 4 dm above the centre, then add H: the height is 10 dm. State the origin and the unit together.

    Revisit the height function

  • “At the top, tangent is 0 because cosine is 0.”

    Cosine is in the denominator of tangent. At the top, the quotient would be 1 divided by 0, which is undefined. The height still exists: it is 11 dm.

    Check the domain of tangent

Exercise 2 · Calculate with signs

Make a half turn from P

Start again from point P in the example and add 180° to its angle. The point reaches the other side of the centre on the same diameter. Find its signed displacements, the values of sine, cosine and tangent, then its height. First predict whether it is above or below O.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A position opposite across the centre reverses the direction of each displacement without changing its length.

Hint 2

The height adds H to the new vertical displacement. For tangent, dividing two negative numbers gives a positive number.

Compare with the solution

The opposite point is to the left and below O: x = -3 dm and y = -4 dm. The radius remains 5 dm; cos is -3/5, or -0.60, and sin is -4/5, or -0.80.

Tangent is (-4/5) ÷ (-3/5) = 4/3, the same as at P. The signs cancel; tangent alone therefore does not distinguish these opposite positions.

The height is 6 + (-4) = 2 dm. It is below the centre while remaining above the ground and within the interval from 1 dm to 11 dm. The result agrees with the geometric prediction.

Check your reasoning

  • Reverse both displacements and keep the radius positive.
  • Explain the signs of the ratios, especially that of tangent.
  • Obtain 2 dm and check the position relative to the centre and the ground.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

Can height determine the position?

Another location Q on the same wheel has displacements x = -3 dm and y = 4 dm. Someone says: “It is at the same height as P, so it must have the same angle and the same trigonometric values.” Check that Q lies on the circle, calculate its values and its height, then correct this claim. P and Q are considered within the same turn.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Q is the reflection of P in a vertical mirror through O: which length and which sign change?

Hint 2

The height formula uses sine. Compare sine, cosine and their quotient separately.

Compare with the solution

Changing the sign of x preserves its square: (-3) squared + 4 squared is 9 + 16 = 25 dm², equal to the square of the radius. Q is indeed on the same wheel.

For Q, cosine is -3/5, sine is 4/5 and tangent is -4/3, or approximately -1.33. The height is 6 + 4 = 10 dm, the same as at P.

Yet P and Q are on different sides of the centre: their directions, and therefore their angles within this turn, differ. Equal height implies equal sine here, since H and R are fixed; it does not require equal cosine or equal tangent. The height function assigns a unique output to each angle, without promising a unique angle from a height.

Check your reasoning

  • Check that the point lies on the circle using the squares of the lengths.
  • Keep sine unchanged and reverse the signs of cosine and tangent.
  • Refute the claim of a unique angle using the two positions on this wheel, rather than merely stating that the claim is false.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1For an angle measured from the right, what do sine and cosine give on a circle of radius R?

Cosine is x/R and gives the horizontal ratio; sine is y/R and gives the vertical ratio. x and y are signed from the centre. R is positive and these ratios have no units.

Recall 2How can you recover a height above the ground from sine?

With H the height of the centre and R the radius, h(θ) = H + R sin(θ). Multiplying by R recovers a length from the centre; adding H changes the origin to the ground. H and R must have the same unit.

Recall 3Why does the height exist at the top when tangent is undefined there?

The height uses sine, which is 1 at the top. Tangent divides sine by cosine, which is 0 there. The quotient is not allowed; the height H + R remains defined.

Recall 4What does it mean for the sine and cosine functions to repeat after a full turn?

In degrees, adding 360° to an angle returns to the same point and gives the same sine and cosine again. The height of the wheel also repeats. This does not mean that it increases throughout the full turn.

Recall 5Does one height for each angle require one angle for each height?

No. Points P and Q, reflected across the vertical line through the centre, are 10 dm above the ground but on different sides. Height is a function of angle; its result does not always determine the angle.
