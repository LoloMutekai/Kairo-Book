Level 4 · C227·M2

Doob-Meyer

Your goalSeparate an arrival count into a predictable compensator and a martingale, then state the assumptions that make this decomposition unique.

Before you start
c226-m5 : integration with jumps · c165-m5 : duality · c171-m5 : multidimensional case · c227-m1 : finite variation

✿01 See the idea

A turnstile counts arrivals into a hall. The counter can only increase, even on a day with fewer visitors than expected. To describe a deviation from expectation, we therefore need to subtract something: the number of arrivals accumulated on average under a stated model.

In our fictional scene, the model predicts 1.5 arrivals per minute. At 4 min, it expects 6, while the counter on the path shown records 4. The residual is -2 arrivals. It can become negative without anyone having left: it is the count minus its cumulative expectation.

The Doob-Meyer decomposition gives this separation a precise meaning. For a submartingale satisfying the assumptions of the theorem, the predictable increasing part is called the compensator. The remaining part is a martingale: given the current information, its future increment has 0 expectation. This does not require its path to be flat or its value to be 0.

We will check this property for a Poisson process with constant intensity. The arrival times form an invented illustrative path, rather than collected data. The calculation explains the role of the theorem; it does not prove the existence of the decomposition for all submartingales.

The rule to remember

Subtract the predictable mean growth under the chosen filtration; then check a conditional expectation. Subtraction along a single path is not enough.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Count arrivals, then subtract the cumulative expectation

The turnstile starts with no arrivals counted. Over the horizon T = 4 min, assume a homogeneous Poisson process with known intensity λ = 3/2 arrivals/min, or 1.5 arrivals/min. The illustrative path has arrivals at 0.5, 1.5, 2.5 and 3.5 min. Let us calculate the compensator and the residual, then check the martingale property under this model.

  1. Specify what is known and when

    Nt counts arrivals up to and including t; N starts at 0. The filtration (ℱt) is the increasing family of available information: here, the history of the counter, completed with null events and made right continuous, with no knowledge of future arrivals. An adapted process has a value at t that is measurable with respect to ℱt.

    ℙ denotes the probability law of the model and E its expectation. For an integrable quantity Y, E[Y | ℱs] denotes its mean given the information at s. For 0 ≤ s ≤ t ≤ T, the Poisson model gives an increment Nt − Ns independent of ℱs, with mean λ(t − s). This assumption implies that N is a submartingale: its conditional future expectation is at least its current count. Equalities and inequalities of conditional expectations are understood to hold almost surely under ℙ.

    The times s, t and T are in minutes; λ is in arrivals/min; N and the processes A and M to be constructed are in arrivals. Probabilities and the indicator of an event have no units. A fractional mean of a count is allowed: only the observations of N must be integers.

    E[Nt∣ℱs]=Ns+λ(t−s)≥Ns

  2. State exactly what Doob-Meyer guarantees

    Here is a version on the closed interval from 0 to T, where T is a finite deterministic horizon. A submartingale is an adapted process whose value at every time has finite absolute expectation and whose conditional future expectation is at least its present value. On a filtered probability space satisfying the usual conditions, every real valued cadlag submartingale X of class D admits the decomposition below. The usual conditions mean that the filtration is complete and right continuous. Cadlag means that paths are right continuous and have left limits.

    Xt=Mt+At;A0=0

    M is a uniformly integrable cadlag martingale; A is a predictable increasing cadlag process, with A initially equal to 0 and E[A at T] finite. Thus the initial value of M equals the initial value of X. Under this normalization, the pair is unique up to indistinguishability: outside a single event of probability 0, the equalities hold for all times simultaneously.

    Class D requires uniform integrability of all the values of X at stopping times τ between 0 and T. A stopping time is a random time for which the information in ℱt tells us at t whether it has already occurred. Uniform integrability requires large values to contribute less and less to expectations, uniformly over this choice of stopping time:

    limK→∞sup0≤τ≤TE[|Xτ|1{|Xτ|>K}]=0

    In this formula, the supremum is taken only over stopping times τ bounded by T. X denotes the general submartingale; M and A have the same unit as X, here arrivals. The threshold K tends to infinity and also has this unit. The indicator factor, written as 1 with an event as a subscript, equals 1 if that event occurs and 0 otherwise. Integrability at each time alone is not the class D assumption.

  3. Understand why predictability is essential

    Predictable means measurable with respect to the sigma algebra generated by adapted left continuous processes. This is a condition on the whole process, related to the information available before the time in question. It does not simply mean being able to read its value after the event. A continuous adapted process is predictable; our deterministic straight line will be a simple case.

    Since A is increasing, it has finite variation over our horizon: its total variation is its increase. But requiring only adaptation and finite variation does not characterize the compensator. The Poisson counter N itself has these properties; yet its jumps occur at times not announced by its past, and N is not predictable in the chosen filtration.

    The decomposition N = 0 + N would therefore be an algebraic separation with an increasing part, but it would not be the Doob-Meyer decomposition. The stated uniqueness really uses predictability. We are applying this theorem; the drawing does not replace its general proof of existence and uniqueness.

  4. Construct the candidate with the correct units

    Since the intensity is constant and known before the arrivals, the mean count accumulated since the start is At = λt. We subtract this quantity from the observed count: Mt = Nt − λt. Multiplying an intensity by a duration does give arrivals. A does not jump at arrival times: it increases continuously while we wait.

    At=λt;Mt=Nt−λt

    At 2 min, the arrivals at 0.5 and 1.5 min have occurred: N is 2. A is (3/2) × 2 = 3 arrivals; M is 2 − 3 = -1 arrival. This is a shortfall relative to the model's expectation.

  5. Finish the path calculation and check it

    At T = 4 min, the arrivals at 2.5 and 3.5 min have brought the count to 4. The cumulative expectation is (3/2) × 4 = 6 arrivals; the residual is 4 − 6 = -2 arrivals.

    Check by reconstruction: M + A = (-2) + 6 = 4 arrivals, exactly the final count. Check using increments between 2 and 4 min: ΔN = 2, ΔA = 3, so ΔM = 2 − 3 = -1 arrival. We also recover (-2) − (-1) = -1 arrival. Δ denotes the final value minus the initial value over this interval.

    This check confirms the subtractions along the path; it does not yet prove the martingale property. We need to reason about possible futures conditional on the past, rather than only the future that was drawn.

  6. Check the martingale property and distinguish it from the path

    Conditional on ℱs, Nt − Ns has mean λ(t − s). The term λt is deterministic, so it remains unchanged inside the conditional expectation. By linearity, we can write:

    E[Mt∣ℱs]=Ns+λ(t−s)−λt=Ns−λs=Ms

    At 2 min, once the count 2 is known, the remaining duration is 4 − 2 = 2 min. The expected increment is 1.5 × 2 = 3 arrivals. The expected final count is therefore 2 + 3 = 5 arrivals; the expected final residual is 5 − 6 = -1 arrival, its present value.

    Yet the path gives M at T = -2 arrivals: this is possible. The conditional equality concerns the average over futures, rather than each realization. Similarly, M has 0 unconditional expectation here because it starts at 0; this does not mean that its conditional expectation is always 0.

  7. Review the assumptions and scope of the example

    N is cadlag, adapted, increasing and integrable, with E[N at T] = λT = 6 arrivals. For every stopping time τ bounded by T, 0 ≤ N at τ ≤ N at T. This domination by the same integrable variable ensures class D. This is a stronger check than a few means at fixed times.

    A = λt is continuous, adapted, predictable, increasing, initially 0 and integrable at T. M is adapted, cadlag and integrable; the preceding conditional equality makes it a martingale. Over this horizon, |Mt| ≤ N at T + λT: this integrable domination also ensures uniform integrability. The constructed pair therefore satisfies the stated version of Doob-Meyer.

    Uniqueness is relative to the chosen law, filtration and normalization. In the general theorem, A need not be deterministic or equal to the unconditional expectation of X. Our formula λt relies on the homogeneous Poisson model; simply having a counter does not justify choosing it.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A martingale is 0 at every time.”

    It is the future increment that has 0 conditional expectation. After 2 min, the residual is -1 arrival; its expectation at the horizon, given this past, is still -1 arrival. Its final realization can be -2 arrivals.

    Repeat the conditional check over possible futures

  • “Any increasing part with finite variation will do for A.”

    Predictability, normalization and integrability conditions are also needed. N is increasing and adapted, but its Poisson jumps are not announced by its past. The algebraic separation with A = N does not satisfy the theorem.

    Reread the role of predictability

  • “My residual fits exactly, so I have found the compensator.”

    A fit to the path shown does not check any conditional mean. An incorrect intensity can even produce a 0 residual at the horizon while leaving it with drift under the model's true law.

    Test subtracting an incorrect intensity

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis using conditional expectation

A 0 residual can hide drift

The turnstile's law remains Poisson with λ = 1.5 arrivals/min. Nevertheless, someone subtracts a rate fixed from the start at r = 1 arrival/min: Bₜ = rt and Rₜ = Nₜ − Bₜ. Calculate R at 2 and 4 min along the path shown. Then calculate E[R at the horizon − R at the intermediate time | ℱ at the intermediate time]. Is R a martingale under the stated law?

Hint 1

Separate the calculation along the path from the expectation under the law that retains intensity λ.

Hint 2

The mean increment of the residual over a duration d is (λ − r)d, even if the observed residual does not change.

Compare with the solution

B at 2 min = 1 × 2 = 2 arrivals, so R = 2 − 2 = 0 arrivals. At 4 min, B = 4 arrivals, so R = 4 − 4 = 0 arrivals. This realization gives the same residual at the times being compared.

But the intensity being subtracted is too low by 1.5 − 1 = 0.5 arrival/min. Over 2 min, the conditional mean increment of R is 0.5 × 2 = 1 arrival, strictly greater than 0. R is therefore not a martingale under this law.

r was fixed in advance: B is indeed predictable. This is not sufficient, because the martingale condition for the residual fails. Choosing r after seeing the final count would add a problem of future information; it would not repair this proof.

  • Calculate the observed residuals and the expected increment as different objects.
  • Use λ for the mean increment of N, and r for the quantity being subtracted.
  • Conclude using a nonzero conditional expectation, without judging by the path alone.

Exercise 3 · Reasoning about the assumptions

Two decompositions or just one?

For the same turnstile, one proposal is Aₜ = λt and Mₜ = Nₜ − λt, and another is Ãₜ = Nₜ with M̃ₜ constantly 0. Each sum recovers N; the increasing parts start at 0 and have finite variation. Does this contradict uniqueness? Also explain how to check class D for the count over our horizon, without replacing a stopping time with a fixed time.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Check every assumption of Doob-Meyer; do not stop at the sum and the increase.

Hint 2

The jumps of N are unpredictable in its natural filtration. For class D, look for a single integrable variable that dominates all the stopped counts.

Compare with the solution

The pair (M, A) constructed in the example satisfies the assumptions. In the other pair, M̃ is indeed a 0 martingale and Ã is adapted and increasing, but Ã = N is not predictable for a Poisson process with positive intensity in the chosen filtration. The second pair is therefore outside the class in which the theorem asserts uniqueness.

For every stopping time τ between 0 and T, the increasing count satisfies 0 ≤ N at τ ≤ N at T. N at T is integrable, with mean 6 arrivals. The stopped counts are therefore uniformly integrable by domination, establishing class D. Merely saying that every Nt has a finite mean does not check this uniformity.

The fact that the compensated residual here also has paths with finite variation does not force it to be constant: it has jumps. We cannot replace predictability with an incorrect argument about all martingales with finite variation.

Check your reasoning

  • Name predictability as the missing assumption for the second pair.
  • Keep the same law and filtration in the comparison.
  • Dominate all the stopped counts simultaneously by the integrable final count.
  • Do not claim to have proved the general theorem with this example alone.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the Doob-Meyer decomposition used here guarantee?

Over a finite horizon and under the usual conditions on the filtration, a cadlag submartingale of class D can be written X = M + A. M is a uniformly integrable cadlag martingale; A is cadlag, predictable, increasing, integrable at the horizon and initially 0. This pair is unique up to indistinguishability.

Recall 2Why can a negative residual be a martingale?

The condition is E[Mₜ | ℱₛ] = Mₛ for s ≤ t, rather than Mₜ being 0. It requires the future increment to have 0 conditional mean. A negative residual can therefore have that same value as its conditional future mean.

Recall 3Why not choose the Poisson counter as its own compensator?

The counter is adapted, increasing and has finite variation, but its jumps are not predictable in the filtration of its past. Predictability is required of the compensator; it rules out this false alternative in the uniqueness theorem.

Recall 4How do we check class D for the count over a finite horizon?

All the counts evaluated at stopping times bounded by the horizon are dominated by the same integrable final count. This domination ensures their uniform integrability, beyond integrability at each fixed time alone.

Recall 5Which property of the model justifies Aₜ = λt?

The increments of the homogeneous Poisson process are independent of the past and have mean λ multiplied by the duration. Subtracting λt then makes the conditional future expectation of the residual equal to the present residual. An isolated counter or a residual that fits one path well does not guarantee this model.
