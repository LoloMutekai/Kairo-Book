Level 2 · C073·M5

Interaction and repeated measures

Your goalCalculate a group–occasion interaction and retain the pairs of readings to assess the spread of changes.

Before you start
c056-m5 : effect size · c065-m5 : prediction interval · c073-m1 : ANOVA · c073-m2 : multiple comparisons · c073-m3 : nonparametric tests · c073-m4 : factorial design

✿01 See the idea

In a workshop on reading technical drawings, group A works with a guide and group B works independently. The same people take a test before and after the workshop, on the same points scale. The question is specific: is the mean change the same in the groups?

Comparing only the final scores mixes what was already different at the start with what changed during the workshop. We therefore start with each person’s change, then compare the mean changes. A difference in changes is an interaction here: the difference between occasions depends on the group, on the additive points scale.

Repeated measures come from the same person on several occasions. Their initial and final scores may be similar because they also reflect their personal level. Two scores from Lisa therefore do not represent two independent people. Pairing means keeping her before score linked to her after score.

All the data in this scene are fictional: 3 people per group, 2 groups and 2 occasions. This is called a mixed design: group varies between people, while occasion varies within each person. Nothing here describes any effectiveness of the guide that has actually been measured.

The rule to remember

For each person, calculate after minus before; then compare the groups’ mean changes; calculate uncertainty from the people and their pairs.

    The descriptive calculations require complete pairs and a comparable scale across occasions. To estimate their uncertainty as below, we also assume that people are independent, including across groups, and that, within each group, changes come from the same distribution with finite variance. We do not assume that the before and after scores for the same person are independent.

  2. Subtract within each pair

    di,g=yi,g,après−yi,g,avant

    In A: Lisa, 15 − 10 = 5 points; Mark, 20 − 14 = 6 points; Noah, 25 − 18 = 7 points. In B: Rosa, 13 − 12 = 1 point; Sami, 18 − 16 = 2 points; Tess, 23 − 20 = 3 points.

    This subtraction removes the part of the person’s level that would be identical on both occasions. It does not remove a change specific to that person or a measurement error. Check each row: before + change must give after again; for Lisa, 10 + 5 = 15 points.

  3. Average over the right number of people

    In A, the sum of changes is (5 + 6) + 7 = 11 + 7 = 18 points. Dividing by 3 people gives d̄_A = 18 / 3 = 6 points. In B: (1 + 2) + 3 = 3 + 3 = 6 points, so d̄_B = 6 / 3 = 2 points.

    Also recalculate the mean scores to check the diagram. A before: (10 + 14) + 18 = 24 + 18 = 42, then 42 / 3 = 14 points. A after: (15 + 20) + 25 = 35 + 25 = 60, then 60 / 3 = 20 points.

    B before: (12 + 16) + 20 = 28 + 20 = 48, then 48 / 3 = 16 points. B after: (13 + 18) + 23 = 31 + 23 = 54, then 54 / 3 = 18 points.

    With the same people and the same weights on both occasions, the mean of the differences equals the difference of the means: 20 − 14 = 6 points in A, and 18 − 16 = 2 points in B. If the people observed change between occasions, this identity no longer necessarily represents a mean individual change.

  4. Form the interaction with a further difference

    I^=d¯A−d¯B=6−2=4 points

    Group A improved by 4 points more than B on average in these data. This number is an additive difference in changes: it is neither the change in A alone, nor a percentage, nor the difference in final scores.

    An interaction in the means does not imply that every person responds identically within a group. It also depends on the scale: our question concerns differences in points, rather than ratios of scores.

  5. Check by regrouping the subtractions

    A minus B before: 14 − 16 = -2 points. A minus B after: 20 − 18 = 2 points. The gap between groups has therefore changed by 2 − (-2) = 4 points: we recover the interaction.

    I^=(y¯A,après−y¯B,après)−(y¯A,avant−y¯B,avant)

    The two ways of grouping are algebraically equivalent. Grouping by changes keeps the pairing visible; grouping by gaps between groups shows why the final gap of 2 points is not enough.

  6. Measure the spread of paired changes

    s_d,g squared is the sample variance of changes in group g, in squared points. The sum denoted ∑ᵢ adds over the people in that group. The denominator n_g − 1 accounts for the fact that the mean was estimated from these same people.

    sd,g2=∑i(di,g−d¯g)2ng−1

    Around 6 points in A, the deviations are -1, 0, 1 point; around 2 points in B, they are the same. Their squares are 1, 0, 1 squared point. The sum is 2 squared points; n_g − 1 = 3 − 1 = 2. Each variance of changes is therefore 2 / 2 = 1 squared point.

    Why keep the pairs? Let s_avant,g squared and s_après,g squared denote the before and after score variances, and c_g their sample covariance. This covariance is the sum of the products of the before and after deviations from their respective means, divided by n_g − 1; it is also expressed in squared points. Expanding the square of a difference gives:

    sd,g2=saprès,g2+savant,g2−2cg

    Discarding the pairs prevents us from assessing c_g. If this covariance is positive, ignoring this term overestimates the variance of change here; if it is negative, it underestimates that variance. Repeated measurement therefore cannot be accounted for simply by doubling a sample size.

  7. Separate individual spread from uncertainty in the contrast

    The variance of changes concerns individuals. The variance of their mean equals the variance of changes divided by the number of independent people: here 1 / 3 = 1/3 squared point per group. V̂(Î) denotes the estimated variance of the contrast Î. Since the groups are independent, the variances of the means are added, even though the means are subtracted.

    V^(I^)=sd,A2nA+sd,B2nB=13+13=23

    The exact result is 2/3 squared point, or approximately 0.67 squared point. The standard error is the square root of this variance, expressed in points. This is sampling uncertainty estimated under our assumptions, rather than the spread of raw scores or a guarantee that the model is appropriate.

    Check the sample size: 3 × 2 = 6 people assumed independent, each with 2 scores; 6 × 2 = 12 readings. Using 12 as the number of people would artificially multiply the amount of independent information.

  8. State the limits of the calculation

    With 2 groups and 2 occasions, the group–occasion interaction in the means directly compares the mean changes. A test on these changes is one way to target this contrast; two separate before–after tests do not test their difference. “Significant in A, not significant in B” therefore does not demonstrate an interaction.

    An observed contrast of 4 points is not enough on its own to establish an interaction in a population. Inference would require a method appropriate for the changes, assumptions about their distribution and an assessment of uncertainty. Classical ANOVA on changes adds, in particular, assumptions of normally distributed errors and a common variance across groups; we do not calculate it here.

    How the groups were formed is not documented: these numbers do not support any causal conclusion about the guide. If a pair is missing, if people are dependent on one another, or if more occasions are added, the method must be reconsidered rather than multiplying independent tests.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The final gap between groups measures their difference in improvement.”

    The final gap is 2 points, but the initial gap was -2 points. Subtract the latter to get 4 points. Also specify the directions A minus B and after minus before before doing any calculations.

    Revisit the check using gaps between groups

  • “Repeated measures give me as many independent people as scores.”

    The 12 scores belong to 6 people. Rebuild one row per person and calculate their changes. For uncertainty, retain their before–after covariance or work directly with the variance of changes.

    Find the pairs in the table again

  • “A test finds a change in A but not in B, so the changes are different.”

    The separate tests compare each change with 0. The interaction directly compares the change in A with the change in B. Reconstruct this contrast and its uncertainty; the labels from separate tests are not enough.

    Repair the reasoning in the last exercise

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis · spread and scope

What the means alone have lost

Calculate the variance of changes in each group, then the estimated variance of their difference in means. Next, imagine that only the scores by group and occasion have been kept, with no links between people. What can still be calculated? Finally, would a difference between the conclusions of two separate before–after tests be enough to prove an interaction?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Centre each change on its mean; divide the sum of squares by n_g − 1.

Hint 2

For independent groups, add the variances of the means. Without the pairs, the before–after covariance is unknown.

Hint 3

A difference in test conclusions is not a test of the difference between changes.

Compare with the solution

In each group, the deviations of changes from their mean are -1, 0, 1 point. The variance is (1 + 0 + 1) / (3 − 1) = 1 squared point. The variance of each mean is 1/3 squared point; the sum is 2/3 squared point for the contrast.

If everyone was measured on both occasions, the marginal means still allow us to calculate Î = 4 points. However, losing the links between readings prevents us from reconstructing the variance of changes: several pairings give the same means but different covariances. The variance obtained with the actual pairs can no longer be justified from the unpaired scores alone.

The separate tests each concern a change of 0. For the interaction, the question is whether changes are equal across groups. Their contrast must be analysed directly with an appropriate method. Neither an observed nonzero contrast nor two different significance labels are enough to establish the interaction in the population.

Check your reasoning

  • Individual variance and the variance of the mean are distinguished, in squared points.
  • The estimated variance of the contrast is 2/3, without using the 12 scores as the independent sample size.
  • The loss of pairs is linked to an unknown covariance, without claiming that the means have disappeared.
  • Direct comparison of changes replaces reasoning based on two separate conclusions.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does our group–occasion interaction compare?

It compares the groups’ mean changes: after minus before in A, minus after minus before in B. It is expressed in points and depends on the order of subtractions and the chosen scale.

Recall 2Why retain each person’s identity across occasions?

It links their before score to their after score. Scores from the same person may be dependent; the number of people does not double because they are measured again.

Recall 3What is lost when the pairs are erased but the means are kept?

With complete data, the contrast of means can still be calculated, but the before–after covariance cannot be recovered, nor can the variance of changes without an additional assumption.

Recall 4Why is the final gap not enough to compare improvement?

It also contains the initial gap. The difference in changes is recovered by subtracting the initial gap between groups from their final gap, in the same order.

Recall 5Why do two different before–after conclusions fail to prove an interaction?

Each separate test compares a change with 0. The interaction compares the changes with each other: it requires a direct contrast and uncertainty that accounts for the pairs and the groups.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the steps: identify the person, group and occasion; retain the pairs; calculate and then average the changes; subtract the mean changes; check using the change in the gap between groups. Then explain why covariance matters for uncertainty, and what neither reading, nor an assisted answer, nor an observed contrast allows you to claim as mastered or demonstrated.
