Level 1 · C054·M5

Newton's method

Your goalConstruct a Newton step by setting an approximation to the slope equal to 0, then check the domain, the cost and the stopping criterion.

Before you start
c033-m5 : affine approximation · c014-m5 : invariants · c054-m1 : extrema · c054-m2 : mean value theorem · c054-m3 : convexity · c054-m4 : boundary conditions

✿01 See the idea

You adjust the brightness of lighting for a scale model with a slider x. A cost J penalizes unsuitable settings: the lower this score, the better the setting suits the model. The slider is allowed between 0 and 4. Everything is fictional: no lighting measurements or energy savings are claimed.

At the starting setting, the slope of the cost is positive, so a small move back to the left reduces the cost. But how far should you move back? Newton also considers how quickly this slope changes: this derivative of the slope is the second derivative of the cost, called curvature here.

Imagine a graph whose height represents the slope of the cost. Draw its tangent at the current setting. The point where this line reaches height 0 proposes the next setting. We set the tangent to the slope equal to 0; the height of the cost does not need to be 0.

A tangent remains a local approximation. The proposed setting may still have a nonzero slope, lie outside the allowed interval or increase the cost. We therefore check the proposal before repeating the process. The diagrams show our example with explicit calculations; they do not promise the same behavior for every cost function.

The rule to remember

To find an interior minimum: read the slope and curvature, propose x − slope/curvature, check the domain and cost, then reevaluate the slope at the new point.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Adjust the slider while checking each proposal

The fictional cost J is the square of (x − 2), multiplied by (x + 4), then divided by 3, on [0 ; 4]. Start at x = 4. We accept stopping if |J′(x)| ≤ 0.25 cost point per slider unit, with an allowed point and a checked cost. We limit this calculation to 2 steps; reaching this limit without meeting the criterion would mean stopping without observed convergence.

  1. Define what we are calculating

    x is the slider position, with no physical unit; J(x) is a penalty score in fictional cost points. J′(x), also written g(x), is the slope: cost points per slider unit. J″(x), also written h(x), measures the change in this slope: cost points per squared slider unit. Primes denote derivatives, not step numbers.

    The cost is a polynomial, so its derivatives exist and are continuous. We assume these formulas are exact, with no measurement noise or evaluation error. The allowed domain remains binding even though the polynomial is defined beyond it. A positive h indicates an increasing slope; the convexity studied earlier will help us interpret the resulting point.

    J(x)=(x−2)2(x+4)3

    Expanding the product and then differentiating gives the following expressions. The expanded form also provides an independent check of costs calculated with the factored form.

    J(x)=x33−4x+423
    g(x)=J′(x)=x2−4
    h(x)=J″(x)=2x

  2. Derive the step from the affine approximation

    Let x be the current point, u a candidate point and s = u − x the signed displacement. Near x, we replace the slope g(u) with its tangent: g(x) + h(x)(u − x). We seek a candidate where this approximation is 0. The sign ≈ reminds us that we have not replaced the entire curve with an exact straight line.

    g(u)≈g(x)+h(x)(u−x)
    0=g(x)+h(x)s⇒s=−g(x)h(x)

    If h(x) is nonzero, adding this displacement to the current point gives the Newton step. As we repeat the process, k is the number of steps already taken, xk is the current point and u is the next proposed point:

    xk+1=xk−J′(xk)J″(xk)

    The quotient has slider units: the cost units cancel, as does a slider factor in the denominator. The rule proposes a point without certifying that it is acceptable. For our search for a minimum, we require positive curvature and will check the domain and cost before continuing.

  3. Calculate the first proposal

    At the start, the square is 4 × 4 = 16. The slope is 16 − 4 = 12 cost points per slider unit; the curvature is 2 × 4 = 8 cost points per squared slider unit.

    Dividing slope/curvature gives 12/8 = 3/2, or 1.50 slider units. We subtract this positive quantity: the displacement s is negative, to the left, as expected from the sign of the slope.

    x1=4−128=5/2=2.50

    The subscript of x counts the steps: the start is labeled 0 and the first proposal 1. This proposal is in [0 ; 4]. Check the construction: 12 + 8 × (2.50 − 4) = 0. This sets the tangent to g equal to 0, but not necessarily g itself.

  4. Check the cost and the actual slope

    Calculate the costs using the factored form. At the start: difference 2, square 4, factor x + 4 equal to 8; hence 4 × 8/3 = 32/3 = 32/3 points, or about 10.667 cost points.

    At the proposal: difference 1/2, square 1/4, factor 13/2; the product is 13/8, then dividing by 3 gives 13/24 points, or about 0.542 cost points. The cost has decreased. The expanded form gives the same fractions.

    But the square of 5/2 is 25/4. The actual slope is therefore 25/4 − 4 = 9/4, or 2.25 cost points per slider unit. It exceeds 0.25: the stopping criterion is not met. This is no contradiction; we had set an approximation equal to 0.

  5. Recalculate at the new point and take the second step

    We keep the new slope 9/4 and recalculate the curvature: 2 × 5/2 = 5. Keeping the initial curvature would produce a different method. The new quotient is 9/4/5 = 9/20, or 0.45 slider units.

    x2=5/2−9/20=41/20=2.05

    This point is still in the allowed interval. Its difference from 2 is 1/20; the square of this difference is 1/400 and the factor x + 4 is 121/20. Their product is 121/8000. After division by 3, J is 121/24000 points, or about 0.0050 cost points: it has decreased again.

  6. Stop according to the stated rule and check in other ways

    The new slope is 1681/400 − 4 = 81/400, or 0.2025 cost points per slider unit. It is positive and below 0.25. The point is allowed and its cost is lower, so our rule permits stopping after 2 steps. We do not claim an exactly 0 slope.

    The factored form of J provides a global check specific to this example. On the interval, the square of (x − 2) is nonnegative and x + 4 is positive. The cost is therefore nonnegative and is 0 only at x = 2, its unique minimum. The point 2.05 remains 0.05 slider units away from this known reference.

    Another check comes from the update rule: for x > 0, the next difference is the square of the current difference divided by 2x. This identity can be checked by substituting the Newton step for u:

    u−2=(x−2)22x

    At the first step, the predicted difference is 4/8 = 1/2; at the second, 1/4/5 = 1/20. These match the differences calculated by subtracting the positions. This identity also keeps the candidates to the right of 2. It is specific to our function; a small slope threshold does not give a universal position error.

    Newton applied to an equation F(x) = 0 similarly uses x − F(x)/F′(x), where F is the function whose root we seek, provided that F′(x) is nonzero. To optimize J, this function is F = J′, which explains the second derivative in the denominator. Setting J itself equal to 0 would solve a different problem, even though the solutions coincide for our particular cost function.

    If the curvature is 0, negative for our search for a minimum, or too small for reliable division, we do not blindly take this step. We keep the known point and indicate the need for another strategy. Even with positive curvature and a convex cost, we must check the proposals and boundaries. A method for choosing or reducing the step would require a separate study.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Newton subtracts the slope from the setting; the denominator is optional.”

    The slope does not directly give a displacement. Dividing it by the curvature produces slider units; setting the tangent equal to 0 explains this quotient and the minus sign. With a positive slope and positive curvature, we move to the left.

    Reconstruct the quotient from the tangent

  • “The tangent meets the axis: I am already at the exact minimum.”

    The line gives 2.50, but the slope curve still has value 2.25 there. Reevaluate the derivatives and the cost. Stopping at a threshold does not prove exact equality either, and a general stationary point still needs to be classified.

    Distinguish the intersections of the line and the curve

  • “A convex cost makes all starting points and all steps acceptable.”

    At the boundary x = 0, our curvature is 0 and the slope is −4: the Newton division is impossible. On a more restricted interval, a proposal can leave the domain. Check the denominator before dividing, then check feasibility before accepting.

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · calculation and justification

What sign should the displacement have?

At the start x = 4, the slope is 12 and the curvature is 8. Calculate the next point and the signed displacement. Someone adds slope/curvature to the setting: what is their proposal, and which check rejects it immediately?

Compare with the solution

The quotient is 12/8 = 1.50 slider units. The displacement is −1.50, so the new point is 4 − 1.50 = 2.50. Subtracting this quotient sets the tangent to the slope equal to 0.

Adding would give 5.50, above the bound 4: the proposal is forbidden even before evaluating the cost. The positive slope already indicated that a small move to the right would increase the cost.

Exercise 2 · Application · changed starting point

Start from the other side

Keep the same cost and domain, but start at x = 1. Calculate the slope, curvature and Newton proposal. Do we go past the known minimum x = 2? Is the criterion |J′| ≤ 0.25 met at the resulting point?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The square of 1 is 1. The slope can be negative even when the slider position is positive.

Hint 2

Subtracting a negative quotient increases x. Reevaluate the slope at the new point without reusing the starting slope.

Compare with the solution

The slope is 1 − 4 = -3 and the curvature is 2 × 1 = 2. Their quotient is -3/2, or -1.50 slider units.

The proposal is 1 − (-3/2) = 5/2, or 2.50. It is allowed, but it is to the right of 2 while the start was to the left. A Newton step can cross the minimum.

At the new point, the slope is 2.25, above 0.25: we cannot stop on grounds of convergence. To check the cost, at the start the difference is −1, its square is 1 and the factor x + 4 is 5. J is therefore 5/3 points. The cost at the candidate, already calculated, is 13/24 points: it is lower. The proposal passes the checks, but we must continue to meet the slope threshold.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain a negative slope and quotient, then a positive displacement.
  • Explain crossing 2 without declaring it automatically forbidden.
  • Test the slope at the candidate and distinguish this test from the cost check.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · a domain objection

The slider can no longer go as low

A constraint on the scale model now reduces the domain to [3 ; 4], with the same cost J. At the point x = 3, calculate the Newton proposal. Can we accept it? Where is the minimum on this new domain? Respond to the statement “At a minimum, the derivative must always be 0.”

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate the square of 3, subtract 4, then calculate 2 × 3. Compare the proposal with the new lower bound.

Hint 2

For every x in the new domain, the square of x is at least 9. Deduce the sign of J′ and whether J increases or decreases.

Compare with the solution

The slope is 9 − 4 = 5; the curvature is 6. The proposal would be 3 − 5/6 = 13/6, or about 2.167. It is below 3, so we reject it. Being able to evaluate the polynomial there does not make the point allowed.

On [3 ; 4], J′(x) ≥ 5 > 0. The cost is strictly increasing there, so its constrained minimum is at the left boundary x = 3, with a nonzero slope. The condition J′ = 0 applies to a differentiable interior minimum, not to all boundary minima.

We do not silently change the bounds or present the rejected proposal as a setting we reached. Here, studying the sign is enough to solve the constrained problem; it provides a proof that the unmodified Newton step did not give.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate 13/6 and justify its rejection using the domain.
  • Establish the sign of the derivative throughout the new interval.
  • Distinguish an interior minimum from a boundary minimum without requiring a 0 slope at the boundary.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which line do we set equal to 0 to construct Newton in optimization?

The tangent to g = J′ at the current point: 0 = J′(x) + J″(x)(u − x). If J″(x) is nonzero, this gives u = x − J′(x)/J″(x). We are not trying to set the tangent to the cost equal to 0.

Recall 2Why does slope/curvature have the units of a displacement?

The slope is in cost points per slider unit; the curvature is in cost points per squared slider unit. Their quotient is in slider units. The signed displacement is the negative of this quotient.

Recall 3Which checks separate a proposal from a justified stop?

Before dividing, check the curvature; before accepting, check the domain and cost; at the candidate, reevaluate the slope and compare it with the stated threshold. Reaching an iteration limit does not mean that this threshold is met.

Recall 4Why does seeking J′ = 0 fail to solve every minimum problem?

A minimum can lie at the boundary of the domain with a nonzero slope. Without convexity, a stationary point can also be a maximum or another point that is not a minimum. We must interpret the solution using the function and the constraints.

Recall 5Why did the first step not yet give a 0 slope?

The point 2.50 sets an affine approximation to the slope equal to 0. The actual slope there is 2.25 and exceeds our threshold 0.25. We must recalculate, then repeat if the proposal remains acceptable.
