Elementary compactness

Your goalSelect a subsequence of positions that converges on a closed rail, then justify why a closed and bounded set of real numbers is compact.

Before you start
c014-m5 : invariants · c031-m5 : asymptotes · c046-m5 : approximation error · c069-m5 : congruences and Euclid · c142-m1 : real numbers and bounds · c142-m2 : Cauchy sequences · c142-m3 : completeness

✿01 See the idea

A cart moves on a rail from 0 m to 8 m, including the endpoints. Imagine an indefinitely continuing list of its positions: staying on the rail does not prevent it from repeatedly heading back toward the other end. Can we still select readings, in order, that approach the same position?

Compactness guarantees this possibility for every sequence of allowed positions. It promises a selection that converges and whose limit remains allowed. It does not promise that the cart will eventually stop or that the whole list will converge.

The useful picture is to divide the rail into halves. At least one half still contains infinitely many readings. Keep such a half and repeat. The retained regions shrink; at each step, we can select a later reading in the new region.

This scene and its values are fictional. We model exact real positions, without sensor noise, and an ideal infinite sequence. A finite recording alone does not prove what would happen indefinitely.

The rule to remember

In ℝ, a closed and bounded set allows us to extract from every sequence in it a subsequence converging to a point of that set. Remember the words “extract” and “in that set”.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Keep readings that approach the right end

The closed rail is K = [0, 8] m. In the pair of readings numbered k, the position at the odd index is 1/k m from the left end and the position at the even index is 1/k m from the right end. Let us calculate positions, intervals and an error threshold, then justify the property for every sequence in a closed and bounded set of real numbers.

  1. Define the positions and their units

    n is the number of a reading; k is the number of a pair. These indices are integers at least equal to 1, with no unit. xn is a position in meters, measured from the left end. The numbers on the right-hand sides below are expressed in meters:

    x2k−1=1k m;x2k=(8−1k) m

    The positions at odd indices are strictly above 0 m and at most equal to 1 m. The positions at even indices are between 7 m and 8 m, without reaching the right end. They therefore all belong to K. We assume neither monotonicity of the whole sequence nor a physical law of motion.

  2. State precisely what compact means

    A subsequence keeps terms in their order, possibly skipping readings: its indices nⱼ are strictly increasing. j numbers the retained terms, rather than the original readings. Converging to ℓ means that, for every tolerance ε > 0 m, all retained terms are at a distance strictly less than ε from ℓ from some index J onward.

    ∀ε>0,∃J,∀j≥J:|xnj−ℓ|<ε

    Here ℓ and ε have the same unit as the positions; J and j are integers with no unit. The symbols ∀ and ∃ mean “for every” and “there exists”. The sequential characterization of compactness in ℝ states: for every sequence in K, there exist such a subsequence and a limit ℓ belonging to K. The selection and the limit may depend on the chosen sequence.

    A set of real numbers is bounded if it fits inside an interval with finite real bounds. It is closed if every sequence of its points that converges in ℝ has its limit in that set. In ℝ, being compact is equivalent to being closed and bounded. The closed rail has these properties; we will see why they are sufficient, then why they are necessary.

  3. Calculate a selection without promising convergence of the whole sequence

    Take k = 4. The odd index is 2 × 4 − 1 = 7; its position is 1/4 = 1/4 m, or 0.25 m. The even index is 2 × 4 = 8; its position is 8 − 1/4 = 31/4 m, or 7.75 m.

    Check by symmetry: the position at the odd index and the distance from the position at the even index to the right end both equal 0.25 m. The sum of their positions is 8 m.

    As k increases, 1/k m tends to 0 m. The odd subsequence therefore tends to 0 m and the even subsequence to 8 m. These limits are distinct. If the whole sequence converged, each of its subsequences would converge to that same limit: its terms would also eventually lie in every tolerance region. The whole sequence therefore does not converge. Compactness is compatible with this back-and-forth motion.

  4. Halve the rail and check the lengths

    The initial length is 8 − 0 = 8 m. Its midpoint is (0 + 8)/2 = 8/2 = 4 m. Keep [4, 8] m: this half contains all readings at even indices, hence infinitely many indices.

    The next midpoint is (4 + 8)/2 = 12/2 = 6 m. Keep [6, 8] m. Then (6 + 8)/2 = 14/2 = 7 m gives [7, 8] m. These regions still contain all readings at even indices.

    The lengths are 4, 2, then 1 m. Independent check: dividing 8 m by 2 at each division gives these same lengths. For subsequent divisions, the region is still visited infinitely often because the readings at even indices approach the right end; infinitely many indices remain even if some early readings are excluded.

  5. Justify the extraction for an arbitrary sequence

    Now take an arbitrary sequence yn of points in a nonempty closed and bounded set K of real numbers. In the scene, this would be another movement program on the same rail. Choose an initial closed interval I = [a, b] containing K, with a < b. a and b are bounds in meters. If K consists of a single point, any selection of readings already converges to that point.

    Divide the interval at its midpoint. If each half contained only finitely many indices of the sequence, their union would contain finitely many: impossible. At least one closed half therefore contains infinitely many indices. Keep one such half and repeat. We are counting indices: the same position repeated indefinitely also works.

    After j divisions, Iⱼ is the retained interval and Lⱼ is its length. The intervals are nested: each contains all the subsequent ones. The length is:

    Lj=b−a2j→0

    At each step, choose an index nⱼ larger than the previous one, with y at that index in Iⱼ. This is possible because infinitely many indices cannot be exhausted by the indices already passed. For p and q at least equal to j, the corresponding selected terms lie in Iⱼ: their distance is at most Lⱼ. Since Lⱼ tends to 0 m, this subsequence is Cauchy.

    The completeness of ℝ, studied in the previous unit, provides a real limit ℓ. The closedness of K then guarantees that ℓ is in K, since all selected terms belong to K. This establishes compactness. Boundedness provided the starting interval; completeness provided a real limit; closedness kept that limit in the allowed set. If K is empty, no sequence of points of K exists: the property is satisfied without having to construct an extraction.

  6. Check why the assumptions are necessary in ℝ

    If K is unbounded, we can choose yn in K with |yn| > n meters. Every subsequence then has distances from the origin that grow without bound; it cannot converge to a real number, because a convergent sequence is bounded. Compactness therefore requires boundedness.

    If K is not closed, the sequential definition of closedness provides a sequence in K that converges in ℝ to a point outside K. Each of its subsequences retains that external limit. It therefore has no subsequence whose limit belongs to K. Compactness also requires closedness.

    These arguments concern subsets of ℝ with the usual distance. We do not extend the “closed and bounded” rule to other spaces here.

  7. Obtain a verifiable tolerance for readings at even indices

    Return to our cart program and select nk = 2k. Write b = 8 m for the right end. The error of the position at the even index is exactly |x at index 2k − b| = 1/k m. Require an error strictly less than ε = 1/10 m, or 0.10 m.

    Since k is positive, 1/k < 1/10 is equivalent to k > 10. The first possible integer is therefore k = 11, corresponding to reading n = 22. Its exact position is 87/11 m; its exact error is 1/11 m, or approximately 0.091 m. The exact inequality 1/11 < 1/10 establishes the check without relying on rounding.

    For the preceding pair k = 10, reading n = 20 is 7.90 m and the distance is exactly 0.10 m. Equality does not satisfy a requirement of being strictly less. For every k ≥ 11, the distance decreases and the tolerance remains satisfied.

    For any positive tolerance, the same reasoning chooses an integer k greater than the quotient of 1 m by ε. This quotient has no unit. The existence of such an integer gives all the thresholds needed for convergence to 8 m, rather than just the numerical threshold of this example.

  8. Remove an endpoint to see what fails

    Suppose the same rail allows only positions strictly between 0 m and 8 m. It is still bounded. Consider only the sequence of positions at odd indices, 1/k m: all its terms are allowed, but its limit is 0 m, now excluded.

    Every subsequence of these positions at odd indices still converges to 0 m. None can therefore provide a limit in the open rail. The open rail is not compact. All the readings can remain allowed while their limit ceases to be allowed: this is exactly why we check closedness.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The sequence stays on a compact rail, so it converges.”

    Compactness guarantees a convergent subsequence. In our program, readings at odd indices tend to 0 m and those at even indices to 8 m: the whole sequence does not converge. Write out the indices of what you select before stating a limit.

    Review the positions at even and odd indices

  • “Boundedness is enough: the positions cannot escape.”

    They can approach a forbidden endpoint. On the open rail, the sequence 1/k m and all its subsequences tend to 0 m, outside the set. Also check that the limits remain allowed.

    Review the rail with its endpoints excluded

  • “I keep the half visited most often in the available readings: the proof is complete.”

    The proof uses infinitely many indices in each retained interval. Counting a finite list guarantees nothing about future readings. We need a mathematical assumption about the sequence, then an argument that applies to every division.

    Review the choice of a half and the general extraction

Exercise 1 · Application · exact calculation

Make the next division

The retained region is [7, 8] m. Divide it at its midpoint, keep the right half and give its exact length, then its decimal form in meters. Why do infinitely many readings at even indices remain in this half?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The midpoint is the average of the bounds, then the length is the right bound minus this midpoint.

Hint 2

Readings at even indices are 1/k m from 8 m. This distance eventually becomes smaller than any fixed positive length.

Compare with the solution

The midpoint is (7 + 8)/2 = 15/2 = 15/2 m, or 7.50 m. The retained half is therefore [7.50, 8] m.

Its length is 8 − 15/2 = 1/2 m, or 0.50 m. Check: the previous length 1 m has indeed been divided by 2.

For k ≥ 2, 1/k m is at most 1/2 m. All these readings at even indices belong to the retained half; there are infinitely many integers k satisfying this condition.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the midpoint and length with their units.
  • Check the division of the length by 2.
  • Justify infinitely many future indices rather than a finite number of drawn points.

Exercise 2 · Application · quantifiers

A first reading is not enough

You keep the even indices. What is the first index in the original sequence guaranteeing an error strictly less than 0.10 m from the right end? Also justify the guarantee for all subsequent readings at even indices. Does the preceding reading at an even index satisfy the condition?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write the error for pair k, then solve the strict inequality before calculating the index in the original sequence.

Hint 2

Pair k corresponds to index 2k. Compare equality at the threshold with a strict inequality.

Compare with the solution

The error is 1/k m. It is strictly less than 1/10 m exactly when k > 10. The first pair is 11, and the index in the original sequence is 2 × 11 = 22.

For every pair k ≥ 11, 1/k ≤ 1/11 < 1/10. This applies to all subsequent terms of the subsequence. The preceding reading at an even index, with index 20, has an error equal to 1/10 m; it fails the strict requirement.

This guarantee does not concern readings at odd indices, which keep returning near the left end. Writing “all subsequent readings” without specifying the selection would be false.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain pair 11 then index 22 without confusing them.
  • Exclude the case of equality at the threshold.
  • State the guarantee for all later terms of the even subsequence.

Exercise 3 · Proof · missing assumption

Is the open rail compact?

The endpoints of the same rail are now forbidden: the allowed positions are (0, 8) m. Someone claims: “It is bounded, and our whole list has subsequences converging in ℝ; it is therefore compact.” Repair this reasoning using the sequence of positions at odd indices. Also explain why the completeness of ℝ is not enough.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The definition requires the limit to belong to the allowed set.

Hint 2

Consider 1/k m as a sequence in its own right on the open rail. Where do all its subsequences converge?

Compare with the solution

The sequence yk = 1/k m belongs to the open rail for every k ≥ 1. It tends to 0 m. All its subsequences have this same limit, which is not allowed: no extraction from this sequence provides a limit in the open rail.

This contradicts the condition “every sequence has a subsequence converging in the set”. A real limit does exist, in accordance with completeness; what is missing is its membership in the rail. Closedness is therefore the assumption to restore by including the endpoints again.

The closed rail [0, 8] m is compact by the proof in the lesson. This conclusion remains compatible with the failure of the whole list in our movement program to converge.

Check your reasoning

  • Provide a sequence entirely within the open rail.
  • Show that none of its subsequences can have an allowed limit.
  • Distinguish a limit in ℝ, a limit in the set and convergence of the whole sequence.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What exactly does compactness guarantee for sequences of positions?

For every sequence in the set, we can select terms with strictly increasing indices that converge to a point belonging to that set. The whole sequence does not have to converge.

Recall 2Why can selection by halves continue even if positions repeat?

An interval containing infinitely many indices cannot be the union of halves each containing only finitely many indices. Keep a half containing infinitely many indices, then select a later reading. We count indices, rather than distinct positions.

Recall 3In the proof on ℝ, what do boundedness, completeness and closedness do?

Boundedness provides an initial interval. The nested halves produce a Cauchy subsequence. The completeness of ℝ gives it a real limit; closedness keeps that limit in the set being studied.

Recall 4Why is the closed rail compact even though our whole list does not converge?

The rail is closed and bounded in ℝ. Our list has an odd subsequence tending to 0 m and an even one tending to 8 m. These distinct limits prevent convergence of the whole sequence while still satisfying the requirement that convergent subsequences exist.

Recall 5Which sequence disproves compactness of the open rail, and why does every extraction fail?

The sequence 1/k m stays in (0, 8) m and tends to 0 m. Every subsequence retains this external limit. Merely finding a real limit is therefore not enough.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the argument without rereading: a bounded rail provides the initial interval; infinitely many indices survive in a half at each step; the lengths tend to 0 m; increasing indices give a Cauchy subsequence; completeness provides the limit and closedness keeps it on the rail. Finish with the objection about back-and-forth motion: compactness concerns an extraction, rather than the cart stopping.
