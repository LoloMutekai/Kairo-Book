Level 1 · C056·M3

Power

Your goalCalculate the probability that a test detects a specific alternative and explain what remains possible after non-rejection.

Before you start
c055-m5 : confidence interval · c056-m1 : hypotheses · c056-m2 : decision errors

✿01 See the idea

An automatic reader reports the colour of a hidden card: red or blue. We want to distinguish random guessing from an ability to recognise the colour. Yet even a capable reader can make mistakes during a short test. How can we tell whether our test gives it a good chance of revealing this ability?

Fix the protocol before seeing the answers: present 2 cards and reject the random-guessing hypothesis only if the 2 answers are correct. S means success on a card; E means error. SS triggers rejection; SE, ES and EE do not. A complete experiment is this series of 2 cards, rather than each card on its own.

Power asks a question in a world where a specific ability already exists: if the probability of success on each card were 3/4, what would be the probability that the series triggers rejection? We calculate the chances of the decision before the series. We do not calculate the probability that the reader has this ability after seeing its answer.

The entire scene is fictional. The probabilities are assumptions chosen for an exact calculation, with no measured result or claimed device performance. We assume the same probability of success on each card and independent successes: knowing the first answer does not change the chances for the next one.

The rule to remember

For a given alternative, add the probabilities of the outcomes that lead to rejection of the null hypothesis. The complement is the risk of missing that alternative.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

How likely is the test to detect the reader's assumed ability?

The protocol consists of n = 2 independent trials. H₀ assumes p = 1/2; the specific alternative H★ assumes p = 3/4. We reject H₀ only for SS. Let us calculate the power, the risk of missing H★ and the false alarm risk.

  1. State what is fixed and what varies

    p is the probability of a correct answer on a card; q = 1 − p is the probability of an error. These are numbers without units. n is a number of trials, here 2. X counts the successes in the series: its possible values range from 0 to 2 successes. The rejection region R is the set of outcomes leading to rejection: here R contains only SS, or X = 2.

    H0, called the null hypothesis, corresponds to random guessing: p = 1/2. H★ denotes a point alternative: p = 3/4, rather than all abilities above chance. The symbol ★ serves as a label. The same n, independence and rule are retained when moving from one world to the other.

    Independence allows us to multiply successive probabilities. Without it, the chance of a second correct answer after a first correct answer is not necessarily p. The rule and the length of the series remain fixed before the answers, with no early stopping.

  2. Define power under the correct probability distribution

    Prp(A) is read as “the probability of event A when the probability of success on each card is p”. π(p), the power at p, is the probability of rejection under this distribution. β(p) is the probability of non-rejection for this same alternative: this is a decision error when that alternative is true. α is the risk of rejection under H0, hence a false alarm. All these probabilities are without units.

    π(p)=Prp(R)=1−β(p)

    The relation uses the same distribution on both sides: rejection and non-rejection are complementary events. Under H0, this probability of rejection equals α; we therefore do not replace β with α in the power formula.

  3. Construct all the outcomes under H★

    Under H★, p = 3/4 and q = 1 − 3/4 = 1/4. SS requires a success followed by a success: its probability is 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16. For SE, it is 3/4 × 1/4 = 3/16; for ES, 1/4 × 3/4 = 3/16; for EE, 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16.

    These outcomes are mutually exclusive and cover all possible answers. We can therefore add their probabilities. These are exact probabilities under the model, with no observed count of detections.

  4. Find the power and check its complement

    Only SS belongs to R. For this particular rule, the power is therefore p squared; this square is not a universal formula for the power of a test.

    π(3/4)=(3/4)2=9/16=56.25%

    To write a percentage, multiply the fraction by 100. The risk of missing H★ is β = 3/16 + 3/16 + 1/16 = 3/8 + 1/16 = 7/16, or 43.75 %. An independent check gives 1 − 9/16 = 7/16, then 9/16 + 7/16 = 1. The sum over all outcomes does equal the total probability.

    The assumed ability concerns each card, with p = 3/4. Yet the test of the entire series detects it only with probability 9/16: it requires every trial to be correct. A probability of success on each card and a power do not concern the same event.

  5. Recalculate the false alarm risk under H₀

    The rule remains SS; only the distribution changes. Under H0, each success has probability 1/2. The false alarm risk is therefore α = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, or 25 %. This deliberately high risk makes our small example easy to draw; it does not provide a threshold to adopt in a study.

    We have thus calculated the probability of the same decision under different assumptions. 1 − α does not give our power: α concerns the world of random guessing, whereas β and the power calculated above concern p = 3/4. Rejection does not prove that p is exactly 3/4 either: several values of p can produce SS.

  6. Interpret non-rejection and prepare a study

    If the observed series is SE, the protocol does not reject H0. This does not demonstrate that the reader is guessing randomly. SE remains possible under H★, with probability 3/16. The total risk of failing to detect the alternative, β, does not become the probability that H★ is true after this observation.

    Power is reported together with the target alternative, the sample size n, the rejection rule and the assumptions. It is planned before collecting the answers; recalculating it by treating an observed result as the truth gives it no additional evidential value. To choose a different sample size, we would need to specify a new rule and also recalculate its false alarm risk. We stop here at the definition and calculation of power.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the table. Reconstruct the outcomes, explain why SS is enough to calculate power here, then name the world in which you calculate α and the world in which you calculate β. Why does SE not settle which hypothesis is true?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Power gives the probability that my hypothesis is true.”

    Start with “suppose p = 3/4”. In this fixed world, calculate the chance of the decision. Going from an assumed hypothesis to a random decision does not give the reverse probability of a hypothesis after the data.

    Review the definition under the correct probability distribution

  • “If the test does not reject, the ability does not exist.”

    Under H★, the outcomes SE, ES and EE do not trigger rejection, even though the ability is assumed to be present. Their total probability is 7/16. Non-rejection leaves this alternative possible; it does not measure its probability after the observation.

    Find the paths without detection again

  • “Being more permissive is enough to obtain a better test.”

    A more permissive rule can detect the alternative more often while also increasing false alarms. Recalculate rejection under H₀ and under H★: power alone does not allow you to choose the rule.

    Compare the risks with a more permissive rule

What the test can miss

Compare with the solution

By taking the complement: β = 1 − 9/16 = 7/16. Using the disjoint paths: β = 3/16 + 3/16 + 1/16 = 7/16, or 43.75 %. Both calculations concern p = 3/4.

SE implies non-rejection under the fixed rule. But SE also has probability 3/16 under H★: this observation therefore does not prove p = 1/2. Nor does β give a probability for a hypothesis after SE.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 7/16 by taking the complement and by adding the outcome probabilities.
  • Name the alternative for which β is calculated.
  • Distinguish non-rejection from proof of the null hypothesis.

Exercise 2 · Recalculate after changing an assumption

A stronger ability

Before any experiment, now consider p = 4/5 for the same fictional reader. Keep n = 2, the SS rule and H₀. Predict the direction of the change, then calculate the power and β. Does the risk α change?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The only rejection outcome remains a success followed by a success.

Hint 2

For α, return to the H₀ world: it has not changed.

Compare with the solution

Power must increase: obtaining SS becomes more likely when each answer has a greater chance of being correct. It equals 4/5 × 4/5 = 16/25, or 64 %. Its complement is β = 1 − 16/25 = 9/25.

α remains 1/4: under H0, p is still 1/2, and the rule remains SS. The 56.25 % in the example and the 64 % in this exercise describe the same test under different alternatives. It therefore has no single power without specifying the alternative.

Check your reasoning

  • Predict an increase, then obtain 16/25 and 9/25.
  • Retain 1/4 under H₀ and explain why.
  • Relate the change in power to the assumed ability, without claiming measured performance.

Exercise 3 · Compare and justify

Detecting more often at what cost?

Start again from p = 3/4 and n = 2. Someone proposes rejecting H₀ whenever there is at least 1 success: SS, SE or ES. Calculate the new power and the new risk α. Is this proposal enough to justify calling it a better test?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

With this rule, only EE does not trigger rejection.

Hint 2

Use the complement of EE under H★, then repeat under H₀.

Compare with the solution

Under H★, EE has probability 1/16. The new power is therefore 1 − 1/16 = 15/16, or 93.75 %. Direct addition gives the same result: 9/16 + 3/16 + 3/16 = 15/16. The risk of missing this alternative falls to 1/16, or 6.25 %.

Under H0, an error on each card has probability 1/2; EE has probability 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. The new false alarm risk is 1 − 1/4 = 3/4, or 75 %, compared with 25 % previously.

The increase in power comes with an increase in the false alarm risk. We cannot conclude “better” by looking only at detection. We must decide what risk is acceptable for the question being studied, then compare protocols that respect this choice. The rule would be chosen before the data, rather than to make an already observed series look favourable.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify EE as the only non-rejection outcome.
  • Calculate 15/16 under H★ and 3/4 under H₀ without mixing the distributions.
  • Compare the increase in power with the increase in the false alarm risk.
  • Reject a judgement based on power alone.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What question does power ask before collecting the answers?

If a specific alternative is true, what is the probability that the fixed protocol rejects the null hypothesis? You must state this alternative, the sample size, the rule and the assumptions.

Recall 2Why does power equal 1 − β, and why can we not replace β with α?

Under the same alternative, rejection and non-rejection are complementary; β is the probability of non-rejection. α is the probability of rejection under the null hypothesis: this calculation uses a different distribution.

Recall 3Why is power not p in the card test?

p concerns success on a single answer. The rule for the series requires 2 independent successes: rejection therefore has probability p × p. This expression depends on our SS rule.

Recall 4What remains possible after an SE series that does not lead to rejection of H₀?

An ability can exist and remain undetected. SE is possible under the alternative; non-rejection does not prove H₀, and β does not become the probability of the alternative after SE.

Recall 5What must be checked when a new rule claims higher power?

Recalculate its false alarm risk under H₀ and specify the alternative. Detecting more often may result from a more permissive rule; power alone is not enough to compare protocols.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct an explanation in this order: protocol and alternative; rejection outcomes; probabilities and independence; sum giving the power; complement β; risk α under H₀; limit of non-rejection. Redo the card calculation, then explain the cost of the more permissive rule. Power concerns a repeated decision in an assumed world. Our calculation adds the outcome probabilities exactly; a simulation can estimate a probability by repeating a fictional protocol. Its result always depends on the chosen world and rule.
