Level 1 · C028·M3

Combinations

Your goalCount sets of distinct packets without confusing different contents with a different order of presentation, then justify the division that removes duplicate counts.

Before you start
c014-m5 : invariants · c011-m5 : binomial · c028-m1 : sum and product rules · c028-m2 : permutations

✿01 See the idea

On a shelf, you have 5 distinct seed packets labelled A, B, C, D and E. You are preparing a set of 3 packets for a planter. There is only one of each packet in this fictional scene; the letters identify the packets themselves and give no information about the plants.

Taking A, then B, then C gives the same contents as taking C, then A, then B. The same packets will go to the planter. If you record each order of taking the packets as a new set, you count these contents several times. Before looking at the first diagram, try to find all the orders of the set ABC.

Here, a combination is a choice of a fixed number of distinct objects, without repetition and without regard to order. “Without repetition” rules out taking A several times; “without regard to order” treats ABC and CBA as equivalent ways of writing the choice. Sets differ through the presence or absence of a packet, never through its position in the list.

To count despite these duplicate representations, we will temporarily write ordered lists, then group those with the same contents. We need to show that every choice of contents has exactly the same number of written representations. This lesson counts possibilities: it assumes no random draw and no probabilities.

The rule to remember

Count the lists without repetition, then divide by the number of orders of each set. This division is justified because all the sets have the same size and contain distinct elements.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

How many sets for the planter?

From the fictional shelf A, B, C, D, E, choose 3 distinct packets. All choices of contents of this size are allowed, and the chosen packets have no different roles. We want the number of possible sets, rather than the order of the actions used to prepare them.

  1. Specify the counts and assumptions

    n denotes the number of available packets; k denotes the size of the set. Here n = 5 and k = 3. These are counts without a physical unit; we specify “packets”, “lists” or “sets” to indicate what we are counting. The packets are distinguishable and none can be chosen several times.

    We write C(n, k), read “n choose k”, for the number of combinations. n and k are integers with 0 ≤ k ≤ n. The size is fixed before counting, and no other constraint excludes any choices of contents.

  2. First count the ordered lists

    For the first position, any packet is possible: 5 choices. For the next position, the packet already taken is removed: 4 choices remain. For the last position, 3 remain. The product rule multiplies these numbers, since every beginning of a list allows the stated number of continuations.

    5×4×3=60 listes ordonnées

    This total distinguishes ABC from CBA. It therefore does not yet answer the question about contents.

  3. Count the representations of one choice of contents

    Fix ABC. Any of its 3 packets can occupy the first position, then 2 choices remain, then 1. This gives 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 written representations, shown in the first diagram. No other choice of contents appears in this group.

    An exclamation mark after an integer denotes its factorial. For a positive integer m, its factorial is the product of the integers from m down to 1. We also adopt the convention that the factorial of 0 is 1: the empty list has only one written representation. The factorial of k therefore counts the permutations of the k packets in a fixed choice of contents.

    k!=3×2×1=6

    Replacing ABC with ADE changes the letters, but neither the number of positions nor the number of their orders. Each set therefore gives a number of lists equal to the factorial of k, and every list belongs to exactly one set. This is why the same division works throughout.

  4. Remove the overcounting

    The 60 lists fall into disjoint groups of 6 written representations. Division counts these groups: the number of lists equals the number of sets multiplied by the number of orders of a set.

    C(5,3)=606=10 lots

    The result is an integer, as a count of sets must be. In reverse, 10 × 6 = 60 recovers the original lists. This arithmetic check is necessary, but the reasoning about equal group sizes justifies the division.

  5. Write the general formula and interpret it in this case

    For positive k, the number of lists without repetition is n × (n − 1) × … × (n − k + 1). There are k factors: we stop after filling k positions. This product is the quotient of the factorial of n by the factorial of n − k: the denominator removes the factors that come after. Dividing again by the factorial of k gives:

    C(n,k)=n!k!×(n−k)!

    In our scene, the factorial of n is 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 120. There are n − k = 2 packets outside the set; the factorial of this number is 2. The full denominator is 6 × 2 = 12.

    C(5,3)=1206×2=10

    If k = 0, the only choice is to take nothing; if k = n, the only choice is to take everything. In both cases, the number of sets is 1, and the factorial convention for the empty list keeps the formula valid. If the requested size exceeds n, no set without repetition is possible; we do not use the factorial of a negative integer.

  6. Check by counting another way

    Instead of choosing the 3 packets in the set, let us choose the 2 packets that will remain on the shelf. Knowing which ones remain determines which ones leave, without ambiguity. This is the correspondence between a choice and its complement, meaning the set of objects that were not chosen.

    The second diagram lists these pairs: with A, 4 later partners; with B, 3; with C, 2; with D, 1. E has no later partner. We get 4 + 3 = 7, then 7 + 2 = 9, then 9 + 1 = 10 pairs. Every pair appears and none is reversed.

    We recover the 10 sets without repeating the division for lists of size k. The same reasoning gives C(n, k) = C(n, n − k). This does not mean that a set and its complement contain the same packets: their numbers of possibilities are equal.

    If each packet were given a different role depending on its position, reversed lists could become different outcomes. If the same packet could be reused, the allowed lists would also change. Before applying the formula, we must therefore identify the assumptions of the scene.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain what the numerator and divisor count, then justify why ADE and ABC have the same number of written representations. Then recover the total using the packets left out. Reciting a result without these justifications is not enough.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “ABC and CBA are different sets because I did not take them in the same order.”

    Empty the lists into the imaginary planter: the packets present are the same. Here, no role depends on their position. The 6 written representations in the first diagram form only 1 set.

    Review the representations grouped around the same contents

  • “I divide the 60 lists by 3, since a set contains 3 packets.”

    The divisor must count the representations of a choice of contents, rather than its elements. List those for ABC: there are 6, which is the factorial of k. The proposed division would give 20 instead of 10; it still leaves duplicate counts.

    Rebuild the group for ABC, then repeat the division

  • “If A is required, I still choose the other packets from all 5 available packets.”

    A already occupies a position and cannot be taken again. Remove it from the available set: 4 packets remain for the 2 free positions. Without removing it, you allow a forbidden repetition.

    Solve the problem where the set must contain A

Hint 1

After the first packet, 4 choices remain. AB and BA have the same contents.

Hint 2

To list the pairs without duplicates, write each pair in alphabetical order.

Compare with the solution

The number of ordered lists is 5 × 4 = 20. A choice of 2 packets has 2 × 1 = 2 orders. This leaves 20/2 = 10 sets.

The complete list is AB, AC, AD, AE, BC, BD, BE, CD, CE, DE. It contains 10 sets and none repeats a packet. Alphabetical order is a rule for writing the pairs to avoid duplicates, rather than a new constraint on their contents.

The total is also the number of sets of 3 packets in the example: each chosen pair corresponds to its complement. This explains the equality beyond simply obtaining the same numerical result.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish the 20 lists from the 10 choices of contents.
  • Justify the divisor 2 using AB and BA.
  • Provide the complete list, without a repeated pair or a packet taken several times.

Exercise 2 · Application · check by separating cases

A packet F arrives on the shelf

Add a distinct packet F: you have 6 packets and still choose 3, without regard to order and without repetition. Calculate the new number of sets. Check it by separating the sets that contain F from those that do not.

Hint 1

The number of orders of a set has not changed; the number of first choices has changed.

Hint 2

A set containing F is determined by the 2 packets that accompany it from among A, B, C, D and E.

Compare with the solution

The number of ordered lists is 6 × 5 × 4 = 120. Each choice of contents still has 6 orders. The quotient gives 120/6 = 20 sets.

Without F, we recover the 10 sets from the example. With F, we choose its 2 accompanying packets from the 5 original packets: the previous exercise gives 10 possibilities. These cases do not overlap and cover all the sets.

The check by addition gives 10 + 10 = 20, equal to the quotient result. The change stays within the same scene: only the number of packets on the shelf has increased.

Check your reasoning

  • Use 6, 5 and 4 for the successive choices, then divide by 6.
  • Obtain 20 sets.
  • Explain why the cases with F and without F are disjoint and cover all possibilities.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · change the free choices

The set must contain A

Return to the original shelf A, B, C, D, E. The set keeps its size 3, but A must be included. Someone announces 20/2 = 10 sets, because they choose the other packets from the original 5. Identify the flaw, calculate the correct number and check it with a complete list.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Put A aside before any calculation: it is already chosen and cannot accompany itself.

Hint 2

Choose only the 2 freely chosen accompanying packets from B, C, D and E; their orders do not distinguish the sets.

Compare with the solution

The proposal keeps A in the set of available accompanying packets. It therefore allows, for example, the pair AB to accompany A, which would reuse A. First remove the required packet: 4 packets remain for 2 positions.

We count 4 × 3 = 12 lists of accompanying packets. Each pair of accompanying packets has 2 orders. The result is 12/2 = 6 sets.

The sets are ABC, ABD, ABE, ACD, ACE, ADE: they all contain A and no packet is repeated. This list confirms the 6 possibilities. The divisor concerns only the free positions; the fixed packet is no longer being permuted in this calculation.

Saying that A “is chosen first” serves to organise the reasoning here. It does not give A a different role in the final set. The contents remain the only thing that distinguishes the outcomes.

Check your reasoning

  • Reduce both the available set and the number of positions still free.
  • Explain the forbidden repetition that the proposal allowed.
  • Find 6 sets and list them without duplicates.
  • Justify why we divide by the factorial of the number of freely chosen accompanying packets.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which assumptions about the shelf make our choice a combination?

The packets are distinct, the size of the set is fixed, no packet is taken again and their order plays no role. All choices of contents of this size are allowed. ABC and CBA therefore denote the same set.

Recall 2Why divide by the factorial of k rather than by k?

The factorial of k counts the orders of a choice of k distinct packets. The lists fall into groups of this size, with one group per choice of contents. For ABC, the 6 orders correspond to just 1 set; k only counts the packets.

Recall 3Reconstruct C(n, k) and explain the role of its factors, without simply reciting the quotient.

n counts the available packets and k counts those in the set, with integers such that 0 ≤ k ≤ n. Divide the factorial of n by the factorial of n − k to count the lists of size k without repetition. Divide again by the factorial of k to treat the orders of the same contents as equivalent: this is C(n, k).

Recall 4Why does choosing 3 packets from 5 give as many possibilities as leaving 2?

Each choice determines exactly the packets not chosen, and conversely. Thus C(n, k) = C(n, n − k). In our scene, the 10 pairs left out correspond to the 10 sets of 3 packets, even though the chosen contents and those left out differ.

Recall 5How many sets are there when taking nothing, taking everything or requesting more packets than exist?

There is 1 empty choice and 1 choice containing the entire shelf. The convention that the factorial of 0 is 1 keeps the formula valid at these boundaries. If the size exceeds n, there are 0 choices without repetition: we do not apply the formula with a negative factorial.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the results, reconstruct the reasoning: define the contents that matter, write the lists without repetition, justify why the factorial of k counts the orders of a choice of contents, then divide. Return to the shelf of 5 packets and the sets of 3, and check the total by counting the packets left behind. Finish by naming a rule change that would prevent this calculation from being applied directly.

What you understand
