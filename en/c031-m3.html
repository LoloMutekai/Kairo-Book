Level 1 · C031·M3

Continuity

Your goalDecide whether a gauge is continuous at the threshold where it reaches its cap, by comparing its limit with its exact value.

Before you start
c006-m5 : composition · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c011-m5 : binomial · c014-m5 : invariants · c031-m1 : limit of a sequence · c031-m2 : limit of a function

✿01 See the idea

You are filling a tank with straight vertical sides. In our model, each litre added raises the water level by 2 cm. The gauge displays this height up to 10 cm, then stays at this cap. As the volume passes 5 L, its graph has a corner: does this mean there is a break?

Just below the threshold, the display is almost at the cap. At the threshold, it reaches the cap. Just above, it stays there. A change of rule does not necessarily create a jump. Continuity requires nearby displayed values to approach the displayed value at the point under study, to any desired degree of accuracy.

Now consider the same gauge with a data entry error: at exactly 5 L, it displays 12 cm; everywhere else it keeps the previous rule. Nearby values remain close to 10 cm. The isolated point no longer meets them: the modified function is discontinuous at the threshold.

The tank, rules and values are fictional. We assume an ideal display, with no rounding, delay or noise, for volumes from 0 to 8 L; the tank can hold all these volumes. The cap applies to the gauge, rather than to the capacity of the tank. A real digital display may have steps caused by rounding.

The rule to remember

At the point under study, check that the value is defined, that the limit exists and that it equals this value. A finite limit alone is not enough.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Check how the gauge joins at 5 L

The normal gauge multiplies the volume by 2 cm/L, then caps the display at H = 10 cm. Let us find its limit at volume a = 5 L, its exact value at this point and the connection between them. The calculations below use numerical values expressed in the stated units.

  1. Name the rule and the point to study

    v denotes the volume in litres; h(v), the displayed height in centimetres. The coefficient c = 2 cm/L converts volume to height. H = 10 cm is the cap and a = 5 L is the volume where the rules join. The domain D is the interval [0 ; 8] L; a is inside this interval.

    The minimum of two numbers, written min, is the smaller of the two. Our rule is h(v) = min(cv, H). It takes cv when cv is less than H, and H as soon as cv reaches or exceeds H. Since ca = 2 × 5 = 10 cm, the rules meet exactly at a.

    h(v)={cvsi 0≤v≤aHsi a<v≤8

  2. Separate the value at the point from the limit

    For a real function f defined at the point a, continuity at a means that its limit as v tends to a is f(a). The symbol lim denotes this limit; the arrow v → a means that v approaches a, with v different from a when finding the limit. The approach uses values from the domain D.

    f continue en a⇔limv→af(v)=f(a)

    The symbol ⇔ is read as “is equivalent to”. The limit must exist as a real number. A function that is undefined at a cannot be continuous at a. A function is continuous on an interval if it is continuous at each of its points, approaching through that interval. Here, we only prove continuity at the joining point a.

    The value h(a) is calculated by substituting a for v: min(2 × 5, 10) = min(10, 10) = 10 cm. This establishes the value at the point; the limit still needs justification.

  3. Try nearby values to guide the reasoning

    Take an offset of 0.1 L on either side of the threshold. Below: v = 5 − 0.1 = 4.9 L. The uncapped height is 2 × 4.9 = 9.8 cm; it is less than H, so h(v) = 9.8 cm. The distance from the cap is 10 − 9.8 = 0.2 cm.

    Above: v = 5 + 0.1 = 5.1 L. The product is 10.2 cm, above the cap; the minimum gives h(v) = 10 cm. The distance from the cap is 0 cm.

    Closer below the threshold: 5 − 0.001 = 4.999 L, so h(v) = 9.998 cm and the distance from the cap is now only 0.002 cm. These trials suggest the limit 10 cm. Even a very large number of trials does not check all volumes arbitrarily close to the threshold: let us use a relation that holds for each of them.

  4. Justify the limit using a distance we can control

    The bars |…| give the absolute value, which here is a nonnegative distance. If v ≤ a, then h(v) = cv and H = ca: |h(v) − H| = c|v − a|. If v > a, the display is H and the distance is 0 cm. In every case within the domain, we obtain the same bound:

    |h(v)−H|≤c|v−a|

    Let e be a strictly positive tolerance for the display, in centimetres. Since c is positive, choosing a volume at a distance strictly less than e/c litres from a is enough to put the display at a distance strictly less than e centimetres from H. This choice is possible for every tolerance e, however small. This justifies the limit beyond the numerical trials.

    limv→ah(v)=H=10 cm

  5. Compare, conclude and check the units

    The limit is 10 cm and h(a) is 10 cm: the normal gauge is continuous at a = 5 L. The corner reflects a change of rule, but the values meet. The curve does not need to keep the same slope.

    Check: c|v − a| is in (cm/L) × L, hence in cm, like |h(v) − H|. As the distance in volume decreases, the bound on height can be made as small as desired. At exactly a, the difference is 0. All three observations agree.

  6. Test the effect of a single incorrect point

    Call the faulty gauge in the second drawing g. It gives g(v) = h(v) for v ≠ a and g(a) = 12 cm. The symbol ≠ means “is different from”. Near a, but away from a itself, the displayed values have not changed: the limit of g remains H = 10 cm.

    limv→ag(v)=10 cm≠g(a)=12 cm

    The distance between the value at the point and the limit is 12 − 10 = 2 cm. The function g is therefore discontinuous at a. The fault can be repaired by changing only g(a), giving it the value 10 cm. Changing this point would still leave the limit unchanged; it would restore equality between the limit and the value at the point.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the steps. Explain why the corner of h is continuous and why the displaced point of g is not. Then reconstruct the distance argument: what must you bring closer, and by how much, to impose a given accuracy on the display?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “There is a corner, so the function is discontinuous.”

    Continuity compares values, rather than slopes. At the threshold, the limit and the value of h are both 10 cm. Repeat the distance check instead of judging the shape of the corner.

    Review the justification of the limit and the conclusion

  • “The limit exists, so the function is continuous.”

    The gauge g has a limit of 10 cm, but g(a) is 12 cm. Write these two facts separately, then compare them. If the value at the point is missing, you likewise cannot conclude that the function is continuous there.

    Review the open circle and the filled point

  • “A few very close values are enough to prove continuity.”

    These values help you explore the behaviour. To conclude, you must control all sufficiently close volumes and check the exact value at the threshold. The bound c|v − a| provides this control; the gauge g is a reminder that an isolated point can escape a series of trials.

    Try imposing a given accuracy in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Choose the correct rule

Using the normal gauge h, calculate the displayed values at volumes 4.8 L, 5 L and 5.2 L. Why does the product cv not always give the displayed value? Do these results alone prove continuity?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate cv for each volume, then compare this product with the cap H.

Hint 2

The minimum takes the smaller value. Separate this calculation from the justification of a limit.

Compare with the solution

At 4.8 L: 2 × 4.8 = 9.6 cm, less than 10 cm; h is therefore 9.6 cm. At 5 L: the product is 10 cm, exactly the cap.

At 5.2 L: the product is 10.4 cm, but the minimum takes 10 cm. The cap changes the display, rather than the volume in the tank.

These results illustrate how the rules join. They do not prove continuity on their own: control over all sufficiently close values is still missing. In the example, the bound on the distance provides this control.

Check your reasoning

  • Give the displayed values 9.6, 10 and 10 cm in order.
  • Justify the choice of the minimum above the threshold.
  • Distinguish numerical trials from a proof of a limit.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis and repair

Correct a single entry

The faulty gauge g keeps h(v) at every volume other than 5 L, but displays 12 cm at exactly this volume. You may change only this value. Which value makes the gauge continuous at the threshold? Justify why it is the required value.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The limit depends on values around the threshold, without using the value at the threshold.

Hint 2

Repairing this value alone must make it equal to the limit already found.

Compare with the solution

For v different from a, g and h have the same values. Their limit at a is therefore the same: 10 cm. The current value 12 cm is at a distance of 2 cm from it: g is discontinuous at a.

We must set g(a) = 10 cm. The limit does not change and becomes equal to the new value at the point. Any other value would leave the two numbers different; this repair is therefore the only possible one if no nearby value changes.

Check your reasoning

  • State the limit before proposing the repair.
  • Find 10 cm and explain the restored equality.
  • Justify uniqueness under the constraint that only one point may change.

Exercise 3 · Independent justification

Guarantee accuracy before choosing a volume

For the normal gauge h, the distance from the cap must be strictly less than 0.02 cm. What strict upper bound on the distance from volume a = 5 L is sufficient, whatever volume is chosen on either side of the threshold? Use the bound from the example. Also check what happens at volume 4.99 L: can this boundary be included when the requirement is strict?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The distance in height is at most c times the distance in volume. Divide the tolerance in cm by c in cm/L.

Hint 2

At volume 4.99 L, calculate the height and then its distance from the cap. Equality does not satisfy “strictly less than”.

Compare with the solution

The bound gives |h(v) − H| ≤ c|v − a|. The tolerance is exactly 1/50 cm. Dividing it by c = 2 cm/L gives 1/100 L, or 0.01 L. Requiring |v − a| < 0.01 L therefore guarantees |h(v) − H| < 0.02 cm for every volume that meets this condition.

At volume 4.99 L, h(v) = 9.98 cm. The distance is 10 − 9.98 = 0.02 cm, exactly the tolerance. We therefore cannot replace the strict condition with a distance less than or equal to 0.01 L if we want to guarantee the requirement for all the volumes concerned.

The condition is sufficient and symmetric about the threshold. It is not necessary: above the threshold, the display stays exactly at the cap, even for some volumes farther away. The guarantee applies to h; the incorrect point of g does not meet the same requirement.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 0.01 L by dividing compatible units.
  • Justify a guarantee for all volumes allowed by the condition.
  • Exclude the boundary because of the equality, without presenting the sufficient condition as necessary.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which checks let you conclude that a function is continuous at a?

The function is defined at a, its limit at a exists as a real number and this limit equals its value f(a). The approaches stay within the domain of the function.

Recall 2Why does the corner where the gauge h reaches its cap not destroy continuity?

Nearby values tend to H = 10 cm and h(a) equals this same number. A change in slope alone does not create a break in values.

Recall 3Why can moving only the value at the threshold preserve the limit and destroy continuity?

The limit uses nearby values, for v different from a. The isolated change leaves them intact, but can make g(a) different from the limit: here 12 cm instead of 10 cm.

Recall 4How does the bound |h(v) − H| ≤ c|v − a| justify the limit of the normal gauge?

For any positive tolerance e in cm, a distance in volume less than e/c litres ensures a distance in height less than e. The accuracy can be as fine as desired, which a finite list of trials does not guarantee.
