Level 2 · C046·M1

Series tests

Your goalDecide whether positive additions can have a finite total, choose a suitable test and recognise when it is inconclusive.

Before you start
c031-m5 : asymptotes · c045-m5 : introductory quadrature

✿01 See the idea

On a table, you extend a ribbon by gluing pieces end to end. The first measures 8 cm; each subsequent piece is half the length of the previous one. You therefore lay down 8 cm, then 4 cm, then 2 cm, then 1 cm. Can the ribbon stay within a fixed length if you continue the rule indefinitely?

The first diagram separates the size of the new piece from the length already assembled. The piece gets smaller, but the running total increases. These are two different sequences. A series studies successive running totals: it converges if these totals tend to a finite length.

The second diagram changes only the cutting rule for the same ribbon: the piece at position k measures 8/k cm, counting from 1. Each piece still gets smaller and smaller. Yet groups of pieces always contribute at least 4 cm; the running total eventually exceeds any fixed length.

This scene and its lengths are fictional. Adding pieces indefinitely is a mathematical idealisation, with no thickness of glue or minimum cutting size. Here we study nonnegative real terms; a rule alternating additions and removals would require further care.

The rule to remember

Observe the running total, then justify its behaviour using an identity or a comparison valid for the entire remaining sequence. Small pieces, or a few numerical trials, do not prove that the total is finite.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Decide whether the ribbon stays finite in length

Keep the first piece a = 8 cm and the dimensionless factor r = 1/2. Calculate the running total, prove its limit, then examine what changes when the cutting rule is replaced. All conclusions concern the whole rule, rather than just the pieces drawn.

  1. Name the piece and the running total

    n is an integer starting at 0: it is the index of the piece, whose length is un. N is the last index retained; Sn denotes the running total up to index n. An index has no unit. a, un and Sn are in centimetres; r is the ratio between successive lengths. The power rⁿ means applying the factor r a total of n times, with r to the power 0 equal to 1.

    un=arn;SN=∑n=0Nun

    The symbol ∑ instructs you to add the terms for all the indicated indices: Sn contains n + 1 pieces. The series, written ∑un, studies this sequence of partial sums. It converges if Sn tends to a finite real number S as n increases without bound; S is then its sum. The symbol ∞ in a summation bound means continuing indefinitely in this way.

  2. Calculate the first running totals and distinguish them from the terms

    Each multiplication gives the length of the next piece: 8 × 1/2 = 4 cm; 4 × 1/2 = 2 cm; 2 × 1/2 = 1 cm. For the ribbon already assembled, add the lengths: 8 + 4 = 12 cm; 12 + 2 = 14 cm; 14 + 1 = 15 cm.

    After 4 pieces, the last addition is 1 cm and the running total is 15 cm. Since the first index is 0, this total is S at index 3, rather than index 4. This calculation checks the diagram, without yet proving a limit.

  3. Start with a finite sum, then take the limit

    Write Sn = a + ar + … + arⁿ. Multiplying this equality by r shifts each power: the first term becomes ar and the last becomes a multiplied by r to the power n + 1. In the difference, the common terms cancel. This gives the following identity for every integer N, before taking any limit at infinity.

    (1−r)SN=a(1−rN+1)

    For 0 ≤ r < 1, r to the power N + 1 tends to 0 and 1 − r is strictly positive. Dividing, then taking the limit, gives S = a/(1 − r). Here 1 − 1/2 = 1/2, so S = 8/(1/2) = 16 cm.

    Independent check: the complete ribbon consists of the first piece a and a copy of the whole ribbon scaled down by r. The candidate value satisfies S = a + rS: 16 = 8 + 1/2 × 16 cm. This check confirms the value; the finite identity and its limit established the existence of S.

  4. Recognise that a necessary condition is insufficient

    If the running totals converge to S, the difference of two successive totals tends to S − S = 0: from index 1 onwards, un is Sn minus the total at index n − 1. A convergent series must therefore have terms tending to 0. If they do not, it diverges. But the converse implication is false.

    For the same ribbon, replace the cutting rule with hn = a/(n + 1). hn is still in centimetres and tends to 0. Call k = n + 1 the position, counted from 1. The first piece measures a. Position 2 adds 4 cm. Positions 3 and 4 add 8/3 + 2 = 14/3 cm, hence at least 4 cm.

    For every integer j ≥ 1, take the following group of positions k:

    2j−1+1≤k≤2j

    The number of pieces is 2 to the power (j − 1). Each measures at least a divided by 2 to the power j. Their total is therefore at least a/2 = 4 cm. The groups are consecutive, with no omissions or repetitions. After m groups, where m is a positive integer, the running total is at least a + m × a/2. This lower bound increases without bound: the series diverges to +∞, despite hn → 0. This rule gives a multiple of the harmonic series.

  5. Use nonnegative terms to compare series

    Adding nonnegative terms makes the partial sums nondecreasing. A principle about real numbers states that a nondecreasing sequence with a fixed upper bound has a finite limit. Here, “bounded above” means that all running totals stay below the same length, independently of the number of additions.

    Let vn and wn be two nonnegative lengths. If 0 ≤ vn ≤ wn from some index m onwards and ∑wn converges, the running totals of vn are also bounded above: ∑vn converges. Conversely, if 0 ≤ wn ≤ vn and the running totals of wn increase without bound, so do those of vn. The pieces before m form a finite sum; they do not change this conclusion.

    For example, any rule vn between 0 and a rⁿ, for our r = 1/2, produces a ribbon of finite length. You can prove that the total exists without knowing its exact value. However, being smaller than a divergent rule settles nothing.

  6. Recognise a geometric comparison using the ratio

    Now assume that un is strictly positive from some index onwards, so that division is possible. Calculate the limit ℓ of the ratio of u at index n + 1 to un. This ratio and ℓ have no unit. The ratio test, also called d’Alembert’s test, gives these conclusions: ℓ < 1 implies convergence; ℓ > 1 implies divergence; ℓ = 1 is inconclusive. Here we assume that this real limit exists.

    ℓ=limn→∞un+1un

    Why the threshold 1? If ℓ < 1, choose a number q strictly between ℓ and 1. From some index m onwards, all ratios are less than q: u at index m + j is at most um qʲ, for every integer j ≥ 0. This gives a comparison with a convergent geometric series. If ℓ > 1, the ratios are eventually greater than a fixed number greater than 1; the pieces no longer tend to 0. When ℓ = 1, neither argument works.

  7. Apply the correct conclusion to our cutting rules

    For un = a rⁿ, the ratios are exactly r = 1/2 = 0.50 at every index: they are strictly below 1, so the test confirms convergence. The sum found earlier is 16 cm; the test alone does not give this value.

    For the cutting rule hn = a/(n + 1), the ratio is (n + 1)/(n + 2) and tends to 1. Although each ratio is less than 1, the limit is at the boundary: the test is inconclusive. The groups of pieces proved divergence. A ratio limit equal to 1 therefore does not imply divergence. The last exercise will give a convergent cutting rule with the same ratio limit.

  8. Check the scope of the conclusion

    For the first cutting rule, the running totals 8, 12, 14, 15 cm increase and stay below 16 cm, as the finite identity predicts. The terms 8, 4, 2, 1 cm decrease: this check clearly distinguishes the sequence of terms from the sequence of running totals.

    The conclusion concerns the existence of a finite sum for positive lengths. It measures neither assembly time nor the accuracy of stopping after a few pieces. Establishing convergence does not by itself tell you how many terms to calculate.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The piece tends to 0, so the total converges.”

    This condition is necessary, but insufficient. With a/(n + 1), the pieces tend to 0, but each group formed by doubling the positions adds at least 4 cm. Reconstruct this lower bound instead of looking only at the last piece.

    Revisit the groups for the harmonic cutting rule

  • “The ratio stays below one: the test has given a conclusion.”

    The test requires a limit strictly less than 1. For a/(n + 1), the ratios are below 1 but approach it. What is missing is a fixed bound q strictly below 1, valid for all ratios sufficiently far along.

    Compare the conclusions in the last exercise

  • “My rule is smaller than a divergent series, so it also diverges.”

    A divergent upper bound settles nothing. To prove convergence, look for a convergent upper bound; to prove divergence for positive terms, look for a lower bound whose running totals increase without bound. Write the inequality in the correct direction.

Exercise 1 · Numerical application

The pieces shrink more slowly

The ribbon still starts with a = 8 cm, but each new piece is 3/4 of the previous one. Before calculating, predict whether the total will be greater than 16 cm. Give the first 3 pieces, their running total and the sum of the series, with a justification of convergence.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The ratio is constant. Compare it with 1 before calculating the sum.

Hint 2

The running total of the first 3 pieces has index 2; the infinite sum comes from a/(1 − r).

Compare with the solution

The reduction is smaller, so the pieces after the first are longer than under the halving rule. The ratio is 3/4 = 0.75, strictly below 1: the series converges.

Pieces: 8 cm; 8 × 3/4 = 6 cm; 6 × 3/4 = 9/2 cm, or 4.50 cm. Running total: 8 + 6 = 14 cm, then 14 + 9/2 = 37/2 cm, or 18.50 cm.

Sum: 1 − 3/4 = 1/4; 8/(1/4) = 32 cm. It is indeed greater than 16 cm. Check: 8 + 3/4 × 32 = 32 cm. The finite running total 18.50 cm and the limiting total 32 cm answer different questions.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify convergence using the constant ratio strictly below 1.
  • Obtain 9/2 cm for the last piece requested and 37/2 cm for the running total.
  • Give 32 cm for the sum and check the direction of its change.

Exercise 2 · Choose and justify a test

An irregular cutting rule with a bound

The cutting rule now varies without a regular ratio. Its lengths vₙ are nonnegative and satisfy vₙ ≤ a(1/2)ⁿ from some index m onwards. The preceding pieces have finite lengths. Can you conclude convergence? Can you state that the sum is 16 cm? Then examine the weaker claim: vₙ ≤ a/(n + 1).

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A comparison may apply only to the part after m. Separate the finite beginning from the rest.

Hint 2

An inequality gives a bound, rather than an equality. The second upper bound is the one whose groups showed divergence.

Compare with the solution

Yes under the first assumption. After m, the running totals of vn are bounded above by those of the geometric cutting rule; they are nondecreasing and bounded. The finite beginning adds a fixed length: the whole series converges. No ratio needs to exist.

The sum is undetermined. If the inequality held from index 0, it would be at most 16 cm; if it holds only from m onwards, the beginning can even exceed this reference value. In either case, the comparison does not give an equality.

With only the upper bound a/(n + 1), no conclusion is possible: the rule vn = 0 has a sum of 0, whereas the rule vn = a/(n + 1) diverges. Both satisfy this new bound. The assumptions are therefore compatible with both behaviours.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify nonnegativity and the convergent geometric upper bound.
  • Explain why a finite beginning does not change convergence.
  • Reject an exact sum and provide counterexamples for the divergent upper bound.

Exercise 3 · Proof and objection

The same ratio limit, opposite conclusions

Compare the cutting rules hₙ = a/(n + 1) and bₙ = a/[(n + 1)(n + 2)], still with a = 8 cm and n starting at 0. Show that both ratios tend to 1. Then decide whether each series converges using another method. For bₙ, look for a difference of consecutive fractions.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In the ratio of b at index n + 1 to bₙ, a factor n + 2 cancels.

Hint 2

Put 1/(n + 1) − 1/(n + 2) over a common denominator.

Hint 3

Write the finite sum of the differences: the interior fractions cancel. For hₙ, revisit the groups, without using the ratio to reach a conclusion.

Compare with the solution

For hn, the ratio is (n + 1)/(n + 2). For bn, it is (n + 1)/(n + 3). Dividing numerator and denominator by n for positive n shows that both limits equal 1. The ratio test is inconclusive in both cases.

For hn, the groups formed by doubling the positions each contribute at least a/2 = 4 cm. After m groups, the running total is greater than or equal to a + m × a/2: it tends to +∞. The series diverges.

For bn, using a common denominator gives bn = a[1/(n + 1) − 1/(n + 2)]. The first pieces are 4 cm and 4/3 cm; their sum is 16/3 cm. Call Bn their general running total. Expanding the differences up to index N cancels everything except the first positive term and the last negative term:

BN=a(1−1N+2)→a

The last reciprocal tends to 0; the running totals therefore converge to a = 8 cm. This cancellation of successive differences is called a telescoping sum. Check: for N = 1, the formula gives 8 × (1 − 1/3) = 16/3 cm again. The same “inconclusive” ratio test result has thus given way to proofs of opposite results.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate both ratios and state their limit 1 without deducing convergence from it.
  • Justify the divergence of hₙ using a fixed lower bound for each group.
  • Show the cancellations in Bₙ and its limit 8 cm.
  • State that an inconclusive test is not a negative result about the series.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must converge for a series to have a sum?

The partial sums Sₙ, obtained by adding all the terms up to index n, must tend to a finite real number S. The term uₙ tending to 0 is insufficient on its own.

Recall 2Why must uₙ tend to 0, and why does the converse fail?

If the running totals tend to S, uₙ is the difference of two successive totals and tends to S − S = 0. But a/(n + 1) tends to 0 while giving groups that each add at least a/2: the series diverges.

Recall 3In which direction should you compare positive terms to prove convergence or divergence?

For convergence, have terms bounded above from some index onwards by those of a convergent positive series. For divergence to +∞, have terms bounded below by those of a positive series whose running totals increase without bound. Being below a divergent series settles nothing.

Recall 4What assumptions and conclusions should you remember for the ratio test?

The terms must be strictly positive sufficiently far along, and the ratio of successive terms must have a real limit ℓ. If ℓ < 1, the series converges; if ℓ > 1, it diverges; if ℓ = 1, the test is inconclusive. Then look for another proof.

Recall 5How can you justify the geometric sum without starting by manipulating an infinite sum?

Subtracting rSₙ from Sₙ for the finite sum gives (1 − r)Sₙ = a multiplied by [1 − r to the power (n + 1)]. For 0 ≤ r < 1, this power tends to 0; the sum is then a/(1 − r).
