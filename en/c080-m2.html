Level 1 · C080·M2

Membrane

Your goalDraw the organization of a membrane, explain its selective permeability, and check a molecule balance without inventing the cause of the crossings.

Before you start
c079-m5 : function and mechanism · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c080-m1 : biomolecules

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a sealed bag of water immersed in a small water bath that is also sealed. The wall of the bag is a lipid membrane; this bag is called a vesicle. It defines an inside and an outside, even when some molecules cross it. We will follow this same vesicle through to the exercises.

The bath contains a small, neutral molecule called A and a large hydrophilic marker called B. Neutral here means having no net electric charge; hydrophilic means interacting favorably with water. A is chosen because it can also dissolve in the lipid core of this membrane. B has no route across under the conditions of our scene. The boundary therefore keeps B out while allowing A through: this is selective permeability.

Why this difference? A phospholipid has a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tails, for which contact with water is unfavorable. In a bilayer, 2 leaflets face each other: the heads touch the water outside or inside; the tails cluster in the middle. A can cross this core. A large, highly hydrophilic molecule such as B crosses it with great difficulty here; we neglect its crossings during the interval studied. A membrane is therefore not a mesh whose hole size is all that matters.

A cell membrane also contains proteins. Some span it and provide routes across for specific substances. A channel is such a route through a protein; its presence does not make the membrane permeable to every substance. Our simplified vesicle contains no transport proteins. It helps us understand the bilayer and the separation of compartments; it does not represent a whole neuron.

The scene and all its numbers are fictional. The small numbers are used for counting and do not represent a real cell. The drawings are not to scale; real lipids and many proteins can move laterally in the membrane. The balance calculation that follows uses given crossings without calculating their rate or explaining their direction.

The rule to remember

To understand a membrane, identify the environments it separates, the organization of its lipids, and the substance being considered. To check an exchange that has already been described, count entries, exits, and what remains on each side separately.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A boundary that allows exchange while remaining intact

In our closed system, the vesicle initially contains 6 molecules of A and the outside bath contains 14. A fictional record gives 5 inward crossings of A and 2 outward crossings during the same interval. Let us find the final counts and check the balance. A and B denote different substances; only A molecules are counted.

  1. Set the boundary and assumptions

    The system includes the bag and its entire outside bath. It is closed to molecules of A. The membrane remains intact; no reaction creates or destroys A, and no molecule of A is held in the membrane at the start or end. Every A molecule then belongs either to the inside or to the outside. We do not calculate water movements.

    We assume the record of crossings is complete. It indicates what crossed, rather than what merely touched the membrane. The same molecule of A can cross and then return: a number of crossings is not necessarily a number of distinct molecules. What if the final number of A molecules in the membrane were no longer negligible? We would need to add that compartment to the balance.

  2. Define each symbol

    I and O denote the initial counts of A inside and outside, in molecules of A. I′ and O′ are the final counts; the prime means “after the interval.” E counts inward crossings, from outside to inside; S counts outward crossings. Each crossing adds or removes 1 molecule from the inside count.

    ΔI, read as “change in I,” is the signed change in the inside count, in molecules of A. A positive value means an inside gain; a negative value means a loss. T is the total count of A in the system, in molecules. E and S are counts over an interval, with no time unit in the denominator: we do not call them rates.

    ΔI=E−S;I′=I+ΔI;O′=O−ΔI

    The signs come from the chosen boundary: an entry into the bag is an exit from the bath. These relations count the given movements; they are not a law for predicting which crossings will occur.

  3. Calculate the inside change

    The 5 entries increase the inside count. The 2 exits decrease it. We therefore subtract exits from entries rather than adding all the crossings.

    ΔI=5−2=3 molécules de A

    The record contains 5 + 2 = 7 crossings in total, but the net inside gain is only 3 molecules of A. The sum tells us the number of events; the difference tells us the change in population.

  4. Update the compartments

    Inside, we add the net gain to what was already present. Outside, we subtract exactly this gain: the molecules have not been created.

    I′=6+3=9 molécules de A
    O′=14−3=11 molécules de A

    Another way to find the inside count: 6 + 5 − 2 = 11 − 2 = 9 molecules of A. Grouping entries before exits in this way makes the calculation easier; it does not reconstruct the actual order in the record.

  5. Check the result and limit the conclusion

    Initially, T = 6 + 14 = 20 molecules of A. At the end, 9 + 11 = 20 molecules of A. The total is conserved and each count remains positive. The gain of the bag, 9 − 6 = 3 molecules, is the loss of the bath, 14 − 11 = 3 molecules.

    I′+O′=I+O=T

    This check can reveal an omission or an incorrect sign. It does not prove that every event was recorded correctly: errors could cancel out. The balance is consistent with the account of A crossing the membrane. On its own, it does not identify the route taken. In our scene, direct crossing of the lipid core is a stated assumption, rather than a mechanism discovered by addition.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculation. Redraw the boundary, mark the directions of entries and exits, then explain why the same change is added to the bag and subtracted from the bath. Where does the assumption of a closed system come in?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The tails touch the water and the heads hide in the center.”

    Start again from the affinities: the heads interact favorably with water; the hydrophobic tails cluster in the lipid core. Draw the water on each side before placing the 2 leaflets. The cross section does not enclose a layer of water in the middle of the bilayer.

    Place heads and tails on the cross section again

  • “A membrane is a sieve: everything small passes through equally.”

    Size is not enough. You must also consider charge, interactions with water and with the lipid core, and the available protein routes. Charged particles, called ions, cross the lipid core alone with great difficulty. In the chosen vesicle, A can pass through and B is kept out during the interval; we do not generalize this result to every substance.

    Review the crossing conditions for A and B

  • “7 crossings mean 7 molecules gained inside.”

    Mark the direction of each crossing. The 5 entries add molecules; the 2 exits remove them. The gain is 3 molecules of A. The 7 crossings may even include the same molecule going across and returning.

    Review the signed balance and its check

Exercise 1 · Recognize and explain

Draw the correct boundary

On paper, draw a local cross section of the membrane of our vesicle. Place the water inside, the water outside, the heads, and the tails. Explain why A can cross while B is kept out in our scene. Does a difference in size alone provide the full explanation?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with the aqueous environments, then place what interacts favorably with water.

Hint 2

The core is lipid. Recall the given properties of A and marker B, and the absence of transport proteins.

Compare with the solution

The hydrophilic heads border the aqueous environments. The hydrophobic tails face each other in the center. It takes 2 leaflets to form the bilayer; the water inside is not between the tails.

A is small, neutral, and able to dissolve in this lipid core. B is large and hydrophilic; no suitable route across is present in our model. We neglect its crossings over the interval. The reasoning combines the nature of the substance and that of the membrane. Saying only “A is smaller” therefore does not explain the whole mechanism.

Check your reasoning

  • The heads touch water on the outside and inside; the tails are in the center.
  • You link the crossing of A to its stated properties and to the lipid core.
  • You present the retention of B as a condition of this scene, without making an absolute rule for all membranes.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and interpretation

Unchanged counts with crossings occurring

Start again from the initial counts: 6 A inside and 14 outside. In a variant of the same fictional interval, the record gives 5 entries and 5 exits. Calculate the inside change, the final counts, the total number of crossings, and the total count of A. Can we say that nothing crossed?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For the inside change, exits have the opposite sign to entries.

Hint 2

The total number of crossings is found using a different operation. Events can cancel out in a balance.

Compare with the solution

The change is 5 − 5 = 0 molecules of A. The inside contains 6 + 0 = 6 molecules of A; the outside contains 14 − 0 = 14.

The number of crossings is 5 + 5 = 10. Crossings therefore occur despite the 0 net balance. The total remains 6 + 14 = 20 molecules of A. Equal counts of entries and exits over this interval do not mean that the molecules were motionless or that the counts will remain equal afterward.

Check your reasoning

  • You distinguish 0 molecules of net change from 10 crossings.
  • You recover the initial counts and conserve the total of 20 molecules of A.
  • You explain why a constant count does not prove that no crossings occurred.

Exercise 3 · Reconstruction and limits of evidence

Find the missing exits

The same vesicle, the same starting point: 6 A inside and 14 outside. The record for another variant mentions 5 entries; the final inside count gives 4 molecules of A. Under the assumptions of the example, how many exits are needed? Also find the final outside count and check the total. Is this balance enough to demonstrate which route A took across?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start from the initial count, add all the entries, then ask how many must be removed to reach the final count.

Hint 2

The total for the closed system provides an independent check of the outside count. Then separate the molecule count from the crossing mechanism.

Compare with the solution

Before subtracting exits, the calculation groups 6 + 5 = 11 molecules of A. To end at 4, we need 11 − 4 = 7 exits. This grouping is arithmetic: it does not claim that all entries preceded the exits.

The inside change is 5 − 7 = -2 molecules of A: this is an inside loss. The outside ends at 14 − 5 + 7 = 16 molecules of A. Using the conserved total, we also find 20 − 4 = 16. Finally, 4 + 16 = 20 molecules of A.

The balance makes these counts consistent. It does not observe the route within the membrane. To attribute the crossing experimentally to the bilayer rather than to another route or a leak, separate checks would be needed. In our model, an intact membrane and direct crossing by A are explicit assumptions. An exact sum does not validate them experimentally.

Check your reasoning

  • You justify 7 exits and the negative sign of -2.
  • You find 16 molecules of A outside using both the balance of crossings and the conserved total.
  • You distinguish consistency of the balance from evidence of a physical mechanism.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Without looking at the diagram, where do you place heads, tails, and water in a bilayer?

The hydrophilic heads border the water on each side; the hydrophobic tails cluster in the core. The 2 leaflets form a membrane, with no layer of water between their tails.

Recall 2Why can the vesicle membrane let A through while keeping B out?

A is small, neutral, and able to dissolve in the lipid core. B is a large hydrophilic marker with no suitable route across in this scene. Permeability depends on the substance and the membrane; size alone is not enough.

Recall 3How can counts remain constant despite crossings?

Equal numbers of entries and exits cancel out. The difference gives the net change; the sum counts all crossings. A 0 balance does not mean that molecules are motionless.

Recall 4What does conservation of A check, and what does it leave to be verified?

In the closed system, with no creation, destruction, or final storage of A in the membrane, the total inside plus outside remains constant. Recovering this total checks the consistency of the counts. It demonstrates neither the accuracy of all recorded events nor the physical route taken.
