Level 2 · C100·M3

Lasso

Your goalCalculate a lasso fit for the working days of a workshop and explain why an absolute penalty can set a coefficient to 0 without proving that the variable is useless.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A workshop wants to predict its electricity consumption from its operating time and the number of parts produced. Adding a coefficient for each piece of information can sometimes reproduce previously observed days more closely, but it can also retain fragile relationships. How can we allow the model to leave out a variable?

The lasso adds a cost for the absolute size of each coefficient. Keeping a coefficient must then offset this cost by reducing the error. The kink in the absolute value at 0 allows some coefficients to stop exactly there; the others move closer to 0.

The first diagram gives all the observations in this example, which are explicitly fictional. The second shows the result after centering and scaling; its coefficients therefore share the same unit. The lesson explains each transformation before using these numbers. No actual savings or future performance are measured.

The rule to remember

Fix the scales and the objective; pay for the absolute size of the coefficients; check the minimum and assess the prediction on separate observations.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Paying to keep a coefficient

Let us use the 4 days in the diagram to fit a linear prediction. The lasso penalizes the coefficients of the transformed variables with λ = 2 kWh; the intercept remains unpenalized. We calculate a training minimum, then make a prediction without a future observation.

  1. Center the variables and make them comparable

    For each day i, hᵢ is the operating time in hours, qᵢ is the output in parts and Eᵢ is the consumption in kWh. i runs through the days from 1 to n, with n = 4. The letters A to D let us track the same observations in the diagram. Total consumption is 34 + 46 = 80 kWh; its mean is 20 kWh.

    xi=hi−2 h1 h;zi=qi−20 pièces10 pièces

    x and z are the transformed variables, with no units. The centers are the training means; the chosen scales are 1 h and 10 parts. In the order shown in the diagram, x = (-1, -1, 1, 1) and z = (-1, 1, -1, 1). We retain these transformations for future days.

    We also center the target: yᵢ = Eᵢ − 20 kWh, giving y = (-4, -2, 2, 4) kWh. The means of x, z and y are 0. The mean of the squares of x and the mean of the squares of z both equal 1; the mean of the products xᵢzᵢ equals 0. This last property, called column orthogonality, will allow us to separate the coefficients. It is chosen for this example and never assumed for every dataset.

  2. State the exact lasso objective

    We predict ŷᵢ = bxᵢ + czᵢ and then Êᵢ = 20 kWh + ŷᵢ. The hat marks a prediction. b and c, in kWh, are the coefficients to choose; the baseline of 20 kWh is an unpenalized intercept. Since the variables are centered, this baseline is also the optimal intercept for this objective.

    J(b,c)=12n∑i=1n(yi−bxi−czi)2+λ(|b|+|c|)

    The symbol ∑ means to add the terms for all the days. The first term is half the training mean squared error. The second is the penalty: |b| is the absolute value of b, meaning its size without the sign. The sum of the absolute values is also called the absolute norm. λ, pronounced lambda, is a nonnegative setting; here λ = 2 kWh. Since x and z have no units and y is in kWh, J and each of its terms are in kWh².

    This choice of the factor 1/(2n) fixes the numerical meaning of λ. A different factor in front of the sum requires converting the setting. The calculations concern these observations and this deterministic objective; no normal distribution for the noise is needed to find its minimum.

  3. Separate the coefficients using the data

    Let α denote the mean of the products xᵢyᵢ and β the mean of the products zᵢyᵢ, both in kWh. These are association scores with the centered target, not probabilities. The signs of x and z let us group the rows in the diagram.

    α=(22+24)−(16+18)4=124=3 kWh
    β=(18+24)−(16+22)4=44=1 kWh

    The contributions from the mean cancel in these differences. Check that yᵢ = αxᵢ + βzᵢ in each row. Without a penalty, b = α and c = β therefore reproduce these consumption values exactly. When we expand the squares, the cross term disappears because of orthogonality, and the means of the squares equal 1. Our objective becomes exactly:

    J(b,c)=12(b−α)2+12(c−β)2+λ(|b|+|c|)

    We can therefore minimize the part that depends on b and the part that depends on c separately. Without this orthogonality and scaling, applying a threshold separately to the least squares coefficients does not generally solve the lasso problem.

  4. Understand the kink at 0

    To handle each part, t denotes any coefficient and r its score: r = α for b, r = β for c. They are in kWh. We minimize f(t) = (t − r) squared divided by 2, plus λ|t|; f is in kWh². The derivative f′ is the slope of this function. For positive t, |t| = t and f′(t) = t − r + λ; for negative t, |t| = −t and f′(t) = t − r − λ.

    At t = 0, the absolute value has a kink: for strictly positive λ, f has no unique derivative. The slope approaching from the left is −r − λ; the slope leaving to the right is −r + λ. 0 is a minimum when the first is nonpositive and the second nonnegative, meaning |r| ≤ λ. On each side, the slope increases with t: these conditions, or a 0 slope on a branch, do give the unique global minimum.

    t∗={r−λsi r>λ0si |r|≤λr+λsi r<−λ

    The star marks the coefficient that minimizes f. This rule is called soft thresholding: it shrinks large scores toward 0 and sets those covered by the threshold to 0. The rule preserves the sign of a score that exceeds the threshold in absolute value. The squared penalty in ridge has no such kink; it can shrink a coefficient without setting it exactly to 0.

  5. Apply the threshold and check the slopes

    For operating time, α = 3 kWh exceeds λ = 2 kWh. We obtain b* = 3 − 2 = 1 kWh. For output, |β| = 1 kWh remains below the threshold: c* = 0 kWh. Choose the branch before subtracting; mechanically calculating β − λ would create an unjustified negative coefficient.

    Check locally, then globally: for b*, the slope is 1 − 3 + 2 = 0 kWh. For c*, the left slope is −1 − 2 and is negative; the right slope is −1 + 2 and is positive. Each part is therefore at its minimum; since they are separate, their sum is too. The selection is exact for this mathematical problem.

  6. Recalculate the cost on the original days

    The regularized fit predicts ŷᵢ = 1xᵢ + 0zᵢ, in kWh. The residuals yᵢ − ŷᵢ are, in order, (−3, −1, 1, 3) kWh. Their sum is 0, consistent with centering. Adding their squares gives 20 kWh².

    J(b∗,c∗)=208+2×(1+0)=2.50+2.00=4.50 kWh²

    An independent calculation using the coefficients gives the same loss: the difference α − b* is 2 kWh; its square is 4 kWh². The square of β − c* is 1 kWh². Their sum 5 divided by 2 again gives 2.50 kWh².

    The unpenalized coefficients make the residuals 0, but the sum of their absolute values is 4 kWh. With the same λ, their total objective is 8.00 kWh², greater than 4.50 kWh². The lasso therefore accepts a less accurate reproduction of the training days to reduce the penalized objective. This comparison alone would not prove optimality; the slopes in the previous step justify it.

  7. Return to predicted consumption

    A new working day at the workshop lasts 3 h and produces 30 parts. Reusing the training centers and scales gives x = 1, z = 1. The model predicts Ê = 20 + 1 × 1 + 0 × 1 = 21 kWh.

    The 0 coefficient removes the dependence on z from this prediction rule. This does not demonstrate that producing parts consumes no energy: it is a property of the fit, the threshold and the chosen variables. Consumption for this new day is not given; no actual error or predictive improvement can be reported.

  8. Keep the result within its proper scope

    The threshold is fixed here to explain the mechanism. To choose it in practice, compare settings on separate validation data, learning the centers and scales from the training data alone. Keep the test set for the final evaluation. A low value of J is not enough to choose λ: J itself changes when λ changes.

    Related variables may share or compete for a role in the prediction; which ones remain can then change with the data. The separate calculation on this page depends on our orthogonality. Neither setting a coefficient to 0 nor reading with help constitutes proof of causality or mastery of a level.

Explain it in your own words

Why does the output coefficient stop at 0 instead of becoming negative? Reconstruct the slopes to the left and right, then explain why this result is not a conclusion about the cause of consumption.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The lasso always subtracts λ from each coefficient.”

    Choose the branch from the score and its sign. A score whose absolute value does not exceed λ gives a 0 coefficient; a strongly negative score moves closer to 0 by adding λ. Here the separate rule assumes orthogonal columns with a mean square of one.

    Revisit the kink and the threshold branches

  • “The coefficient is 0, so this activity consumes nothing.”

    The model removes a dependence for the chosen threshold and data. It does not isolate a causal effect. Repeating the calculation with a lower threshold already shows that the same variable can return.

    Vary the threshold in the exercises

  • “I can change units without adjusting the penalty.”

    Changing the scale of a variable changes the size of its coefficient, and therefore the absolute cost paid for an identical prediction. Revisit the variables with no units and learn their transformations from the training data alone. The factor in front of the sum of squares must also remain explicit.

    Review the scales and the stated objective

For b, α = λ: the right slope at 0 is −3 + 3 = 0 kWh and the left slope is negative. The minimum remains b = 0 kWh. Since |β| is below the same threshold, c = 0 kWh too. The rule therefore always predicts the baseline of 20 kWh, including for days with different activity levels.

A 0 slope at the boundary does not create an interval of solutions: as soon as t moves away along a branch, the slope increases. ✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What trade-off does the lasso objective impose, and what do we leave out of the penalty here?

It adds a squared training loss and λ times the sum of the absolute sizes of the coefficients. The intercept is unpenalized. The scales and the factor in front of the loss must be fixed before λ can be given a meaning.

Recall 2Why does an interval of scores give an exactly 0 coefficient?

For f(t) = (t − r) squared divided by 2 + λ|t|, the slopes at 0 are −r − λ on the left and −r + λ on the right. When |r| ≤ λ, they bracket 0 slope and the minimum is t = 0. Otherwise, move r toward 0 by λ without changing its sign.

Recall 3When can we apply this threshold separately to the scores of each variable?

For the stated objective, the variables must be centered, have a mean square of one and be mutually orthogonal. The expansion then separates the coefficients. With related columns, applying the threshold once to the least squares coefficients is generally insufficient.

Recall 4What does a 0 coefficient establish, and what physical conclusion remains out of reach?

The fitted rule no longer depends on this variable for the chosen data, transformations and threshold. It proves neither that the activity consumes nothing nor that its causal effect is 0. Changing the threshold or the data can change the selection.

Recall 5How can we prepare to choose λ without treating the fit as future performance?

Learn the transformations from the training data alone, compare settings by their error on separate validation data, and keep a final test independent of the choice. The penalized training J does not demonstrate a future gain, and its values cannot be compared directly across different λ settings.
