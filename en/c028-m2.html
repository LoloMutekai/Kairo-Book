Permutations

Your goalCount the possible orders of all the distinct objects, explain each factor, and check that no order is omitted or counted more than once.

Before you start
c014-m5 : invariants · c011-m5 : binomial · c028-m1 : sum and product rules

✿01 See the idea

You are arranging cards A, B, C and D on a small display shelf. They are read from left to right. ABCD and BACD display the same cards, but visitors encounter them in a different order. We want to count all the different rows you can make.

The scene and its quantities are fictional. The 4 cards are distinct, every position in the row is filled, and each card appears exactly 1 time. For now, there are no restrictions on which cards are next to each other or on reserved positions. A permutation is an ordering of all these objects.

Place A at the beginning: B, C and D remain. Then place B: C and D remain. The available supply decreases with each move. If you had started with D, the same number of cards would remain, but with different letters. Here, the number of choices depends on the position to fill, rather than the letters already placed.

Recall the product rule: for each initial choice, every allowed continuation is possible. Multiplying the numbers of successive choices counts complete rows. Adding these numbers would count individual decisions rather than rows.

The rule to remember

To order all the distinct objects, use each exactly 1 time: the number of choices decreases by 1 at each position, until the last object is forced.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

All the rows on the shelf

How many complete rows can you make with A, B, C and D, using each exactly 1 time? The left and right ends are marked: reading the row backwards produces a different order. We are counting possibilities without assigning probabilities to them.

  1. Define exactly what counts

    n is the number of cards; here n = 4. A row is a sequence of n letters read from left to right. Each letter must appear exactly 1 time. Pn denotes the number of these rows. n is an integer with no physical unit; Pn is a count of rows, also with no physical unit.

    The assumptions are essential: distinct objects, all used, no reuse, and no position restrictions. If the letters no longer distinguish the cards, rows that were previously different may become indistinguishable; you must then count again.

  2. Count the allowed beginnings

    Any of the 4 cards can go in the first position. For each choice, 4 − 1 = 3 cards remain for the second position: the card placed is removed from the supply. The product rule gives 4 × 3 = 12 beginnings of length 2.

    For example, after A you can write AB, AC or AD. After B you can write BA, BC or BD. The letters change, but the number of possible continuations stays the same.

  3. Complete each row

    After the first 2 cards, 2 remain: each beginning therefore has 2 possible extensions. We obtain 12 × 2 = 24 beginnings of length 3.

    The last card is then forced, but there is indeed 1 way to place it. Multiplying by 1 preserves the count; multiplying by 0 would wrongly claim that no row can be completed.

    P4=4×3×2×1=24 rangées

  4. Justify that there are no duplicates

    Every sequence of allowed choices produces a complete row. Conversely, a given row lets you recover exactly those choices by reading its letters in order. Sequences of choices that differ at a position produce rows that differ at that position. The product therefore omits no row and counts no row more than once.

    The tempting approach of keeping 4 choices everywhere gives 4 × 4 × 4 × 4 = 256 sequences. This calculation allows AAAA or ABAC. It addresses a different problem, since our cards cannot be reused.

  5. Name the product

    For an integer n at least equal to 1, the factorial of n is the product of the integers from n down to 1. The notation below is read as “n factorial”: the exclamation mark is a mathematical symbol. For n at least equal to 2, the product expands as shown. The ellipsis continues the factors, decreasing by 1 each time, without going below 1.

    Pn=n!=n×(n−1)×…×1
    4!=24;1!=1;0!=1

    The factorial of 0 equals 1 by convention: with no cards at all, there is still a single possible row, the empty row. This convention preserves the reasoning that “all the objects have been placed, and there is one way to add nothing”.

    For n at least equal to 1, you can also fix the first card: n choices, then the factorial of n − 1 orders for the others. This gives the following relation, a way to reconstruct the formula rather than recite it.

    n!=n×(n−1)!

  6. Check by inserting the last card

    Set D aside. The rows of A, B and C are ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB and CBA: 6 rows, in agreement with 3 × 2 × 1 = 6.

    In ABC, D can be inserted before A, between A and B, between B and C, or after C: DABC, ADBC, ABDC, ABCD. There are 4 insertion positions for each of the 6 rows. We recover 6 × 4 = 24 rows, as in the previous product.

    This check introduces no duplicate: removing D from a final row recovers both the starting row and the position where D was inserted. It verifies the 24 possibilities through another construction.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why the supply decreases, why the last factor is nonzero, and how removing D lets you check the result. Then reconstruct the factorial and its assumptions.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “ABCD and BACD contain the same letters, so they are the same possibility.”

    Our outcome describes a row read from left to right. A is in a different position in the 2 cases: the permutations are different. Before counting, decide whether the question distinguishes positions or only the objects present.

    Compare positions in the rows

  • “Any card can go in each position, so I keep 4 choices everywhere.”

    A card that has been placed is no longer available. Write the remaining supply after each move: 4, then 3, then 2, then 1 choices. The constant product would also count forbidden reuse, such as AAAA.

    Revisit the count of beginnings

  • “The last card is forced, so I multiply by 0.”

    A forced placement offers 1 possibility. A count of 0 would mean that nothing fits and the row is impossible. Once all the cards have been placed, the unique empty continuation also explains why the factorial of 0 equals 1.

    Review the last factor and the empty row

Exercise 1 · Application and checklist

A occupies the first position

On the same shelf, require A to be at the far left. How many possible rows remain with the 4 cards? Give the product, then write all the rows to check your result.

Hint 1

The first position now offers 1 possibility. Count the cards that are still free.

Hint 2

Group the rows by the letter immediately after A; for each of these letters, swap the other cards.

Compare with the solution

A is fixed. There are 3 distinct cards left to order: 1 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 rows.

The list is ABCD, ABDC, ACBD, ACDB, ADBC, ADCB. Each row does start with A and contains each card once. The beginnings AB, AC and AD each give 2 continuations: 3 × 2 = 6. The initial factor 4 from the unrestricted case no longer applies: the first letter is no longer a choice.

Check your reasoning

  • Use 1 possibility for the fixed position and 3 for the next.
  • Obtain 6 rows, with no repeated letter or duplicate in the list.
  • Explain why the constraint changes the calculation in the example.

Exercise 2 · Reconstruction by insertion

Add card E

Remove the constraint on A, then add card E, which is different from the others. All 5 cards must appear once. Calculate the new number of rows. Check it by inserting E into the original rows and explain why each final row is obtained once.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The product now starts with 5 and continues by decreasing each factor by 1.

Hint 2

In ABCD, mark the gaps before A, between the letters and after D. Removing E must let you recover a single original order.

Compare with the solution

The new count is the factorial of 5: 5 × 4 × 3 × 2 × 1 = 5 × 24 = 120 rows.

A row of 4 cards offers 4 + 1 = 5 insertion gaps. In ABCD, this gives EABCD, AEBCD, ABECD, ABCED or ABCDE. Each original row gives the same number, so 24 × 5 = 120.

Removing E from a final row recovers its original row; the position of E recovers the chosen gap. This inverse operation guarantees that no result has been counted more than once.

Check your reasoning

  • State the 5 factors and the result of 120 rows.
  • Include the gaps at both ends in the count.
  • Justify uniqueness by removing E, going beyond the mere equality of the products.

Exercise 3 · Transfer with an adjacency constraint

Keep A and B next to each other

Return to the 4 cards A, B, C and D. A must be immediately to the left of B. How many rows satisfy this? Then also allow B immediately to the left of A, while keeping the cards next to each other. Find the new total and explain why the cases do not overlap.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Temporarily join A and B into a block AB. Which objects can you move freely on the shelf?

Hint 2

For the other orientation, use block BA. Can a row have A immediately to the left of B and B immediately to the left of A at the same time?

Compare with the solution

With the order AB required, treat AB as an object, alongside C and D. There are 3 distinct objects to order: 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 rows. Here they are after opening the block back up: ABCD, ABDC, CABD, CDAB, DABC and DCAB.

If BA is also allowed, each row of the grouped objects allows 2 internal orders. The total becomes 2 × 6 = 12 rows. The same number is obtained by adding 6 rows with AB and 6 with BA.

The cases are separate: A and B each appear only once, so no order can contain both the adjacency AB and the adjacency BA. Conversely, every row where A and B are adjacent can be grouped unambiguously into a block. We have therefore counted all the required rows, once each.

Check your reasoning

  • Count 3 objects after grouping, and keep the other cards distinct.
  • Distinguish the 6 rows with AB from the total of 12 when both 2 orientations are allowed.
  • Justify both the grouping and the absence of overlap between the AB and BA orientations.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why are ABCD and BACD 2 different permutations?

A permutation gives the order of all the distinct objects, each used once. A and B change positions; the sequence read from left to right changes, even though the same cards are present.

Recall 2Reconstruct the count of rows of A, B, C and D without directly citing a factorial.

The first position offers 4 choices; since the chosen card is removed, the following positions offer 3, 2 and 1. The product rule gives 24 rows. Each row recovers a single sequence of choices.

Recall 3Under which assumptions is the number of rows the factorial of n?

You need n distinct objects, all used exactly once, positions whose order matters, and no additional restriction. A fixed position or a required adjacency means you must count again.

Recall 4Why does the last position offer 1 possibility, and why does the factorial of 0 equal 1?

The last card is forced, but it can be placed in only one way. Once all the cards are placed, the unique continuation consists of adding nothing: there is a single empty row. 0 possibilities would mean an impossible placement.

Recall 5How can you verify the 24 rows by setting D aside?

List the 6 orders of A, B and C. In each, insert D into the 4 possible gaps, including the ends: 6 × 4 = 24. Removing D unambiguously recovers the starting order and the chosen gap.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the reasoning: specify what makes the rows different, state the assumptions, remove each chosen object from the supply, multiply the numbers of choices, and name this product “n factorial”. Finish with an insertion check and its inverse operation. Reading guide · recognizing a reordering

What you understand
Reordering changes positions while keeping the same objects. The factorial count concerns all possible orders under the stated assumptions. It does not say how many orders a specific program will produce. This reading guide adds no canonical milestone to this chapter.
