Level 1 · C034·M2

Integral

Your goalRead a definite integral as signed accumulation, calculate it for a linearly varying flow rate, and distinguish the change in volume from the volume already present.

Before you start
c033-m5 : linear approximation · c034-m1 : Riemann sums

✿01 See the idea

A pump fills a tank. At the start, it delivers 2 L/min; at the end of 4 min, it delivers 6 L/min. Its flow rate increases steadily between these times. Looking only at the final flow rate does not tell us how much water has entered over the whole interval.

Imagine thin vertical strips under the flow rate curve. The width of a strip is a duration, and its height is a flow rate: their product represents a small volume. By refining the partition, we aim for an accumulation that no longer depends on the strips chosen. The definite integral is the name for this limit, when it exists.

The first diagram shows this accumulation. The second reminds us that the tank already contained 5 L: the amount entering during the interval and the amount finally in the tank are different quantities.

The entire situation and all its values are fictional. We assume an ideal pump, a flow rate that varies exactly linearly, a tank large enough to hold the water, and no leaks. Water flowing in is the positive direction; reversing the pump will give a negative flow rate. The number of litres present will remain positive as long as the tank is not empty.

The rule to remember

The integral of a flow rate over an interval gives the signed change in volume during that interval. Then add the initial volume to obtain the final contents.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

How much water enters while the flow rate increases?

The pump starts at time a = 0 min and runs until b = 4 min. The initial flow rate is 2 L/min; it increases by 1 L/min each minute. The tank initially contains 5 L. Let us first find the volume that enters, then the final contents.

  1. Describe the flow rate and its units

    t is the time in minutes since the pump started. a and b are the endpoints of the interval being studied, in minutes, with a < b. q(t) is the signed flow rate, in L/min. qa denotes the flow rate at the start, 2 L/min, and k its slope, 1 L/min²: each additional minute adds 1 L/min to the flow rate.

    q(t)=qa+k(t−a)

    The duration is b − a = 4 − 0 = 4 min. The increase in flow rate is 1 × 4 = 4 L/min; q(b) = 2 + 4 = 6 L/min. Here the straight line is the assumed exact model, rather than an approximation of readings.

    We assume q is continuous on the closed interval [a, b]. This assumption is sufficient to guarantee the existence of the Riemann integral used here. A positive flow rate is not necessary for the integral to exist; it will allow us to interpret the whole region as water flowing in.

  2. Read the new notation

    I=∫abq(t)dt=limmaxiΔti→0∑i=1nq(ξi)Δti

    I names the result, in litres. The ∫ sign indicates a definite integral; a and b say from which time to which time we accumulate. The group dt indicates that we partition time: it is neither an additional flow rate nor a factor equal to the entire duration.

    In the sum we have already encountered, n is the number of pieces and i is their index, both without units. Δtᵢ is the width of a piece in minutes, and ξᵢ is a time chosen within it, also in minutes. The ∑ sign adds their contributions. maxᵢ Δtᵢ is the greatest width: making it tend to 0 forces all the pieces to become thin.

    The notation with lim means that these sums tend to the same number, regardless of the partitions and points chosen, as this maximum width tends to 0. A sum for a given partition is an approximation; the integral is the common limit. Here q is positive: I is also the area under the curve, expressed in litres.

  3. Recover bounds using rectangles

    Divide the interval into 4 strips of width 4 ÷ 4 = 1 min. At times 0, 1, 2 and 3 min, the flow rates are respectively 2, 3, 4 and 5 L/min. Their sum is 14 L/min; multiplying it by 1 min gives 14 L.

    These rectangles use the flow rate at the left endpoint. The line rises, so they stay below it. At the right endpoints, the flow rates are 3, 4, 5 and 6 L/min, giving 18 L/min in total; the rectangles give 18 L and extend above the line. We already know that 14 L ≤ I ≤ 18 L.

    Increasing the number of strips would tighten the bounds. To obtain the exact value here without working through a long list of rectangles, let us use the geometry of the straight line.

  4. Account for the initial flow rate maintained throughout

    The pink rectangle has a width of 4 min and a height of 2 L/min. It represents what would have entered if the pump had maintained its initial flow rate throughout the interval.

    Vrectangle=2×4=8 L

    V with the label “rectangle” names this partial volume. We still need to add the effect of the increase, because the flow rate does not remain constant.

  5. Add the gradual increase

    The increase goes linearly from 0 to 4 L/min. It forms the triangle above the rectangle. Its base is 4 min and its height is 4 L/min. Two copies of this triangle form a rectangle with the same base and height, so we divide their product by 2.

    Vtriangle=4×42=162=8 L

    V with the label “triangle” denotes the volume added by the increase alone. Using the final increase throughout the duration would count this triangle twice.

  6. Form the integral, then find the final contents

    The regions meet without overlapping. Their volumes add together:

    I=∫04q(t)dt=8+8=16 L

    The volume entering is exactly 16 L under our model. If Va is the contents at the start and Vᵦ the contents at the end, both in litres, conservation of volume with no leaks gives Vᵦ = Va + I = 5 + 16 = 21 L. The integral gives a change; finding the contents also requires an initial condition.

  7. Check using another construction

    The entire region is a trapezoid. Place an identical rotated copy against it: the sloping edges fit together to form a rectangle of width 4 min and height 2 + 6 = 8 L/min. The doubled region measures 4 × 8 = 32 L; a single copy measures 32 ÷ 2 = 16 L. We recover I without using the two partial volumes again.

    The result is indeed between 14 and 18 L. The units give (L/min) × min = L. Finally, changing the initial volume would change the final contents, but not the integral of the flow rate: the calculation of I used only the flow rate and the endpoints.

    This geometric construction is sufficient for our linearly varying flow rate. The theorem that will connect an integral to an antiderivative will be studied in the next unit; no antiderivative has been used here.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The final flow rate directly gives the volume that entered.”

    6 L/min is a flow rate at an instant, rather than a volume. The product flow rate × duration works for a constant flow rate; here we need to account for the increase. Review the units, then the two regions under the line.

    Review the rectangle and the gradual increase

  • “I have a sum of rectangles, so I already have the exact integral.”

    With the same 4 strips, the left endpoints give 14 L and the right endpoints give 18 L. The definition seeks a limit independent of these choices. A finite sum can sometimes be exact, but this needs justification; here neither sum equals I.

    Reread the common limit and the bounds

  • “The integral is the contents of the tank; it cannot be negative.”

    The integral measures the change between the chosen endpoints. To obtain the contents, add the initial volume. With the positive direction set towards the tank, a reversed pump removes water: its contribution is negative. A negative change does not mean negative contents.

    Try the signed withdrawal in the last exercise

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

The pump reverses direction

After the 4 min in the example, the same pump is reversed for 1 min at a constant flow rate of -2 L/min. The tank does not empty, and there are still no leaks. Someone adds the volumes moved in both directions and calls this the change in volume. Calculate the actual change since the pump started and the final contents; respond to this objection using units and signs.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The flow rate is now constant: its integral is its product with the duration, keeping the sign.

Hint 2

The amount already present at the start is added to the total change. It is not included in the sum of the integrated flow rates.

Compare with the solution

During the withdrawal, the change is -2 L/min × 1 min = -2 L. Its negative value means that water flows out, without implying a negative volume present.

Each phase has its own integral: the flow rate is continuous during the increase, then constant during the withdrawal. Splitting the interval isolates the change of direction; a single instant adds no volume in this model with a bounded flow rate. Over the whole operation, the changes over successive intervals add with their signs. The total change is 16 + (-2) = 14 L. The final contents are 5 + 14 = 19 L.

Counting the water moved in each direction as positive would give 16 − (-2) = 18 L. This total volume moved answers a different question. Inflow and outflow offset each other in the contents, even though both have moved water through the pump.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the negative sign of the -2 L change during withdrawal.
  • Obtain a change of 14 L and 19 L present.
  • Explain why the 18 L moved is not the change in volume.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What distinguishes a definite integral from a sum over a few rectangles?

The integral is the common limit of the sums as the greatest width of the pieces tends to 0, regardless of the partitions and points chosen. A finite sum is exact only if this is justified. Continuity on the closed interval is sufficient here to guarantee this limit.

Recall 2In the integral of q(t) between a and b, what do the limits of integration and dt tell us? What unit do you expect?

a and b set the starting and ending times of the accumulation. dt indicates that we partition the time variable. Flow rate in L/min multiplied by durations in min gives an integral in L.

Recall 3Why does the area under the curve require care if the pump is reversed?

The integral counts contributions with their signs. A flow rate below the time axis gives a negative change. Geometric area, counted positively, does not indicate the direction of movement; it no longer directly represents the change.

Recall 4Why is the integral of the flow rate not enough to determine the final contents of the tank?

It measures the change over the interval. You also need to know the initial contents and add the signed change to them, assuming that no other flow is involved.

Recall 5How can you reconstruct our integral without an antiderivative or a long list of rectangles?

Separate the starting flow rate maintained throughout the duration from the linear increase: a rectangle, then a triangle. The triangle is half the rectangle with the same base and height. Add their volumes and check the units and the bounds given by the sums.

From a rate to change over a duration

What you understand
A rate and a duration produce a change. With a variable rate, you need to accumulate over the whole interval. The direction of the rate and the initial contents remain two distinct pieces of information.
