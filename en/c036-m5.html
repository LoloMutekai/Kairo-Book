Level 1 · C036·M5

Annotated graphs

Your goalBuild a verifiable annotation from a reading, its units and a calculated difference, without turning an observation into an explanation.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You show someone how a cup of water cools. A line slopes down on the page. Without a title or units, that person cannot tell whether you are showing a temperature, an amount of water or a price. An annotation is a short text attached to a specific place on the graph to say what to read there.

In our entirely fictional scene, the same probe reads 60 °C at the start, 54 °C after 5 min and 52 °C after 10 min. Time is counted from the initial reading. The notebook also records the removal of the lid at 5 min, just after the reading. These data are used to learn how to present a graph; they are not real readings.

The middle point must remain identifiable even if the color disappears. Its label gives its time and temperature; a short line points to it. The title names the scene, the axes name the quantities and their units, and the caption states where the values come from. These elements let you check the drawing without guessing its author’s intention.

The points represent only the stated readings. The line segments help you follow their order; they do not prove that the temperature followed exactly these straight lines between readings. No probe accuracy is provided: we do not add an invented error bar.

The rule to remember

Attach each annotation to an identifiable data point; distinguish a recorded value, a calculated difference and a noted event; state the units, the source and what the reading establishes.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Write an annotation you can reconstruct with a pencil

We want to add the change since the start to the label for the reading at 5 min. The 3 fictional readings come from the same cup and the same probe, using the same unit. The removal of the lid is a note in the notebook, separate from the measured values.

  1. Define the data and symbols

    t denotes the time elapsed in minutes since the initial reading; T denotes the temperature read in degrees Celsius. The table below gives a pair (t; T) on each row. It has no missing reading. We do not assume that any value between these times is known.

    t (min)T (°C)0605541052

    The subscripts for the start and reference readings will identify the initial row and the row chosen for the annotation. The symbol Δ, read as “delta,” denotes a difference: the value at the end minus the value at the start.

  2. Check the positions before commenting

    On the horizontal axis, the tick marks are 5 min apart; 0 + 5 = 5 min recovers the time of the middle point. On the vertical axis, add 4 °C at each tick mark: 48, 52, 56, 60 °C. The reading of 54 °C lies between 52 and 56 °C.

    The spacing is regular on each axis: these are linear scales. The vertical axis starts at 48 °C to make the differences easy to read. This displayed range is stated; a point’s height above the base of the drawing therefore does not represent its full temperature.

  3. State the duration being compared

    Δt is the duration between the initial reading and the chosen reading. Subtracting their times checks which period our statement refers to. This is a duration in minutes, distinct from the temperature change.

    Δt=trepère−tdépart=5−0=5 min

  4. Calculate the change, then its direction

    ΔT is the temperature at the reference point minus the temperature at the start. Subtracting temperatures in the same unit gives a difference in degrees Celsius. The sign indicates the direction of the change; it does not replace the final temperature.

    ΔT=Trepère−Tdépart=54−60=-6 °C

    The change is negative: the cup has cooled between these readings. At the reference point, the temperature is still 54 °C; it is not -6 °C. We have calculated neither an instantaneous rate of change nor the effect of removing the lid.

  5. Write the text and attach it to the correct point

    A complete annotation is: “At 5 min: 54 °C; change since 0 min: -6 °C.” It states the time, the value and the reference for the difference. The graph keeps a short label and its pointer line; the fuller explanation can stay in the caption to avoid hiding the data.

    Add separately: “Lid removed just after the reading at 5 min, according to the fictional notebook.” This sentence locates an event in time. Writing that it caused the drop would require additional evidence. The drop between readings in fact begins before this removal. The title and source must remain visible when the figure is used on its own.

  6. Check using the starting value

    Work back through the calculation: adding the change to the starting temperature must recover the annotated reading. This check verifies both the sign and the choice of rows in the table.

    60+(-6)=54 °C

    We do recover 54 °C. Then check the drawing: the same time as the label, the point at the correct height, explicit units, a stated vertical range and a source. A neat line guarantees none of these correspondences.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The label -6 gives the temperature of the point.”

    Name the quantity: 54 °C is the reading; -6 °C is its change from 60 °C. Fix the annotation by adding the time, the unit and the words “change since the start,” then check by adding the change back.

    Revisit the calculation and its check

  • “The line drops almost to the bottom of the drawing, so the water is almost at 0 °C.”

    Read the lowest tick label: it is 48 °C. The axes show a range of only 12 °C. Keep this information and replace the visual impression with the recorded values; when comparing figures, check their limits and units.

    Reread the axes and caption

  • “The note about removing the lid proves that it caused the drop.”

    The note reports an event at a given time. The curve reports readings; putting them together does not measure the specific effect of this action. Replace “caused” with “was removed just after this reading” and leave the cause as an open question.

    Review how to compose the annotation

The point gives (5 min; 54 °C). Swapping the numbers would give a time and a temperature that do not match this reading.

The reading changes from 60 °C to 54 °C between the endpoints of the interval. The line segment does not demonstrate a steady drop at every instant; the precise path between the readings remains unknown.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and writing

Annotate the last reading

For the same cup, annotate the reading at 10 min with its change since the start. Also give the duration being compared and check the result by adding the changes over the successive intervals.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

To calculate a change, start with the final reading and subtract the reference reading.

Hint 2

Calculate the change between 5 and 10 min separately, then add it to the change already found before the reference point.

Compare with the solution

Duration: 10 − 0 = 10 min. Change over this duration: 52 − 60 = -8 °C. The annotation becomes: “At 10 min: 52 °C; change since 0 min: -8 °C.”

Over the interval after the reference point, 52 − 54 = -2 °C. The check gives (-6) + (-2) = -8 °C, identical to the direct difference of -8 °C. The intermediate reading cancels out in the sum: we have indeed compared the same endpoints.

The change is not the final temperature, and it does not describe everything that happened between the readings. Its reference point must therefore remain in the annotation.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain -8 °C over 10 min and retain the final reading of 52 °C.
  • Recover the total difference by adding the successive changes.
  • Write the time, the unit and the comparison reference in the annotation.

Exercise 3 · Critical review

Fix a caption that claims too much

Someone reuses the same figure, removes the vertical tick labels and writes: “Removing the lid caused all this cooling.” Propose a verifiable caption. Explain what would change if the vertical axis were redrawn from 0 °C to 60 °C, keeping the same readings.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Restore the units, limits and source before commenting on the event.

Hint 2

Changing the displayed range changes the apparent size of the differences; recalculate the temperature difference to see what stays the same.

Compare with the solution

One possible caption is: “Cup of water, 3 fictional readings from the same probe. Time since the start in minutes, temperature in °C. Vertical axis limited to 48–60 °C. At 5 min, 54 °C; lid removed just after this reading according to the notebook. The line segments guide the reading; the specific effect of removing the lid has not been established.” The tick labels must also be restored on the figure.

With a vertical axis starting at 0 °C and the same drawing height, the drop would look smaller. The readings would remain 60, 54 and 52 °C; the total change would remain -8 °C. The display geometry changes, not the data.

The reading before the lid was removed already shows a drop. This sequence of events must be distinguished from evidence of the action’s effect; the figure alone cannot isolate that effect.

Check your reasoning

  • Restore the tick labels, units, vertical range and fictional source.
  • Replace the causal claim with the event, its time and the limit of what it establishes.
  • Explain why a different scale preserves the calculated change.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What makes an annotation verifiable?

It identifies a point or period, names the quantity and its unit, and states the reference if it gives a difference. The data and source let you reconstruct it.

Recall 2For the cup, why do 54 °C and -6 °C not mean the same thing?

54 °C is the reading at 5 min. -6 °C is the difference from the starting reading, 60 °C. The check consists of adding this difference to the starting reading.

Recall 3What should you check before interpreting the height of a drop?

The limits, tick labels and units of the axes. Here the vertical axis starts at 48 °C; the base of the drawing is not 0 °C. A different displayed range changes the appearance, not the difference between readings.

Recall 4Why does a note about removing the lid not measure its effect?

It locates an event in the sequence of readings. It does not separate what results from this action from what would have happened without it. The annotation must report the event without attributing the entire drop to it.
