Level 3 · C168·M4

Probabilistic convergence

Your goalDistinguish almost sure convergence, convergence in probability, convergence in Lᵖ and convergence in distribution by following a rare probe error whose spikes grow.

Before you start
c164-m5 : Fubini-Tonelli · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c165-m5 : duality · c168-m1 : measurable variables · c168-m2 : conditional expectation · c168-m3 : independence

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a fictional test bench for calibrating probes. As the calibration index increases, an anomaly affects a smaller proportion of the possible probes. But its amplitude increases. Can we say that the error disappears? That depends on what we want to control: a sample path, the probability of a deviation, a mean or a distribution.

At index 4, the model gives a spike of 4 °C with probability 1/4. At index 16, the spike reaches 16 °C, with probability 1/16. The product amplitude × probability is always 1 °C. A less probable anomaly is therefore not enough to make its mean contribution negligible.

Each probe receives a number U drawn uniformly between 0 and 1, excluding the endpoints. It keeps this same number at every index. The anomaly occurs if U does not exceed the reciprocal of the index. The bands in the diagram represent the draws that meet this condition; they do not represent an observed frequency.

This rule and all its values are fictional. The index is a dimensionless model index; no duration, hardware performance or real reading is attributed to the scene.

The rule to remember

Name the mode of convergence before drawing a conclusion. A probability tending to 0 controls rarity; convergence in Lᵖ also controls the size of the errors.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Spikes that disappear from every sample path but not from the mean

The draw U is uniform on (0, 1) and stays fixed throughout the sequence. At integer index n ≥ 1, the error Xₙ equals na if U ≤ 1/n, and 0 °C otherwise, with a = 1 °C. Let us study convergence to 0 error.

  1. Define the variables on the same space

    The space Ω = (0, 1) carries the Borel σ-algebra, denoted by ℱ, and the probability measure ℙ given by length. An outcome ω is a point in this interval; U(ω) = ω. The letter n ranges over the positive integers. The indicator of an event A, written below, equals 1 if A occurs and 0 otherwise. It is dimensionless.

    Xn=na1{U≤1n}

    a is a reference amplitude in °C; Xn and the candidate limit X = 0 °C are temperature errors, never absolute temperatures. The variables are measurable because the events used are intervals. ℙ(A) is the probability of A; 𝔼 denotes expectation under ℙ. The Xn share U: their independence is not an assumption.

  2. Calculate the distribution at specific indices

    The length of (0, 1/n] is 1/n: this is the probability of the spike. The complement puts all its probability mass at 0. We then multiply each error by its probability to calculate the expectation.

    ℙ(Xn=na)=1n;𝔼Xn=na×1n=a

    At index 4: spike probability 1/4 = 0.2500, dimensionless; probability of 0 error 3/4; mean 4 × 1/4 = 1 °C. At index 16: probabilities 1/16 = 0.0625 and 15/16; mean 16 × 1/16 = 1 °C.

    Check: the complementary probabilities add up to 1 at every index. In the diagram, the spike amplitude is multiplied by the same factor by which the probability is divided. The means therefore remain equal, without rounding.

  3. Control the probability of a deviation

    Convergence in probability to X means: for every fixed tolerance ε > 0, ℙ(|Xn − X| > ε) tends to 0 as n tends to infinity. ε is in °C; the probability is dimensionless. Fix ε before letting n increase.

    Xn→ℙX⇔∀ε>0,ℙ(|Xn−X|>ε)→0

    Here X is 0. Once na > ε, only the spike exceeds the tolerance: the required probability equals 1/n and tends to 0. For ε = 0.5 °C, indices 4 and 16 give 1/4 and 1/16, respectively. The calculation for every ε proves convergence; these values at finite indices only illustrate it.

  4. Follow an entire sample path

    Almost sure convergence requires that the set of ω for which Xn(ω) tends to X(ω) have probability 1. The word “almost” allows an exceptional set of probability 0. We now fix ω, hence a probe, before examining all indices.

    ℙ({ω:Xn(ω)→X(ω)})=1

    For every ω > 0, choose an integer N > 1/ω. Then, for every n ≥ N, 1/n < ω: the indicator is 0 and the error stays 0. Here this works for every ω in Ω, which is stronger than almost surely. The index N can depend on ω. Drawing a new U to replace the old one at each index would change the sequence being studied.

    Why does this imply convergence in probability in general? For fixed ε, set Bn = ⋃k≥n {|Xk − X| > ε}: an error still exceeds ε at some index from n onward. The Bn form a decreasing sequence; under almost sure convergence, their intersection has probability 0. Continuity of a finite measure on a decreasing sequence gives ℙ(Bn) → 0. Since {|Xn − X| > ε} is contained in Bn, its probability also tends to 0.

  5. Test the mean size of the error in Lᵖ

    Fix a finite, dimensionless real number p, p ≥ 1. For variables whose moments of order p are finite, convergence in Lᵖ to X means that 𝔼|Xn − X|ᵖ tends to 0. This moment has units of °C raised to the power p; its pth root is the distance in Lᵖ, in °C. For p = 1, this is the mean absolute error.

    𝔼|Xn−0|p=(na)p1n=apnp−1

    Each moment is finite at every index. Yet for p = 1, it remains equal to a = 1 °C; for p > 1, it grows without bound. There is therefore no convergence to 0 error in any of these Lᵖ spaces. The rarity 1/n is offset, or outweighed, by the growth of the spikes.

    Do not automatically interchange expectation and limit: 𝔼(lim Xn) = 0 °C, whereas lim 𝔼Xn = 1 °C. Dominated convergence would require a single integrable variable bounding all the errors from above; almost sure convergence does not supply this assumption.

  6. Compare distributions: convergence in distribution

    The cumulative distribution function Fn(t) = ℙ(Xn ≤ t) describes the distribution at index n. The threshold t has the same units as Xn. We say that Xn converges in distribution to X if Fn(t) tends to F(t) = ℙ(X ≤ t) at every continuity point of F. This definition compares distributions; it does not require the variables to be defined on the same space.

    For X = 0 °C, F(t) equals 0 if t < 0 °C and 1 if t ≥ 0 °C. It is discontinuous at 0 °C. For t < 0 °C, Fn(t) = 0. For every fixed t > 0 °C, eventually na > t, so Fn(t) = 1 − 1/n → 1. All required points are covered: Xn converges in distribution to 0 error.

    At t = 0 °C, the definition requires nothing here, because F has a jump there. In this example, Fn(0) also tends to 1. This does not allow us to require convergence at discontinuity points in every problem.

    A constant limit is a special case: convergence in distribution to 0 also implies convergence in probability to 0. Indeed, for every ε > 0, the thresholds −ε and ε are continuity points of F, and the probability of a deviation is bounded above by Fn(−ε) + 1 − Fn(ε), which tends to 0. This special converse does not hold for an arbitrary random limit.

  7. Justify the other arrows

    For the arrow from Lᵖ to probability, set An = {|Xn − X| > ε}. On An, |Xn − X|ᵖ exceeds εᵖ. Outside An, the error raised to the power p remains nonnegative. Expectation therefore preserves the following inequality:

    εpℙ(An)≤𝔼|Xn−X|p

    Divide by εᵖ, which is strictly positive and fixed. If the moment tends to 0, so does the probability. The quotient is indeed dimensionless. This upper bound does not let us infer convergence of the moment from convergence in probability: our probe provides a counterexample.

    For the arrow from probability to distribution, fix t and ε > 0. If the deviation does not exceed ε, X ≤ t − ε implies Xn ≤ t, and Xn ≤ t implies X ≤ t + ε. Writing qn = ℙ(|Xn − X| > ε), we obtain:

    F(t−ε)−qn≤Fn(t)≤F(t+ε)+qn

    First let n tend to infinity: qn vanishes. Then let ε decrease to 0. At points where F is continuous, the bounds approach F(t). This argument explains the continuity condition without adding an independence assumption.

  8. State conclusions that can be checked

    For this sequence alone: almost sure convergence, convergence in probability and convergence in distribution to 0 °C; no convergence in Lᵖ to 0 for finite p at least equal to 1. The decisive point is that the probability of the spike decreases, but its contribution to the moment does not.

    Check on a single probe: U = 3/8 = 0.375, dimensionless. Its reciprocal is 8/3. From index 3 onward, n > 8/3 and the error is always 0 °C. Check over the whole space: at index 16, the mean remains 1 °C. These calculations address different questions, and their results are compatible.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the formulas. Explain why a fixed probe eventually becomes exact while the model's mean error remains positive. Then rewrite the definition of convergence in probability, placing “for every ε” correctly.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The probability of a large deviation tends to 0, so the mean error tends to 0.”

    Probability ignores the size of the spike once the threshold is exceeded. Recalculate amplitude × probability: na × (1/n) = a. For a conclusion in Lᵖ, check the corresponding moment.

    Revisit the moment calculation in Lᵖ

  • “Almost surely means that at every index the error is 0 with probability one.”

    It concerns the limit of an entire sample path, outside a fixed exceptional set. Here, at every finite index, a spike remains possible; but for every fixed strictly positive U, it eventually stops occurring. The index at which it disappears depends on U.

    Revisit the choice of an index depending on the probe

  • “Convergence in distribution requires Fₙ(t) → F(t) for absolutely every t.”

    The definition requires this limit at the continuity points of F. Locate the jump at 0 °C in the limiting distribution, then distinguish negative and positive thresholds. Convergence in distribution alone does not control a mean.

    Reread the calculation of the cumulative distribution functions

Exercise 1 · Numerical application

Make the probability strictly small enough

Keep ε = 0.5 °C. You require ℙ(|Xₙ| > ε) < 1/20, or 0.05, dimensionless. Does index 16 meet this requirement? Find the smallest integer index that does and give its exact probability.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The spike already exceeds ε. Use the probability 1/n.

Hint 2

Pay attention to the strict inequality: compare n with 20.

Compare with the solution

At index 16, 1/16 > 1/20: the requirement is not met. Since the denominators are positive, 1/n < 1/20 is equivalent to n > 20.

The smallest integer is 21. Its probability is 1/21, strictly less than 1/20. At index 20, it would be exactly 1/20, which the strict inequality excludes. This is a finite threshold; convergence requires a property for every fixed tolerance.

Check your reasoning

  • Reject 16 by comparing probabilities.
  • Obtain 21 and justify the strict inequality.
  • Keep ε in °C and δ = 1/20 dimensionless.

Exercise 2 · Proof for a sample path

Find the last spike for the same probe

Fix U = 3/8 for all indices. Calculate the error at indices 2 and 3, then prove that it stays 0 at every later index. A simple list of values is not enough.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare U with 1/n at each index.

Hint 2

The function n ↦ 1/n decreases: once below U, it stays there.

Compare with the solution

At index 2, the threshold is 1/2 and U = 3/8 is below it: the error is 2 °C. At index 3, the threshold is 1/3, less than 3/8: the error is 0 °C.

For n ≥ 3, 1/n ≤ 1/3 < 3/8. The indicator therefore stays 0. The last spike is at index 2. This sample path illustrates the mechanism; the almost sure convergence proof in the lesson covers every ω by choosing N > 1/ω.

Check your reasoning

  • Find 2 °C, then 0 °C.
  • Use an inequality valid at every later index.
  • Distinguish a fixed sample path from the whole probability space.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and justification

Limit the spike on the same test bench

A limiter in the fictional model replaces the spike na with the constant spike a, without changing the draw U or the threshold 1/n. Call this new error Zₙ. Study its convergence in Lᵖ for every finite p ≥ 1. Calculate its mean absolute error at index 16. Does the result change the conclusion about Xₙ?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Zₙ equals a on the band, and 0 elsewhere.

Hint 2

The moment is amplitude raised to the power p × probability of the band.

Compare with the solution
Zn=a1{U≤1n};𝔼|Zn|p=apn→0

For every fixed p, the moment is finite and tends to 0: Zn converges to 0 error in Lᵖ. For p = 1 at index 16, the mean absolute error is 1 × 1/16 = 1/16 °C, or 0.0625 °C. It then decreases toward 0 °C.

The limiter changes the size of the spikes and therefore the sequence. It does not change the conclusions already proved for Xn. For Zn, the constant a also bounds all the errors from above and is integrable: the obstacle to controlling the means has disappeared in this model.

Check your reasoning

  • Write the moment with a raised to the power p.
  • Prove the limit for fixed p and give 1/16 °C at the requested index.
  • Attribute the new result to Zₙ; retain the counterexample Xₙ.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must you fix before letting n tend to infinity in convergence in probability?

A tolerance ε > 0, in the same units as the error. For every such fixed tolerance, the probability ℙ(|Xₙ − X| > ε) must tend to 0.

Recall 2What is the difference between following a probe and examining a probability at a single index?

Almost sure convergence concerns the limit of every sample path, except on a set of probability 0. Convergence in probability controls the probability of a deviation at each index; it does not directly say which sample paths return to that deviation.

Recall 3Why does our error converge almost surely without converging to 0 in Lᵖ?

For every fixed positive U, the threshold 1/n eventually drops below U. But the moment is aᵖ times n raised to the power p − 1: it remains equal to a for p = 1, and grows for p > 1. Rarity does not offset the size of the spikes.

Recall 4Where must you test convergence of the cumulative distribution functions?

At every continuity point of the limiting cumulative distribution function. Points where it jumps are not required. This compares distributions without guaranteeing convergence of the moments.

Recall 5Reconstruct the arrows in the diagram and explain the one starting from Lᵖ.

Almost sure convergence implies convergence in probability; convergence in Lᵖ, for finite p ≥ 1, implies convergence in probability; convergence in probability implies convergence in distribution. For the arrow from Lᵖ, εᵖ ℙ(|Xₙ − X| > ε) ≤ 𝔼|Xₙ − X|ᵖ. Divide by fixed εᵖ, then take the limit.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the definitions by naming what converges: sample paths, probabilities of deviations, moments or cumulative distribution functions. Then recover the arrows and compare na × (1/n) with a × (1/n). Explain why the first mean does not vanish and why the limiter in the exercise changes this conclusion.
