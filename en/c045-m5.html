Level 1 · C045·M5

Introductory quadrature

Your goalApproximate the volume poured into a tank using trapezoids, check the units, and distinguish an approximation, a known error, and a simple comparison of meshes.

Before you start
c034-m5 : mean value · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c045-m1 : integration by parts · c045-m2 : partial fractions · c045-m3 : trigonometric substitutions · c045-m4 : improper integrals

✿01 See the idea

A pump fills a tank. Its flow rate increases: looking only at the final displayed value does not tell us how much water entered over the whole period. The volume corresponds to the area under the flow rate curve, from starting the pump to stopping it.

We have fictional readings: 2 L/min at the start, 3 L/min after 1 min, and 6 L/min after 2 min. None of these numbers comes from a real measurement. We assume that all the water pumped enters the tank, with no leaks or outflow.

Between two readings, replace the curve with a line segment. The area under this segment forms a trapezoid: its width is a duration, and its heights are the flow rates at the endpoints. Its area corresponds to a volume. Adding these small volumes gives a quadrature, meaning a numerical approximation of an integral.

To check this approximation, our example also provides a fictional exact law for the flow rate. The first diagram shows this curve; the second shows how the trapezoids represent it. In an experiment with readings alone, the curve between them would remain unknown.

The rule to remember

On each interval, multiply its duration by the average of its two endpoint flow rates; add the resulting volumes. The calculation is exact for the chosen line segments, but may only approximate the volume under the actual curve.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Estimate the water entering over 2 min

On the interval from 0 to 2 min, the flow rates at times 0, 1, and 2 min are respectively 2, 3, and 6 L/min. We will calculate the trapezoidal volumes, then use the known fictional law to measure their error.

  1. Define the target and assumptions

    t denotes elapsed time, in minutes; q(t) denotes the flow rate at that time, in L/min. V denotes the volume that has entered, in litres. The bounds a and b are the start and end, with a = 0 min and b = 2 min. We assume q is continuous and nonnegative on this finite interval. This is not an improper integral.

    The fictional law is q(t) = q0 + c t squared, with q0 = 2 L/min. The coefficient c is expressed in litres divided by the cube of a duration in minutes, as the following formula shows. Thus, c multiplied by t squared is indeed a flow rate. The subscript in q0 means “initial flow rate”. The model gives exactly the stated readings.

    c=1Lmin3
    q(t)=q0+ct2
    V=∫abq(t) dt

    The integral accumulates products of flow rate × duration. The symbol dt represents an element of duration, so the result has the unit (L/min) × min = L. The volume already present before starting the pump is not included in V.

  2. Divide the time period

    n is the number of intervals, with no unit; here n = 2. The step size h is the common duration of each interval: h = (b − a)/n = (2 − 0)/2 = 1 min. The partition points are therefore 0, 1, and 2 min.

    We need 3 readings to form 2 intervals. The middle point ends the first and starts the second; sharing it creates no additional duration.

  3. Construct the area of a trapezoid

    On an interval of duration h, let qg denote the flow rate on the left and qd the flow rate on the right, both in L/min. Replacing the curve with the line segment joining these values assumes a linear variation between them. A denotes the volume under this segment, in litres.

    A=hqg+qd2

    Why use this average? A rectangle of height qg contributes h × qg. The triangle added or subtracted contributes h × (qd − qg)/2. Their sum is h × (qg + qd)/2. The formula therefore also works for a descending segment. Here, both segments rise.

  4. Calculate each small volume

    First interval: the average flow rate along the segment is (2 + 3)/2 = 2.50 L/min. Multiplying by 1 min gives 2.50 L. We have averaged flow rates before multiplying by a duration.

    Second interval: (3 + 6)/2 = 4.50 L/min; then 1 × 4.50 = 4.50 L. The middle flow rate appears in both trapezoids because they share this endpoint.

    Let T be the sum of the trapezoidal volumes, in litres. The intervals are adjacent and overlap only at an instant of 0 duration; their volumes add together.

    T=2.50+4.50=7 L

  5. Compare with an exact integral

    The polynomial model is deliberately simple: we can check the quadrature using an antiderivative. F(t) denotes an accumulation in litres whose derivative is q(t). We can choose F(t) = q0 t + c t cubed/3. Differentiating the cubic term gives c t squared.

    F(t)=q0t+ct33

    At t = 2 min, the constant part accumulates 2 × 2 = 4 L. The square of the bound is 4 and its cube is 8; the curved part contributes 1 × 8/3 = 8/3 L. At the starting bound, F(0 min) is 0 L.

    The exact volume for the model is V = F(b) − F(a) = 4 + 8/3 = 20/3 L, or approximately 6.6667 L. The word exact refers to this fictional law; a real pump may follow a different law.

  6. Measure the difference and check it another way

    The signed error is E = T − V, in litres. Here E = 7 − 20/3 = 1/3 L, or approximately 0.3333 L. Its positive sign means an overestimate. Decimals make the result easier to read; the earlier calculations keep the exact fractions.

    The curve bends upwards: we say it is convex. The line segments between its points lie above it, which explains the sign we found. This direction of error depends on the curvature; it does not hold for every function.

    An independent check of the areas: the first rectangle contributes 2 × 1 L and its triangle adds (3 − 2) × 1/2 L. This gives 2.50 L again. The same decomposition gives 4.50 L for the second interval.

    Finally, since the flow rate stays between 2 and 6 L/min over 2 min, the volume must lie between 4 L and 6 × 2 = 12 L. Both the exact integral and the quadrature satisfy these bounds; this rough check does not prove that they are equal.

  7. Understand what refinement allows

    Reducing the step size means making a finer partition and obtaining the flow rates at the new points. For a continuous function on this closed interval, the trapezoidal approximations converge to the integral as the largest step size tends to 0. This alone does not give the error of a particular partition.

    The exercises compare a single trapezoid with finer trapezoids. With only a table of readings, adding invented points on the same line segments creates no information about the actual curve. Instrument error also remains distinct from the error caused by replacing the curve with segments.

Explain it in your own words

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I add the flow rates and get the volume.”

    A sum of flow rates is still in L/min. Associate a duration with each contribution. For the first interval, the average 2.50 L/min must be multiplied by 1 min to give 2.50 L.

    Revisit the construction and units of the trapezoid

  • “I average all the readings and multiply by the total duration.”

    The endpoints do not have the same weights as the interior points in a sum of trapezoids. Start again interval by interval: the middle point contributes on both sides, whereas the start and end each contribute only once.

    Review how the middle point is shared

  • “Two meshes give almost the same result, so the error is certified.”

    Their difference compares two approximations. Without information about the curve between readings, it does not automatically bound the difference from the true volume. In our model, only the supplied antiderivative gives this error directly; the final exercise separates these two situations.

    Examine the justification in the last exercise

The long segment lies above the two shorter segments in this example. We predict a larger estimate. The average of the endpoint flow rates is (2 + 6)/2 = 4 L/min. The width is 2 min.

The estimated volume is 2 × 4 = 8 L. The error is 8 − 20/3 = 4/3 L, or approximately 1.3333 L. It is positive and greater than 1/3 L.

Compare with the solution

There are 4 intervals of 0.50 min. Each interior flow rate is shared by two trapezoids: the two halves add together. Each endpoint belongs to only one trapezoid.

We can therefore combine the calculations: T fine = 1/2 × [(2 + 6)/2 + 9/4 + 3 + 17/4] L.

The interior flow rates total 19/2 L/min. Half the sum of the endpoint flow rates is 4 L/min. The weighted sum is 27/2 L/min; multiplying by 1/2 min gives 27/4 L, or 6.75 L.

The error is 27/4 − 20/3 = 1/12 L, or approximately 0.0833 L. The ratio of the errors is (1/3)/(1/12) = 4. In this comparison for our parabola, dividing the step size by 2 divides the error by 4; this observation is not enough to certify the error for another curve.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and justification

What accuracy can you claim?

The required tolerance for the volume is 0.10 L. In the first case, you know our fictional exact law. In the second case, you know only the readings on the fine mesh. Can you certify that the estimated volume meets this tolerance in each case? Also explain what the difference between 8 L and 7 L measures.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare the exact error for the fine mesh with the tolerance when the law is given.

Hint 2

If only the points are known, imagine a variation in flow rate between two points, without moving those points.

Compare with the solution

With the exact law from the exercise, the error for the fine mesh is 1/12 L, approximately 0.0833 L. It is less than 0.10 L: the tolerance is met for this model and these calculations. This is a comparison with a known reference.

If only the readings are known, they do not determine the volume between the recorded times. The flow rate can rise and fall rapidly between two readings, even if the curve remains continuous and positive. Information that limits these variations is missing, so the same tolerance cannot be certified.

The difference between the coarse meshes is 8 − 7 = 1 L. It compares two quadratures. Yet the error of the second is 1/3 L in our model: these quantities are different. We can recommend taking readings at shorter intervals and studying the regularity of the flow rate; we cannot create an error bound solely from agreement between approximations.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish a known law from readings alone.
  • Justify meeting the tolerance using 1/12 L in the first case.
  • Identify the missing information in the second case, without claiming that accuracy has been established.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does quadrature calculate in the pump example?

It approximates the volume that has entered, the integral of the flow rate over a time period. It adds local volumes, with unit L. It is neither the final flow rate nor a simple sum of flow rates.

Recall 2How can you reconstruct the contribution of an interval without reciting the result?

Replace the flow rate curve with the line segment joining its endpoint values. Its average flow rate is their sum divided by 2; multiply this average flow rate by the duration of the interval. Justify this using a rectangle and a triangle.

Recall 3Why do an interior flow rate and an endpoint flow rate have different weights?

An interior reading ends one interval and starts the next: it appears in two half sums. An endpoint belongs to only one half sum. With equal step sizes, interior readings therefore count in full and endpoint readings count for half.

Recall 4Does exact fraction arithmetic make the volume exact?

It makes the sum of the areas under the line segments exact. The curve may follow a different path. For our model, 7 L differs from the volume 20/3 L by 1/3 L, despite exact arithmetic.

Recall 5What must you distinguish after refining the partition?

The difference between two approximations, the error relative to a known integral, and a justified error bound are three different pieces of information. Readings alone do not certify what happens between them.
