Level 3 · C103·M5

Regularization

Your goalCalculate a quadratic penalty on weights, derive its effect on the gradient, and distinguish the regularized objective from validation error.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A workshop wants to predict how long a bicycle delivery will take from the route length. Its training record contains just one route of 1 km, completed in 30 min. This record is fictional: it is enough to follow the calculation, but not to learn a model usable on the road.

The model multiplies the scaled distance by a weight w. A larger value of w gives a longer duration for the same route. With w = 3, it reproduces the record exactly; with w = 2, it differs from it. If only this record matters, reproducing its duration is the best choice.

Regularization adds a preference to the training objective. Here, weights with large absolute values incur a penalty, even when the record is reproduced perfectly. The model can therefore accept an error on the record to reduce this penalty. This preference aims to limit overfitting: adapting to the particular details of the records at the expense of future routes. It can also erase a useful relationship. The coefficient λ, read as “lambda”, sets the price of this tradeoff.

We study the quadratic penalty, often called L2: it uses the square of each penalized weight. This does not establish that the next duration will be predicted better. To find out, we need routes kept out of fitting. The two diagrams separate the calculation of a prediction from the choice of objective.

The rule to remember

Minimize training error plus a penalty on the weights; choose the strength of this penalty using validation error measured separately.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Choose a weight for delivery duration

The training record gives x = 1 and y = 3 after scaling. Let us compare w = 3 and w = 2 with λ = 1/2 = 0.5, then find the exact minimum of the objective. All observations are fictional.

  1. Set the units and identify what remains adjustable

    x is the route length divided by 1 km; y is its duration divided by 10 min. The observed duration of 30 min therefore becomes y = 3. ŷ, read as “y hat”, is the predicted duration on this same scale. To recover the duration in minutes, multiply ŷ by 10 min.

    The model is ŷ = wx: w is a dimensionless real weight. We isolate a linear output neuron in a dense network; the other parameters remain fixed, and its bias, the term added to wx, is fixed at 0. The input to this neuron is simply x here. This restriction makes the penalty calculable by hand; it does not describe the entire training of a deep network.

    A single observation is used for fitting. We assume its values are finite, the scale is fixed before the comparison, and the coefficient λ ≥ 0 is chosen independently of the updates to w. The weight w is the learned parameter; λ is a hyperparameter, meaning a setting for learning.

  2. Build the regularized objective

    L is the training error: here, the square of the difference between ŷ and y. R is the penalty before weighting, the square of the weight. J is the total objective to minimize; λ determines how much R counts relative to L. These quantities are all dimensionless because the scales are explicit.

    L(w)=(wx−y)2;R(w)=w2
    J(w)=L(w)+λR(w)

    For several weights, R adds their squares; the choice of penalized weights must be stated. Here, only w is penalized. In a network, the bias may follow a different convention. A small sum of squares alone does not guarantee a simple function for every architecture and parameterization.

    With several observations, L will be understood here as the mean of the squared errors. Using their sum would change the relative weight of the same penalty. Similarly, some implementations divide the squares by 2: read this convention before transferring a setting.

  3. Evaluate the weight that reproduces the record

    With w = 3, the prediction is 3 × 1 = 3. Subtract the target to obtain the signed difference: 3 − 3 = 0. Its square is L = 0. Yet the square of the weight is R = 3 × 3 = 9.

    The weighted penalty is λR = (1/2) × 9 = 9/2. Add the two contributions to the objective: J = 0 + 9/2 = 9/2 = 4.5, dimensionless. 0 error on the record therefore does not make J 0.

  4. Accept an error to reduce the total cost

    With w = 2, the prediction becomes 2. The difference is 2 − 3 = -1; its square is 1. The square of the weight is 4, giving λR = (1/2) × 4 = 2.

    J(2)=1+2=3<4.5

    Training therefore prefers this candidate despite its larger error. We have compared two weights with the same λ and on the same scale; no additional observation was used to produce this result. We still need to check that this candidate really is the best among all possible weights.

  5. Find the minimum over all weights

    Since x = 1, J is the sum of the square of w − y and λ times the square of w. J′ denotes its derivative with respect to weight w, with y and λ fixed. The derivative of the penalty adds 2λw to the error gradient: subtracting this term during descent pushes toward 0 with an appropriate step size, including when w is negative.

    J′(w)=2(w−y)+2λw

    At the stationary point, this derivative is 0. Collect the terms containing w, then divide by the positive coefficient 1 + λ. The symbol w* here denotes the weight that minimizes J.

    (1+λ)w=y;w*=y1+λ=33/2=2

    This weight matches our second candidate. To prove that it really minimizes J, rather than relying only on a 0 derivative, expand and rewrite the objective in another form:

    J(w)=3/2(w−2)2+3

    The square is nonnegative and its coefficient 3/2 is positive. The lowest cost is therefore 3, reached only at w = 2. This proof is specific to our quadratic problem with a single weight; a 0 derivative does not prove a global minimum for an arbitrary dense network.

  6. Check the forces that balance each other

    At weight w = 2, the gradient of the error alone is 2 × (2 − 3) = -2. It pushes w upward during gradient descent. The penalty gradient is 2 × (1/2) × 2 = 2; it pushes w downward. Their sum is 0. The tradeoff settles while the training error remains 1.

    In plain gradient descent with step size η, the next weight would be w − ηJ′(w). η, read as “eta”, is a positive, dimensionless step size in our scaled variables. Before the error term, the penalty transforms w into (1 − 2ηλ)w. This factor represents a contraction without a change of sign when 0 ≤ 2ηλ ≤ 1; an excessively large step cannot be described simply as “gently reducing the weights”.

    If λ = 0, the penalty disappears and the minimum returns to w = 3, which reproduces the record. For this problem, increasing λ brings the optimal weight closer to 0. This can also lead to underfitting: predictions that are too constrained to represent the intended relationship.

  7. Keep a measurement separate from the choice of weight

    Cost J is used to fit w; it deliberately adds two objectives. To choose λ, compare predictive error alone on validation data that were not used for the gradient. Adding the penalty to this error would change the question: we would no longer be measuring prediction quality alone.

    Normalization is fitted on the training data, then applied to the other data. After selecting λ through validation, reserve an independent test set for the final evaluation. Validation consulted to choose a setting contributes to that choice; it does not become independent evidence of its future quality.

    The final exercise will use another record from the same workshop. Its result will illustrate the calculation of error on data kept out of fitting; a fictional route will demonstrate neither actual overfitting nor a general benefit of regularization.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading the equations, explain why a larger training error can accompany a lower total cost. Then derive the two gradient terms and say what data would be missing to judge future predictions.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A positive penalty must increase the weight.”

    The penalty is a cost. It is its gradient that is subtracted from the weight during descent. The term proportional to w pushes toward 0 with an appropriate step size; at the minimum in our calculation, it balances the error gradient.

    Review the check of the two gradients

  • “Just choose the strongest possible penalty.”

    A smaller weight can worsen predictions. Calculate the stronger setting in the exercises, then compare the errors on the same validation record. Total costs built with different values of λ do not represent the same objective.

    Compare the strong setting and validation

  • “My training error is 0, so I generalize.”

    The model can reproduce the available record without predicting another route well. Keep validation data out of the gradient calculation and a separate test out of the choice of λ. The result of the fictional scene remains an example, not a measurement of actual effectiveness.

    Review the separation of fitting and evaluation

Compare with the solution

For w = 3, the difference is 0; L = 0, R = 9, λR = 9/2, J = 9/2 = 4.5. For w = 2, the difference is -1; L = 1, R = 4, λR = 2, J = 3.

Exercise 2 · Vary a setting

Reduce the weight further

Keep the same record and replace λ with 2. Derive w* from the equation for a 0 gradient. Calculate L, λR and J at this new minimum. Why is this smaller weight not enough to conclude that the model is better?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

With x = 1, the equation for the stationary point is (1 + λ)w = y.

Hint 2

The optimal weight is y divided by 1 + λ. The training error still needs to be recalculated.

Compare with the solution

The denominator becomes 1 + 2 = 3. Therefore w* = 3/3 = 1. The coefficient of the square remains positive: this is indeed the minimum of this problem.

The difference from the target is 1 − 3 = -2; its square is L = 4. The square of the weight is 1; the weighted penalty is 2 × 1 = 2. The cost is J = 4 + 2 = 6, dimensionless.

The weight has decreased, but the error on the record has increased. The imposed preference is stronger. Directly comparing this J with the previous J does not determine the best λ: the objectives have changed. We need to compare the same predictive error, without a penalty, on the same validation data.

Check your reasoning

  • Derive the weight from the equation, rather than guessing it.
  • Use the new λ in the penalty.
  • Distinguish a smaller weight from better predictions.

Exercise 3 · Evaluation and limits of evidence

Another route from the workshop

A fictional validation record, kept out of the gradients, gives 2 km and 40 min, meaning x = 2 and y = 4. Compare weights 3, 2 and 1, obtained respectively with no penalty, with setting λ = 1/2 and with setting λ = 2. Calculate the predicted durations in minutes and the squared errors on the model scale. What can you select? What remains to be established?

Hint 1

Multiply each weight by x = 2. For the error, subtract y = 4 before squaring.

Hint 2

To return to minutes, multiply the prediction by 10 min. Do not add any penalty to the validation error.

Compare with the solution

With w = 3, ŷ = 6, so the predicted duration is 60 min. The scaled difference is 6 − 4 = 2; its square is 4, dimensionless.

With w = 2, ŷ = 4, giving 40 min. The difference is 0 and its square is 0. With w = 1, ŷ = 2, giving 20 min; the difference is -2 and its square is 4.

Among these stated settings, λ = 1/2 minimizes the error on this record. No penalty and the strong penalty give the same validation error here, with predictions on opposite sides of the target. This equality does not mean their models are equivalent.

The record was used to select λ: it is now part of selection. The choice still needs to be evaluated on a separate test relevant to the intended deliveries. With a single fictional route, we have shown the operations and their limits; no general improvement or actual performance has been established.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the quadratic penalty change in learning?

The objective becomes training error plus λ times the sum of the squares of the penalized weights. A larger error may be accepted to reduce this total; the prediction formula stays the same.

Recall 2What term does the penalty λ times the square of w add to the gradient?

It adds 2λw. In plain descent, this term multiplied by the step size is subtracted. It pushes toward 0 with an appropriate step size; it does not replace the error gradient.

Recall 3Why can the same λ act differently in two training runs?

The variable scales, the penalized weights and the loss convention matter. A sum of errors and a mean do not give the same relative weight to the same penalty. Factors such as division by 2 must also be checked.

Recall 4What quantity should be used to choose λ, and what data should be kept afterward?

Compare the same predictive error without a penalty on validation data, kept out of the gradients. Keep a separate test out of the choice of λ to evaluate the selected setting.

Recall 5Does a smaller weight or 0 validation error prove better generalization?

No. A smaller weight demonstrates a stronger constraint in this parameterization; validation that has been consulted contributes to the choice. Our fictional route illustrates a calculation without establishing the future quality of the model.

What you understand
