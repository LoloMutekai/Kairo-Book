Level 4 · C238·M3

Simulation

Your goalConstruct the values of fractional Brownian motion on a finite grid by transforming independent Gaussian variables, then check the resulting covariance and the limits of the simulation.

Before you start
c235-m5 : divergence · c232-m5 : small-time limits · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c238-m1 : fractional covariance · c238-m2 : Hurst index

✿01 See the idea

A test bench must supply a synthetic signal to a display at 9 s and then at 25 s after the start. We want to reproduce fractional Brownian motion with index H = 0.25. Producing numbers that zigzag is not enough: the values at different times must vary together in the right way.

Imagine independent Gaussian shocks as separate inputs to a mixer. If the same input contributes to several outputs, it creates a link between them. The mixing rule lets us calculate this link before any random draw: it is the covariance, the mean of the product of deviations from the means.

The normal distribution, also called the Gaussian distribution, is determined by its mean and variance. To construct a joint Gaussian distribution at several times, we will combine independent Gaussian variables linearly. The variance sets the spread of an output; the covariance sets its link with another. Both checks are necessary.

The entire scene and all its values are fictional. The signal has a dimensionless amplitude. Normalized time is t = τ/T, where τ is the duration in seconds and T = 1 s is the reference duration; t and H are dimensionless. We simulate only the specified times. No real readings or fitting of a volatility model are involved.

The rule to remember

Choose the times and the covariance, construct a mixture that reproduces it, then feed independent Gaussian variables into it. Check the distribution on the grid before judging the appearance of the drawing.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Construct the signal values while preserving their link

At the start, B_H(0) = 0. The test bench then requests X_a = B_H(t_a) and X_b = B_H(t_b), with t_a = 9, t_b = 25 and H = 1/4 = 0.25. Let us construct their distribution, work through a realization by hand, then check an increment.

  1. Define precisely the distribution to simulate

    B_H(t) denotes standard fractional Brownian motion: a centered Gaussian process, hence with mean 0, whose covariance is R_H(s,t). Gaussian here means that every linear combination of a finite number of its values follows a normal distribution. s and t are nonnegative normalized times; H is the Hurst index, strictly between 0 and 1. The units were set in the intuition section.

    RH(s,t)=12[s2H+t2H−|t−s|2H]

    The bars |t − s| give the positive duration between the times. C is the covariance matrix of the requested values: its entry C_ij is R_H(t_i,t_j), where i and j identify the times. Its diagonal entries are the variances. Here the labels are a and b. The deterministic initial point stays separate: adding it as a random variable would give a row of 0 covariances.

  2. Calculate the covariance on our grid

    Since 2H = 1/2, the powers become square roots. The times are chosen so that these roots are exact: √9 = 3, √25 = 5; their difference is 25 − 9 = 16 and √16 = 4. Check: 3 × 3 = 9, 5 × 5 = 25 and 4 × 4 = 16.

    We obtain C_aa = 3, C_bb = 5 and C_ab = C_ba = (3 + 5 − 4)/2 = 4/2 = 2. The variances and covariances are dimensionless, since the amplitude is dimensionless.

    C=[3225]

    This matrix is symmetric. Its determinant, the product of the diagonal entries minus the square of the covariance, is 3 × 5 − 2 × 2 = 15 − 4 = 11. With C_aa strictly positive and this determinant positive, it is positive definite: every nonzero linear combination of the outputs has positive variance.

  3. Find a mixture whose rows give the right products

    A column vector collects the values. C has size 2 × 2. We seek a matrix A such that C = AAᵀ; the transpose Aᵀ exchanges rows and columns. Matrix multiplication takes the dot product of each row of A with every other row. A diagonal entry is therefore the sum of the squares of a row; off-diagonal entries are the sums of products of corresponding entries in two rows.

    A=[11100021]

    This rectangular factor, chosen for this test bench, uses 4 inputs for 2 outputs. First variance: 1 × 1 + 1 × 1 + 1 × 1 + 0 × 0 = 3. Second: 0 × 0 + 0 × 0 + 2 × 2 + 1 × 1 = 5. Covariance: 1 × 0 + 1 × 0 + 1 × 2 + 0 × 1 = 2. We recover every entry of C.

    The factor is not unique and can be rectangular. On another grid with a positive definite covariance matrix, a square triangular factor can be calculated by Cholesky factorization, a method for obtaining C = AAᵀ with A triangular. Our small mixing rule cannot be reused for other times or another H without recalculating the covariance.

  4. Justify why the mixture produces the right distribution

    Z is a column of 4 independent variables Z_a, Z_b, Z_c, Z_d, each with normal distribution N(0, 1): mean 0, variance 1. A has size 2 × 4, and X = AZ is the column of the 2 outputs X_a and X_b. The inputs, outputs and coefficients of A are dimensionless. Uppercase letters denote random variables; their particular values will be written in lowercase.

    X=AZ

    The expectation E denotes the mean under the model distribution. E[X] = 0 because the inputs are centered. The covariance of Z is I, the identity matrix of size 4 × 4: diagonal entries 1, other entries 0. Thus Cov(X) = AIAᵀ = AAᵀ = C, where Cov denotes the covariance matrix. Independence eliminates the cross terms between inputs.

    A linear transformation of independent Gaussian variables is jointly Gaussian. Its mean and covariance determine this joint distribution: we therefore obtain exactly the distribution of (B_H(t_a), B_H(t_b)) in exact arithmetic. The check AAᵀ alone would not suffice with non-Gaussian inputs; it would then guarantee only the second moments.

  5. Work through a chosen realization

    To follow the operations with pencil and paper, set z_a = 1, z_b = 2, z_c = -1, z_d = -1. These are fictional values chosen to explain the calculation; no random draw has been carried out. They do not form a sample that can validate the normality of the inputs.

    The first row of A gives x_a = z_a + z_b + z_c = 1 + 2 + (-1) = 3 + (-1) = 2. The second gives x_b = 2z_c + z_d = 2 × (-1) + (-1) = -2 + (-1) = -3.

    We add the known starting point: the values at times 0, 9, 25 are 0, 2, -3. The increment between the last times is their difference, x_b − x_a = -3 − 2 = -5, dimensionless. This particular value is neither its mean nor its variance.

  6. Check an increment by another route

    Let ΔX = X_b − X_a denote the random increment variable, and Var its variance. Under the model distribution, Var(ΔX) = C_bb + C_aa − 2C_ab. Thus 5 + 3 − 2 × 2 = 8 − 4 = 4. The fractional covariance also gives |t_b − t_a|^(2H) = √16 = 4: the two routes agree.

    Let us recover this result directly from the inputs: ΔX = −Z_a − Z_b + Z_c + Z_d. The sum of the squares of its coefficients is 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4, because of independence and unit variances. Moreover, Cov(X_a, ΔX) = C_ab − C_aa = 2 − 3 = -1. Simulating these increments separately as if they were independent would lose this link.

  7. Separate the exact grid from what remains to simulate

    To produce several independent paths, keep C and A, then supply each path with a new column Z of independent Gaussian variables. A pseudorandom generator seed serves to reproduce a sequence; copying the same column Z simply produces the same result.

    The claimed exactness concerns the distribution at the requested times. The lines on the display interpolate the points; they do not simulate the distribution between these points. Adding times requires calculating their covariances with all the others and factorizing the new matrix. On a computer, rounding in the factorization and generator quality also remain to be checked.

    A collection of paths would then be used to compare empirical means and covariances with theoretical values, accounting for sampling fluctuations. Neither the appearance of our curve nor the correct product AAᵀ proves that a generator is correctly implemented, that an actual run has taken place, or that the model fits an observed signal.

Explain it in your own words

Hide matrix A, then reconstruct why the shared shock produces the desired covariance. Explain the difference between the value -5 of our increment and its variance 4, then name exactly the times at which the distribution has been reproduced.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I draw an independent Gaussian variable at each time and adjust only the variances.”

    The distributions at each time would be correct separately, but their covariance would be 0 instead of 2. The variance of their difference would be 8 instead of 4. Reconstruct the mixture and the products of its rows.

    Review the construction of A and the increment check

  • “The covariance is correct, so I can replace the Gaussian variables with any centered numbers.”

    Centering, independence and unit variance preserve AAᵀ, but do not guarantee a Gaussian distribution. Random signs give a discrete distribution. Gaussian inputs are needed to identify the entire joint distribution of the fractional Brownian motion values.

    Reread why mean and covariance suffice here

  • “The drawing connects all the points: I have simulated the entire continuous path.”

    The segments are a display convention. The calculation establishes only the distribution on the requested grid. Simulating an intermediate point requires its covariances with the existing points, rather than simply reading the segment.

    Reread the accessible description of the drawn realization

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Distinguish realization from spread

For the same signal, first calculate the realized increment between t_a and t_b. Then recover its theoretical variance from C, and then from the shock coefficients. Why do the results differ?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The realization uses x_b − x_a. The variance uses the joint distribution of the variables X_a and X_b.

Hint 2

Expand ΔX in terms of Z_a, Z_b, Z_c, Z_d; add the squares of its coefficients.

Compare with the solution

The realization is -3 − 2 = -5, dimensionless. It describes only this chosen column of inputs.

The theoretical variance is 5 + 3 − 2 × 2 = 4. The coefficients of ΔX are −1, −1, 1, 1; the sum of their squares is also 4. This variance describes the spread across draws from the model, rather than the square of our particular difference.

The fractional formula confirms √16 = 4. The realization can be negative; a variance is always nonnegative.

Check your reasoning

  • You give -5 for the realization and 4 for the variance, labeling them.
  • You retain the covariance term in the difference.
  • You reconstruct ΔX from the inputs and justify the sum of squares.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosing a simulation

Two correct marginal distributions, one incorrect joint distribution

For the same display, someone replaces our mixture with Y_a = √3 U and Y_b = √5 V, where U and V are independent Gaussian variables with mean 0 and variance 1. Are the distributions at each time correct? Calculate the covariance of the outputs and the variance of their difference. Can we keep this method for our grid?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Multiplying a variable by a coefficient multiplies its variance by the square of the coefficient.

Hint 2

The independence of U and V makes the cross covariance 0; then compare with matrix C.

Compare with the solution

Y_a and Y_b denote the outputs of the proposed method; U and V are its inputs. They are all dimensionless. Var(Y_a) = 3 and Var(Y_b) = 5, so the centered Gaussian distributions taken separately are correct.

The covariance is 0, because the inputs are independent and are not shared. The variance of Y_b − Y_a is 5 + 3 − 2 × 0 = 8, whereas the model requires 4. The correct spread at each time is not enough.

We reject this method for the requested joint distribution. The repair consists of introducing weighted sharing of the inputs, then checking every entry of AAᵀ = C; getting only the diagonal entries right would leave the defect intact.

Check your reasoning

  • You distinguish the marginal distributions from the joint distribution.
  • You obtain covariance 0 and variance of the difference 8.
  • You relate the rejection to the required covariance 2, with a precise repair.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

A perfect mean does not validate a generator

The test bench keeps only our column z and its opposite −z. It thus displays the paths (2, -3) and (-2, 3), then announces: “The means are 0, the simulation is validated.” Calculate the empirical means and second moments using the divisor 2. What do these checks prove? What additional check would you propose?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The mean of opposite values is automatically 0. For the second moments, average the squares and cross products.

Hint 2

Since the empirical means are 0, these second moments form the empirical covariance with the requested divisor. Compare its entries with C, then examine how the columns were obtained.

Compare with the solution

Mean at t_a: (2 + (-2))/2 = 0/2 = 0. Mean at t_b: (-3 + 3)/2 = 0/2 = 0. Choosing opposite values forces these results.

The means of the squares are 4 at t_a and 9 at t_b, because the two paths have the same squares. The cross product is -6 for each, so its mean is also -6. We obtain [[4, -6], [-6, 9]], to compare with [[3, 2], [2, 5]]. The divisor 2 defines empirical moments here; we are not using a variance estimator corrected for bias.

0 means therefore do not validate the distribution. A collection this small would fluctuate even with a good generator; its disagreement with C would not, by itself, suffice to diagnose a malfunction. Here, what we chiefly know is that the columns are chosen and opposite, without independent Gaussian draws across paths: they do not carry out the stated procedure.

Opposite pairs can be used in estimation methods, but their dependence must then be taken into account. To check our procedure, we would need to inspect how the inputs are drawn, produce an independent collection according to the stated protocol, then compare means and covariances with their target while analyzing fluctuations. This exercise establishes no miraculous sample size or success threshold.

Check your reasoning

  • You recover both 0 means and the empirical covariance with the correct divisor.
  • You explain why forced centering validates neither Gaussianity nor independence.
  • You distinguish a small-sample fluctuation from a known defect in the procedure.
  • You propose a check of the inputs and outputs without claiming actual validation.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be specified before drawing a fractional Brownian path?

The times, H and the joint covariance C_ij = R_H(t_i,t_j), with a 0 mean. Correct marginal variances are not enough to specify the links between times.

Recall 2Why does X = AZ produce covariance C when AAᵀ = C?

Independent, centered inputs with unit variance have the identity I as their covariance. Thus Cov(X) = AIAᵀ = AAᵀ = C. Gaussian inputs are also needed to obtain the target Gaussian distribution.

Recall 3How can you check a difference between two simulated values?

Its variance is C_bb + C_aa − 2C_ab. Here it is 4, which agrees with √16. You can also expand the difference in terms of the inputs and sum the squares of the coefficients.

Recall 4What does “exact distribution on the grid” mean, and what remains to be checked on a computer?

The joint distribution at only the requested times is that of the process in exact arithmetic. The segments do not simulate intermediate times; the generator, rounding and implementation remain to be checked separately.

Recall 5Why does replaying the same seed or a chosen column fail to validate the distribution?

Repetition shows only that an output is reproducible. Checking a distribution also requires examining the drawing procedure and a collection of realizations produced according to the protocol, accounting for fluctuations in its statistics.
