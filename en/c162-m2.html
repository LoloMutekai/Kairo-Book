Level 2 · C162·M2

Adaptation

Your goalAdapt the conditions of an attention exercise to an explicit constraint, preserve its instructions, and distinguish feasibility, reported experience, and evidence of effectiveness.

Before you start
c161-m5 : limits of application · c087-m5 : measurements and limits · c162-m1 : formal practices

✿01 See the idea

At the library, Mina has planned an attention exercise before her next class. Her instructions are to look at a point on the table as her attention anchor, notice when her attention moves away from it, and return to the anchor without blaming herself for the distraction. She had planned 10 min of practice, but she only has 9 min, including time to settle in and review the exercise.

Adapting here means changing the conditions of the exercise to address an identified constraint. Mina can shorten the duration without removing the action of noticing and returning. The duration changes; the instructions remain recognizable. Mindfulness here does not require having no thoughts at all.

The anchor, also called the focus of attention, is what we choose to return to. Posture, duration, and location are conditions of practice. Before changing a condition, Mina writes down the problem: here, a lack of time. She chooses to change the duration and keeps the same anchor, posture, and location. This makes her adjustment possible to describe; it is not enough to prove its effect.

The entire scene and its durations are fictional. Mina aims to make her plan feasible, with the option to stop or postpone it. The chosen minutes are used for calculation; they are neither a recommended duration for the reader nor a dose whose effectiveness has been established.

The rule to remember

Name the constraint, choose the setting to change, keep explicit instructions, then check feasibility and what was actually observed separately.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A 10 min plan in a 9 min time slot

Mina sets aside 1 min to settle in and 1 min to write her review. She is considering changing the exercise from 10 to 6 min, with the same visual anchor. Let us check the schedule before any attempt.

  1. Define what is being adjusted

    C denotes the total time slot, I the time to settle in, B the review time, and D the planned exercise duration. All these quantities are in minutes, abbreviated min. We assume that these time periods follow each other without overlapping and that their planned durations add up. Any additional delays must fit within the spare time.

    R will be the time set aside outside the exercise; A the time available for the exercise; T the total time committed; M the remaining spare time. These quantities are also in minutes. The letters do not denote any measurement of brain activity. The instruction to return to the anchor and the right to stop are preserved.

  2. Subtract the time already set aside

    Let us add the time periods that are not for the exercise itself: R = I + B = 1 + 1 = 2 min. We subtract them from the time slot because they occupy part of it.

    A=C−R=9−2=7 min

    7 min is a feasibility limit under our assumptions. This calculation does not identify the duration that would produce the best effect. If the time slot were shorter than the time already set aside, no positive exercise duration could fit in this plan: it would need to be reorganized or postponed.

  3. Show why the initial plan overruns

    For D = 10 min, T = R + D = 2 + 10 = 12 min. Comparing T with the time slot gives an overrun of 12 − 9 = 3 min. The exercise duration alone already exceeded the time slot; including the transitions gives the actual overrun of the plan.

    This finding is enough to justify a schedule adjustment. It does not mean that Mina lacks willpower or that she must complete the initial duration at all costs.

  4. Assess the shortened version

    Mina chooses D = 6 min. This value is a fictional proposal, below A = 7 min. The choice leaves some free time instead of filling the available time exactly.

    T=R+D=2+6=8 min
    M=C−T=9−8=1 min

    The scheduling criterion is D ≥ 0 min and M ≥ 0 min. It is satisfied. A plan that fits the schedule may still not suit Mina when she tries it; feasibility does not replace her choice.

  5. Check and describe the change

    Let us reconstruct the time slot another way: I + D + B + M = 1 + 6 + 1 + 1 = 9 min. Each time period is counted, and none overlap. The reduction in the planned duration is 10 − 6 = 4 min.

    To express the proportion of the duration retained, r denotes the ratio of adapted D / initial D. A ratio here is a division of durations in the same unit: the minutes cancel, so r has no unit.

    r=610=0.60

    The exact fraction is 3/5; 0.60 is its decimal representation with no unit. Reverse check: (3/5) × 10 min = 6 min. This ratio compares only the planned durations. It gives neither a proportion of benefit retained nor a proportion of attention.

  6. Prepare the review without overstating it

    Before the attempt, Mina notes: “I am shortening the duration to fit my schedule; I am keeping the visual anchor and the instruction to return.” After the attempt, she can record the time actually spent on the exercise, any interruption, and what seemed easy or difficult to her. A planned schedule is not yet a completed exercise.

    A reported experience is what a person says about their experience. If Mina writes “this version seemed easier to start,” the sentence describes how she experienced this attempt. It does not measure her attention or establish that the change in duration caused an improvement. Context, expectations, or fatigue may also differ; their role is not isolated here.

    To make a comparison clear, we can start by changing only the duration and recording the other conditions. This choice helps describe the attempts; by itself, even with repetition, it does not turn a personal account into evidence of effectiveness. Developing measures of attention belongs to a later unit.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, explain why the initial plan overruns, reconstruct the spare time in the shortened version, and state what the duration ratio can compare. Then describe the action that stays the same when the duration changes.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The entire time slot is available for practice.”

    The time slot also includes settling in and reviewing the exercise. Mina subtracts their 2 min before choosing the duration. Reconstruct the time slot again to check that no time periods have been omitted or counted twice.

    Recalculate the available time and spare time

  • “If a thought appears, the adapted version has failed.”

    The instructions specifically include noticing a distraction and returning to the anchor. They do not ask for all thoughts to be eliminated. Preserving this action lets us state what remains of the exercise when its duration changes.

    Find the preserved instructions in the diagram

  • “It was easier, so my attention improved.”

    “Easier” is a reported experience. It should be kept as such, specifying the attempt concerned. The page provides neither a measure of attention nor a comparison that would allow an effect to be attributed to the chosen setting.

    Examine the personal account and its limits in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Shorten it further without forgetting the transitions

In the same 9 min time slot, Mina shortens her adapted version by a further 1 min. The time to settle in and review remains the same. Calculate the new exercise duration, total time committed, and spare time. State which instructions she keeps.

Hint 1

Start with 6 min for the exercise; subtract the stated minute from that duration only.

Hint 2

Then add the 2 min of transitions and subtract this total from the time slot.

Compare with the solution

The duration becomes 6 − 1 = 5 min. The time already set aside remains 2 min; the total time committed is therefore 5 + 2 = 7 min.

The spare time is 9 − 7 = 2 min. Check: 1 + 5 + 1 + 2 = 9 min. The spare time has increased by the minute removed from the exercise.

Mina keeps the same anchor, notices when her attention moves away from it, and returns to it. The calculation checks her schedule; it does not rank this version as better or worse.

  • Describe the preserved instructions without requiring an absence of thoughts.

Exercise 2 · Solving under a constraint

The library closes earlier

For another attempt by Mina in the same place, the time slot is only 5 min. Keep 1 min to settle in and 1 min for review. Does her 6 min version fit? Find the maximum exercise duration that would fit in this plan, then explain what no spare time means.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The time set aside outside the exercise stays the same; the time slot is the quantity that changes.

Hint 2

At the maximum possible duration, the exercise fills all the time left after the transitions. This leaves no time in reserve for an unexpected delay.

Compare with the solution

The 6 min version still commits 8 min, which is 8 − 5 = 3 min too much. It does not fit in the new time slot.

The available time is 5 − 2 = 3 min. This value is the arithmetic maximum under the stated assumptions. With this duration, the total is 1 + 3 + 1 = 5 min, giving 0 min of spare time.

Any additional minute would overrun the time slot. The maximum is therefore neither a requirement nor a duration demonstrated to be effective. Mina can choose a shorter duration or postpone the exercise; if the transitions take longer than expected, the plan with no spare time must be revised.

Check your reasoning

  • Establish the overrun of 3 min before proposing the adjustment.
  • Find the limit of 3 min and the spare time of 0 min.
  • Distinguish the scheduling maximum, personal choice, and effectiveness.

Exercise 3 · Analysis and rewording

What the review actually tells us

Mina compares her old 10 min plan with a 6 min attempt. For this attempt, she also replaced the visual anchor with the sensation of her hands touching the table. She writes: “I found it easier to start; this adaptation therefore improves my attention, and I must keep it.” Rewrite her conclusion and prepare the next attempt that is easier to describe.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Separate the changed conditions, the reported experience, and the conclusions that would require other evidence.

Hint 2

An old plan is not an initial measurement. For the next attempt, state the setting being studied and the conditions Mina intends to keep.

Compare with the solution

Possible rewording: “During this attempt, with a shorter duration and a different anchor, starting seemed easier. I do not know whether my attention changed or which change explains that experience. I can decide to try this version again without considering it superior.”

The comparison confuses an earlier plan with a completed attempt; it also changes both the duration and the focus of attention. The duration ratio, 3/5, remains a scheduling calculation: it does not become a fraction of effectiveness retained. The personal account does not justify a requirement to continue either.

To study only the change in focus of attention, Mina can plan the same duration of 6 min and the same transitions, in the 9 min time slot. She records the chosen anchor in advance, keeps the location and posture the same as far as possible, then describes what actually happened. The time budget remains 8 min committed and 1 min of spare time.

This preparation makes differences easier to identify. It does not isolate all changes in context or prove a causal effect; evidence of effectiveness would require a question, observations, and a comparison designed for that question.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep Mina’s reported experience while removing the unsupported conclusion about her attention.
  • Identify the changes in duration and focus of attention, as well as the absence of an initial measurement.
  • Prepare the next attempt with explicit conditions and an explicit objective, without claiming effectiveness or a requirement to continue.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What changes and what stays the same in Mina’s adaptation?

The planned duration changes to fit the schedule. Mina keeps the visual anchor and the instruction to notice a distraction and return. She keeps the choice to stop or postpone the attempt.

Recall 2How can you find the time available for the exercise and check the time slot?

Subtract settling in and review from the time slot: 9 − (1 + 1) = 7 min. For the chosen duration, reconstruct settling in + exercise + review + spare time = time slot. The calculation checks a planned schedule.

Recall 3Does the ratio 3/5 between durations give the proportion of benefit retained?

It compares 6 min with 10 min and has no unit. No relationship between duration and benefit has been established here, so no proportion of benefit or attention can be deduced from it.

Recall 4How can you use “this version seemed easier” without overstating its scope?

Attribute it to the person and the attempt concerned, describe the conditions, and keep this reported experience as a personal account. It is neither a measure of attention nor evidence that the setting caused a gain.
