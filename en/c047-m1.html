Level 2 · C047·M1

Direction field

Your goalConstruct the slopes of a direction field, read local change from it and distinguish a tangent from a solution.

Before you start
c045-m5 : introductory quadrature · c046-m5 : approximation error · c033-m5 : linear approximation

✿01 See the idea

A cup is at 50 °C in a room at 20 °C. Before looking for its future temperature, ask a smaller question: in which direction does its temperature begin to change, and how fast? All the values in this scene are fictional; we are studying a model, not a cooling measurement.

Imagine a map with time on the horizontal axis and the possible temperature of the cup on the vertical axis. At each point, a short line segment indicates the slope that the model requires of a curve passing through that point. The set of these segments is called a direction field. They describe possible states, not a collection of readings from the same cup.

In our model, the warmer the cup is relative to the room, the faster its temperature falls. At room temperature, the segment is horizontal. Below it, the segment rises: the same cup, if it had been colder than the room, would begin to warm up. Read the segments from left to right, as time advances.

A solution would be a curve whose tangent follows the segment of the field at each of its points. The field gives a local rule; it is not already that curve. The second drawing shows a tangent on its own: its triangle is used to convert a slope into a small approximate change.

The rule to remember

At each point, calculate the required derivative, then draw its direction. To test an entire curve, compare its derivative with the derivative required by the field all along its path.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the cooling rule to the short segments

The room stays at A = 20 °C. At time t = 0 min, the cup is at 50 °C. The fictional coefficient k = 1/10 per minute is constant. Let us construct the field, then calculate what its tangent predicts over h = 0.50 min.

  1. Define the rule and the unknowns

    t denotes the elapsed time, in minutes; T(t), the unknown temperature of the cup at that instant, in °C. T′(t) is its derivative, meaning its instantaneous rate of change, in °C/min. A is the constant ambient temperature. k is positive, in reciprocal minutes. The difference T(t) − A is a temperature difference, in °C.

    T′(t)=−k(T(t)−A)

    This differential equation relates the unknown function to its derivative. We assume a uniform temperature in the cup, a room kept at the same temperature, no added heating and a differentiable change over time. The model simplifies heat transfer; its coefficient has not been measured here.

    To construct the map before knowing T, we use θ for a possible temperature, in °C, and f(t, θ) for the required slope at the point with coordinates (t, θ). θ is read as “theta”. It is not a second temperature actually observed.

    f(t,θ)=−k(θ−A)=k(A−θ)

  2. Calculate the slope at the start

    Point P has coordinates (0 min, 50 °C). The difference from the room temperature is 50 − 20 = 30 °C. The minus sign in the rule turns this positive difference into a negative rate: the cup begins to cool.

    f(0,50)=−110×(50−20)=-3 °C/min

    Check using another expression: A − θ = -30 °C, then k × (A − θ) again gives -3 °C/min. The units multiply correctly: reciprocal minutes times a difference in °C gives °C/min. This slope is not a temperature.

  3. Fill in the map without inventing a trajectory

    At θ = 40 °C: A − θ = -20 °C, so f = -2 °C/min. At θ = 30 °C: the signed difference is -10 °C, so f = -1 °C/min. Each multiplication uses the same coefficient k = 0.10 per minute.

    At θ = A = 20 °C, the difference is 0 °C and f = 0 °C/min: the segment is horizontal. At θ = 10 °C, A − θ = 10 °C and f = 1 °C/min: the segment rises.

    Repeat these directions at times 0, 2 and 4 min. The expression f does not explicitly contain t, so a given temperature has the same slope at each of these instants. This does not mean that the cup stays on that row: when its temperature changes, it encounters another slope.

    Checking the signs confirms the intuition: the slope is negative above A, 0 at A and positive below it. The magnitude of the slope increases with the distance from A. A row of segments corresponds to several possible states; joining them horizontally would generally not give a solution.

  4. Use the tangent to move forward a little

    Let s = f(0, 50) denote the starting slope, in °C/min. For a short duration h, in minutes, linear approximation locally replaces the curve with its tangent. Here ΔT denotes the temperature change on this line; L(h), its temperature after h. The symbol ≈ means “approximately equal”.

    ΔT=sh;L(h)=T(0)+sh;T(h)≈L(h)

    Over h = 0.50 min, the tangent gives ΔT = -3 × 0.50 = -3/2 °C, or -1.50 °C. Hence L(h) = 50 + (-3/2) = 97/2 °C, or 48.50 °C.

    This is an exact result on the line and an approximation for the cup. Check: (48.50 − 50)/0.50 = -3 °C/min, the required slope. The predicted value is below the starting temperature and above A, which is consistent here. None of these checks provides a bound on the error for the curve.

  5. Recognize what a solution would have to satisfy

    A candidate function must satisfy T′(t) = f(t, T(t)) at every instant of its interval, and pass through the initial point if one is specified. A correct tangency only at P is not enough. The last exercise will show that a line fails even though it starts at P with the correct slope.

    We can already check a simple case without solving the equation: the constant function T(t) = A has derivative 0 °C/min, equal to f(t, A) = 0 °C/min. It satisfies the rule, but not our starting temperature of 50 °C.

    The sampled field helps us read local directions. This construction proves neither the existence nor the uniqueness of a curve passing through an arbitrary point; those questions come in the next unit. We have neither solved the full evolution of the cup nor studied the stability of an equilibrium.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The slope -3 means that the cup is at -3 °C.”

    The point gives a temperature of 50 °C; the segment gives its derivative, -3 °C/min. To obtain a temperature change on the tangent, multiply this slope by a duration in minutes.

    Reread the units and the tangent triangle

  • “The segments in a given row are identical: the cup keeps its temperature.”

    The row groups different possible points at the same temperature. A solution must follow the local directions: on rows where the slope is not 0, a horizontal curve would contradict the field.

    Review the map of possible states

  • “I extend the initial slope: the resulting line is the solution.”

    The tangent keeps its initial slope; the field requires a new slope when the temperature changes. Test the derivative of the line at the next point, as in the last exercise. A good tangent at the start does not prove that a curve is a solution on an interval.

    Test the line away from the starting point

Exercise 1 · Local application

Add a segment between the rows

In the same model, consider the point (2 min, 35 °C). Calculate its slope, with its unit, and draw its direction. At the point (4 min, 35 °C), would a different slope be needed? Justify your answer without following a fictional trajectory between these points.

Hint 1

The difference to calculate is A − θ. Time appears in the coordinates, but not in the expression for our rule.

Hint 2

Also compare your slope with those of the 30 °C and 40 °C rows.

Compare with the solution

At 35 °C, the difference above room temperature is 15 °C; A − θ is therefore -15 °C. Multiplication by k gives -3/2 °C/min, or -1.50 °C/min. The segment slopes downwards when read towards the right.

The slope is the same at time 4 min, because f does not depend explicitly on time. We are comparing possible points, without claiming that the actual cup passes through both. Check: 35 °C is halfway between 30 and 40 °C, and the slope is halfway between -1 and -2 °C/min, since the rule is linear in temperature with a constant term.

Check your reasoning

  • Write the signed difference before multiplying and obtain -1.50 °C/min.
  • Distinguish the direction of the segment from the temperature at the point.
  • Justify the equality of the slopes using the expression for f, without confusing the points with observations.

Exercise 2 · Reconstructing the field

Recover the erased coefficient

The rule remains f(t, θ) = −k(θ − A), with A = 20 °C, but k has been erased. We know that the slope at 40 °C is -2 °C/min. Recover k with its unit, then the slopes at 10 °C and at A. Check by substituting k into the original information.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At 40 °C, first calculate the distance from the ambient temperature. The absolute value of the cooling rate is k times this distance.

Hint 2

Divide 2 °C/min by 20 °C. The quotient must have units of reciprocal minutes.

Compare with the solution

The difference is 40 − 20 = 20 °C. The absolute value of the slope is 2 °C/min. Therefore k = 2/20 = 1/10 per minute, or 0.10 per minute. The negative sign comes from the rule; k itself is positive.

At 10 °C, A − θ is 10 °C: the slope is 1 °C/min. At A, the difference is 0 °C and the slope is 0 °C/min. Check against the given information: −(1/10) × 20 = -2 °C/min. We recover the same field, without having observed the path of the cup.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover a positive k and explain its unit.
  • Give an upward slope below A and a 0 slope at A.
  • Substitute k back into the original information to check the calculation.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and counterexample

Can a good tangent be a bad solution?

With the initial coefficient k = 1/10 per minute, we propose g(t) = 50 + (-3)t, where t is expressed in minutes and g(t) in °C. This line passes through the correct starting point. Compare its derivative with the field at time 0, then at time 2 min. Is it a solution on the interval between these instants? Explain what this failure changes about the local calculation over h = 0.50 min.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The derivative of g is constant. At the second instant, calculate g(t) before evaluating f(t, g(t)).

Hint 2

A solution must satisfy the equation at every instant of the interval. A single disagreement is enough to reject a candidate.

Compare with the solution

The derivative g′ is -3 °C/min everywhere. At the start, g(0) = 50 °C and f(0, g(0)) = -3 °C/min: the initial value and slope are correct.

At time 2 min, the change on the line is -3 × 2 = -6 °C, so g(2) = 44 °C. The field requires k × (20 − 44) = (1/10) × (-24) = -12/5 °C/min there, or -2.40 °C/min.

But g′ is still -3 °C/min. The difference g′ − f is -3/5 °C/min, different from 0: the line fails the test, so it does not solve the equation on this interval. This difference compares derivatives at the same point; it is not the temperature error relative to the unknown solution.

The calculation at h = 0.50 min remains exact on the tangent and only approximate for the cup. The disagreement does not rule out a local approximation; it rules out presenting this line as an exact solution or assigning it a bounded error without further argument.

Check your reasoning

  • Check the value and slope at the start, then test another point.
  • Obtain 44 °C and compare -2.40 with -3 °C/min.
  • Reject the exact solution while retaining the meaning of a local approximation.
  • Do not call the difference between derivatives a temperature error.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do a point and its short segment represent in the direction field for the cup?

The point specifies a time and a possible temperature. The segment gives the slope f(t, θ) required by the model for a curve passing through that point; it does not represent a reading or a future temperature.

Recall 2Without numerical calculation, work out the direction of the segments above A, at A and below it.

With k positive, f = k(A − θ) is negative above A, 0 at A and positive below it. The segments slope downwards, are horizontal or slope upwards as time advances.

Recall 3Why do the segments repeat along a given row, even though the cup does not keep that temperature?

Our f depends on temperature, without depending explicitly on time. The row therefore compares possible points at the same temperature; a cup whose temperature changes leaves that row and encounters other slopes.

Recall 4How does a slope in °C/min give an approximate temperature, and where does the approximation occur?

Multiply the slope by a short duration in minutes to obtain a change in °C, then add the starting temperature. The calculation is exact on the tangent; replacing the curve with this line is the approximation. No error bound follows from this alone.

Recall 5What test distinguishes a solution from a curve with the correct direction only at the start?

Check that the derivative of the candidate equals f(t, temperature of the candidate) at every instant of the interval, and that it passes through the required starting point. A single point of disagreement is enough to reject the candidate as a solution on that interval.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct this sequence: name the axes and their units; define the rule that gives the derivative; evaluate this rule at a point; check the sign and unit; convert a slope into a small change on the tangent; say what would have to be checked over an entire interval to call a curve a solution. Then redo the calculation and the counterexample from memory. Reading, recognizing a correction or answering after a hint never amounts to achieving a level.
