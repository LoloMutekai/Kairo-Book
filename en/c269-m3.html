Level 4 · C269·M3

Effects and uncertainty

Your goalExtract an effect in its unit, reconstruct its interval, and write a conclusion that distinguishes direction, precision, and practical importance.

Before you start
c225-m5 : report and limitations · c224-m5 : limits of documentary evidence · c145-m5 : policy evaluation · c261-m5 : model uncertainty · c009-m5 : reading notes · c269-m1 : question and search · c269-m2 : study selection

✿01 See the idea

In a review of attention training, a report describes faster responses after training. The mean time is 500 ms in the control group and 488 ms in the trained group. The observed difference is 12 ms. But obtaining a difference in this sample does not give its exact value in the target population.

Imagine a marker on a ruler: the point marks our estimate; the line segment shows the sampling uncertainty calculated around it. A short segment means a more precise estimate under the stated assumptions. However, it may be in the wrong place if the measurement or design is biased.

The 0 ms marker means “no mean difference”. A second marker, set at 10 ms before reading the results, represents a reduction judged useful for this task. Our segment lies on the favorable side of 0 but crosses this second marker: establishing a direction and establishing sufficient importance require different conclusions.

The entire scenario and all its values are fictional. No real article is summarized and no real gain is claimed. The threshold for usefulness is a teaching convention with no practical validation. Study selection is assumed to be complete; this unit teaches how to read a selected result, without constructing a meta-analysis.

The rule to remember

Always report the contrast together with its direction, unit, interval, method, and the limitations that prevent a stronger conclusion.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What can this difference of 12 ms tell us?

The fictional report compares separate groups randomly assigned to training or a control activity of the same duration. Each includes 100 people in the analysis. The planned outcome is each person’s mean time for correct responses on the same final task. The supplied summaries are: means of 500 and 488 ms; standard deviations of 30 and 40 ms; reported standard error of 5 ms. Let us check this standard error, then construct the approximate 95 % normal interval. The document does not provide response accuracy: we draw conclusions about the measured time, without concluding that attention has improved overall.

  1. Define the effect the review aims to estimate

    T denotes the control group and E the trained group. μ_T and μ_E are the unknown means in the populations corresponding to this design and task, in milliseconds (ms). Our target Δ = μ_T − μ_E is their difference. The observed means, written x̄_T and x̄_E, estimate it as Δ̂: the hat indicates an estimate.

    Δ^=x¯T−x¯E=500−488=12 ms

    Subtracting in this order makes a reduction in time positive. 0 ms means equal means, rather than an absence of individual variation. We keep milliseconds so that the size of the difference remains interpretable for this task. An effect measured on another score would require a justified mapping before any numerical comparison.

  2. Check what the uncertainty measures

    n_T and n_E are the numbers of people, with no physical unit; here each is 100. s_T and s_E are the standard deviations between people, in ms. Their squares estimate variances in ms². The standard error u estimates the spread of Δ̂ if the same sampling were repeated; it is also in ms. It does not describe the spread of individual times.

    u=sT2nT+sE2nE

    For independent people within each group, the variance of the sample mean is estimated by the squared standard deviation between people divided by the sample size. For independent groups, the variances of the means add even when the means are subtracted. We obtain 900/100 = 9 ms² and 1600/100 = 16 ms², then 9 + 16 = 25 ms². Its positive square root is 5 ms: 5 × 5 = 25 ms² confirms the reported value.

    We assume comparable measurements, finite variability, and an acceptable normal approximation for this difference in means. Having 100 people per group does not by itself guarantee this approximation. Repeated trials from the same person do not count as that many independent people. A paired design would require accounting for dependence, and therefore a different estimate of u.

  3. Move from the standard error to the margin

    The chosen interval is Δ̂ ± k u. The symbol ± means subtracting and then adding the same margin; k is a coefficient with no unit. For the central 95 % interval under the normal approximation, we use the rounded coefficient k = 1.96 = 196/100. This coefficient comes from the method and is not estimated from the means.

    m=ku=1.96×5=9.8 ms

    m denotes the margin, in ms. Multiplying by k scales up the standard error to construct the chosen two-sided interval. The exact fractional value of this margin is 49/5 ms; no intermediate rounding is needed. With estimated standard deviations, this normal interval remains approximate. A Welch interval would use a coefficient from a Student’s t distribution with appropriate degrees of freedom, rather than our fixed k.

  4. Calculate the bounds and check the segment

    L and U denote the lower and upper bounds, respectively, in ms. Subtract the margin from the effect to obtain L; add it to obtain U.

    L=12−9.8=2.2 ms;U=12+9.8=21.8 ms

    Result: Δ̂ = 12 ms; approximate 95 % normal interval: [2.2 ; 21.8] ms. Check the center: (2.2 + 21.8)/2 = 12 ms. Check the width: 21.8 − 2.2 = 19.6 ms, or 2 × 9.8 ms. These checks detect a sign error or a margin added on only one side.

  5. Separate direction from practical importance

    The entire interval is above 0 ms. Under this design and approximation, the results support a favorable mean difference in response time. This does not fix the effect at 12 ms: in the sense of this procedure, the data remain compatible with a much smaller or larger reduction.

    The threshold for usefulness τ, the Greek letter tau, is 10 ms and was set before the result for this exercise. Since 2.2 < 10 < 21.8, the interval includes effects below and above τ. We therefore do not establish that the reduction exceeds this threshold; nor do we establish that it is below it. The point estimate above τ does not resolve this uncertainty.

    If an interval crossed 0, we would need to examine the effect sizes it still allows, rather than write “no effect”. A lack of sufficient support for a difference and evidence of a negligible effect are different conclusions. The latter would require a range of negligible effects defined in advance and suitable precision.

  6. Write the review entry and state its scope

    A defensible wording is: “Fictional study, independent groups, 100 people per group; control − training contrast in time for correct responses: 12 ms, approximate 95 % normal interval [2.2 ; 21.8] ms. Favorable direction under the assumptions; exceeding the teaching threshold of 10 ms remains uncertain; response accuracy is undocumented.” In a real review, the study reference and exact location of these data would need to be added.

    The 95 % level describes the procedure: if the design and assumptions were repeated, about 95 % of intervals constructed this way would cover the fixed target difference. Once calculated, this interval either contains that difference or does not; we do not assign it a probability of 95 % here. This segment does not contain 95 % of the people either.

    This uncertainty describes sampling under the model. It does not automatically quantify systematic timing error, different exclusions between groups, or a speed–accuracy trade-off. The reported randomization supports interpretation of the contrast, but adherence to it, handling of missing data, and measurement quality would require their own evidence. A narrow interval does not replace that evidence.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The segment contains the times of almost everyone.”

    The segment concerns Δ, a difference in means. The standard deviations s describe the spread between people; the standard error u describes uncertainty in the estimate. Identify their units and what varies for each quantity.

    Revisit the definition of standard error

  • “0 is excluded, so training must provide a useful improvement.”

    Also compare the entire interval with the threshold τ. Here it crosses 10 ms; a reduction too small for this criterion remains compatible with the results. Response accuracy must also be examined before discussing overall improvement.

    Review the 0 and usefulness markers

  • “There is a 95 % chance that the true effect is in this interval.”

    In the frequentist interpretation used here, 95 % describes the repeated coverage of a procedure under its assumptions. Rewrite the sentence by varying the samples and their intervals, rather than assigning a probability to the fixed parameter.

    Reread the scope of the conclusion

The report ultimately chooses the training − control contrast. Without changing the observations, give the effect and its approximate 95 % interval. Has the interpretation of time changed?

Multiplying an interval by a negative number reverses the order of its bounds.

Hint 2

The new lower bound is the negative of the old upper bound.

Compare with the solution

New effect: 488 − 500 = -12 ms. The bounds become 0 − 21.8 = -21.8 ms and 0 − 2.2 = -2.2 ms. The interval is therefore [-21.8 ; -2.2] ms, with the smaller bound on the left.

Check: -12 − 9.8 = -21.8 and -12 + 9.8 = -2.2 ms. The trained group’s time is still shorter: the physical meaning is the same. The subtraction convention now makes a reduction negative; the corresponding favorable threshold would be −10 ms.

Exercise 2 · Independent calculation · conditional projection

Do more people make the effect larger?

To plan a more precise study in the same scenario, replace each sample size with 400 people. Assume the same means and standard deviations solely for this calculation. Recalculate u, the margin, and the interval using the same k. Compare the width and draw a conclusion relative to the 10 ms threshold. This is a conditional projection, without predicting future results.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The sample size is multiplied by 4; each contribution to the variance of the difference is divided by 4.

Hint 2

The square root of this factor is 2. Check the new standard error by squaring its value.

Compare with the solution

Contributions: 900/400 = 2.25 ms² and 1600/400 = 4.00 ms²; sum 6.25 ms². Thus u = 2.5 ms, since 2.5 × 2.5 = 6.25 ms². The sample sizes were multiplied by 4, and the standard error was divided by 2.

Margin: 1.96 × 2.5 = 4.9 ms. Effect unchanged under our assumption: 12 ms. Bounds: 12 − 4.9 = 7.1 and 12 + 4.9 = 16.9 ms. The interval is [7.1 ; 16.9] ms.

Its width is 9.8 ms, compared with 19.6 ms previously; their ratio is 0.5. The center remains 12 ms: precision increases in this projection, without an increase in the assumed effect. The 10 ms threshold remains inside the segment; exceeding this threshold is still not established. More people do not automatically correct measurement bias.

Check your reasoning

  • Divide the variances by the sample size factor, then take the positive square root of their sum.
  • Calculate the new margin and bounds without shifting the center.
  • Compare the entire segment with the threshold and recall that the projection is conditional.

Correct an overstated conclusion

A note about the initial fictional study claims: “The effect is 12 ms; its interval excludes 0, so the useful threshold of 10 ms is exceeded and attention is improved.” Write a corrected result entry, then respond to “the interval has already resolved all uncertainties”. Do not add any result absent from the report.

Possible correction: “Control − training difference estimated at 12 ms in time for correct responses; approximate 95 % normal interval [2.2 ; 21.8] ms, independent groups of 100 people. The results support a mean reduction in time under the assumptions, but do not settle whether the fictional 10 ms threshold is exceeded.”

The report does not provide response accuracy. This conclusion about time cannot be converted into an overall improvement in attention or evidence of a cognitive mechanism. The segment quantifies conditional sampling uncertainty; it does not validate the timing, exclusions, or adherence to the design.

To answer the objection, separate what can already be calculated from what needs documentation: outcome definition, handling of excluded trials and people, comparability of measurements, and response accuracy. Here, no additional data are available; these points remain to be checked, without turning them into invented results.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be defined before interpreting the sign of an effect?

The measured quantity, its unit, and the order of subtraction. Here Δ = control mean − training mean, in ms; a positive effect corresponds to a shorter time in the trained group.

Recall 2How does the standard deviation of individual values differ from the standard error of the effect?

The standard deviation describes the spread between people. The standard error estimates the spread of the effect estimate across repeated samples under the design. Both are in ms here, but concern different things.

Recall 3What does the 95 % level of our approximate interval mean?

Under the assumptions and with repeated use of the procedure, about 95 % of intervals would cover the fixed target difference. This level does not assign a probability to that parameter within the interval already obtained and does not describe the distribution of people.

Recall 4Why is excluding 0 insufficient to establish that the useful threshold is exceeded?

The bounds must also be compared with the threshold set in advance. Here [2.2 ; 21.8] ms excludes 0 but crosses 10 ms: reductions below and above the threshold remain compatible with the results.

Recall 5What limitation does a narrower interval fail to resolve on its own?

Measurement or exclusion bias, for example. Better sampling precision does not demonstrate the validity of the design, the absence of a speed–accuracy trade-off, or a cognitive mechanism.
