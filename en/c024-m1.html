Population and sample

Your goalDefine the group you want to describe, identify the part observed and give each proportion the correct denominator.

Before you start
c003-m5 : scientific notation · c006-m5 : composition · c009-m5 : reading notes · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales

✿01 See the idea

At closing time in a library, the book cart holds the 24 books returned this Monday. You want to know which ones are damaged. You examine the 6 books nearest to you, then stop. What you have seen concerns these books; your question concerns the entire cart. Keep this difference in mind before counting.

The population is the entire group the question concerns: here, the books returned this Monday at this library. The word does not necessarily refer to people. An individual, or statistical unit, is an element of this group: here, 1 physical book. Copies of the same title remain distinct individuals.

The sample is the part of this population actually examined: here, the 6 books. The variable is what you record for each individual. We record “damaged” if it has at least 1 torn page, and “intact” otherwise. These words describe only this criterion. Knowing the list of all the books does not mean knowing their condition.

The entire scene and all its values are fictional. We assume a fixed batch, distinct books, no duplicate entries in the notebook and condition recorded without error using the same criterion. Examining all the books in the population would be a census. Examining a part does not guarantee that it resembles the whole: accessible books may differ from those underneath. Data collection designs will be covered in the next unit.

The rule to remember

First write “which elements, where and when?” Then count those you have actually observed. A proportion describes the group in its denominator; it does not automatically describe a larger group.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Describe what the notebook lets us know

The fictional batch contains 24 books. The 6 examined have the identifiers A, C, F, J, P and X. The notebook records: A damaged; C intact; F intact; J intact; P damaged; X intact. No condition has been recorded for the other books. What part of the batch has been examined, and what do we know about its condition?

  1. Define the group before counting

    The population includes all the books returned this Monday, rather than all the books in the library or those that will be returned tomorrow. We call its size N, meaning its number of individuals. Here N = 24 books. The lowercase letter n denotes the sample size: n = 6 books.

    The letter d counts the damaged books observed: d = 2 books, those labelled A and P. We count books, rather than different titles, pages or copied entries. The conditions are 0 < n ≤ N and 0 ≤ d ≤ n; they express a nonempty sample contained in the batch.

  2. Separate observed and unobserved books

    Each book belongs either to the sample or to the unexamined part. Subtracting the observed count from the total count therefore gives the number of books whose condition remains unknown.

    N−n=24−6=18 livres non examinés

  3. Measure the proportion of the batch examined

    Let f be the fraction of the batch examined. The numerator counts the books examined; the denominator counts all the books in the target group. Books divided by books gives a proportion with no unit. Multiplying a proportion by 100 expresses it as a percentage.

    f=nN=624=0.25

    The exact fraction is 1/4; here the decimal quotient is exact. The proportion examined is 0.25 × 100 = 25 %. This percentage concerns the quantity examined, rather than its condition.

  4. Describe condition within the sample

    We call p the proportion of damaged books among those examined. We must divide d by n because we know the condition of the books in this sample. This p describes the observed group; we give it no probabilistic interpretation.

    p=dn=26≈0.333

    The exact fraction is 1/3, or approximately 33.3 %. The symbol ≈ means “approximately equal”: the decimal representation has been rounded. The correct statement is “2 of the 6 books examined are damaged”. The 18 others could have a different distribution.

  5. Check the counts and the scope of the answer

    Reading the notebook shows that A and P are damaged; C, F, J and X are intact according to our criterion. By subtraction, n − d = 6 − 2 = 4 intact books. The two methods agree.

    Within the sample: 2 + 4 = 6 books. Within the population: 6 + 18 = 24 books. The proportions f and p are between 0 and 1, but these arithmetic checks do not prove that the sample represents the batch.

    The notebook shows that 25 % of the batch has been examined; approximately 33.3 % of the sample is damaged; the total number of damaged books in the batch remains unknown. Choosing the nearest books does not justify automatically extending their proportion to all the books.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The population is the books I examined.”

    Return to the question: it concerns all the books returned this Monday. The books examined form the sample. A group becomes the population when it is the target group, rather than simply because it is accessible.

    Review the nested boxes

  • “To describe the damaged books in the sample, I divide by the entire batch.”

    The observed proportion compares 2 damaged books with 6 books whose condition has been examined. Dividing by 24 would count the damaged books already identified relative to the batch; it would give no information about the condition of the unexamined books. Name the reference group before dividing.

    Revisit the two quotients

  • “I examined a large part, so the entire batch has the same condition.”

    The number examined is not enough. The books near you may differ from the others. Construct two possible sets of conditions for the unexamined books: if they give different totals without contradicting the notebook, the total for the batch has not been established.

Exercise 2 · Numerical application

Examine more books from the same batch

Start from the original notebook. You examine 3 additional books, all different from those examined before and all intact. The batch remains fixed. Calculate the new count examined, the number still to examine, the proportion of the batch examined and the proportion damaged among those examined. Keep exact fractions, then give percentages to one decimal place.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The new books already belong to the batch: N does not change. No damaged book is added to the notebook.

Hint 2

Both proportions use the new count examined, but in different positions.

Compare with the solution

The sample reaches 6 + 3 = 9 books. There remain 24 − 9 = 15 unexamined books. The batch still contains 24 books because examining a book does not mean adding a returned book.

The proportion of the batch examined becomes 9/24 = 3/8, or 37.5 %. The number of damaged books observed remains 2; their proportion in the sample becomes 2/9 = 2/9, or approximately 22.2 %.

Check: 4 + 3 = 7 intact books, so 2 + 7 = 9 books examined. The proportion examined increases while the observed proportion damaged decreases. These changes answer different questions; neither alone gives the condition of the 15 remaining books.

Check your reasoning

  • You keep N = 24 and d = 2, and obtain n = 9.
  • You relate 3/8 to the batch and 2/9 to the enlarged sample.
  • You keep exact fractions and state that the inexact percentage is rounded.

Exercise 3 · Justification using a counterexample

The same notebook, different batches

Return to just the 6 books in the original notebook, before the additional examination. A colleague states: “The proportion damaged in the entire batch must be 1/3.” First imagine that all the unexamined books are intact, then that they are all damaged. Calculate the two total numbers of damaged books. Do these two situations contradict the notebook? What do you tell your colleague?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Keep the conditions of the books already examined. You can change only those of the 18 others.

Hint 2

In one situation, no damaged book is added; in the other, every unexamined book adds a damaged book.

Compare with the solution

If all the unexamined books are intact, the entire batch contains 2 + 0 = 2 damaged books. If all are damaged, the total becomes 2 + 18 = 20 damaged books. The 4 intact books already seen remain intact in both situations.

Both situations agree with exactly the same notebook. It therefore does not distinguish between their different totals. The proportion 1/3 correctly describes the original sample; it could coincide with the proportion in the batch, but the observations do not require this.

A defensible answer: “We observed 2 damaged books out of 6 examined. For the entire batch, the conditions of the 18 others are still missing.” These imagined situations show a limit of the information; they do not say which situation actually occurs.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain 2 and 20 without changing the conditions already observed.
  • You explain why identical observations can leave several possible totals.
  • You replace the claim about the batch with a description explicitly limited to the sample.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you define a population before counting?

Name the elements the question concerns, the place and the time period. Here, all the books returned this Monday at the library, including those whose condition remains unknown.

Recall 2What is the difference between the individual and the variable in the cart?

The individual is a physical book. The variable is its condition according to the chosen criterion: at least 1 torn page, or none.

Recall 3What distinguishes a sample from a census?

Here the sample covers part of the target group; the census examines all its individuals. Having examined an entire small population does not automatically provide information about a larger population.

Recall 4What do n/N and d/n compare?

N counts the population, n the books examined and d the damaged books observed. n/N is the proportion of the batch examined; d/n is the proportion damaged among those examined. Both proportions have no unit.

Recall 5Why does the notebook not give the proportion damaged in the entire batch?

The conditions of the unexamined books are missing. Several distributions remain compatible with the same observations; the number examined and a correct quotient do not prove that this part resembles the entire batch.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the sequence: question and population boundary; book as individual and condition as variable; sample actually examined; counts N, n and d; reference group for each quotient; justified conclusion and missing information. Then explain what would change if the question concerned only the books examined.

What you understand
A count describes the elements actually retained. To relate these elements to a target population, you must also know where they came from and what was excluded. The cart illustrates the distinction; it does not describe actual data collection in the project.
