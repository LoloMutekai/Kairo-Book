Level 3 · C164·M1

Sigma-algebras

Your goalDecide whether a family of subsets of a set is a sigma-algebra, produce a missing subset that prevents it from being one, and construct the smallest sigma-algebra containing a given subset of a finite set.

Before you start
c143-m5 : uniform convergence · c058-m5 : change of variables · c012-m5 : counterexamples

✿01 See the idea

A drawer contains 4 imaginary cards, labelled a, b, c and d. Someone takes a card without showing it. A reader cannot read the labels: they can only see which half of the drawer the card comes from, the left half containing a and b, or the right half containing c and d. Which yes-or-no questions about the card can they decide?

They can answer “Is the card on the left?”, “Is it on the right?”, “Is it somewhere in the drawer?” (always yes) and “Is it nowhere in the drawer?” (always no). They cannot answer “Is it card a?”, because they cannot distinguish a from b. Each question corresponds to a subset of the set Ω of cards: the cards for which the answer is yes. Cards that cannot be distinguished form a block.

A sigma-algebra is the list of subsets about which we allow ourselves to ask a question. It follows three common-sense rules: we can always ask about “any card”; if we can ask a question, we can ask its negation, which corresponds to the complement of the subset; if we can ask a sequence of questions, we can ask “Is at least one of them true?”, which means forming their union.

In a finite set, a sequence of subsets has only finitely many distinct subsets: the drawer example therefore never tests the word “countable”. An exercise on integers will do that. The scene is imaginary, with no actual draw or probability: we only count subsets. Assigning a weight to these subsets belongs to the next unit, not this one.

The rule to remember

A sigma-algebra ℱ on Ω is a family of subsets of Ω that contains Ω and is closed under complements and countable unions; to test it, look for a subset whose complement is missing, or a union of subsets that is missing.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Four imaginary cards, two halves of a drawer

On Ω = { a, b, c, d }, test two families of subsets: U = { ∅, { a, b }, { c, d }, Ω } and V = { ∅, { a, b }, { b, c }, Ω }. Then construct the smallest sigma-algebra W containing { a }. The cards are imaginary; the task is only to count subsets, with no random draw.

  1. Define the vocabulary and assumptions

    Ω is a nonempty set, here the set of 4 cards. A subset A of Ω is a set contained in Ω; its complement is Ω ∖ A, consisting of the cards in Ω that are not in A. |A| denotes the number of cards in A, measured in cards. The family ℱ is a set of subsets of Ω: its elements are subsets, not cards.

    pleinΩ∈ℱcomplémentaireA∈ℱ⟹Ω∖A∈ℱunion dénombrable(An)⊂ℱ⟹⋃n=1∞An∈ℱ

    In the third row, (An) is a sequence of subsets indexed by integers n ≥ 1. The three rows form the definition; the properties below follow from them but add no further conditions. Since Ω is finite, we will only write unions of 2 subsets; the checking step will explain what this choice does not test.

  2. Test U: complements and unions

    Ω is in U by construction. For complements, count rather than list: the complement of A contains |Ω| − |A| cards.

    |Ω∖A|=|Ω|−|A|
    |{c,d}|=4−2=2 fiches

    This is indeed the number of cards in { c, d }, and this complement is in U; the complement of Ω is empty (0 cards), so ∅ is in U, and conversely. Unions: { a, b } ∪ { c, d } = Ω, taking a union with ∅ changes nothing, and taking a union with Ω gives Ω. U has only 4 subsets: any sequence of subsets in U has the same union as at most 4 of its terms, so it has already been checked. U is a sigma-algebra with 4 elements.

  3. Find the counterexample in V

    V contains { a, b } but not its complement { c, d }: the second rule already fails. Let us also examine the union, using the counting formula for two subsets that may overlap.

    |A∪B|=|A|+|B|−|A∩B|
    |{a,b,c}|=2+2−1=4−1=3 fiches

    The subsets in V have 0, 2, 2 and 4 cards: none has 3 cards, so { a, b, c } is not in V. A single missing subset is enough to disprove the property; this is the logic of a counterexample, which refutes a rule asserted “for every subset”. V is not a sigma-algebra.

  4. Construct the smallest sigma-algebra containing { a }

    Start with { a }. The whole-set rule requires Ω; the complement rule requires ∅ and the complement of { a }, whose size is 4 − 1 = 3 cards, namely { b, c, d }. Unions create nothing new: { a } ∪ { b, c, d } = Ω.

    W={∅,{a},{b,c,d},Ω}

    W has 4 subsets. The rules require every sigma-algebra containing { a } to contain these 4 subsets, and W itself is closed under the required operations: it is therefore the smallest. Check: the two blocks { a } and { b, c, d } have 1 + 3 = 4 cards, meaning all the cards in Ω, with no overlap.

  5. Count the decidable subsets

    Suppose Ω is covered by k disjoint nonempty blocks. A decidable subset is a union of blocks: each block is included or excluded, giving 2 choices per block. Different selections of blocks give different subsets, because the blocks are disjoint and nonempty; their complements are the unions of the remaining blocks.

    |ℱ|=2k

    With 2, 3 and then 4 blocks for our 4 cards, we obtain:

    2×2=4, 4×2=8, 8×2=16

    There are 16 subsets of Ω in total; the sigma-algebra for the halves of the drawer has 4, so 12 subsets, such as { a }, remain beyond what the reader can distinguish. This accounts for 0.25 of the subsets: a simple count, not a probability.

  6. Check the example and state its limits

    First check: ∅ belongs to every sigma-algebra, because it is the complement of Ω. Second: in U, W and the full sigma-algebra, the complement of an element is an element, as is the union of two elements. Third: the calculation accompanying this page enumerates the 16 subsets again using bit encoding and mechanically closes each family under the required operations; it reproduces these counts.

    Limitation: this finite check does not test infinite unions. It therefore does not prove that a rule valid for two subsets is enough for an infinite sequence; the exercise on integers shows that it is not.

Explain it in your own words

The elements of V are perfectly valid subsets of Ω: why is V not a sigma-algebra? Name the rule that fails and the missing subset without rereading. Then explain why the finite case does not test the word “countable”.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A family closed under unions of two subsets is already a sigma-algebra.”

    In an infinite set, the rule applies to sequences. On the integers, the subsets that are finite or have finite complements are closed under unions of two subsets but not under countable unions: return to the last exercise and exhibit the sequence that makes the rule fail.

    Return to the exercises

  • “The elements of a sigma-algebra are the points of the set.”

    ℱ is a family of subsets. The card a belongs to Ω, but { a } is a subset; { a } is absent from U even though a is in Ω. Always check the level: point, subset, family of subsets.

    Review the drawer-halves idea

  • “The smallest sigma-algebra containing { a, b } is { ∅, { a, b }, Ω }.”

    The complement { c, d } is missing: the complement rule requires it. The generated sigma-algebra includes ∅, { a, b }, { c, d } and Ω. After each addition, test complements and then unions again.

    Work through the construction of W again

Exercise 1 · Explained application

Only three cards

On Ψ = { a, b, c }, three imaginary cards, let G = { ∅, { a }, { b, c }, Ψ }. Is G a sigma-algebra? How many subsets of Ψ do not belong to G?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Count the cards in the complement of { a } rather than guessing it.

Hint 2

To count all subsets of Ψ, decide for each card whether it is in the subset or not.

Compare with the solution

The whole set Ψ is in G. The complement of { a } has 3 − 1 = 2 cards; it is { b, c }, which is in G, and conversely. The complement of Ψ is ∅, and the complement of ∅ is Ψ. Union: { a } ∪ { b, c } = Ψ. Since G has only 4 subsets, these checks suffice: G is a sigma-algebra.

Subsets of Ψ: 2 × 2 × 2 = 8, with an in-or-out choice for each card. Missing from G: 8 − 4 = 4, namely { b }, { c }, { a, b } and { a, c }. Check: these four subsets are indeed distinct from those in G.

Check your reasoning

  • Check all three rules, not just closure under complements.
  • Justify the count using a binary choice for each card.
  • List the missing subsets, not just their number.

Exercise 2 · Guided transfer

Complete a family

On Ψ = { a, b, c }, let H = { ∅, { a }, { b }, Ψ }. Show that H is not a sigma-algebra by naming a missing subset. Then give the sigma-algebra generated by { a } and { b }, and the number of subsets to add to H.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Find the complement of { a } in Ψ.

Hint 2

After adding a complement, look for unions of two subsets, then the complements of the new subsets.

Compare with the solution

The complement of { a } has 2 cards: it is { b, c }, which is absent from H. The family H therefore violates the complement rule. Another failure: { a } ∪ { b } = { a, b } is also absent, and its complement has 3 − 2 = 1 card, namely { c }.

We now have { a }, { b } and { c }, the three blocks of one card each: the generated sigma-algebra contains all unions of blocks, giving 8 subsets, meaning all subsets of Ψ. H has 4: we must add 8 − 4 = 4, namely { c }, { a, b }, { a, c } and { b, c }.

Check your reasoning

  • Explicitly name a missing subset: this is a counterexample.
  • Repeat the checks after each addition: complements, then unions.
  • Relate the result to the blocks, rather than to a random attempt.

Exercise 3 · Reasoning with a counterexample

Why “countable” matters

Let Ω be the set of integers k such that k ≥ 1, and let E be the family of subsets of Ω that are finite or have finite complements. Assume that E contains Ω and is closed under complements and unions of two subsets. Is E a sigma-algebra? Consider the singletons containing the integer 2k, for k ≥ 1.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Each singleton is a finite subset: is it in E?

Hint 2

Look at the union of all these singletons, then its complement: are they finite?

Compare with the solution

For every k ≥ 1, the singleton containing the integer 2k is finite, so it is in E. Their countable union is the set of even integers. It is infinite, and its complement, the set of odd integers, is also infinite. The union is therefore neither finite nor does it have a finite complement: it is not in E.

The third rule fails while the other two hold. E is not a sigma-algebra: closure under unions of two subsets does not replace closure under countable unions. This result relies on the stated assumption; it does not identify the smallest sigma-algebra containing all singletons.

Check your reasoning

  • Exhibit the precise sequence of subsets in E whose union is outside E.
  • Justify why neither the union nor its complement is finite.
  • Name the rule that fails and those that hold.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What are the three conditions for a family ℱ of subsets of Ω to be a sigma-algebra?

ℱ contains Ω, contains the complement of each of its elements, and contains the union of every countable sequence of its elements. Its elements are subsets of Ω, not points.

Recall 2Why does every sigma-algebra contain ∅ and the union of two of its elements?

∅ is the complement of Ω. For A and B, the sequence A, B, B, B, … has union A ∪ B, which therefore belongs to the sigma-algebra.

Recall 3What is the smallest sigma-algebra containing a subset A that is neither empty nor equal to Ω?

It consists of ∅, A, the complement of A and Ω: the whole-set rule, the complement rule and unions require nothing more.

Recall 4How many subsets are in the sigma-algebra formed by unions of k disjoint nonempty blocks covering Ω?

2 to the power k, because each block is included or excluded; with k = 4 blocks of one card each, we recover all 16 subsets of Ω.

Recall 5Why does “union of two subsets” not replace “countable union”?

On the integers, the subsets that are finite or have finite complements are closed under unions of two subsets; the union of singletons containing even numbers is infinite and has an infinite complement, so it lies outside the family.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Name Ω and the family ℱ of its subsets; check that Ω is in ℱ; for each subset, look for its complement, then for unions, finite and countable; a single missing subset disproves that the family is a sigma-algebra; to generate one, add Ω, ∅, complements and unions until the family is closed under these operations; count 2 to the power k subsets for k blocks; finally, state what the finite case does not test.
