Level 2 · C050·M1

Cholesky

Your goalBuild a triangular Cholesky factor, justify its conditions, and use it to solve a system without forming an inverse.

Before you start
c048-m5 : positivity · c040-m5 : least squares

✿01 See the idea

A small positioning table has two settings that jointly affect its movement. Increasing the horizontal setting can also move the table vertically. To reach the requested position, we therefore need to determine the settings together, rather than divide each displacement separately by a coefficient.

We use a fictional model of this table. The settings and displacements are normalized: each number is a ratio to a fixed reference, so all quantities on this page are dimensionless. The coefficients are chosen for the calculation; they do not describe a machine that has been measured.

The matrix A contains the coupling coefficients. Cholesky decomposes it into a triangular factor L and its transpose Lᵀ. In a triangular system, one equation directly gives an unknown; we can then substitute its value into the other equation. The coupled problem thus becomes two short passes.

The first diagram shows the shape of the factors. The second follows the information during the solution process. Before reading the results, identify which equation lets you start each pass.

The rule to remember

For a real symmetric positive definite matrix, build A = LLᵀ with L lower triangular and its diagonal strictly positive; then solve Ly = b, followed by Lᵀx = y.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Find the table settings

The fictional model is written Ax = b, with x = (u, v)ᵀ for the two settings. The coefficients of A are 4, 2, 2, 10, and the requested displacement b is (8, 22)ᵀ. Let us build the factor, then find the settings, keeping exact values.

  1. Define the system and the Cholesky requirements

    x is the column of unknown settings u and v; b is the column of requested displacements. A is a real 2 × 2 matrix, assumed to represent our fictional linear model exactly. a and d denote its diagonal coefficients; c denotes the two equal off-diagonal coefficients. A is therefore symmetric: Aᵀ = A. The symbol ᵀ swaps rows and columns; parentheses followed by ᵀ express a column from a horizontal list.

    A=[42210];x=[uv];b=[822]

    A real symmetric matrix is positive definite if zᵀAz > 0 for every nonzero real column z. This condition, reviewed in the prerequisite on positivity, guarantees a unique lower triangular factor L with a strictly positive diagonal such that A = LLᵀ. Here we will also check positivity through a sum of squares. Symmetry alone does not guarantee it.

    All coordinates of x, b, and the test vector z are dimensionless. The coefficients of A, L, and Lᵀ are dimensionless too. The roots below are the positive square roots in the real numbers.

  2. Read the product to see what to find

    Write L with three unknowns: p at the top left, q at the bottom left, and r at the bottom right. The top right entry is 0. The values p and r must be strictly positive. Multiplying a row of L by a column of Lᵀ gives:

    L=[p0qr];LLT=[p2pqpqq2+r2]

    Comparing this product with A gives these relations: the square of p equals a, the product pq equals c, and the sum of the squares of q and r equals d. They impose an order: find p, then q, then r. Squaring means multiplying a number by itself.

  3. Take the first positive root

    The first entry of A must be the square of p. We take its positive root to satisfy the requirements and fix the sign of the factor.

    p=a=4=2

    Check: 2 × 2 = 4, and 2 > 0. Taking the root of every entry of A would not reproduce a matrix product: the off-diagonal entry must satisfy pq = c.

  4. Find the coupling coefficient

    Since pq must give c and p is nonzero, division isolates q. In general, this coefficient can be negative: only the diagonal entries of L are required to be positive.

    q=cp=22=1

    Check: 2 × 1 = 2, the required off-diagonal coefficient.

  5. Remove the part already accounted for and check positivity

    The last entry of LLᵀ adds the square of q and the square of r. The square of q is 1. Subtracting it from d leaves 10 − 1 = 9; it is this strictly positive remainder whose root we must take.

    r=d−q2=9=3

    Check: 3 × 3 = 9 and 1 + 9 = 10. The factor is now fully determined:

    L=[2013]

    To check positive definiteness without assuming the conclusion, take a test vector z = (s, t)ᵀ, with real, dimensionless s and t. Expanding zᵀAz directly gives the sum of squares below. If the sum is 0, the second square forces t to be 0; the first then forces s to be 0. The sum is therefore strictly positive for every nonzero z.

    zTAz=(2s+1t)2+9t2

  6. Forward substitution: solve Ly = b

    Let y = (h, k)ᵀ be the dimensionless intermediate column. Since Ax = L(Lᵀx), we first solve Ly = b. The first row contains only h; the second then uses this known h.

    [2h=81h+3k=22]

    First row: h = 8/2 = 4. In the second row, qh = 1 × 4 = 4. Subtract this known contribution: 22 − 4 = 18, then divide by r: k = 18/3 = 6.

    Check forward substitution: 2 × 4 = 8 and 4 + 3 × 6 = 22. We have y = (4, 6)ᵀ, without having found x yet.

  7. Back substitution: solve Lᵀx = y

    The transpose is upper triangular. Its last row contains only v: this time, start at the bottom, then substitute into the first row.

    LT=[2103];[2u+1v=43v=6]

    Last row: v = 6/3 = 2. The contribution qv is 1 × 2 = 2. First row: subtract qv from h, giving 4 − 2 = 2, then u = 2/2 = 1. The required settings are x = (1, 2)ᵀ, dimensionless.

    L was built using only A: changing the displacement b does not require repeating the factorization. The second exercise reuses this same factor.

  8. Check the factors and settings against the original data

    Reconstructing A checks the factor independently of the substitutions. The first entry is 4, the off-diagonal entries are 2, and the last is 10: we recover every entry of A.

    LLT=[2013][2103]=[42210]

    Returning to the original system then checks the solution. Horizontal displacement: 4 × 1 + 2 × 2 = 4 + 4 = 8. Vertical displacement: 2 × 1 + 10 × 2 = 2 + 20 = 22. These values match b; the residual Ax − b is 0.

    These equalities are exact for the fictional model. They do not verify the displacement of a real table. In calculations with rounding, we would also examine the difference between A and LLᵀ as well as the residual; this page uses exact fractions.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculation. Recover the order p, q, r from the product LLᵀ, then explain why forward substitution comes before back substitution. What do LLᵀ = A and Ax = b check separately?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “We just need to take the root of every entry.”

    Matrix multiplication involves sums of products. The off-diagonal coefficient requires q = c/p; to find r, subtract the square of q from d, then take the positive root. Start again from these identities before taking a root.

    Reread the factor product and the remainder under the root

  • “LᵀL and LLᵀ give the same matrix.”

    Taking a transpose does not let us swap the order of a product. With our L, the first entry of LᵀL is 5, whereas A starts with 4. Keep the order A = LLᵀ; the exercise on the reversed product reveals the difference.

    Recalculate both products in the first exercise

  • “The matrix is symmetric and its diagonal is positive, so Cholesky works.”

    Positivity is required in every nonzero direction. The last exercise preserves symmetry and positive diagonal entries, but makes the remainder under the root 0. A 0 diagonal entry in L violates the strict requirements and prevents the planned divisions.

    Find the direction mapped to 0 in the limiting variant

Exercise 2 · Independent application · keep fractions

Another position, the same factor

The table and A stay the same. The requested displacement becomes b′ = (1, 2)ᵀ, dimensionless; the prime simply indicates this new request. Reuse L to calculate y′, then x′. Check the settings in Ax′ = b′, keeping exact fractions.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In forward substitution, h′ = 1/2. Then subtract qh′ from 2 before dividing by r.

Hint 2

In back substitution, v′ comes from the last row. Do not mistake y′ for the setting x′.

Compare with the solution

Forward substitution: h′ = 1/2 = 1/2. The contribution qh′ is 1/2; the remainder is 2 − 1/2 = 3/2. So k′ = (3/2)/3 = 1/2. The column y′ is (1/2, 1/2)ᵀ.

Back substitution: v′ = (1/2)/3 = 1/6. The contribution qv′ is 1/6; the remainder is h′ − qv′ = 1/3. So u′ = (1/3)/2 = 1/6. The exact setting is x′ = (1/6, 1/6)ᵀ, dimensionless, or approximately (0.167, 0.167)ᵀ for readability only.

Exact check in the original model: 4 × (1/6) + 2 × (1/6) = 1; 2 × (1/6) + 10 × (1/6) = 2. We recover b′. Fractions avoid confusing an exact equality with a rounded result.

Check your reasoning

  • I reuse L without taking new roots.
  • I distinguish y′ from x′ and justify the order of substitutions.
  • I check both coordinates of Ax′ using fractions, before any rounding.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer · examine the assumptions

A positive diagonal is not enough

In a fictional variant of the same table, only the coefficient d is replaced by 1. A₀ therefore has rows (4, 2) and (2, 1). Can we build the factor with a strictly positive diagonal required in this lesson? Calculate the last remainder, then test the direction z = (−1, 2)ᵀ. What must we state before solving a system with this variant?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

p and q depend only on a and c; they stay the same. So subtract the square of q from 1.

Hint 2

Calculate A₀z. If a nonzero direction is mapped to 0, strict positivity fails.

Compare with the solution

The remainder is 1 − 1 = 0. It would force r = 0, contradicting r > 0. The matrix is still symmetric, and its diagonal entries 4 and 1 are positive; these facts alone were therefore insufficient.

For z = (−1, 2)ᵀ, the first coordinate of A0z is 4 × (−1) + 2 × 2 = 0, and the second is 2 × (−1) + 1 × 2 = 0. Thus zᵀA0z = 0 with nonzero z: A0 is not positive definite.

We can write A0 = LLᵀ with a factor that has a 0 diagonal entry, but it is not the factor with a strictly positive diagonal required here. The planned back substitution would require division by 0. We must state that this method fails under these assumptions, then examine the model and the request; do not invent a unique solution or silently change a coefficient. With a negative remainder, no real square root would be available.

Check your reasoning

  • I calculate the 0 remainder and identify the division that has become impossible.
  • I provide the nonzero direction and check A₀z = 0.
  • I limit my conclusion to the strict Cholesky requirements and identify the model as needing review.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which assumptions and convention make the Cholesky factor unique?

A must be real, symmetric, and positive definite. We require L to be lower triangular with a strictly positive diagonal, and A = LLᵀ. A positive diagonal of A alone is not enough.

Recall 2For A with rows (a, c), (c, d), how do you recover p, q, and r without reciting the numbers on this page?

Expand LLᵀ with L having rows (p, 0), (q, r). The square of p equals a; pq equals c; the sum of the squares of q and r equals d. Take the positive p = √a, then q = c/p. Subtract the square of q from d and take the positive root for r. Check the remainders before taking roots.

Recall 3Why must we solve Ly = b before Lᵀx = y?

Ax = b can be written L(Lᵀx) = b. The intermediate vector y equals Lᵀx. Forward substitution gives y from b; back substitution then gives x from y. L can be reused as long as A does not change.

Recall 4Why check LLᵀ = A and Ax = b separately?

The first check examines the factor; the second examines the substitutions and the resulting settings. Our exact equalities verify the fictional model; they do not establish any real displacement of the table.

Recall 5What does a 0 or negative final remainder mean?

A 0 remainder forces a 0 diagonal entry in L and falls outside the strict requirements; the divisions needed to solve the system may become impossible. A negative remainder rules out this real root. We must reexamine the assumptions instead of silently changing the model.

Then explain why a 0 remainder prevents the strict requirements from being met.
