Level 4 · C237·M4

Poisson chaos

Your goalDecompose a pair count into orthogonal Poisson components, recover its variance, and explain the correction for repeated events.

Before you start
c236-m5 : Hörmander · c228-m5 : incompleteness · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c237-m1 : Stein’s method · c237-m2 : fourth moment · c237-m3 : contractions

✿01 See the idea

A workshop doorway records arrivals during a fixed time window. The manager counts all pairs of distinct arrivals, regardless of their order or how close together they occur in time. With 3 arrivals, each arrival can be linked to the others: the diagram shows 3 pairs. The scene and its values are fictional.

The number of pairs grows faster than the number of arrivals. Its deviation from the mean therefore cannot be described solely by a multiple of the count’s deviation. We will separate a mean, a linear fluctuation, and a quadratic remainder with a specific correction.

A chaos decomposition separates fluctuations into spaces of distinct orders that are mutually orthogonal under a given probability distribution. Here, orthogonal means that the product of a fluctuation of one order and a fluctuation of another order has mean 0. Each space is called a chaos. This does not make the fluctuations independent.

We assume a Poisson model with mean λ = 2 arrivals per window. This mean is fixed before counting; it is not replaced by the observation. We will verify an exact decomposition under this model, without claiming that the actual doorway follows it.

The rule to remember

Under a Poisson distribution, forming the orthogonal quadratic term requires subtracting N from the square of the deviation N − λ. Once the components are centered and orthogonal, their variances add.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From pairs of arrivals to orthogonal components

The model gives a mean of λ = 2 arrivals in the window; the fictional observed count is n = 3. Let us calculate the number F of pairs, its decomposition, and its variance under the Poisson distribution. All pairs of distinct events are included.

  1. Specify the distribution, units, and quantity to decompose

    N is the random number of arrivals; n is a possible value of N. λ is its strictly positive expectation. The letter ℙ denotes probability, and 𝔼 denotes expectation under this model. The number k is a nonnegative integer; k! is its factorial, the product of the integers from 1 to k, with 0! = 1. The letter e is the base of the natural exponential.

    ℙ(N=k)=e−λλkk!

    The count N, its parameter λ, and the polynomials below are dimensionless. The word “arrivals” specifies what is counted; F counts pairs, and its variance will be given in pairs squared. λ is a mean per window, rather than a rate per second.

    F=N(N−1)2

    Choose one arrival, then another distinct arrival: this gives N(N − 1) ordered pairs. Dividing by 2 removes the ordering. The Poisson distribution has finite moments of all orders: F is therefore square integrable, meaning that 𝔼[F squared] is finite. If arrivals are clustered or the mean varies randomly between windows, this model alone no longer justifies the following calculations.

  2. Build the second order term by excluding repetitions

    Write Cq(N; λ) for the Charlier polynomial of order q with the following normalization. q is a nonnegative integer and indicates the order of the chaos. The first terms are:

    C0=1;C1=N−λ
    C2=N(N−1)−2λN+λ2
    C2=(N−λ)2−N

    The square of the centered count includes the contribution of an event paired with itself. The term −N removes it. The terms −2λN and +λ squared then compensate the ordered pair count. Replacing −N with −λ would give a centered polynomial but would leave a component of order 1.

    Connection with multiple integrals: η denotes a Poisson random measure on time, μ its deterministic intensity measure, A our window, and 𝟙A its indicator function, equal to 1 in A and 0 elsewhere. Assume that μ is diffuse, with no mass at any single instant, and that μ(A) = λ is finite. Then N = η(A). The compensated measure η − μ subtracts the mean. The compensated integral of order 1, I1(𝟙A), equals C1. The multiple integral of order 2, I2(𝟙A ⊗ 𝟙A), equals C2: it counts distinct events before compensation. Here, the symbol ⊗ denotes the kernel (s, t) ↦ 𝟙A(s)𝟙A(t), where s and t are instants.

    This second order formula is therefore not the square of an ordinary integral. We study only functions of the count in this window; a variable that also depends on the arrival times would require other kernels.

  3. Check centering and orthogonality

    To calculate without approximation, introduce (N)r = N(N − 1)…(N − r + 1), the falling product of r factors; (N)0 = 1. In this notation, r is a nonnegative integer. The distribution given above yields 𝔼[(N)r] = λ to the power r: terms with k less than r are 0, then k! cancels, and the index change j = k − r leaves the sum of the probabilities of a Poisson distribution, equal to 1.

    Set X = N − λ. Expanding N squared and N cubed in terms of these falling products gives N squared = (N)2 + N and N cubed = (N)3 + 3(N)2 + N. It follows that 𝔼[X] = 0, 𝔼[X squared] = λ, and 𝔼[X cubed] = λ. Thus C2 = X squared − X − λ is centered.

    𝔼[C1C2]=𝔼[X3]−𝔼[X2]−λ𝔼[X]=0

    To also recover the quadratic norm, use N to the power 4 = (N)4 + 2×3(N)3 + (2×3 + 1)(N)2 + N. The same expansion gives 𝔼[X to the power 4] = 3λ squared + λ. Expanding (X squared − X − λ) squared then gives:

    𝔼[C12]=λ;𝔼[C22]=2λ2

    These means of squares are the squared quadratic norms of the polynomials, rather than those of their weighted contributions to F. Orthogonality allows the variances to be added; it does not establish independence. Here, C2 is even a function of C1, since N = C1 + λ.

  4. Recover the components of the pair count

    Rearrange the identity defining C2: N(N − 1) = C2 + 2λN − λ squared. Then replace N with C1 + λ and divide by 2. Each operation preserves the variable F exactly:

    F=λ22+λC1+12C2

    The mean is λ squared / 2, because the other terms are centered. The projection onto the first chaos is λC1; the remainder (1/2)C2 belongs to the second chaos. Here, an orthogonal projection is the component whose difference from the variable remains orthogonal to the chosen space. The order refers to the compensated polynomial, rather than the observed number of arrivals.

  5. Carry out the calculation for the observed window

    Direct count: n − 1 = 2, then n(n − 1) = 3 × 2 = 6 ordered pairs. The number of unordered pairs is 6 / 2 = 3 pairs.

    Polynomials: C1 = 3 − 2 = 1; its square is 1. C2 = 1 − 3 = -2. The correction does use 3, the observed count, rather than 2, its mean.

    Mean: λ squared = 4, so 4 / 2 = 2 pairs. Linear contribution: 2 × 1 = 2 pairs. Quadratic contribution: (1/2) × (-2) = -1 pair.

    Recomposition: 2 + (-1) = 1 pair of deviation from the mean; 2 + 1 = 3 pairs. This is the result of the direct count. The negative component is not a number of pairs physically removed: it is a signed fluctuation in a decomposition.

  6. Calculate the variance and check it by another method

    The variance Var(F) is 𝔼[(F − 𝔼[F]) squared]. The cross product of the contributions has mean 0. What remains is the squares of the coefficients multiplied by the squared quadratic norms of the polynomials:

    Var(F)=λ2λ+(12)22λ2=λ3+λ22

    With λ = 2, the squared norms are 2 and 8. The linear contribution therefore has variance 4 × 2 = 8 pairs squared. The square of the quadratic coefficient is 1/4; its contribution has variance (1/4) × 8 = 2 pairs squared. Total: 8 + 2 = 10 pairs squared.

    Independent check: expand the square of the ordered pair count. The identity (N)2 squared = (N)4 + 4(N)3 + 2(N)2 distinguishes products using four distinct events, those sharing one event, and those using the same pair. This identity can also be checked by expanding the polynomials.

    𝔼[F2]=λ4+4λ3+2λ24

    Numerically: λ cubed = 8, λ to the power 4 = 16, 4λ cubed = 32, and 2λ squared = 8. The numerator is 16 + 32 = 48, then 48 + 8 = 56. Thus 𝔼[F squared] = 56 / 4 = 14 pairs squared. Subtracting the square of the mean, 4, gives 10 pairs squared again. This check concerns the entire distribution, rather than the square of the result from a single window.

  7. Place the example within the chaos decomposition

    For a single count N with a Poisson distribution of positive parameter λ, the polynomials Cq are defined at all orders by the following generating function. t is an auxiliary real variable near 0; the sum describes the coefficients of its power series expansion:

    e−λt(1+t)N=∑q=0∞Cq(N;λ)tqq!

    In other words, Cq is q! times the coefficient of t to the power q in this expansion. With a second auxiliary variable s, the expectation of the product of the two generating functions is exp(λst), using 𝔼[a to the power N] = exp(λ(a − 1)) for positive a. Comparing the coefficients in s and t gives 𝔼[Cp Cq] = 0 if p differs from q, and 𝔼[Cq squared] = q! λ to the power q. p is another nonnegative integer order. This factor q! is part of our normalization.

    The chaos of order q for this single count is the space of multiples of Cq. The Poisson decomposition theorem states that every square integrable variable Y = g(N), where g is a deterministic function of the count, has a unique expansion:

    Y=𝔼[Y]+∑q=1∞aqCq;aq=𝔼[YCq]q!λq

    The sum converges in mean square: the expectation of the square of the remainder tends to 0 as the truncation order increases. The aq are the projection coefficients, with the units of Y. The existence of the expansion relies on the completeness of the Charlier polynomials; comparing the generating functions establishes their orthogonality here, but does not by itself establish this completeness.

    For the entire Poisson measure, a single order contains multiple integrals with various symmetric kernels; it is no longer restricted to multiples of a single Cq. Our F depends only on N: its terms of orders higher than 2 are 0, and its decomposition is finite and exact, with no assumption of convergence to a normal distribution. Chain rules for adding a point are covered in the next unit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The centered square minus its mean is already the second chaos.”

    The candidate (N − λ) squared − λ is centered, but its product with N − λ has expectation λ. A first order component therefore remains. Subtract N, rather than λ, from the centered square, then check orthogonality again.

    Revisit the construction and the mean product

  • “Orthogonal chaoses must be independent.”

    A 0 mean product is enough to add variances, but not to factor all probabilities. Here C2 = C1 squared − C1 − λ: the components share the same count and are linked by this deterministic relation.

    Answer the recall question about independence

  • “The variance of the remainder equals the norm of the polynomial.”

    The remainder is (1/2)C2, and its variance includes the square 1/4 of the coefficient. The squared norm 8 must therefore be multiplied by 1/4. The result is 2 pairs squared, rather than 8.

    Check the error of the linear projection

Exercise 1 · Direct application

One arrival, no pairs

In the same workshop and with λ = 2, suppose the window contains only 1 arrival. Calculate C1, C2, then F using the decomposition. Check the result by counting the distinct pairs.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Use C2 = (N − λ) squared − N, with N = 1.

Hint 2

The model’s mean remains 2 pairs; the observation does not change λ.

Compare with the solution

C1 = 1 − 2 = -1. Its square is 1, so C2 = 1 − 1 = 0. The linear part is -2 pairs, and the quadratic part is 0.

The decomposition gives 2 + (-2) + 0 = 0 pairs. This is correct: an arrival cannot form a pair with itself. The mean 2 remains a mean over all possible windows.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep λ fixed by the model.
  • Use the observed count in the correction of the square.
  • Recover the 0 count without removing the model’s mean.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis with proof

A centered polynomial that retains a first order component

Someone proposes W = (N − λ) squared − λ as a component of order 2. Using X = N − λ, 𝔼[X] = 0, 𝔼[X squared] = λ, and 𝔼[X cubed] = λ, calculate 𝔼[W] and 𝔼[XW]. Then decompose W into the actual chaoses. Keep λ = 2 to interpret the result.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Centering tests only the product with a constant. The first chaos also requires testing the product with X.

Hint 2

Compare W with C2 = X squared − X − λ.

Compare with the solution

𝔼[W] = λ − λ = 0. But 𝔼[XW] = 𝔼[X cubed] − λ𝔼[X] = λ = 2, rather than 0. W is centered without being orthogonal to the first chaos.

The identity W = C2 + C1 shows the component that remains. The correction consists of subtracting C1 = N − λ from W; this recovers the centered square minus N. This check holds for every strictly positive λ, rather than just our fictional parameter.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish centering from orthogonality.
  • Obtain a nonzero mean product with X.
  • Identify exactly the linear component to remove.

Exercise 3 · Transfer to an approximation

What is lost by keeping only the mean and the first chaos?

The manager uses G = λ squared / 2 + λ(N − λ), an affine function of N, to approximate F. Calculate G for our observation n = 3, then the error F − G. Determine the mean squared error under the model. Explain why G minimizes this error among all affine functions of N and why this does not establish a good model of actual arrivals.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The exact remainder F − G is already a component of the decomposition.

Hint 2

Another affine function H gives F − H = (F − G) + (G − H). The mean product of these two terms is 0.

Compare with the solution

At n = 3, G = 2 + 2 = 4 pairs, whereas F = 3 pairs. The error F − G is -1 pair. An affine approximation can therefore give a count different from the exact count.

Over the entire distribution, F − G = (1/2)C2. Its expectation is 0, and its mean squared error is (1/4) × 8 = 2 pairs squared. The squared error of one window does not replace this mean.

𝔼[(F−H)2]=𝔼[(F−G)2]+𝔼[(G−H)2]

H denotes any other affine function of the count. G − H belongs to orders 0 and 1, so it is orthogonal to the remainder of order 2. The last term is nonnegative: G minimizes the squared error in this class. This optimality depends on the chosen Poisson distribution; no actual observation has validated this distribution or its parameter.

Check your reasoning

  • Separate the observed error value from the mean squared error.
  • Use the squared coefficient of the remainder.
  • Justify the minimum using the orthogonality of the remainder to every affine function.
  • Limit the optimality to the affine class and the stated model.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why must N be subtracted from the centered square to form the quadratic Poisson term?

The centered square includes the contributions of an event paired with itself. Removing them gives C2 = (N − λ) squared − N, centered and orthogonal to C1 = N − λ. Subtracting only λ would leave a first chaos component.

Recall 2Reconstruct the decomposition of the unordered pair count, justifying the coefficients.

Starting from N(N − 1)/2 and rearranging C2 gives F = λ squared / 2 + λC1 + (1/2)C2. The mean is the constant term; the others are centered.

Recall 3How do you calculate the variance of a component aq Cq of strictly positive order q?

Multiply the square of aq by 𝔼[Cq squared] = q! λ to the power q. The variances of distinct orders add because of orthogonality. For the remainder of F: (1/4) × 8 = 2 pairs squared.

Recall 4Why does orthogonality not make the first chaoses of our count independent?

It imposes only a 0 mean product. Here C2 is the function C1 squared − C1 − λ. If N equals 0, C1 equals −λ and C2 equals λ squared; if N equals 1, C2 equals (1 − λ) squared − 1. For λ = 2, these two values differ. The second term therefore depends on the information provided by the first.

Recall 5What is the difference between the chaoses of a single count and those of an entire Poisson measure?

For a function of N alone, each order is spanned by Cq. For an entire measure, the kernels of multiple integrals can also distinguish event positions. Our finite decomposition of the pair count covers only the first situation and proves no normal approximation.
