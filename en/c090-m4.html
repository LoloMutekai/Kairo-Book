Level 2 · C090·M4

Seasonality

Your goalRecognize a pattern with a known period, calculate its seasonal deviations after removing a trend, and check what remains unexplained.

Before you start
c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c037-m5 : controlled cleaning · c090-m1 : timestamping · c090-m2 : autocovariance · c090-m3 : trend

✿01 See the idea

In a print shop that operates day and night, completed jobs are counted in successive windows of 8 h. The daytime window is busier than the night and evening windows. The next day, this pattern returns while the overall activity level rises. All the data in this scene are fictional.

The trend here describes the rise in level from one day to the next. Seasonality describes a pattern that returns at the same calendar positions, with a known period. The word is not limited to the seasons of the year: our seasonality is daily. A phase is a position in this cycle: here, night, daytime or evening.

The pattern returns after 3 observations of 8 h, or 24 h. What repeats is a deviation from the level for that day, which need not be the raw count. The repetition is approximate: the exact number of jobs can vary around the pattern.

A single peak is not enough. Waves of varying duration do not by themselves give a seasonal period. Even a resemblance between nearby observations does not prove seasonality: we need to identify the same phases over several cycles and examine the trend separately. We use a short example to make the calculation visible, without claiming to establish a real rhythm in the print shop.

The rule to remember

Specify the sampling interval and period; remove the supplied level; group matching phases; estimate their deviations; reconstruct each observation with its residual.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Recover the daily profile of the print shop

The 6 observations in the diagram cover 2 complete days. The supplied level is 40 jobs per window on the first day, then 40 + 4 = 44 on the next. We want to find the night, daytime and evening deviations, keeping exact fractions.

  1. State the calendar and assumptions

    The windows are contiguous: from 0 h to 8 h for night, from 8 h to 16 h for daytime and from 16 h to 24 h for evening. We assume days of the same duration, no clock changes, no missing windows and a constant definition of a completed job. The period p = 3 counts observations; its duration is p × 8 h = 24 h.

    d identifies the day and j the phase, in the order night, daytime, evening. Y with subscripts d and j is the observed count; T with subscript d is the level for that day supplied by the exercise. This level represents the trend here and is constant within the day. We do not estimate it again. In a real study, justifying it would be a prior task.

    S with subscript j is the seasonal deviation assumed to be identical from one day to the next; e with subscripts d and j is the residual. Y, T, S and e are all in jobs per window of 8 h. The model is additive: the amplitude of the pattern stays fixed in jobs when the level changes. If it grew in proportion to the level, we would need to reconsider this choice. These identities do not require independent residuals; we infer neither an uncertainty interval nor a statistical test from them.

    Yd,j=Td+Sj+ed,j

  2. Remove the level before comparing phases

    Let r be the deviation after removing the level: r = Y − T, with the same subscripts and unit. It still contains the seasonal component and residual. Subtracting the level for each day prevents the overall rise from being confused with the pattern.

    Day 1: night 36 − 40 = -4; daytime 49 − 40 = 9; evening 35 − 40 = -5 jobs per window.

    Day 2: night 39 − 44 = -5; daytime 54 − 44 = 10; evening 39 − 44 = -5 jobs per window. The pattern after removing the level is similar from one day to the next, without being exactly identical.

  3. Average observations from the same phase

    M denotes the number of days used, here M = 2, with no unit. g with subscript j is the mean of the r values for phase j over these days. The mean therefore does not mix night and daytime. Each phase here has the same number of observations.

    Night: (-4 + (-5))/2 = -9/2 = -9/2, or -4.5 jobs per window. Daytime: (9 + 10)/2 = 19/2 = 19/2, or 9.5 jobs per window. Evening: (-5 + (-5))/2 = -10/2 = -5 jobs per window.

    This grouping estimates a common profile under the assumption of repetition. It does not demonstrate that this assumption will describe subsequent days.

    gj=r1,j+r2,jM;M=2

  4. Center the pattern to separate the seasonal component from the level

    To fix the allocation between the level and seasonal component, we require the sum of S over a cycle to be 0. Without this convention, adding the same amount to all levels and subtracting it from all phases would preserve the reconstructed observations. Centering removes this ambiguity about the offset; it does not prove that the chosen trend is correct.

    c is the mean of the g values over the p phases, in the same unit. Here, the first phases give -4.5 + 9.5 = 5; then 5 + (-5) = 0. Therefore c = 0/3 = 0 jobs per window.

    We set S = g − c for each phase: night -4.5 − 0 = -4.5; daytime 9.5 − 0 = 9.5; evening -5 − 0 = -5 jobs per window. The profile was already centered. If c were nonzero, we would also need to add c to the level to preserve the same fitted total.

    Snuit+Sjournée+Ssoirée=0;Sj=gj−c

  5. Reconstruct an observation and identify the residual

    For the daytime window on the first day, level + seasonal component is 40 + 9.5 = 49.5 jobs per window. The observed count was 49. The residual is therefore 49 − 49.5 = -0.5 jobs per window.

    Reconstruction check: 49.5 + (-0.5) = 49 jobs, exactly the initial observation. A residual is not automatically a data entry error or an anomaly: it is the deviation not described by the chosen components.

    Mean components can be fractional, even though each observed job is counted as a whole. 9.5 is a mean deviation in the profile; it does not require completing a particular half job.

    ed,j=Yd,j−Td−Sj

  6. Remove the seasonal component while keeping what remains

    The seasonally adjusted value is Y − S. For this window: 49 − 9.5 = 39.5 jobs. This is T + e, or 40 + (-0.5), rather than the level T alone. Removing an estimated seasonal component removes neither the trend nor the residual.

    Between the daytime windows on days 1 and 2, the raw count increases by 54 − 49 = 5 jobs. The common seasonal deviation cancels in the difference; the 5 jobs combine the rise in level 4 and a change in residual 5 − 4 = 1. A change between matching phases therefore does not necessarily measure the trend alone.

  7. Check by another method and limit the conclusion

    Let us check night without starting from the individual deviations. The mean of the night counts is (36 + 39)/2 = 75/2 = 37.5 jobs per window. The mean of the levels is (40 + 44)/2 = 84/2 = 42. Their difference is 37.5 − 42 = -4.5, matching the night profile.

    The checks are consistent: a seasonal sum of 0, exact reconstruction and the same profile from another sequence of operations. They check our calculation, rather than the existence of a lasting rhythm. With only 2 fictional cycles and a supplied trend, we have neither established a cause for the pattern nor assessed its stability on later observations.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Night seasonality is the mean of the night counts.”

    37.5 jobs is a raw mean; it contains the level. The night seasonal profile is the deviation -4.5 jobs after removing that level. Repeat the check using the difference of means and identify what each mean contains.

    Review the removal of the level and the final check

  • “I can delete a missing row and keep counting phases.”

    The phase is tied to the calendar. Deleting a window and then assigning phases from position alone shifts the following phases. Keep the timestamp and flag the missing window; our balanced calculation does not allow us to replace it with 0 jobs or silently change the period.

    Reread the calendar and initial assumptions

  • “After removing the seasonal component, I have recovered the exact trend.”

    The calculation gives Y − S = T + e. Here 39.5 still differs from 40 because of the residual -0.5. A consistent decomposition does not guarantee that its components correctly describe the real print shop.

    Reconstruct the window, then remove the seasonal component

Does the new count contradict the pattern?

Hint 1

Compare the new count with level + seasonal component, rather than just the observation from the previous day.

Hint 2

Can a data point used to fit the profile serve on its own as a check on new data?

Compare with the solution

The previous profile expected 57.5 jobs. The residual is 60 − 57.5 = 2.5 jobs per window. This isolated deviation does not distinguish between usual residual variation, an incorrect supplied level, a change in the pattern or a counting problem.

Refitting the profile is a possibility to consider; judging it on the same observation, however, reuses the information that built it. To examine how well it holds up, we would need to retain the calendar, level rule and profile stated before the new observations, then compare the deviations over several later cycles. This exercise establishes neither a threshold for change nor a sufficient number of cycles.

  • Keep several possible explanations for an isolated deviation.
  • Separate fitting from comparison with later observations, without inventing a conclusive threshold.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What makes the print shop pattern seasonal, and what does its period measure?

The pattern is assumed to return at the same calendar phases. The period p = 3 counts observations; with windows of 8 h, it corresponds to 24 h. A single wave is not enough to establish this return.

Recall 2Why remove the level before averaging the nights?

A raw mean still contains the level for that day. Removing each level lets us group deviations from the same phase, under the assumption of a common additive profile. The correctness of the supplied level remains an assumption.

Recall 3Why require the seasonal sum over the cycle to be 0?

To fix the allocation between the level and seasonal component: otherwise, an offset can be added to the level and subtracted from each phase without changing the total. Centering removes the mean of the profile; a nonzero offset removed from the profile must be transferred to the level.

Recall 4What remains after removing the seasonal component from the observation?

Y − S = T + e: the level and residual. In our daytime window, 49 − 9.5 = 39.5 jobs, still different from the level 40.

Recall 5What do exact reconstruction and successful centering prove?

They check the consistency of the calculation. They demonstrate neither the stability of the pattern on new days nor its cause. Reusing only the observations that were used to fit the profile to judge it does not provide this comparison with future data.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the sequence: calendar and period → supplied level → deviations after removing the level → means by phase → centered profile → reconstruction and residual. Then explain why removing the seasonal component preserves T + e, and distinguish algebraic checks from comparison with new days.
