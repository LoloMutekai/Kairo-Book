Asynchrony

Your goalShow, through a numerical example, that the covariance of two series published at different times comes from the overlaps between their intervals, then measure what is lost by aligning them on a common grid.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

Two screens display quotes from two feeds that, by construction, track the same underlying price. The values in this example are fictional: none comes from a market. Feed A publishes a new price every 6 seconds, and feed B every 4 seconds. Both therefore report the same thing, but at times that do not coincide. This is asynchrony.

Covariance compares two price changes over the same time interval. If the intervals of A and B overlap only partly, only the shared part carries a common movement; the rest of each interval is a movement that the other feed has not yet published.

To obtain a table in which both feeds have a price at the same time, it is tempting to copy the last known price of B onto a common grid. This price may be out of date: a change in B then lands in the wrong grid cell. We will measure what this practice removes in our example, and compare it with a sum that combines only intervals that overlap.

The calculation is exact under three assumptions of the example, which are not market findings. First, both feeds read exactly the same efficient price, without the microstructure noise covered in the previous unit. Second, changes in this price over intervals with no shared part have mean 0, are uncorrelated, and have a variance of 1 squared basis point per second. Finally, publication times are fixed in advance and do not depend on prices. A basis point (bp) is one hundredth of a percent.

The rule to remember

The expected value of the product of the changes in A and B over two intervals is σ² times the duration of their overlap: without an overlap, it carries no covariance.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Two clocks, the same price

A window of 12 s. Feed A publishes at times 0, 6 and 12 s; feed B at times 0, 4, 8 and 12 s. The variance rate of the common price is σ² = 1 bp² per second (a fictional value). Let us calculate the target covariance, then how much of it is recovered by two methods: the sum over overlaps and a grid of 6 s using the last known price.

  1. Define the quantities, units and assumptions

    Y(t) denotes the common efficient price, in bp, at time t in seconds. ΔA is the change in feed A over one of its intervals I, between two consecutive publications by A; ΔB is the change in feed B over one of its intervals J. Since both feeds read Y exactly, a change is the difference in Y between the two ends of the interval. E[·] denotes the average over the randomness in the model.

    Assumptions: changes in Y over intervals with no shared part have mean 0 and are uncorrelated; the variance of Y(t′) − Y(t) is σ² × (t′ − t), in bp²; publication times are fixed in advance. The target is the covariance of the two total changes over the window, of duration T = 12 s: it is σ² × T = 12 bp², since both feeds carry the same total movement.

  2. Divide each clock into intervals

    Feed A has two intervals: from 0 to 6 s, lasting 6 s, then from 6 to 12 s, lasting 6 s. Feed B has three: from 0 to 4 s, from 4 to 8 s and from 8 to 12 s, lasting 4, 4 and 4 s.

    Check: the durations for each clock do add up to the window, 6 + 6 = 12 s for A and 4 + 4 + 4 = 12 s for B. Each time within the window therefore belongs to just one interval of A and just one interval of B.

  3. Find the expected value of a product of two changes

    Imagine dividing each change into small pieces of time. The product of two pieces taken at different times has mean 0, because these changes have mean 0 and are uncorrelated. Only pieces taken at the same time count: each contributes σ² per second. This leaves σ² times the shared duration, denoted by ℓ.

    E[ΔA⋅ΔB]=σ2×ℓ(I∩J)

    Units: σ² in bp² per second, ℓ in seconds, product in bp². If I and J have no time in common, ℓ is 0 and the product contributes no covariance: it should then be left out.

  4. Add up the overlaps

    The first interval of A overlaps the first of B for 4 s (from 0 to 4) and the second of B for 2 s (from 4 to 6). The second interval of A overlaps the second of B for 2 s (from 6 to 8) and the third for 4 s (from 8 to 12). The other two pairs do not overlap.

    Why add: the sum of products over all overlapping pairs partitions the window without gaps or double counting. Check for each interval of A: 4 + 2 = 6 s, equal to the duration of its first interval; 2 + 4 = 6 s, equal to that of its second. The total is 12 s, the full window.

    E[C^recouvrement]=1×(4+2+2+4)=12 bp²

    This sum is called an overlap estimator, of the Hayashi–Yoshida type. Here its expected value recovers the target exactly, 12 bp².

  5. Use a grid with the last known price

    Take the grid at times 0, 6 and 12 s, on which A publishes at each point. At time 6 s, the latest quote from B is from 4 s: B is therefore read with a delay of 2 s. On the grid, its changes cover 0 to 4 s, then 4 to 12 s.

    Now pair changes by position: the first grid interval of A, from 0 to 6 s, with that of B, from 0 to 4 s, with an overlap of 4 s; the second, from 6 to 12 s, with that of B, from 4 to 12 s, with an overlap of 6 s. The total is 10 s, giving an expected value of 1 × 10 = 10 bp².

    The loss is 12 − 10 = 2 bp², or σ² × 2 s: the period from 4 to 6 s, seen by A in its first interval, is counted by B in the second and is no longer paired. The grid recovers the fraction 0.83 of the target.

  6. Check by a second method and state the limits

    Independent check, performed in the calculation script: each second, the efficient price rises or falls by 1 bp with equal probability, which does give a variance of 1 bp² per second. The script enumerates all possible paths, calculates each estimator on each path and then takes the exact average: it obtains 12 bp² for the overlaps and 10 bp² for the grid, as in the calculation by hand.

    Limits: these are expected values under the assumptions of the example, rather than results from a particular path or a real dataset. There is no microstructure noise, no publication times that respond to prices, and no measure of the dispersion of the estimators. This example does not tell us how the loss changes if the grid changes.

Explain it in your own words

Explain in your own words why two intervals with no shared part contribute no covariance, then why, in this example, the grid using the last known price loses exactly the duration between the latest quote from B and the grid time.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I can pair the changes in two series by position, the last with the last.”

    Position is not time. Two lists of different lengths describe different intervals; in the example, pairing by position recovers only 6 bp² instead of 12 bp². First check which clock produces each list.

    Redo the exercise on pairing by position

  • “Copying the last known price does not change the covariance.”

    An outdated price shifts a change into the next grid cell. In the example, a delay of 2 s causes an expected loss of 2 bp², leaving a fraction 0.83 of the target.

    Revisit the grid using the last known price

  • “The calculation in the example applies to any real quotes.”

    These are expected values under three assumptions: the same price without noise, changes with mean 0 that are uncorrelated, and times fixed in advance. A real path gives a different number, and times that respond to prices or microstructure noise require a different calculation.

    Reread the assumptions of the example

Exercise 1 · Explained application

A grid with a single step

Keep the clocks from the example. Place the common grid only at times 0 and 12 s and copy the last known price of B. What is the expected value of the recovered covariance? What is gained and what is lost compared with the grid of 6 s?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At time 12 s, both feeds publish: the last known price of B has no delay.

Hint 2

There is only one grid interval: find the duration of its overlap with itself.

Compare with the solution

The single grid interval lasts 12 s and each feed carries its total change over it, so the overlap is 12 s. The expected value is 1 × 12 = 12 bp², the target: no delay, so no loss.

What is lost does not appear in this expected value: only a single product remains, all intermediate quotes are discarded, and there is no longer any information about covariance over subperiods. The dispersion of an estimator built from a single product has not been calculated here.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify that B has no delay at either grid time.
  • Give the expected value with the unit bp².
  • Distinguish the exact expected value from what is discarded by keeping only one product.

Exercise 2 · Guided calculation

A different clock for B

Feed B now publishes at times 0, 5 and 12 s; A stays at 0, 6 and 12 s, with the same σ² = 1 bp² per second. Calculate the expected value of the sum over overlaps, then that of the grid of 6 s using the last known price, and the loss from the grid.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write down the intervals of B, then, for each interval of A, those of B that intersect it.

Hint 2

On the grid, feed B at time 6 s is read using its quote from time 5 s.

Compare with the solution

Overlaps: the first interval of A overlaps the first of B for 5 s (from 0 to 5) and the second of B for 1 s (from 5 to 6); the second of A overlaps the second of B for 6 s (from 6 to 12), and does not overlap the first. Total: 6 + 6 = 12 s, giving 1 × 12 = 12 bp², the target.

Grid: B is read with a delay of 1 s; its grid changes cover 0 to 5 s, then 5 to 12 s. The overlaps for matching positions are 5 and 6 s, totalling 11 s, giving 11 bp². The loss is 12 − 11 = 1 bp², or σ² × 1 s, and the fraction retained is 0.92.

Check your reasoning

  • List the pairs of intervals that overlap and exclude the one with no shared part.
  • Check that the total overlap equals the window.
  • Relate the loss to the delay in B at the grid time.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

Pairing by position

A program receives a list of the changes for A (one per interval) and a list of those for B, without the times. To make the lengths equal, it keeps the last elements of the longer list, then pairs elements one by one. Apply it to the situation in the example: what expected covariance does it obtain, and what must be checked before using such an alignment?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A has two changes and B has three: which changes in B does the program keep?

Hint 2

For each pair, calculate the overlap between its two intervals.

Compare with the solution

The program keeps the last two changes in B, from 4 to 8 s and from 8 to 12 s. First pair: A from 0 to 6 s with B from 4 to 8 s, overlap 2 s. Second pair: A from 6 to 12 s with B from 8 to 12 s, overlap 4 s. Total 6 s, giving 1 × 6 = 6 bp².

This is the fraction 0.50 of the target of 12 bp². Position coincides with time only if both lists come from the same clock and describe the same intervals. Before using this approach, you must therefore establish that the clocks are identical, for example by retaining the times; reading the code is not enough to know this.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify the two changes in B that are retained.
  • Calculate an overlap for each pair, with the unit.
  • State the condition that makes pairing by position acceptable.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does asynchrony mean in this unit?

Two series read the same price but publish at different times: their intervals of change do not coincide.

Recall 2What is the expected value of the product of two changes over two intervals?

σ² times the duration of their overlap, under the assumptions of the lesson; 0 if they have no time in common.

Recall 3Why does the grid using the last known price lose covariance in the example?

An outdated price places a change in B in the next grid cell; the shared part between the latest quote from B and the grid time is no longer paired.

Recall 4Which assumptions make the numbers in the example exact?

The same efficient price without noise, changes with mean 0 that are uncorrelated over intervals with no shared part, variance σ² per second, and publication times fixed in advance.

Recall 5Why is pairing two series by position not enough?

Position is not time: two lists may describe different intervals. In the example, pairing by position recovers the fraction 0.50 of the target.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Name the clock of each feed and its intervals; state the assumptions (the same price without noise, uncorrelated changes with mean 0 and variance σ² per second, fixed times); write the expected value of a product as σ² times the overlap; sum the overlaps to recover the target; observe that using the last known price and pairing by position lose part of it.
