Level 3 · C172·M2

GBM

Your goalSolve geometric Brownian motion, calculate its exact transition, and distinguish a sample path from its mean.

Before you start
c171-m5 : multidimensional case · c052-m5 : convergence protocol · c172-m1 : existence and uniqueness

✿01 See the idea

A workshop tracks the cost of a material using a positive index measured in points. Today it is 100 points. The same relative shock produces a larger change in points when the index is high: the noise acts in proportion to the current value. Adding a fixed amount of noise would describe a different situation.

Geometric Brownian motion, abbreviated GBM, expresses this idea: a steady trend and random fluctuations act in proportion to the index. The word geometric indicates change through multiplicative factors. The logarithms of these factors add together.

The natural logarithm turns the ratio of the final value to the initial value into a signed displacement. Its inverse, the exponential function, turns this displacement back into a strictly positive factor. An adverse shock can therefore lower the index without making it cross 0 in this model.

The setting, parameters, and chosen shocks are fictional. They are used to solve an equation; they are neither a record of prices nor a validated forecast. We assume constant coefficients and continuous Brownian noise with no jumps. The model does not represent every possible aspect of how the cost of a material changes.

The rule to remember

The noise is proportional to the state. In the logarithm, its variance requires a correction to the drift; exponentiating then preserves positivity.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

An adverse shock to the workshop index

Initially, the index is 100 points. Time is measured in months; the relative drift μ is 0.12 per month, and the intensity σ is 0.40 per square root of a month. The duration h is 0.25 months. For the calculation, we specify a standardized shock Z = -1. Let us find the exact final value, then what its distribution tells us before this shock is known.

  1. Set up the equation and its units

    St is the value of the index at time t, in points. The start is t = 0 months; the initial S is deterministic and strictly positive. Wt is standard Brownian motion: it starts at 0, its sample paths are continuous, and its increments over disjoint intervals are independent. Over a duration h, ΔW = Wt+h − Wt has a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance h. The symbol Δ means a difference between the final and initial values.

    dS=μSdt+σSdW

    Time subscripts are omitted in this short notation. It denotes an Ito integral equation: the trend term is integrated over time and the random term with respect to W. dW is not an ordinary velocity. The coefficients μ and σ are constant, with σ nonnegative. μ has units of inverse months; σ of inverse square roots of months; W and ΔW of square roots of months. Thus μS dt and σS dW are both in points.

    The noise coefficient σS increases with the state: multiplying the state by a factor multiplies its absolute fluctuations by the same factor. The functions μS and σS of the state are globally Lipschitz and have linear growth: the existence and uniqueness assumptions from the previous unit apply. We still need to find this solution.

  2. Find the correction using Ito

    Try St = initial S × exp(Yt), with Yt = at + σWt. Y is dimensionless; a is a logarithmic drift per month, still unknown. exp is the natural exponential function. For the function f(y) = initial S × exp(y), the first and second derivatives both equal f(y). The Ito formula therefore adds a variance term to the relative drift:

    dSS=(a+σ22)dt+σdW

    This contribution comes from the quadratic variation of Brownian motion, represented by dW × dW = dt in Ito calculus. An ordinary differentiation rule would miss it. To recover exactly the given equation, we must choose:

    a=μ−σ22

    The constructed solution is positive at every finite time, since the exponential is positive and the initial value is positive. We did not assume positivity in order to take a logarithm: we obtained it by checking an exponential solution. Uniqueness identifies this solution as the solution of the equation.

  3. Read the exact transition and its distribution

    For any t and strictly positive h, divide the solution at time t + h by the solution at time t. The initial factors cancel. Setting L = ln(St+h/St), where ln is the natural logarithm, gives:

    L=ah+σΔW
    St+h=Stexp(ah+σΔW)

    We can write ΔW = √h × Z, where Z has a standard normal distribution: its mean is 0 and its variance is 1. A new disjoint interval requires a new independent Z. L has a normal distribution with mean ah and variance (σ × σ)h; these parameters are dimensionless. Since the index is the exponential of this logarithm, its conditional distribution given the initial value is called lognormal when σ is positive.

    This is an exact transition at the chosen times, under this model with constant coefficients. It gives the values at those times; by itself it does not describe all movements between them. General numerical discretization will be studied in the unit devoted to it.

  4. Calculate the logarithmic trend

    Start with the square of the intensity, because Ito involves a variance: σ × σ = 2/5 × 2/5 = 4/25 per month. The correction is 4/25 ÷ 2 = 2/25 per month. Subtract it from the given relative drift:

    a=0.12−0.08=0.04 par mois

    Then multiply by the duration: ah = 1/25 × 1/4 = 0.01, which is dimensionless. The drift of the index and the drift of its logarithm are therefore not interchangeable.

  5. Scale the shock and finish the calculation

    The variance of a Brownian increment is proportional to the duration; its standard deviation is therefore proportional to the square root of that duration. Here √h = 0.50 in square roots of months: multiplying this quantity by itself gives 1/4 months. For the chosen shock Z: ΔW = 1/2 × (-1) = -1/2 in square roots of months.

    The logarithmic shock is σΔW = 2/5 × (-1/2) = -0.20, which is dimensionless. Add it to ah: L = 0.01 + (-0.20) = -0.19. The exact result in points is:

    Sh=100exp(-0.19) points

    The exponential is left in exact form; no approximate decimal value of the index is needed. Since L is negative, its exponential factor is between 0 and 1: the final value is positive and below 100 points. Z is an input value chosen for the exercise; this choice assigns no positive probability to an exact point of a continuous distribution.

  6. Check by returning to the model

    First check: the logarithm of the ratio of the answer to the initial value gives -19/100 again. Subtracting the shock -1/5 and then dividing by the duration 1/4 recovers 1/25 per month. Adding back the Ito correction of 2/25 per month recovers μ = 0.12 per month, the coefficient in the original equation.

    Second check: the stated variance of the logarithm is (σ × σ)h = 4/25 × 1/4 = 0.04, which is dimensionless. Its standard deviation σ√h is 0.20, whose square is indeed 0.04. Replacing √h with h would fail this check.

    If σ were 0, the correction and fluctuations would disappear together: we would recover St = initial S × exp(μt). Positivity and this limiting case check the form of the solution; they do not validate its suitability for the workshop.

  7. Distinguish the mean, median, and chosen shock

    The expectation is the mean specified by the distribution over all possible realizations. For our continuous distribution, the median has equal probability on either side. Before the future shock is known, if the known initial value is s points, they are:

    E[St+h|St=s]=sexp(μh)
    Médiane au départ s=sexp(ah)

    E denotes expectation; the vertical bar means “given the initial value.” For the mean, the random term cannot simply be removed from inside the exponential: a centered normal variable ΔW with variance h satisfies E[exp(σΔW)] = exp[(σ × σ)h/2]. This factor exactly offsets the correction in ah. The median instead uses the center of the logarithm and the fact that the exponential is increasing.

    In our setting: the mean is 100 × exp(3/100) points, since μh = 0.03. The median is 100 × exp(1/100) points. The difference between their exponents is 0.02 = (σ × σ)h/2. Our adverse shock gives 100 × exp(-19/100) points: an increasing mean therefore does not rule out a decreasing realization.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I solve it like an ordinary equation and exponentiate μt + σWₜ.”

    Apply Ito to this proposal: the exponential would add (σ × σ)/2 to its relative drift. This term must be subtracted in the exponent to recover μ. Checking the exponential solution also avoids assuming its positivity before proving it.

    Recover the correction using Ito

  • “A step h gives a shock hZ.”

    Brownian motion has variance h, so its standard deviation is √h. The product hZ would have variance h × h when durations are expressed in the chosen unit. Check the square of the shock coefficient before calculating the sample path.

    Correct the noise scaling

  • “The mean is above the initial value: the index must rise.”

    The mean describes an entire distribution, whereas a sample path uses a particular shock. Our chosen shock gives a negative logarithm despite positive μ. Also distinguish the mean from the median, which is obtained using the logarithmic drift.

    Compare the realization, median, and mean

Exercise 1 · Diagnosis followed by calculation

Correct the square root of the duration

For the same index and h = 0.25 months, someone replaces ΔW with hZ. With Z = -1, calculate their shock and log ratio. What variance do they obtain for this incorrect increment when Z follows its normal distribution? Then give the correct scaling and logarithm.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The variance of a constant coefficient multiplied by Z is the square of that coefficient, since Var(Z) = 1.

Hint 2

Keep the trend ah = 0.01; only the way the noise is scaled needs correcting.

Compare with the solution

Treating durations as numbers in units of months, the incorrect method produces hZ = -1/4. Its noise term is 2/5 × (-1/4) = -0.10; its logarithm becomes 0.01 + (-0.10) = -0.09. This result corresponds to a different noise rule.

The numerical variance of the incorrect increment is h × h = 1/16, instead of 1/4 for Brownian motion over this duration. In physical units, hZ would even be in months, whereas ΔW must be in square roots of months. Both checks reveal the error.

The correction is ΔW = √h Z = -1/2 in square roots of months. Its coefficient √h has square h, as required. The noise is again -0.20 and the logarithm -0.19; the exact final value is 100 × exp(-19/100) points.

Check your reasoning

  • I obtain -0.09 for the incorrect method and -0.19 after correction.
  • I distinguish variance h from standard deviation √h.
  • I also point out the incompatible units.

Exercise 3 · Reasoning about the distribution

When does the index exceed its initial value?

Before choosing Z, return to the initial interval. Find the condition on Z for the final value to exceed 100 points. Someone then claims: “Since μ is positive, exceeding the initial value is certain and the mean corresponds to Z = 0.” Refute both claims without calculating a decimal probability.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

An exponential exceeds 1 exactly when its exponent exceeds 0.

Hint 2

In L = ah + σ√h Z, the coefficient of Z is 0.20 and is positive. For the mean, use the expectation of the exponential given in the example.

Compare with the solution

Exceeding the initial value is equivalent to L > 0. Solve 0.01 + 0.20 Z > 0 by dividing by a positive coefficient: Z > (0 − 1/100)/1/5 = -1/20. At equality, the index returns exactly to its initial value.

A standard normal distribution also assigns nonzero probability to values below this finite threshold: a rise is therefore not certain. The shock -1 in the example is in this region and does give a decrease.

Z = 0 gives the center of the logarithm, hence the median 100 × exp(1/100) points. The mean is 100 × exp(3/100) points; its exponent is higher by 0.02. The fact that the noise is centered before exponentiation does not remove its contribution to the mean after exponentiation.

Check your reasoning

  • I find the threshold -1/20 and preserve the direction of the inequality.
  • I distinguish the possibility of a decrease from the probability of a single point.
  • I justify the difference between median and expectation.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the factor S mean in the noise σS dW?

The absolute intensity is proportional to the current state. If the state is multiplied by a factor, the noise coefficient is too; the relative intensity σ remains constant in this model.

Recall 2Why must a variance term be subtracted from the drift of the logarithm?

Applying Ito to the exponential of a drift a and a noise term σW adds (σ × σ)/2 to the relative drift. Choosing a = μ − (σ × σ)/2 recovers μ in the original equation.

Recall 3How can you reconstruct a transition over a duration h and check its noise?

Multiply the initial value by exp[(μ − (σ × σ)/2)h + σ√h Z], where Z is centered normal with variance 1, independent of the past. The logarithm of the ratio has variance (σ × σ)h; squaring its standard deviation σ√h must recover that variance.

Recall 4What guarantees a strictly positive index at every finite time?

A strictly positive initial value and an exponential with a finite exponent. This result concerns the exact solution of the model; it does not follow from the word cost alone or from an arbitrary bounding rule.

Recall 5Why does setting the shock equal to its mean fail to give the mean of the index?

The exponential is nonlinear. A 0 shock gives the median s exp[(μ − (σ × σ)/2)h]; the expectation is s exp(μh), because E[exp(σΔW)] contributes the factor exp[(σ × σ)h/2]. An increasing mean does not guarantee any particular sample path.
