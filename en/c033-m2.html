Level 1 · C033·M2

Composition

Your goalDifferentiate a chain of transformations by evaluating each slope at the right point and tracking its units.

Before you start
c031-m5 : asymptotes · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c033-m1 : derivative

✿01 See the idea

On a fictional test bench, a knob controls a resistive light. The knob first affects the electrical voltage; the voltage then determines the power dissipated. To understand the effect of the knob, we need to track this intermediate quantity.

At setting 2, the model gives 5 V, then 5 W. V stands for volt, the unit of voltage; W stands for watt, the unit of power. These are fictional data chosen for the calculation, with no readings or instructions for adjusting a real device.

A small turn follows the same steps. Near the chosen setting, the voltage changes at a rate of 2 V per unit of setting. Near this voltage, the power changes at a rate of 2 W per volt. These sensitivities multiply: each describes a different link in the chain.

Composition means taking the output of the first function as the input to the next. The derivative, introduced in the previous unit, measures a local sensitivity: the rate of change as the change in input tends to 0. The diagrams separate the values along the chain from the slopes that relate their changes.

The rule to remember

To differentiate a composition, evaluate the outer derivative at the intermediate value, then multiply by the inner derivative. Check the point, assumptions, and units.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the knob to the power dissipated

We use the model U = ax + b and P = (U × U)/R, with a = 2 V, b = 1 V, and R = 5 Ω. The symbol Ω stands for the ohm, the unit of resistance; the product V × V divided by Ω gives W. What is the sensitivity of the power to the setting x₀ = 2?

  1. Name the functions and state the assumptions

    x is the dimensionless setting, taken to be strictly positive. U is the voltage in volts; P is the power in watts. a is the voltage–setting slope, in volts per unit of x; b is the voltage offset, in volts; R is a strictly positive resistance, in ohms. These parameters remain constant during the calculation. We assume this model is valid in a neighbourhood of the setting being studied, with no interruption or change in resistance.

    Call the inner function g: g(x) = ax + b. Call the outer function f: f(u) = (u × u)/R, where u denotes a voltage input in volts. The composite function F gives the power directly from the setting. The symbol ∘ is read as “composed with”: f ∘ g applies g, then f.

    F(x)=(f∘g)(x)=f(g(x))=(ax+b)2R

  2. Follow the values before the slopes

    x0, read as “x at the chosen point”, equals 2. The intermediate voltage is U0 = g(x0) = 2 × 2 + 1 = 4 + 1 = 5 V. Its square is 25 in volts squared. The power is F(x0) = 25/5 = 5 W.

    Keep track of the voltage U0: it is the input actually received by f. The setting x0 and the voltage U0 have different roles and different units, even if some numerical values might happen to coincide.

  3. State the rule at the right point

    A prime, as in g′, denotes a derivative with respect to the input of the function. If g is differentiable at x0 and f is differentiable at g(x0), the chain rule gives the following formula. Differentiable here means that the rate of change has a finite limit as the increment tends to 0. Our affine and quadratic functions meet these conditions.

    F′(x0)=f′(g(x0))×g′(x0)

    Why a product? h denotes a nonzero change in the setting, with no units. ΔU = g(x0 + h) − g(x0) denotes the change in voltage, in volts. In our model, ΔU = ah is nonzero for nonzero h. The overall rate splits exactly into the power–voltage rate multiplied by the voltage–setting rate.

    F(x0+h)−F(x0)h=f(U0+ΔU)−f(U0)ΔU×ΔUh

    As h tends to 0, ΔU also tends to 0. The factors tend to f′(U0) and g′(x0). The intermediate unit cancels: (W/V) × (V per unit of x) gives W per unit of x. This justification using a quotient requires nonzero ΔU; the general rule still holds if g′(x0) is 0, without dividing by this derivative.

  4. Differentiate the inner transformation

    For the voltage, g(x0 + h) − g(x0) = a(x0 + h) + b − (ax0 + b) = ah. Dividing by h leaves a. The offset b disappears in this difference: it changes the voltage reached, but does not change the slope of g.

    g′(x0)=a=2 V par unité de réglage

  5. Differentiate the outer transformation

    v is now a nonzero voltage increment, in volts. Expanding the square of u + v and then subtracting the square of u gives 2uv + v × v. After division by Rv, the rate is 2u/R + v/R. As v tends to 0, the last term disappears.

    f′(u)=2uR

    Evaluate this slope at u = U0 = 5 V. The numerator 2 × 5 equals 10 V. Dividing by R = 5 Ω gives f′(U0) = 2 W/V. The identity V/Ω = W/V follows from W = (V × V)/Ω.

  6. Multiply the sensitivities and interpret the result

    F′(x0)=2(ax0+b)R×a

    Numerically: 2 W/V × 2 V per unit of setting = 4 W per unit of setting. The positive sign means that a sufficiently small increase in the setting increases the power in this model.

    This derivative concerns the power dissipated. We have no model relating this power to perceived brightness; the result therefore does not quantify that brightness.

  7. Check by expanding before differentiating

    Take the numerical values of the quantities in V, Ω, and W. We can expand the function of the setting as shown below. The coefficients come from a × a, 2ab, and b × b; they therefore come from the model, with no further adjustment.

    F(x)=4x2+4x+15 W

    The derivative of the square of x is 2x, by the difference of squares calculation used above; the derivative of x is 1 and that of a constant is 0. We obtain F′(x) = (8x + 4)/5. At x0: (8 × 2 + 4)/5 = (16 + 4)/5 = 20/5 = 4 W per unit of setting. This second route recovers the product of the slopes.

  8. Check using a finite change and distinguish it from the limit

    Choose h = 0.1. The new setting is 2.1, the voltage is 5.2 V, and the power is 5.408 W. The change in power is 5.408 − 5 = 0.408 W. Dividing by 0.1 gives 4.08 W per unit of setting, which differs from the derivative 4.

    This is not a rounding error. For nonzero h small enough to stay within the domain, the exact expansion gives [F(x0 + h) − F(x0)]/h = F′(x0) + (a × a/R)h. The coefficient a × a/R equals 0.8 W, since x is dimensionless. The difference from the limiting rate is therefore 0.8 × 0.1 = 0.08 W per unit of setting, matching 4.08 − 4 = 0.08.

    As h tends to 0, this term disappears. The expansion establishes the limit; a single finite change would only have provided a numerical check.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations and redraw the setting–voltage–power chain. Explain why the intermediate voltage appears in f′, why a multiplies this slope, and how the units cancel. Why does the rate obtained with h = 0.1 still differ from the derivative?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I have differentiated the power with respect to the voltage, so I am done.”

    f′(U₀) = 2 W/V measures the effect of one volt. The question concerns one unit of the knob setting: also multiply by g′(x₀) = 2 V per unit of setting. The final unit and the value 4 change together.

    Revisit the product of the sensitivities

  • “The rule is f′(x₀) × g′(x₀).”

    f receives a voltage. Its input is g(x₀), which here is 5 V; x₀ is a dimensionless setting. First reconstruct the values, then evaluate each slope at the input it receives. The last exercise explains the repair in detail.

    Repair the incorrect evaluation point

  • “The derivative gives the exact rate for any turn of the knob.”

    The derivative is a limiting rate. With h = 0.1, the exact rate is 4.08 W per unit of setting, whereas the derivative is 4. The extra term (a × a/R)h explains this difference and tends to 0 with h.

    Revisit the check using a finite change

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · follow the values

Another position of the same knob

Hint 1

The voltage is the intermediate quantity: start with g(x) = ax + b.

Hint 2

The value you have just obtained replaces u in f(u) = (u × u)/R.

Compare with the solution

The inner function is g: setting to voltage. It gives 2 × 1 + 1 = 3 V. The outer function is f: voltage to power. It gives (3 × 3)/5 = 9/5 = 9/5 W, or 1.8 W.

Apply g, then f, even though the notation f ∘ g places f on the left. Reversing the functions would feed a power into g, which expects a setting; that would be a different calculation, without the stated meaning.

The slope changes with the setting

Calculate g(3), then replace u with this voltage in f′(u) = 2u/R.

Hint 2

Then multiply by a. For the check, use F′(x) = (8x + 4)/5.

Compare with the solution

The voltage is 2 × 3 + 1 = 7 V. The outer slope becomes 2 × 7/5 = 14/5 = 14/5 W/V. Multiplying by 2 V per unit of setting gives 28/5 W per unit of setting, or 5.6 W per unit of setting.

The check using the expanded expression gives (8 × 3 + 4)/5 = 28/5. The overall slope therefore exceeds 4 W per unit of setting. g is affine and keeps its slope a; f is quadratic and its slope depends on the voltage reached. The evaluation point carries this change through the composition.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · diagnose a line of reasoning

A correct product at the wrong point

At setting x₀ = 2, someone writes f′(x₀)g′(x₀), inserts 2 in place of the voltage in 2u/R, then reports (2 × 2/5) × 2 = 1.6 W per unit of setting. Their multiplications are correct. Locate the error in reasoning and repair it, explaining more than just the correct result.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

What type of quantity does f accept? Write down the quantity actually produced by g.

Hint 2

The intermediate voltage is 5 V. Evaluate f′ again at this voltage, then keep the same inner slope.

Compare with the solution

The reported arithmetic does give 4/5 × 2 = 4/5 × 2 = 8/5. But it supplied f′ with the dimensionless setting 2, whereas f′ expects a voltage. The error comes before the multiplication.

The correct input is g(x0) = 5 V. Thus f′(g(x0)) = 2 × 5/5 = 2 W/V. Multiplying by g′(x0) = 2 V per unit of setting gives 4 W per unit of setting. The offset b does not appear in g′, but it remains in g(x0): losing it would change the voltage at which f′ is evaluated.

Check your reasoning

  • You recognise that the arithmetic giving 1.6 is correct for the incorrect substitution.
  • You explicitly repair the input to f′: g(x₀), in volts.
  • You explain why b still affects the overall slope and check the final unit.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In F = f ∘ g, which function acts first, and what quantity does it pass to the next?

g acts first. On the test bench, it transforms the setting x into the voltage g(x). This voltage becomes the input to f, which gives the power.

Recall 2Reconstruct the rule for differentiating F = f ∘ g at x₀, including its assumptions and evaluation points.

F′(x₀) = f′(g(x₀)) × g′(x₀), if g is differentiable at x₀ and f is differentiable at g(x₀). The outer derivative is evaluated at the output of g. The rule does not require g′(x₀) to be nonzero.

Recall 3Why multiply W/V by V per unit of setting? What question does the result answer?

The change in setting acts through the voltage. The product cancels the intermediate unit V and gives W per unit of setting: the local sensitivity of the power to the knob.

Recall 4In g(x) = ax + b, why can b still affect F′ even though it disappears from g′?

g′ equals a, but the voltage received by f is ax + b. Since f′(u) = 2u/R depends on u, changing b changes the evaluation voltage and therefore f′(g(x)).

Recall 5Does a difference quotient calculated for h = 0.1 establish the value of the derivative on its own?

It checks a finite change. Establishing the derivative requires the limit as h tends to 0. Here the quotient is F′(x₀) + (a × a/R)h; the extra term disappears in the limit.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without support, reconstruct the setting–voltage–power chain, then the formula F′(x₀) = f′(g(x₀)) × g′(x₀). Name the assumptions, the point for each slope, and the intermediate unit that cancels. Recover 4 W per unit of setting and explain why a finite difference quotient can differ from it.
