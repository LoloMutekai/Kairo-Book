Level 2 · C092·M1

Physical clock and content counter

Your goalCalculate a physical duration and a total weighted by content separately, then state what comparing them actually allows us to conclude.

Before you start
c035-m5 : finite covariance and conditioning · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c090-m5 : stationarity · c053-m5 : proxy, construct and validity · c062-m5 : prior audit and sensitivity

✿01 See the idea

A machine goes through a log of passages. It rereads a passage it has already encountered, then encounters a new passage. Its clock shows the elapsed time in both cases. To distinguish the content, we add a calculated counter that advances faster during the new passage.

In this fictional scene, rereading takes 10 s and the new passage takes 20 s. The counter adds 1 unit per second of rereading and 1.50 units per second of new content. A counter unit, written u, is a convention of our model: it measures neither words understood nor learning.

The log is divided into intervals whose duration and content we know. The rate is the number of units added per second. During an interval where this rate stays constant, multiplying duration by rate gives the increment to the counter. We then add the increments, without replacing the durations with the number of log entries.

The second drawing keeps the same duration and replaces the new passage with rereading. The counter changes because its rule depends on content. This arithmetic result is not enough to establish that the machine has a subjective experience. All durations and content scores on this page are invented.

The rule to remember

Keep both the measured duration and the calculated counter. Content sets the rate; duration sets how long this rate applies. State the rule and units before interpreting the difference between them.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Follow the rereading, then the new passage

The log starts at t = 0 s, changes content at t = 10 s and ends at t = 30 s. The counter starts at 0 u. The novelty score is 0 during rereading and 1 during the new passage. Let us calculate the increments without confusing timestamps, durations and running totals.

  1. Define the rule and its units

    t is a timestamp relative to the start of the session, in physical seconds, written s. Δt is a duration between two timestamps; Δ means “change”. C is the running counter total, in units u. r is its rate, in u/s. n is a dimensionless novelty score between 0 and 1; here it is assigned by the problem, not detected by a sensor.

    r0 is the base rate, set to 1 u/s. The subscript in r0 denotes this base, not an interval. α, the letter alpha, is a dimensionless coefficient chosen to equal 1/2. We define the rate by the following rule:

    r(n)=r0(1+αn)

    We assume ordered timestamps from the same clock, successive intervals with no gaps or overlaps, a constant rate within each interval and no counter reset. The rule is a deterministic teaching choice: it is not a psychological law.

  2. Subtract timestamps to obtain durations

    Let A be the rereading interval and B the interval for the new passage. The subscripts A and B, placed below a quantity, identify the interval concerned. An end timestamp is not the duration of the passage: the start timestamp must be subtracted.

    ΔtA=10−0=10 s
    ΔtB=30−10=20 s

    The total elapsed time T is 10 + 20 = 30 s. An independent check: last timestamp minus first timestamp, giving 30 − 0 = 30 s. The number of intervals cannot replace these durations.

  3. Calculate the rate for each type of content

    For A, the score n is 0: the factor 1 + 1/2 × 0 is 1. The rate for A is therefore 1 × 1 = 1 u/s. For B, n is 1: the factor is 3/2 and the rate is 3/2 u/s, or 1.50 u/s.

    rB=1×(1+12×1)=1.50 u/s

    The coefficient does not use the timestamp. This does not make the running total independent of duration: each rate still has to apply throughout its interval.

  4. Multiply each rate by the correct duration

    The subscript i denotes the current interval, A or B. ΔC with subscript i is the counter increment over this interval. C with a subscript indicating before or after is the running total at the corresponding boundary of the interval:

    ΔCi=riΔti;Caprès=Cavant+ΔCi

    A adds 1 u/s × 10 s = 10 u. B adds 3/2 u/s × 20 s = 30 u. Seconds cancel in the products; the results are indeed in counter units.

    After A, C is 10 u. After B, C is 0 + 10 + 30 = 40 u. The increment from B and the final running total are different quantities.

  5. Check against a session of rereading throughout

    With n always equal to 0, the same duration would have produced 1 × 30 = 30 u. Only B receives an additional rate equal to r0α. This additional rate applies for 20 s: 1 × 1/2 × 20 = 10 u. Adding this extra increment to the neutral total gives 30 + 10 = 40 u again.

    Another check: our rate stays between 1 and 1.50 u/s. Over 30 s, C must therefore stay between 30 and 45 u. The result 40 u is within these bounds. This check verifies the calculation under our rule, not the relevance of the novelty score.

  6. Compare totals while retaining durations

    To summarize the session, E denotes the average rate weighted by durations, in u/s. Here the initial counter is 0, so its total increase equals the final running total. Since T is strictly positive:

    E=ΔCA+ΔCBΔtA+ΔtB=4030=4/3 u/s

    The decimal value is E ≈ 1.33 u/s. Keep the exact fraction for subsequent calculations. If the duration were 0, this ratio would be undefined.

    Averaging the rates without accounting for durations gives (1 + 1.50)/2 = 1.25 u/s. Applying it for 30 s would produce 37.50 u, instead of 40 u. The passage with the higher rate lasted longer: it must carry more weight.

    We can now reconstruct a calculated counter and compare it with the clock. We have neither predicted the next content nor measured understanding or experienced duration. Those interpretations would require a definition and additional evidence.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Why is the last timestamp not the duration of B? Then explain how the counter depends on both content and time, and justify the denominator of the average rate.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Content drives the counter, so physical time is no longer needed.”

    Content selects r; the increment remains r × Δt. With the same content, extending an interval extends the accumulation. Revisit the units of the product to see what would disappear if you removed duration.

    Reread the calculation of the increments and their units

  • “It is enough to average the rates of the log entries.”

    A log entry does not represent a uniform duration. Calculate the increments, add them, then divide by the total time. The simple average works for intervals of equal duration; otherwise, it can be wrong, as it is here.

    Compare the average rate with the rates of the intervals

  • “A larger counter proves that the machine has experienced a longer time.”

    The rule produces conventional units. Its calculation establishes a behavior of the model, not a subjective experience. Keep the physical duration, the running total and the interpretation that would still need support separate.

    Distinguish existing code from missing evidence

Exercise 2 · Application · check a more detailed log

More entries, the same session

Return to the initial log. We split the new-content interval B into 2 intervals of equal duration, each with n = 1. No content or physical duration is added. Calculate the final counter. Explain when this subdivision preserves the result.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The duration of each half is 20/2 s.

Hint 2

The rate stays constant over B; distribute it over the two durations.

Compare with the solution

Each half lasts 10 s and adds 3/2 × 10 = 15 u. B therefore adds 15 + 15 = 30 u. Including A: 10 + 30 = 40 u.

The distributive property checks the result: multiplying the rate for B by the sum of the durations of its halves is equivalent to multiplying it by the whole duration of B. Subdivision preserves the running total if the rate is identical in the pieces, if they cover B exactly and if the counter is not reset. Counting entries would have changed the measure even though the session is unchanged.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 30 u for B and 40 u in total.
  • Justify the result using the distributive property and state the conditions for subdivision.
  • Distinguish the number of entries from duration and the running total.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · a total does not recover a sequence

Can content be inferred from the counter alone?

Still using the same rule, a variant lasts 30 s with a constant score n = 2/3. Calculate its rate and running total, then compare them with the initial log. Does the pair (total duration, final counter) allow us to recover the sequence of rereading followed by new content, or to conclude that there was a subjective experience?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The score can take any value between 0 and 1, even though the first log used only the endpoints.

Hint 2

First calculate αn. Then compare the totals, followed by the scores over time.

Compare with the solution

αn = 1/2 × 2/3 = 1/3. The factor is 1 + 1/3 = 4/3. The constant rate is therefore 4/3 u/s. Over 30 s, it produces 40 u.

This is the same final pair (30 s, 40 u) as in the initial log. Yet a constant score of 2/3 does not reproduce a score of 0 followed by 1. The calculation has compressed the sequence: the totals do not allow us to reconstruct it uniquely.

Check: the constant additional rate 1/3 u/s, multiplied by 30 s, gives 10 u; added to 30 u, it gives 40 u again. To distinguish the sequences, we must keep the intervals and their scores. None of these numerical traces establishes a subjective experience on its own.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do a timestamp, a duration and a number of entries differ?

A timestamp locates an interval boundary. The duration is the difference between its end and start timestamps, in seconds. The number of entries describes how the log is divided; it gives the duration only if a known recording frequency allows it.

Recall 2How do content and duration enter into the counter?

The score n selects the rate r = r₀(1 + αn), in u/s. Over an interval where this rate stays constant, the increment is r × Δt, in u. The increments are added to the previous running total.

Recall 3How can rates be summarized when intervals have different durations?

Add the increments, then divide by the strictly positive total duration. The result is in u/s. The simple average of the rates ignores durations; the weighted average using those durations recovers the running total.

Recall 4Why should splitting an interval into pieces leave the running total unchanged?

At a constant rate, r × (total duration) is the sum of the products r × duration for each piece. The pieces must cover the same interval with no gaps or overlaps, with the same rate and no reset.

Recall 5What cannot be concluded from a duration–counter pair?

It does not recover a unique content sequence: several sequences can give the same totals. It also does not prove understanding, learning or a subjective experience; the counter is defined by a rule.
