Log, metric and trace

Your goalChoose between an event, a numerical summary and the path of a request; calculate a duration and a mean while stating exactly what was observed.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You reserve a book on a library website. The response is slow. The log reports “reception started”, then “catalogue reading started”, then “saving started”, then “response ready”. These events provide reference points. One question still needs an answer: which part of the path took time for your request?

A log records individual events: what was reported, when, by which part of the program and, if the field was provided, for which request. An event is an entry at a particular point in time. Its content can be structured into fields; it does not have to be a sentence of free text. The order in which entries arrive does not replace their timestamps.

A metric is a numerical quantity tracked with a name, a unit and a scope. Here, it is the mean duration of requests completed within a window. It helps show the service as a whole; by itself, it does not retain the events of your reservation. The number of requests measured is part of its definition.

A trace brings together the path of a request. It links spans, meaning operations with a start and an end. Here, parent span R contains the Reception, Reading and Saving spans. The link to the parent states that these stages belong to this path. A log with the right identifiers can help reconstruct a trace; the words log and trace therefore do not necessarily refer to separate files.

The library and all the records on this page are fictional. R is the identifier of the request being followed; A, B and C are the other requests in the same record. The diagrams show observations prepared for the exercise, with no measurements taken from a real service.

The rule to remember

First ask what you are looking for: a specific event, a quantity within a scope, or the linked stages of a request. Then check the fields needed and the coverage of the record.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Find where time is spent along the path of R

The window runs from 0 ms inclusive to 2000 ms exclusive, measured from the start of the record. The 4 requests A, B, C and R finish within it; all their durations are available. A lasts 120 ms, B 160 ms and C 200 ms. For R, the log gives the times 1000, 1040, 1220 and 1320 ms. Let us calculate its trace and then the metric for the window.

  1. Set the boundaries of what is measured

    All the times for R come from the same monotonic clock: a counter that does not go backwards, with a common origin. A millisecond, written ms, is a second divided by 1000. The window lasts 2000 − 0 = 2000 ms, or 2 s. These are relative positions on this counter, rather than calendar times.

    The duration of a request starts at reception and ends when the response is ready. It does not measure the path of the response back to the library user. N denotes the number of requests completed and measured within the window: N = 4, in requests. In this scene, the record is complete, with no duplicates or losses; no request is still in progress when the window closes.

  2. Turn events into span boundaries

    For R, t_r = 1000 ms marks the start of Reception; t_l = 1040 ms the start of Reading; t_e = 1220 ms the start of Saving; t_f = 1320 ms the response being ready. The letters r, l, e and f serve only to name these boundaries. Here, the start of a stage is exactly the end of the previous one.

    Linking these events requires finding the same identifier R, the named stages and their stated order. Nearby lines in a log are not enough: a neighbouring line could belong to A. We attach the stage spans to parent span R. This link gives the structure of the path, beyond a simple list of timestamps.

  3. Subtract to obtain the stage durations

    A duration is the difference between end and start; it is not obtained by adding clock positions. d_r, d_l and d_e denote the durations of Reception, Reading and Saving, in ms. The subscripts identify the stages. Our assumptions make these differences nonnegative.

    dr=tl−tr=1040−1000=40 ms

    Reading: d_l = 1220 − 1040 = 180 ms. Saving: d_e = 1320 − 1220 = 100 ms. Reading has the longest duration. This indicates where to look within R; it does not yet explain why this stage took that long.

  4. Check the trace another way

    D denotes the total duration of parent span R, in ms. Take its endpoints directly: D = t_f − t_r = 1320 − 1000 = 320 ms.

    The stages are contiguous, in sequence, with no part omitted. Their sum gives 40 + 180 = 220 ms, then 220 + 100 = 320 ms. This recovers the difference between the endpoints: each internal boundary that is added is also subtracted.

    (tl−tr)+(te−tl)+(tf−te)=tf−tr

    This equality checks the arithmetic and the stated partition. If stages overlap, their sum can count the same period several times; if a wait is not represented, the stages no longer cover the parent. In these cases, keep the endpoints for the total duration and examine the structure before adding.

  5. Build a metric from the right set

    The chosen metric is the mean duration per request completed within this window, for this service. d_A, d_B and d_C denote the total durations of requests A, B and C, all in ms. S denotes the sum of the total durations of A, B, C and R, in ms; m denotes their mean, in ms per request. Each request contributes exactly once.

    Adding gives d_A + d_B = 120 + 160 = 280 ms; adding d_C gives 280 + 200 = 480 ms. Adding R gives S = 480 + 320 = 800 ms. We do not add the children of R, which are already included in its total duration.

    m=SN=dA+dB+dC+DN=8004=200 ms par demande

    A mean duration is usually displayed in ms; “per request” makes the calculation explicit here. The name of the quantity, the service, the window boundaries and the count must accompany the number. The 2 s describe the observation window: this is not the divisor for this mean.

  6. Check the mean and retain what it does not tell us

    Working backwards, 4 requests × 200 ms per request gives 800 ms again, the observed sum. The mean lies between the shortest duration 120 ms and the longest 320 ms. These checks detect a calculation or denominator error; they do not certify the collection of the data.

    The summary 200 ms does not state that all requests have this duration: R takes 320 ms. Nor does it say which stage of R takes up this time. The trace shows the 180 ms of Reading, and the log allows us to return to the corresponding events. Together, these observations make the question more precise; by themselves, they do not establish the cause of the wait or that the book was actually reserved.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The service mean tells me what happened to R.”

    The metric brings requests together. To recover R, look for its identifier and events, then their links and boundaries. Here, the mean is 200 ms while R lasts 320 ms; the summary does not retain this individual detail.

    Compare the window summary with the trace of R

  • “I add the parent and all its stages to be thorough.”

    The stages are contained in the parent. Adding 320 ms to the 320 ms of its children would give 640 ms by counting the same path again. Start from the endpoints, then check separately that the children cover the parent.

    Repeat the check using the endpoints

  • “If a duration is missing, I put 0 and keep the count.”

    A missing duration is not a duration of 0 ms. If appropriate, calculate the mean for only the measured requests, displaying their count and the incomplete coverage. The mean for the entire scope remains unknown without additional information.

    Repair the incomplete record in the missing durations exercise

Exercise 2 · Calculate with incomplete coverage

The duration of R is no longer available

A variant of the same record: your file no longer contains the duration or boundaries of R, or the overall sum. A separate counter still indicates 4 completed requests. You only have A = 120 ms, B = 160 ms and C = 200 ms. Without reusing the result from the lesson, calculate what you can publish and state what remains unknown. What would the unjustified replacement of the missing value with 0 ms give?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The counter of completed requests and the number of available durations no longer count the same set.

Hint 2

Add only the known durations and divide by the number of requests actually measured.

Compare with the solution

The number of available durations is 4 − 1 = 3. Their sum is 120 + 160 + 200 = 480 ms. The mean for the measured requests is therefore 480/3 = 160 ms.

An honest report is “observed mean duration: 160 ms; 3 durations available for 4 completed requests”. This is not the mean of all requests in the window: the duration of R, and therefore the overall sum, remain unknown in this variant. The loss of R can change the summary; nothing justifies assuming the remaining observations are representative.

Replacing the missing value with 0 ms would divide 480 by 4 and display 120 ms. This operation describes a record in which a request would have a duration of 0. That record was invented; the separate counter does not provide this duration.

Check your reasoning

  • I calculated the mean for the measured subset and stated its count.
  • I kept the overall mean as unknown.
  • I explained why a duration of 0 would be an invented additional observation.

Exercise 3 · Audit a line of reasoning

Too much time in the same trace

Using the complete record, a colleague adds parent span R of 320 ms and its children of 40, 180 and 100 ms. She reports a delay of 640 ms for the library user, then blames the catalogue as the definite cause. Repair the calculation and the conclusion. If the children overlapped, would their sum be enough to measure the delay?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Ask yourself whether the parent duration already includes the time of the children.

Hint 2

Start from the first and last boundaries. Locating elapsed time and demonstrating its cause require different evidence.

Compare with the solution

The delay measured on this page ends when the response is ready: 1320 − 1000 = 320 ms. The children total 40 + 180 + 100 = 320 ms. Here they partition the parent; adding them to the parent produces the double count 640 ms. These boundaries do not measure the delay until display to the library user.

Reading is the longest observed part, at 180 ms. It can contain waiting and other operations not detailed here. The trace locates an interval to examine; it does not demonstrate a definite cause in the catalogue. The diagnosis needs to be made more precise, and an observation sought that can distinguish between the explanations.

With overlapping children, the same period could be included in several durations. Their sum would no longer necessarily be the parent delay. Calculating from the endpoints would remain appropriate if those endpoints covered the intended path on the same clock; the links between spans would help explain the overlaps.

Check your reasoning

  • I rejected double counting by justifying the relationship between parent and children.
  • I gave the exact boundaries of the calculated delay.
  • I separated the located interval from its supposed cause.
  • I stated the assumption of continuity without overlap before adding the children.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be added to events to reconstruct the path of R?

Find the identifier of the same request, the boundaries of each operation and the relationships between spans. Nearby lines do not prove that they belong to the same path.

Recall 2What makes a mean duration interpretable?

The name of the quantity, its measurement boundaries, its unit, the service, the window, the measured count and the coverage. The mean summarises this set; it does not retain the individual path of R.

Recall 3When can the parent duration be recovered by adding the durations of the children?

When the children cover the entire parent in sequence, with no gaps or overlaps, and with comparable boundaries. The parent already contains this time: it is not added to its children.

Recall 4Does a lost duration allow a mean to be calculated using the original count?

Not by treating the absence as a duration of 0. Without other information, you can calculate the mean of the measured subset, stating its coverage; the overall mean remains unknown.

Recall 5The trace locates a long reading stage: what can we conclude?

Time elapsed between the observed boundaries of this stage. The cause of this interval, the completeness of the instrumentation and the actual effect of the reservation require suitable evidence.
