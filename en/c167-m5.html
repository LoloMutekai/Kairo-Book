Level 3 · C167·M5

Initial and boundary value problem

Your goalFormulate a heat problem on a bar, make the initial and boundary data agree, and verify an exact solution throughout the domain.

Before you start
c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c049-m5 : series solutions · c127-m5 : Green and Stokes · c167-m1 : heat · c167-m2 : waves · c167-m3 : Laplace · c167-m4 : Green

✿01 See the idea

A bar is hotter in the middle than at its ends. You know how heat spreads through it. That does not yet describe the whole experiment: how was the bar prepared, and what is then imposed at its left and right ends?

The initial condition is a snapshot of the whole bar at the start. The boundary conditions describe what happens at the ends during the experiment. A snapshot does not replace these instructions: the ends could be kept hot, cooled or insulated.

In this single setting, controlled baths cool the ends at the same rate. The middle is initially hotter. The values are fictional: the bar is 2 m long, the boundaries start at 20 °C and the centre at 30 °C. We follow only the 4 s covered by the model.

We choose data that admit an exact polynomial solution. The whole profile will move down without changing its curvature. This is a property of this particular match between the initial profile and the baths; with different baths, its shape would generally change too.

The rule to remember

A solution must satisfy the equation inside the bar, the initial profile and the instructions at the ends together. Also check that they agree where the initial time meets the boundaries.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A bar and baths that cool together

The bar has length L = 2 m and diffusivity α = 0.1 m²/s. Its initial profile is f(x) = θ + a x(L − x), with θ = 20 °C and a = 10 °C/m². The baths impose g(t) = θ + k t at both ends, with k = -2 °C/s, until T = 4 s. Find the temperature at x = 0.5 m and t = 3 s, after verifying a solution over the whole bar.

  1. Write the problem before looking for the formula

    x is the position measured from the left end, in metres; t is elapsed time, in seconds. L is the length, and T is the duration under study. u(x,t), in degrees Celsius, is the unknown temperature. The constant coefficient α measures diffusion, in square metres per second.

    ut is the derivative of u with respect to time with x held fixed, in °C/s. ux is its derivative with respect to position, in °C/m, and uxx is its second spatial derivative, in °C/m², with t held fixed. The product αuxx therefore has the same unit as ut. The factor a is in °C/m²: a x(L − x) is a temperature difference.

    ut=αuxx pour 0<x<L et 0<t≤T
    u(x,0)=f(x)=θ+ax(L−x)
    u(0,t)=u(L,t)=g(t)=θ+kt

    The initial profile applies at every position, including both ends; the boundary instructions apply throughout the duration, including the start. Prescribing a temperature is a Dirichlet condition. Prescribing a heat flux, which here amounts to a spatial derivative up to a physical factor, would be a different condition, called a Neumann condition. We do not impose both at the same end.

    Assumptions: a homogeneous bar, uniform temperature within each cross section, no internal source and no lateral heat exchange. The baths are assumed to follow their instructions exactly. For the verification and uniqueness argument below, we require u, ux, uxx and ut to be continuous up to the initial time and the boundaries. This model is not a calibration for a real material.

  2. Make the snapshot and the baths agree at the corners

    At the left end, x = 0 makes x(L − x) vanish. At the right end, x = L makes L − x vanish. Thus f(0) = f(L) = θ = 20 °C. Initially, g(0) = θ = 20 °C too: the profile and the baths prescribe the same temperature at each corner.

    This agreement is necessary for continuity at the corners. By itself, it is not enough to guarantee all the regularity required for an arbitrary problem. Here, the polynomial solution we will construct lets us check regularity and the equation directly.

  3. Let the equation determine the cooling

    Try u(x,t) = θ + a x(L − x) + c(t). The function c, in °C, is a vertical displacement of the profile that is still unknown. Its time derivative, denoted c′, is in °C/s. This is a candidate to verify, not a general form for solutions of the heat equation.

    Expanding a x(L − x) gives aLx minus a times the square of x. Differentiating with respect to x gives ux = aL − 2ax and uxx = −2a. The curvature is constant and negative: uxx = -20 °C/m². In time, ut = c′(t). The equation therefore requires:

    c′(t)=−2αa=1/10×-20=-2 °C/s

    The equation has fixed the rate at which the profile shifts. It has not yet fixed c at the start. The rate k stated for the baths is exactly the one this profile requires.

  4. Use the initial condition to fix the remaining constant

    To recover f(x) at t = 0, we need c(0) = 0 °C. Integrating the constant rate gives c(t) = kt, with no additional offset. We obtain:

    u(x,t)=θ+ax(L−x)−2αat

    The time term is kt. At each end the parabolic term vanishes: the temperature becomes θ + kt, exactly the prescribed bath temperature. If the baths were kept at θ, this same formula would no longer work, even with the same initial profile.

  5. Check the identities throughout the domain

    Equation: ut = k = -2 °C/s and αuxx = 0.1 × (-20) = -2 °C/s, for every x and every t in the domain. Initial condition: the term kt vanishes and leaves f(x). Boundaries: the term a x(L − x) vanishes and leaves g(t), throughout the experiment.

    These are identities between functions. Checking only the central temperature at a single time would not replace them. Our polynomial is smooth up to the corners; even the initial rate of change of the bath temperatures agrees here with α times the second spatial derivative of the initial profile at the ends. We have therefore constructed an exact solution of the stated problem.

  6. Perform the requested calculation, then check it independently

    At x = 1/2 m = 0.5 m, the distance remaining to the right boundary is L − x = 2 − 1/2 = 3/2 m. Their product is 1/2 × 3/2 = 3/4 m². Multiplying by a gives the excess temperature of the profile: 10 × 3/4 = 15/2 °C = 7.5 °C.

    The initial temperature at this position is θ + 15/2 = 55/2 °C. After 3 s, the change with time is k t = (-2) × 3 = -6 °C. Adding gives:

    u(1/2,3)=20+15/2+(-6)=43/2 °C=21.5 °C

    Check using another decomposition: the baths are at θ + kt = 14 °C; adding the excess of 7.5 °C gives 21.5 °C again. By symmetry, the position L − x = 1.5 m gives the same value at this time.

    At the same time, the centre x = L/2 = 1 m retains an excess temperature of 10 °C and reaches 24 °C. Our value is indeed between the bath temperature of 14 °C and the peak temperature of 24 °C. This check follows from the known parabola; a plausible value alone would not prove the equation.

  7. Why the same data cannot give another regular solution

    Suppose U and V are regular solutions of this same problem. Their difference w = U − V, in °C, satisfies wt = αwxx, but its initial profile and boundary values are 0, since the data cancel when subtracted.

    Define E(t) as the spatial integral of the square of w between the ends; dx indicates integration over position. It adds squared differences and is therefore nonnegative. Its unit is °C²·m; it measures the difference, rather than physical thermal energy. Its time derivative E′ is in °C²·m/s.

    E(t)=∫0Lw2 dx

    Differentiating the square gives E′ as the integral of 2wwt. Replace wt with αwxx, then integrate by parts in position. The boundary term is 2α[wwx], where the brackets mean the value at the right end minus the value at the left end, and wx is the spatial derivative of the difference, in °C/m. It vanishes because w is 0 at the ends. What remains is the negative of an integral of squares:

    E′(t)=−2α∫0L(wx)2 dx≤0

    Since α is positive, E cannot increase. Now E(0) = 0 and E cannot be negative: E remains 0. Continuity of w then forces w to be 0 everywhere, so U = V. Uniqueness is established within the stated regularity class. We have not proved an existence theory for all data or for all partial differential equations.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the solution. Draw the locations where the data are known, recover the displacement c(t) from the curvature, then explain why the boundaries must be checked separately. In the uniqueness proof, identify exactly where the 0 initial profile and 0 boundary values are used.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “It satisfies the heat equation, so it must be the correct temperature.”

    The equation describes the local law. A different preparation or different baths give a different problem. Repeat the checks as identities: the equation, the initial condition, then each boundary throughout the duration. A correct temperature at a single point is not enough.

    Repeat the checks throughout the domain

  • “The boundary is cold, so it is insulated.”

    A cold bath imposes a temperature and can receive heat. An insulated end imposes 0 flux, which here means a 0 spatial derivative. These are different data: our baths impose g(t), without also prescribing insulation.

    Reread the Dirichlet and Neumann conditions

  • “The profile and the baths can contradict each other initially; diffusion will sort it out.”

    If the profile and the bath demand different values at the same corner, no solution continuous over the whole closed domain can obey both. The data must be corrected, or a different notion of solution and its regularity must be stated. The exercise about conflicting corner data makes this limitation precise.

    Diagnose the mismatch at the initial corner

Exercise 1 · Identify and justify

The instruction that contradicts the snapshot

Keep the initial profile of the bar. A specification changes only the instruction for the right bath to “25 °C from the start, then constant temperature”. Classify the profile and the baths as an initial condition or boundary conditions. Can a solution continuous up to the initial right corner satisfy this new specification? Quantify the mismatch.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Evaluate the profile at x = L and compare it with the new instruction at t = 0.

Hint 2

The corner is a single point in space and time: a continuous temperature cannot have two values there.

Compare with the solution

The profile f is the initial condition, imposed over the whole bar at the start. The bath instructions are boundary conditions, imposed at each end over time.

At the right corner, f(L) = 20 °C whereas the new bath requires 25 °C. The difference is 25 − 20 = 5 °C. There is therefore no solution continuous up to this corner that satisfies these data as stated.

One possible repair is to give the right bath an initial value equal to 20 °C, then specify how it changes with time. Returning to g(t) restores the problem we solved. Sudden contact can be studied with a solution that is not continuous at the corner; this requires a different formulation, which this diagnosis does not address.

  • I locate the prescriptions in space and time.
  • I calculate the mismatch of 5 °C at the same corner.
  • I limit my conclusion to the required continuity, without ruling out every less regular formulation.

Exercise 2 · Independent calculation

On the other side of the bar, at the final time

Return to exactly the baths used in the example. Calculate u at x = 1.5 m and t = 4 s. Keep the fractions exact. Then check the temperature of each bath and of the centre at this time. Explain why the requested point retains the same excess temperature above the baths as its symmetric counterpart.

Hint 1

The product x(L − x) does not change when the two factors are exchanged.

Hint 2

First calculate the bath temperature θ + kt; then add the spatial excess.

Compare with the solution

The distance to the right boundary is 1/2 m. The spatial product is therefore 3/2 × 1/2 = 3/4 m² and the excess is a × 3/4 = 15/2 °C. This is the same product as in the example, with the factors exchanged.

Over 4 s, the change is (-2) × 4 = -8 °C. The baths reach 20 + (-8) = 12 °C. The requested temperature is 12 + 15/2 = 39/2 °C = 19.5 °C.

At the centre, the excess remains 10 °C: the central temperature is 12 + 10 = 22 °C. We also recover 30 + (-8) = 22 °C from the initial central temperature. The requested result is above the baths and below the centre. The symmetry comes from x(L − x) and the identical instructions at the ends.

Check your reasoning

  • I obtain 19.5 °C and include the unit.
  • I recalculate the baths at the new time, instead of keeping 14 °C.
  • I check that the result lies between the boundary and central temperatures and justify the symmetry.

Exercise 3 · Verify a candidate

The data are satisfied, but what about the equation?

A candidate v adds the term ηt x(L − x) to the solution u, with η = 1 °C/(m²·s). It satisfies the same initial profile and the same bath conditions. Prove this, then calculate its residual R = vₜ − αvₓₓ. Evaluate R at the centre x = 1 m at t = 3 s. Does this candidate solve the problem without a source?

v(x,t)=u(x,t)+ηtx(L−x)
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The added term vanishes initially and at each end. Yet it remains nonzero inside the bar after the start.

Hint 2

Its time derivative is ηx(L − x); its second spatial derivative is −2ηt. Subtract α times the second derivative from the first.

Compare with the solution

At t = 0, the factor t makes the addition vanish. At x = 0 or x = L, the factor x(L − x) makes it vanish. The initial and bath conditions are therefore preserved as functions, not just at the central point.

Since u already satisfies the equation, its contributions cancel in R. This leaves R(x,t) = ηx(L − x) + 2αηt, in °C/s. In the interior, these terms are positive for the chosen data and positive t: the residual cannot be 0.

Numerical check at the centre: the time derivative is k plus η times the square of half the length, so vt = -2 + 1 = -1 °C/s. Also, vxx = −2a − 2ηt = -20 − 6 = -26 °C/m². Thus αvxx = -13/5 °C/s and R = -1 − (-13/5) = 8/5 °C/s = 1.6 °C/s.

The residual measures the source that would have to be added for v to satisfy a modified heat equation. The stated problem has no source: v does not solve it. Satisfying the data therefore never removes the need to check the equation.

Check your reasoning

  • I check the initial condition and both boundaries by showing that the factors vanish.
  • I recover the positive sign of the term 2αηt in the residual.
  • I calculate 8/5 °C/s and reach a conclusion about the equation without a source, including the units of the derivatives.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be specified in addition to the heat equation for our bar?

The domain in position and time, the coefficient and assumptions, the initial temperature profile and the instructions at the ends during the experiment. The class of solutions sought also determines what verification means.

Recall 2What agreement does a continuous solution require at the initial right corner?

The profile evaluated at the right end and the initial bath instruction must agree: f(L) = g(0). This agreement is necessary for continuity; by itself, it does not prove every property of a regular solution.

Recall 3Why does a bath at a prescribed temperature not imply an insulated end?

The bath fixes the temperature, a Dirichlet condition, and can exchange heat. Insulation imposes 0 flux, a Neumann condition, which here translates into a 0 spatial derivative.

Recall 4Which checks distinguish a solution of the problem from a plausible formula?

Check the equation at all interior positions and times, the profile over the whole bar initially, the instructions at each boundary throughout the duration, and agreement at the corners within the required regularity. A numerical check at a single point remains only a partial check.

Recall 5Where are the identical data used in the uniqueness proof?

Subtracting them gives a 0 difference initially and at the boundaries. The initial condition makes the integral of the squared difference initially 0; the boundaries cancel the boundary term in integration by parts. The integral can neither increase nor become negative: it remains 0, so the continuous difference is 0.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking, reconstruct this sequence: locate the equation, the initial condition and the boundaries on a space–time rectangle; check the corners; let the curvature fix the rate, then let the initial condition fix the constant; check the solution everywhere before evaluating it at a point. Finally, explain why subtracting two solutions turns their shared data into zeros that help establish uniqueness.
