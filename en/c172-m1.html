Level 3 · C172·M1

Existence and uniqueness

Your goalConstruct a solution to a stochastic differential equation, check a sufficient criterion and explain what uniqueness compares.

Before you start
c171-m5 : multidimensional case · c052-m5 : convergence protocol

✿01 See the idea

A cart moves along an ideal rail with no stops. A motor gives it a constant velocity; unpredictable fluctuations push it sometimes one way, sometimes the other. We want to write a rule that defines its position at every instant. Before calculating a path, we need to know whether this rule defines one, and whether it can define several for the same fluctuations.

Imagine giving two people the same starting position, the same rule and the same complete record of fluctuations. Existence requires that they can reconstruct a position consistent with the rule. Pathwise uniqueness requires their reconstructions to coincide at every instant, except on an event of probability 0.

If you give them different fluctuations, their carts may reach different final positions. This does not contradict uniqueness: the noise is one of the inputs that must stay identical. The model can therefore have a unique solution and produce varied paths.

The scene and all its values are fictional. The rail is represented by the entire real line: no position limits, collisions or braking controls are included. The fluctuation values chosen below are synthetic calculation data, rather than an observed record.

The rule to remember

Fix the starting position and the same Brownian motion; construct a process that satisfies the integral equation; then compare all solutions subject to these same inputs.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A starting position, a velocity and a specified noise

The cart starts at 2 m. Its velocity v is 1.50 m/s and its noise coefficient a is 0.50 m/√s. Over the duration T = 4 s, we are given W at the intermediate time 2 s: 1/2 √s, then at T: -1 √s. First construct the entire solution, then calculate these positions.

  1. Give the rule a precise meaning

    t is elapsed time in seconds; T is a finite time horizon. Xt is the position in metres and xᵢ the initial position, which is deterministic here. Wt denotes a real Brownian motion, starting at 0. With time in seconds, we assign it the unit √s: its increments have mean 0, variance equal to the corresponding duration and are independent of past information.

    Ft denotes the information available up to t. We fix a probabilistic framework with the usual conditions: this information increases with time, is right-continuous and contains events of probability 0. W is Brownian motion relative to this information. An adapted process uses only Ft at time t: it does not anticipate future fluctuations.

    b(t,x) is the drift, in m/s; a(t,x) is the noise coefficient, in m/√s. For our cart, b(t,x) = v and a(t,x) = a are constant. The symbol d is integral notation: we do not assume that W has an ordinary velocity.

    dXt=b(t,Xt)dt+a(t,Xt)dWt
    Xt=xi+∫0tb(s,Xs)ds+∫0ta(s,Xs)dWs

    s is a time variable that runs through the integral. The first integral accumulates the drift; the second is an Ito integral, introduced in c171-m5. In each integral, the product has unit m. This integral identity is the contract to check.

  2. Separate strong existence and pathwise uniqueness

    A strong solution is constructed from the already specified starting position and Brownian motion, without adding auxiliary randomness. It is adapted, continuous and satisfies the integral identity, almost surely for all instants in the interval. “Almost surely” means with probability 1, allowing an exceptional event of probability 0.

    Pathwise uniqueness compares X and another solution Y on the same probability space, with the same W and the same starting position. They must be indistinguishable: there is a single event of probability 1 on which they are equal at every instant. P denotes probability, and the symbol ∀ means “for all”.

    P(∀t∈[0,T],Xt=Yt)=1

    These definitions do not say that there is only one possible path for all fluctuations. They specify the inputs kept identical in the comparison.

  3. State a sufficient criterion, with its assumptions

    Here is a standard criterion for the scalar case. The deterministic functions b and a are measurable in (t,x). Throughout the chosen interval, they are globally Lipschitz in position: an input difference cannot amplify their difference beyond the bounds below. x and y are any two positions; the finite constants L_b and L_a depend on neither position nor time.

    |b(t,x)−b(t,y)|≤Lb|x−y|
    |a(t,x)−a(t,y)|≤La|x−y|

    L_b is expressed per second and L_a per square root of a second. These units make each inequality dimensionally consistent. We also need at most linear growth, uniformly in time. Fix a reference length ℓ > 0, in metres; B ≥ 0 is in m/s and S ≥ 0 in m/√s.

    |b(t,x)|≤B(1+|x|ℓ)
    |a(t,x)|≤S(1+|x|ℓ)

    Finally, the starting position is known from the initial information and has a finite second moment: the expected value of its square is finite. E denotes this expectation. Under these assumptions, a strong solution exists and is pathwise unique over the chosen finite horizon. These bounds ensure that no explosion occurs over this horizon: the position does not become infinite in finite time.

    E[|xi|2]<∞

    The word “sufficient” matters: a coefficient that fails this criterion needs further study. This failure alone establishes neither absence of a solution nor multiple solutions. We use this theorem here; the construction and direct proof for our particular case follow.

  4. Check the criterion for the cart

    For all positions x and y, b(t,x) − b(t,y) = v − v = 0 m/s. Likewise, a(t,x) − a(t,y) = 0 m/√s. We can therefore choose L_b = 0 per second and L_a = 0 per square root of a second. A constant coefficient satisfies the condition even if it is nonzero.

    Take ℓ = 1 m, B = 1.50 m/s and S = 0.50 m/√s. The factor 1 + |x|/ℓ is always at least 1, so the growth bounds hold. The constant coefficients are measurable. The deterministic starting position has second moment 4 square metres, a finite quantity.

    The theorem applies to the model on the entire rail. A stop or a contact rule would require a separate model; no such assumption has been included in the scene.

  5. Construct the solution before calculating a final value

    The integral of the constant velocity is vt. For the noise, the integral of a constant against W is a multiplied by the increment Wt − W at the start; this starting value is 0. This leaves:

    Xt=xi+vt+aWt

    This formula constructs X from W, without additional noise. It is adapted because Wt is available at t, and almost surely continuous because W is. It has the correct initial value at the start. The integrability condition for the noise is checked directly: the integral of the square of a over the interval is that square multiplied by t, a finite quantity; at T it is 1 square metre.

    Substituting the integrals into the contract gives exactly the proposed formula at every instant. This proves existence for this equation. No time approximation is involved. On a finite closed interval, the continuity of W bounds each path outside the exceptional event: the solution does not explode.

  6. Calculate the final position while keeping the units

    The numerical noise data are fixed only for the exercise. For T = 4 s and W at T equal to -1 √s, the accumulated drift is vT = 3/2 × 4 = 6 m: we multiply a velocity by a duration.

    The random displacement is aW at T = 1/2 × (-1) = -1/2 m, or -0.50 m. Its sign is negative because the supplied fluctuation is negative; the noise coefficient remains positive.

    Without the fluctuations, the final position would be 2 + 6 = 8 m. With them, we add the displacements to the initial position:

    XT=2+6+(-1/2)=15/2 m=7.50 m

    The result is 0.50 m behind the final position without noise. This sign check does not prove the theorem, but it can detect a calculation error.

  7. Check by splitting the interval

    At 2 s, W = 1/2 √s. The drift displacement is 3/2 × 2 = 3 m; the noise displacement is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 m. The intermediate position is 2 + 3 + 1/4 = 21/4 m, or 5.25 m.

    There are 4 − 2 = 2 s left. We must use the remaining noise increment: -1 − 1/2 = -3/2 √s. Reusing W at T as a new increment would incorrectly count the fluctuations already received.

    The remaining drift is 3 m; the remaining noise is 1/2 × (-3/2) = -3/4 m. We obtain 21/4 + 3 + (-3/4) = 15/2 m, or 7.50 m, as with the direct calculation.

    The check is exact: the durations add up, and the Brownian increments telescope to give W at T minus W at the start. The intermediate values do not describe positions between the supplied instants; connecting these points with line segments would not trace the actual Brownian motion.

  8. Prove uniqueness for all candidate solutions

    Let Y be another solution with the same starting position and the same W. Its coefficients are the same constants, so its integral identity requires Yt = xᵢ + vt + aWt. Subtracting its identity from that of X gives Xt − Yt = 0 m at every instant, on the event where the identities hold. This establishes pathwise uniqueness for this equation over the entire interval, beyond just the final position.

    With a different Brownian motion, the subtraction would retain a term for the difference in noises: it would not necessarily give 0. The exercise comparing noises uses precisely this distinction.

    We have proved existence and uniqueness for the specified model. This does not validate its suitability for a real cart, a numerical algorithm or the operation of a program. The assumptions of the theorem and the arithmetic check serve distinct purposes.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A unique solution means that every trial reaches the same place.”

    The Brownian motion is one of the specified inputs. Uniqueness compares the same starting position and the same noise on the same probability space. Change the noise in the second exercise and explain why the final position can change.

    Compare the noises in the exercises

  • “My calculation gives a finite position, so I have proved existence.”

    A final value does not construct an adapted, continuous process satisfying the integral identity at every instant. Start again from Xₜ = xᵢ + vt + aWₜ, then check each of these properties.

    Revisit the construction of the solution

  • “If the coefficient is not globally Lipschitz, no solution exists.”

    The stated criterion is sufficient. Its failure leaves the question open. In the variant without noise in the last exercise, we even construct several solutions: existence and uniqueness are decided separately.

    Study the variant in the last exercise

Exercise 2 · Calculation and interpretation

Does a different final position contradict uniqueness?

Keep the same starting position, velocity and noise coefficient. For another synthetic realization, W at T is now 1 √s. Calculate the new final position, then its difference from the one in the example. Can these different final positions be used to disprove pathwise uniqueness?

Hint 1

The accumulated drift remains 6 m; only the contribution aW at T changes.

Compare with the solution

The contribution of the new noise is 1/2 × 1 = 1/2 m. The final position is therefore 8 + 1/2 = 17/2 m, or 8.50 m. The difference from the example is 17/2 − 15/2 = 1 m.

This difference does not disprove pathwise uniqueness: the specified noises differ. To disprove it, we would need to find different solutions with the same starting position and the same Brownian motion, on the same space. Comparing final positions alone is also insufficient to prove equality of entire paths.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and proof on paper

The criterion fails: what can we still conclude?

A mathematical variant on the same rail: the cart starts again from 0 m, the noise is removed and the rule becomes b(t,x) = 2√(c|x|), with c = 1 metres per second squared. Check that Xₜ = 0 m and Yₜ = c multiplied by the square of t are two solutions for t ≥ 0. Compare their positions at T = 4 s. Where does the Lipschitz assumption fail?

Xt=0;Yt=ct2;t≥0
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Without noise, the integral identity can be checked by integrating the drift or by checking a derivative and the starting position.

Hint 2

For Y, its derivative is 2ct; for t ≥ 0, √((ct) squared) = ct.

Hint 3

Compare b(t,ε) with b(t,0) for a strictly positive position ε tending to 0.

Compare with the solution

c is a positive constant in metres per second squared, so √(c|x|) is a velocity. For X identically 0, the derivative and the drift are both 0 m/s. For Yt = c multiplied by the square of t, the derivative is 2ct. Substituting Y into b gives 2√((ct) squared) = 2ct because t ≥ 0 and c > 0. Both processes are deterministic, continuous, adapted and start at 0 m; they also satisfy the integral identity.

At T, X is 0 m and Y is 1 × 4 × 4 = 16 m. Its derivative there is 8 m/s, equal to the drift 2√(1 × 16) m/s. Existence is therefore established and uniqueness is false, even with a shared Brownian motion that is unused because the noise coefficient is 0.

For a position ε > 0, the ratio |b(t,ε) − b(t,0)|/|ε − 0| is 2√(c/ε), in units per second. It has no finite bound as ε tends to 0. The Lipschitz condition fails near the starting position. Constructing the distinct solutions, rather than this failure alone, proves non-uniqueness here.

Check your reasoning

  • You check the initial condition and the drift for each solution.
  • You keep the units of c and obtain 16 m for the second final position.
  • You locate the failure of the Lipschitz condition and distinguish failure of the criterion, existence and non-uniqueness.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must you construct to prove strong existence in our scene?

A process built from the specified starting position and Brownian motion, adapted and continuous, that satisfies the integral identity almost surely at every instant. Here Xₜ = xᵢ + vt + aWₜ constructs it explicitly.

Recall 2Which inputs must remain identical when you compare two solutions?

The same starting position and the same Brownian motion, on the same probability space. The solutions must coincide at every instant on an event of probability 1; different noises can give different paths.

Recall 3What are the assumptions of the stated sufficient criterion?

Measurable coefficients, globally Lipschitz in position and with at most linear growth uniformly over the finite horizon; a starting position known initially with finite second moment, in the specified Brownian framework. The criterion ensures strong existence and pathwise uniqueness.

Recall 4Why does the second part of the calculation use final W minus intermediate W?

The integral of a constant coefficient against W over this interval equals that coefficient multiplied by the corresponding increment. Reusing final W alone does not represent the noise received since the intermediate instant.

Recall 5What can you conclude if the coefficient is not Lipschitz?

The criterion no longer decides the question. Another proof is needed. In the variant without noise, constructing the 0 and quadratic solutions proves their existence, then their difference proves non-uniqueness.
