Level 1 · C019·M5

Clocks and persistence

Your goalChoose a clock for assigning dates or measuring time, then reconstruct a recovery from a checkpoint that was actually saved.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You are copying a batch of 12 records on a computer. When you pause, 4 copies are confirmed. The displayed time moved backwards during the work: should you report a negative duration? Then the computer restarts. Should the new program trust what the old one displayed, or read back what was retained?

A wall clock assigns dates: it places an event on a calendar and against a time reference. Its setting can change. A monotonic clock measures an interval: its readings do not move backwards because of an adjustment to wall clock time. Its origin is arbitrary; a single reading is not a date.

Persistence means retaining a state beyond the end of a process, on a medium intended for that purpose. A recovery point, also called a checkpoint, describes a confirmed boundary in the work. Seeing it in memory and having saved it are different facts.

The entire scenario and its numbers are fictional. We copy records into files named by their record numbers, from an identified batch that stays unchanged. Copying a record again replaces its content with the same content. No sending or other external action is associated with this copy.

The rule to remember

Assign dates to events with the wall clock, measure each segment with the same monotonic clock, then resume from a saved and verified state.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Pause the copying, then resume it

The initial segment runs from 10000 to 13500 ms on the monotonic counter. The wall clock starts at 600000 ms and is moved backwards by 60000 ms. After 4 records, the program saves a checkpoint, shuts down normally, and then the machine restarts. Let us calculate the retained duration and how the copying continues.

  1. Define the quantities and the boundary

    M_d and M_f denote the monotonic readings at the start and end of a segment, in milliseconds, written ms. Δ is the duration of this segment, in the same unit; the symbol Δ is read as “change”. W_d and W_f denote the wall clock readings at the same events, also in ms. C is the backward adjustment applied to the wall clock. One second, written s, contains 1000 ms.

    N is the total number of records; n is the last confirmed record number, and therefore also the number of confirmed copies, since record numbers start at 1 with no gaps. We require 0 ≤ n ≤ N. E is the cumulative duration of the confirmed segments, in ms. The checkpoint retains the batch identity, n and E; its optional wall clock date serves only to place the event in time.

    A single process writes the copies and the checkpoint. We stop only between completed copies. We assume that the monotonic readings come from the same machine during the same boot session within each segment, with no sleep. Our duration measures the elapsed time between these readings, including waits, rather than the time during which the processor is actually computing.

  2. Measure before assigning a date

    Subtracting the start reading from the end reading eliminates the arbitrary origin of the counter. This operation is valid within the segment described.

    Δ=Mf−Md=13500−10000=3500 ms

    To express this duration in seconds, divide by 1000: 3500 ÷ 1000 = 7/2 s, or 3.50 s. Inverse check: 10000 + 3500 = 13500 ms; the duration is positive.

  3. Construct the wall clock counterexample

    In our fictional model, the clocks advance at the same rate except during the adjustment. Without the adjustment, the final wall clock reading would be 600000 + 3500 = 603500 ms. The backward adjustment C gives 603500 − 60000 = 543500 ms.

    Wf−Wd=Δ−C=3500−60000=-56500 ms

    Subtracting the wall clock readings would give -56.50 s. The work did not travel back in time: the instrument assigning its dates was changed. Check: -56500 + 60000 = 3500 ms. Even a positive wall clock difference would not be enough to rule out a smaller adjustment.

  4. Save a consistent state

    At the chosen boundary, the copies for record numbers 1 through 4 are complete and verified. We set n = 4 records and E = 3500 ms. The checkpoint must describe these copies, the correct batch and this duration together. Displaying “saved” before knowing the result of the write would be a promise.

    To prevent a reader from seeing a partly replaced file, one method is to prepare a complete temporary file, then replace the checkpoint file on the same file system, using an operation that guarantees an indivisible replacement. This is the atomicity of the replacement. It does not make the copies and the checkpoint together atomic.

    Durability raises another question: what will remain after the failure being considered? Successfully reading the file back confirms the content that is currently readable; it does not by itself prove that this content will survive a power cut. Synchronizing the file and, depending on the system, its directory are among the guarantees to examine. Here, we assume that the copies and the checkpoint are retained after a normal restart; we do not simulate a storage failure. If a write fails, the new checkpoint remains unconfirmed.

  5. Read back before resuming

    The new process does not receive the variables of the old one. It opens the retained file and checks the batch identity, the consistency of the record number and the corresponding copies. It finds n = 4. The next record number is n + 1 = 5; the remaining work is N − n = 12 − 4 = 8 records.

    Check: the 4 confirmed records and the 8 remaining records cover all 12 records in the batch. If the file is unreadable, the batch has changed or the claimed copies are missing, report an unverified recovery; do not turn this problem into 0 progress or success.

  6. Measure a new segment and add the durations

    After the restart, the monotonic counter reads 2000 ms at the start, then 6000 ms after 4 new copies. Its new origin is not linked to that of the previous boot session. Subtracting 10000 from 2000 would give -8000 ms, or -8 s: this operation across boot sessions is meaningless for measuring our work.

    The new duration is 6000 − 2000 = 4000 ms, or 4.00 s. Add this duration to E, rather than adding one raw reading to another raw reading.

    Enouveau=E+Δ=3500+4000=7500 ms

    The exact cumulative duration is 15/2 s, or 7.50 s. It excludes the machine downtime and the interval between the measured segments. It must not be reported as the duration from the initial launch to the end of all the work.

  7. Check the new boundary

    The confirmed record number becomes 4 + 4 = 8. After saving and reading back the new checkpoint, 12 − 8 = 4 records remain; the next is record 9. Coverage check: 8 + 4 = 12. Time check: 7500 − 3500 = 4000 ms.

    A useful recovery receipt identifies the batch, the checkpoint read back, the copies checked and the outcome of the attempt. It links a claim to specific evidence. A file named “receipt” is not automatic proof, however: its content and checks must correspond to the current attempt.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “An end time minus a start time always gives the duration.”

    A wall clock adjustment can change the time during the work. In our copying task, the subtraction gives -56.50 s for a segment lasting 3.50 s. Measure the segment with comparable monotonic readings; keep wall clock time for assigning its date.

    Revisit the wall clock counterexample

  • “I will save the monotonic counter and continue the subtraction after the restart.”

    The new origin is not guaranteed to be comparable to the old one. Retain the duration already calculated, then add the duration measured between the new start and the new end. Do not count downtime without defining another measurement.

    Reconstruct the cumulative duration after recovery

  • “The program displayed its progress, so that must be what will be recovered.”

    The counter in memory can go beyond the last saved checkpoint. Resume from the boundary that was read back and verified. Preparing a state, successfully writing it and verifying recovery from it are separate steps; an indivisible replacement does not prove every aspect of resistance to failure.

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · choose, then calculate

The counter for a test segment

In another trial of the same copying task, with no restart during the segment, the monotonic counter goes from 20000 to 22750 ms. Wall clock time is adjusted during this segment. Which pair of readings do you choose for its duration? Calculate it in ms, then in s, and give an inverse check.

Hint 1

Choose readings whose origin remains comparable throughout the segment.

Hint 2

To convert a duration from ms to seconds, divide by 1000.

Compare with the solution

The monotonic readings measure this segment despite the wall clock adjustment. The difference is 22750 − 20000 = 2750 ms.

The exact duration is 2750/1000 = 11/4 s, or 2.75 s. The inverse check 20000 + 2750 = 22750 recovers the end reading. The monotonic readings alone do not give the wall clock date of the segment.

Exercise 2 · Application · reconstruct the state

The information that survives shutdown

Start from the verified initial checkpoint: 4 copies and 3500 ms accumulated. The machine restarts. The new segment runs from 2000 to 6000 ms and confirms 4 additional copies. Give the new checkpoint as a record number and cumulative duration, the next record number, then what you cannot say about downtime.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate a duration using only the readings from the new segment.

Hint 2

Add the confirmed copies and the segment durations separately.

Compare with the solution

New segment: 6000 − 2000 = 4000 ms. Cumulative duration: 3500 + 4000 = 7500 ms = 7.50 s. The record number becomes 4 + 4 = 8; the next is 8 + 1 = 9.

The checkpoint still associates the correct batch with these quantities. Once its write and read-back are confirmed, it describes 8 copies and 7.50 s measured. There are 4 copies left. These readings provide no duration for downtime; the cumulative duration measures only the stated segments.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 7500 ms without subtracting readings taken before and after the restart.
  • Associate record number 8, next record number 9 and the batch identity.
  • Distinguish the prepared checkpoint from its confirmed saving, and exclude downtime from the cumulative duration.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · refute a claim

The display had advanced further

A variation on the copying task: after the first confirmed checkpoint at record number 4, the program reaches 7 copies in memory, then stops abruptly. No new checkpoint was saved. On recovery, the copies covered by the old checkpoint are verified; the following copies still need checking. At which record number should you resume, and how many copies should you repeat with our method? Someone claims to know the exact time spent on the work already done from the old checkpoint alone: respond to that claim.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A number displayed before the failure is not a new checkpoint that has been read back.

Hint 2

The time taken by operations after the last checkpoint was not retained here. Do not assign it a value.

Compare with the solution

The confirmed boundary remains 4. Resume at record number 5 and repeat 12 − 4 = 8 copies. The display of 7 would have suggested that only 5 remained. There are 7 − 4 = 3 copies beyond the boundary; the check 8 − 5 = 3 recovers this difference.

Some additional copies may still exist. Our procedure repeats them because their result is not confirmed by the checkpoint. This is acceptable here: the batch is unchanged and each record number receives exactly the same content. For an action such as sending something, this property would need to be examined separately before any replay.

The checkpoint attests only to the cumulative duration of 3.50 s at its own boundary. The duration between that boundary and the failure is unknown; the total time already spent therefore cannot be reported exactly. Replaying does not recover this missing measurement. A reliable receipt identifies the old checkpoint, the current attempt and what was checked again.

Check your reasoning

  • Resume at record number 5 with 8 copies to repeat, without crediting the display alone.
  • Explain why this replay does not create additional copies in the defined scenario.
  • Keep the unsaved duration unknown and limit the scope of the receipt.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What question does a wall clock address, and why can the difference between its readings mislead?

It places an event on a calendar. An adjustment can shift its readings during the work; their difference may then include this adjustment instead of measuring only the interval.

Recall 2What must you check before subtracting monotonic readings?

They must belong to a comparable domain: here, the same clock during the same boot session, with no sleep. Their arbitrary origin disappears in the difference. Readings taken on either side of a machine restart cannot be joined in this way.

Recall 3What does our checkpoint retain, and what must be checked when reading it back?

The identity of the unchanged batch, the confirmed record number and the cumulative duration already calculated. Recovery checks the batch, the consistency of the record number and the claimed copies; the old process display is not enough.

Recall 4Why does an indivisible replacement not prove survival through every failure?

The atomicity of the replacement prevents a partially replaced file from being presented, under the guarantees of the system used. Durability concerns what remains after a defined failure; it requires examining storage and its synchronization. Reading back the file now does not simulate a power cut.

Recall 5How can you cross a restart without inventing a duration?

Retain the durations calculated at confirmed boundaries, then add the durations of new segments measured using new comparable pairs. Keep downtime and lost segments unknown when nothing measured or retained their durations.

Reconstruct the reasoning
