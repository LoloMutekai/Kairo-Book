Level 2 · C086·M4

Autonomic stress response

Your goalExplain an autonomic response to a demanding situation, calculate a change in heart rate and separate this observation from its presumed cause.

Before you start
c085-m5 : development and environment · c083-m5 : transfer · c084-m5 : misleading hindsight · c086-m1 : homeostasis · c086-m2 : reward · c086-m3 : motivation

✿01 See the idea

You are sitting, waiting to give a presentation. You hear that your turn is coming: your heart speeds up even though you have not decided to make it beat faster. Then your turn is postponed; you stay seated and the increase in heart rate eases. This brief episode will be our only scene.

The autonomic nervous system regulates the activity of the heart and other internal organs, among other functions, without requiring a voluntary command for every beat. The word autonomic describes this mode of control; it does not mean that the body is independent of the brain or the situation.

In this scene, stress means the response to a demand perceived as difficult to manage. Its autonomic component prepares bodily adjustments. The announcement can be enough: the response does not require physical effort to have already begun. However, it does not take the same form in everyone.

The scene and all the counts are fictional. They let us follow one possible response, without providing a stress threshold, a diagnosis or a personal result. First look at the pathways; we will then calculate what the counts actually let us describe.

The rule to remember

Follow the situation, the autonomic control and the observed change; a change in the heart alone does not reveal nerve activity or the emotion experienced.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the presentation announcement to a return towards the starting value

In the same fictional scene, we count 36 beats before the announcement, 48 at the announcement and 39 after the postponement. Each window lasts 30 s. The person stays seated, without speaking or making any physical effort during the windows. Let us describe the change, then examine what it establishes about the autonomic mechanism.

  1. Understand the mechanism before assigning numbers to it

    Brain circuits, particularly in the hypothalamus and brainstem, coordinate control signals to the organs. The hypothalamus is a region involved in internal regulation; the brainstem is a part of the brain that extends towards the spinal cord and contains circuits that regulate the heart, among others. The sympathetic and parasympathetic pathways are nerve pathways through which these signals reach the body.

    At the heart, an increase in sympathetic input can speed up the beats. Parasympathetic input, transmitted particularly through the vagus nerve, can slow them down. Tone means background activity: withdrawal of a parasympathetic brake can therefore contribute to an increase in heart rate, even without attributing the whole increase to greater sympathetic input.

    These pathways do not form a switch that necessarily turns one off when the other acts. Their effect depends on the organ and the situation. The words sympathetic and parasympathetic do not refer to pleasant and unpleasant emotions. Stress also involves hormonal responses; our counts do not measure them and they remain outside the scope of this unit.

  2. Define the quantities and the assumptions for comparison

    N denotes the whole number of beats counted in a window. T is its strictly positive duration, in seconds. f is the average frequency over this window, in beats per minute, abbreviated beats/min. c is the conversion constant: c = 60 seconds per minute. The labels before, announcement and after indicate the point in the scene; they do not refer to different people.

    In this exercise, we assume that the counts and durations are exact and that the same counting method is used. The posture and absence of physical effort during the windows make the comparison easier to interpret. They are not enough to isolate a cause: breathing, earlier movements or other changes can also play a part. Our before value is a reference for this episode, not a personal reference established over time.

    An average frequency does not describe the duration of each interval between beats. Variation in these intervals is called heart rate variability; the totals N alone do not allow us to calculate it.

  3. Convert the count without inventing beats

    Dividing N by T gives beats per second. Then multiplying by c changes the unit to beats per minute. This does not assume that the rhythm stays constant throughout the window or that it will stay constant afterwards.

    f=NT×c

    Here, conversion means multiplying each count by 60 ÷ 30 = 2. The seconds cancel out; the minute remains in the denominator of the unit.

  4. Calculate comparable heart rates

    Before: 36 ÷ 30 × 60 = 72 beats/min. At the announcement: 48 ÷ 30 × 60 = 96 beats/min. After the postponement: 39 ÷ 30 × 60 = 78 beats/min.

    These values are average frequencies in separate windows. We compare the frequencies rather than confuse a count in beats with a frequency in beats per minute.

  5. Describe the rise and the return without turning them into a diagnosis

    Δ, read as delta, denotes the difference here between the rate at the announcement and the rate before it. Its unit is still beats per minute. We subtract the starting value to describe the change specific to this comparison.

    Δ=fannonce−favant=96−72=24 batt./min

    After the postponement, the difference from the starting value is 78 − 72 = 6 beats/min. The drop since the announcement is 96 − 78 = 18 beats/min. The rate moves closer to the starting value without having returned to it exactly. Neither the rise nor the drop gives an amount of stress or a separate measurement of each nerve pathway.

  6. Check the result by another route

    Because the durations are identical, we can start by subtracting the counts: 48 − 36 = 12 beats. Converting this difference gives 12 × 2 = 24 beats/min, exactly the rise found by subtracting the frequencies.

    Check the return: the initial rise is split between the observed drop and what remains above the starting value. We obtain 18 + 6 = 24 beats/min. It would be inconsistent to claim a complete return while retaining a difference of 6 beats/min.

  7. Separate a possible explanation from an established cause

    The increase in heart rate after the announcement is consistent with an autonomic adjustment to the perceived demand. It can combine an increase in sympathetic input with withdrawal of the parasympathetic brake. This record alone cannot distinguish these contributions or demonstrate that the announcement is the sole cause.

    The postponement and the drop make a return towards a less activated state plausible. They do not prove that all feelings of tension have disappeared. To examine the explanation, we would need to document the context, the subjective experience and the quality of the measurements, among other things, then arrange an appropriate comparison. No number on this page provides a universal threshold.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Autonomic means that the brain is not involved.”

    Here, autonomic means that you do not command every beat. A situation perceived by the brain can change control signals to the organs. Start again with the pathway from the announcement, then name the nerve pathways.

    Trace the pathway from the announcement to the heart

  • “The heart is speeding up: parasympathetic activity must have stopped.”

    A frequency is an outcome, not a separate reading of the control signals. An increase in sympathetic input, withdrawal of the parasympathetic brake or a combination of the two can contribute to an increase in heart rate. Revisit the definition of tone and the limits of counting.

    Reread the mechanism and the assumptions

  • “The heart rate is dropping: we have proved that stress has disappeared.”

    The drop describes a change in the heart; the subjective experience and the other responses remain unknown. In our scene, the rate even remains 6 beats/min above the starting value. An exact return would not have been enough to prove the absence of stress either.

    Distinguish observation from interpretation in the exercises

Compare with the solution

Observation: the average heart rate goes from 72 to 96 beats/min, then to 78 beats/min. The rise is 24 beats/min; the drop is 18 beats/min. These changes are calculated, without direct measurement of the nerve control signals.

Possible mechanism: the perceived demand can change autonomic control. Increased sympathetic input and withdrawal of the parasympathetic brake are possible contributions; their shares remain unknown. The subjective experience after the postponement also remains unknown.

We can ask whether the person had just been walking quickly before the window, and document their breathing or movements during the record. A separate personal account is needed for the subjective experience. This information would help interpretation; it does not by itself constitute a causal demonstration.

  • Suggest relevant information without presenting it as evidence already obtained.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does autonomic mean in the presentation scene?

Among other functions, the system regulates the heart without a voluntary command for every beat. The brain and the perception of the situation still contribute to this control.

Recall 2How can a weaker brake contribute to an increase in heart rate?

Parasympathetic input, particularly vagal input, slows the heart. Its withdrawal can contribute to an increase in heart rate, as can greater sympathetic input. The heart rate alone does not distinguish these contributions.

Recall 3How do you convert N beats counted over T seconds to beats per minute?

Divide N by T, then multiply by 60 seconds per minute. The result is an average frequency over the window, not a count actually observed during the next minute.

Recall 4In the scene, how do you distinguish a drop from a complete return?

The drop compares 96 with 78 beats/min: 18 beats/min. The return compares 78 with the starting value 72: 6 beats/min remains. Neither difference alone measures the subjective experience.

Recall 5What does the increase in heart rate after the announcement fail to prove?

It proves neither a single cause, nor the share of each autonomic pathway, nor a specific emotion. It also does not provide the intervals needed to calculate heart rate variability.
