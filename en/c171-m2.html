Level 3 · C171·M2

Isometry

Your goalCalculate the second moment of a sum of Brownian shocks weighted by adapted integrands, then explain why the cross terms vanish and why adaptedness is essential.

Before you start
c170-m5 : Strong Markov property · c057-m5 : Hessian · c165-m5 : duality · c171-m1 : adapted integrands

✿01 See the idea

A fictional point counter advances in steps of duration Δt = 0.25 s. During each step, a noise process W, a Brownian motion, gives it a random shock ΔW; the counter then receives a number of points equal to a weight H multiplied by this shock. All values in the scene are fictional: they are used for calculation and do not describe a real system.

The weight for the next step can depend on what the counter has just seen, for example remaining cautious after a negative shock. It cannot depend on a shock that has not yet occurred. This constraint is called adaptedness, introduced in c171-m1: at the start of each step, the weight is determined by the information already available.

The question in this unit is: what is the second moment E[I²] of the total I, that is, the mean of its square? Expected answer: add, step by step, the mean of the squared weight multiplied by the duration Δt. Cross products between two different steps average to 0, because the shock in the later step is centered and independent of what comes before it.

This is called an isometry because the mapping that turns the weight H into the total I preserves size in the quadratic sense: the square root of the second moment of I, in points, equals the square root of the mean of the integral of H² with respect to time. An isometry preserves lengths; here, length is measured by this square root.

Units: time is expressed in seconds (s). A Brownian increment has variance equal to its duration, so ΔW is expressed in √s; the weight H in points per √s (pt/√s); the total I in points (pt); and its second moment in pt². The product (pt/√s)² × s does give pt²: the units agree.

This unit stays within the elementary case where H is constant during each step. The general Ito integral is the subject of c171-m3; no limiting argument is used here.

The rule to remember

For a total of adapted weights multiplied by Brownian shocks, the second moment is the sum of the means of H² multiplied by the durations: the cross terms vanish thanks to centering and adaptedness.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Two steps, a weight that uses the past

Over two steps of duration Δt = 0.25 s, set H₀ = 3 pt/√s, known at time 0 s, and H₁ = 2 pt/√s if ΔW₀ > 0, H₁ = 1 pt/√s otherwise (fictional values). Calculate E[I²] for I = H₀ΔW₀ + H₁ΔW₁, then check the result using four paths.

  1. Define the quantities, units, and assumptions

    The times are t0 = 0 s, t1 = 0.25 s, and t2 = 0.50 s. For k = 0 then k = 1, ΔWk = W(tk+1) − W(tk) is the noise increment during step k, in √s. The total is the sum of the contributions from each step.

    I=∑kHkΔWk

    Assumptions used without proving them again: (a) each ΔWk is centered, with mean 0 and variance Δt; (b) ΔWk is independent of everything known at time tk, hence of Hk and previous shocks; (c) each Hk is adapted and square integrable, meaning that E[Hk²] is finite. Properties (a) and (b) are those of Brownian motion recalled in prerequisite c170-m5; if they are unclear, review them first. Here H takes only two possible values, so (c) is immediate.

    In our example, the sum reduces to two terms.

    I=H0ΔW0+H1ΔW1

  2. Expand the square

    To calculate E[I²], square the sum. Expectation is linear, so handle each term separately: two diagonal terms and one cross term.

    I2=H02(ΔW0)2+H12(ΔW1)2+2H0ΔW0H1ΔW1

  3. The diagonal terms

    For a fixed step k, Hk is known at time tk, hence independent of ΔWk. The expectation of the product of the squares is the product of their expectations, and E[ΔWk²] equals Δt, the variance of a centered shock.

    E[Hk2(ΔWk)2]=E[Hk2]·E[(ΔWk)2]=E[Hk2]Δt

    For k = 0, H0 is deterministic: E[H0²] = 3² = 9 pt²/s, giving 9 × 0.25 = 9/4 = 2.25 pt².

    For k = 1, H1 equals 2 if ΔW0 > 0 and 1 otherwise. The distribution of ΔW0 is centered and symmetric, so each case has probability 0.5.

    E[H12]=1/2×4+1/2×1=5/2

    This mean square equals 2.5 pt²/s, giving 5/2 × 0.25 = 5/8 = 0.625 pt². Note: H1 depends on ΔW0, but not on ΔW1, the only shock involved in the second step.

  4. A two-point model to check the algebra

    To check the cross term and the total without integrating a normal distribution, temporarily replace each ΔWk with an independent shock equal to +0.50 or −0.50 √s, each with probability 0.5. They have the same mean 0, the same variance since 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25 s, and the same symmetry. This model is not a Brownian motion: it only checks the second-moment calculation, whose assumptions are (a) and (b).

    Possible contributions: H0ΔW0 equals ±1.5 pt (that is, 3 × 0.50). After ΔW0 > 0, H1ΔW1 equals ±1.0 pt; after ΔW0 < 0, it equals ±0.5 pt.

  5. The cross term vanishes

    The factor H0ΔW0H1 is known at time t1; ΔW1 is centered and independent of this factor. The expectation of the product is therefore the product of the expectations, one of which is 0.

    E[H0ΔW0H1ΔW1]=E[H0ΔW0H1]·E[ΔW1]=0

    We can see this with our values: for each fixed ΔW0, the two outcomes of ΔW1 give opposite contributions. After ΔW0 > 0, the sum of the two outcomes is 1 + (-1) = 0. After ΔW0 < 0, it is 1/2 + (-1/2) = 0. If H1 had depended on ΔW1, these two outcomes would no longer have been symmetric.

  6. Conclude using the isometry

    The two diagonal terms remain; the cross term contributes 0. For a weight constant during each step, this is the isometry identity in sum form, with units of pt².

    E[I2]=E[H02]Δt+E[H12]Δt=9/4+5/8=23/8 pt²

    The result is 23/8 = 2.875 pt². In integral form, the same identity is written below, with T = 0.50 s; here the integral of H² with respect to time equals H0² × Δt + H1² × Δt for each path, and we then take the mean.

    E[(∫0THdW)2]=E[∫0TH2dt]

  7. Check using the four paths

    Using the two-point model, calculate I = H0ΔW0 + H1ΔW1 on each path, then its square.

    (+, +): H1 = 2, I = 1.5 + 1.0 = 2.5 pt, I² = 25/4 pt².

    (+, −): I = 1.5 − 1.0 = 0.5 pt, I² = 1/4 pt².

    (−, +): H1 = 1, I = −1.5 + 0.5 = -1.0 pt, I² = 1 pt².

    (−, −): I = −1.5 − 0.5 = -2.0 pt, I² = 4 pt².

    E[I2]=25/4+1/4+1+44=23/24=23/8 pt²

    The mean of the squares, 23/8 pt², agrees with the isometry result. The mean of the four values of I is also 0 pt. This check validates the algebra; it does not replace the general Brownian case.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Since H₁ depends on ΔW₀, the cross term can no longer vanish.”

    Only the most recent shock, ΔW₁, matters: it is centered and independent of everything known at time t₁, including H₁ and ΔW₀. The product H₀ΔW₀H₁ is known at t₁; dependence of H₁ on the past causes no problem.

    Review the cross-term step

  • “I can choose Hₖ by looking at the shock in that same step.”

    Then Hₖ is no longer adapted and the second moment is no longer the sum of E[Hₖ²]Δt. In the last exercise, the difference from the isometry result is 0.75 pt² for the fictional values in the counterexample: the cross term no longer vanishes.

    Redo the non-adapted counterexample

  • “E[Hₖ²] can be replaced with the square of E[Hₖ].”

    The mean of the square exceeds the square of the mean whenever Hₖ varies. For H₁ in the example, squaring the half-sum of 2 and 1 gives a value smaller than 2.5, the true value of E[H₁²]; using the former would underestimate the result.

    Review the diagonal terms

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

Constant weights

On the same grid of two steps of duration Δt = 0.25 s, take H₀ = 2 pt/√s and H₁ = 3 pt/√s, with no randomness (fictional values). Calculate E[I²] using the isometry and give the unit.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

When Hₖ is deterministic, what is E[Hₖ²]?

Hint 2

Add the two diagonal terms; the cross term is 0 for the same reason as in the example.

Compare with the solution

With no randomness, E[Hk²] = Hk². The sum of the squares is 4 + 9 = 13 pt²/s, which is multiplied by Δt.

E[I2]=(4+9)×1/4=13×1/4=13/4 pt²

The result is 13/4 = 3.25 pt². Check: with constant weights, I is a sum of two independent centered variables; the variance of a sum of independent variables is the sum of the variances, 4 × 0.25 + 9 × 0.25, giving the same value.

Check your reasoning

  • Square each weight before multiplying by Δt.
  • Discard the cross term and explain why.
  • Give the unit as pt², not pt.

Exercise 2 · Application with dependence

A weight that switches off

Keep H₀ = 3 pt/√s. Take H₁ = 2 pt/√s if ΔW₀ > 0 and H₁ = 0 otherwise (fictional values). Explain why H₁ is adapted, calculate E[I²], then check using the paths of the two-point model.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

H₁ depends only on ΔW₀: is this known at time t₁?

Hint 2

Calculate E[H₁²] using the probability of each case, then multiply by Δt.

Hint 3

For the check, start again from the values of I in the example when ΔW₀ > 0; when ΔW₀ < 0, H₁ is 0.

Compare with the solution

H1 depends only on ΔW0, already observed at time t1: it is adapted. The cross term therefore vanishes as in the example.

Diagonal term at k = 1: E[H1²] = 0.5 × 4 + 0.5 × 0 = 2 pt²/s, giving 2 × 0.25 = 1/2 = 0.5 pt². Including the term at k = 0, E[I²] = 9/4 + 1/2 = 11/4 = 2.75 pt².

Check using the paths: if ΔW0 > 0, I equals 2.5 or 0.5, with squares 25/4 and 1/4. If ΔW0 < 0, H1 = 0 and I = -1.5 for both outcomes, with square 9/4. Mean: (25/4 + 1/4 + 9/4 + 9/4) / 4 = 11/4 pt², the same result.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify adaptedness by dependence on ΔW₀ alone.
  • Calculate the mean of the square using the probabilities.
  • Check independently using the four paths.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosing a counterexample

An anticipating weight

On the same grid, keep H₁ = 3 pt/√s constant and set H₀ = 2 pt/√s if ΔW₀ and ΔW₁ have the same sign, H₀ = 1 pt/√s otherwise (fictional values). (a) Why is H₀ not adapted? (b) What would the isometry formula give if applied without checking its assumptions? (c) Calculate E[I²] using the four paths of the two-point model. (d) Interpret the difference.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

H₀ must be known at time t₀: which shock does it depend on?

Hint 2

For (c), calculate I = H₀ΔW₀ + H₁ΔW₁ on (+, +), (+, −), (−, +), and (−, −), choosing H₀ according to the signs.

Hint 3

For (d), compare with the result of the naive formula and think about the cross term.

Compare with the solution

(a) H0 depends on ΔW1, the shock in the second step, which has not yet occurred at time t0: H0 is not adapted, so assumption (c) of the isometry is violated.

(b) Applying the formula without checking its assumptions would give E[H0²] × Δt + H1² × Δt. Here H0 equals 2 or 1 with probability 0.5, so E[H0²] = 5/2 and the formula would give 5/2 × 0.25 + 9 × 0.25 = 23/8 pt².

(c) Path (+, +): H0 = 2, I = 1.0 + 1.5 = 2.5 pt, square 25/4. Path (−, −): I = −2.5 pt, same square. Path (+, −): H0 = 1, I = 0.5 − 1.5 = -1.0 pt, square 1. Path (−, +): I = 1 pt, same square. Mean: (25/4 + 1) / 2 = 29/4 / 2 = 29/8 pt².

(d) The difference is 29/8 − 23/8 = 3/4 = 0.75 pt². This is twice the mean of the cross term, which is no longer 0: H0 correlates H0ΔW0 with ΔW1. The isometry requires adaptedness.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify the dependence of H₀ on ΔW₁.
  • Give all four paths with the correct signs of H₀.
  • Relate the difference to the nonzero cross term.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does “Hₖ adapted” mean for the total I = Σ HₖΔWₖ?

Hₖ is determined by the information available at time tₖ, before the shock ΔWₖ: it can depend on the past, never on ΔWₖ or the future.

Recall 2What is E[I²] for an elementary sum with square-integrable adapted weights?

The sum of E[Hₖ²]Δtₖ, that is, the mean of the integral of H² with respect to time; it is not the sum of the squares of the means of Hₖ.

Recall 3Why do the cross terms of I² vanish?

For two steps k < m, HₖΔWₖHₘ is known at time tₘ; ΔWₘ is centered and independent of this past, so the mean of the product is 0.

Recall 4What are the units of Δt, ΔW, H, I, and E[I²] in the scene?

Δt in s, ΔW in √s, H in pt/√s, I in pt, and E[I²] in pt²; (pt/√s)² × s gives pt².

Recall 5What does the two-point check fail to show?

It checks the second-moment algebra using centering, variance, and independence; it describes neither a true Brownian motion nor more general integrands.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define ΔWₖ, the adapted weight Hₖ, and the total I; expand I² into diagonal and cross terms; show that each diagonal term equals E[Hₖ²]Δt by independence of ΔWₖ; show that each cross term vanishes because the most recent shock is centered; add, check the units in pt², and recalculate using the paths; finally recall that a non-adapted weight breaks the identity, with a difference of 0.75 pt² in the fictional counterexample.
