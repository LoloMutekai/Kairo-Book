Level 2 · C078·M1

Pixels, coordinates and normalization

Your goalPlace a marker on an image, convert its position to fractions of the width and height, then recover its pixel coordinates without changing conventions.

Before you start
c027-m5 : complex numbers · c040-m5 : least squares · c036-m5 : annotated charts · c022-m5 : untrusted input and injection

✿01 See the idea

You need to place a cross back on a photo of a rectangular plate. Marker P was annotated at 300 pixels from the left edge and 150 pixels from the top edge. The photo is 800 pixels wide and 600 pixels high. If the image is enlarged, copying the same numbers will not place the cross at the same fraction of the frame.

Imagine each edge as a ruler. To locate the cross, you can give a distance in pixels from the start of each ruler, or the fraction of each ruler already covered. The second description depends on the dimensions chosen as references: moving the same number of pixels does not represent the same fraction horizontally and vertically.

A pixel is an image cell that holds a value, such as a colour. Coordinates describe a position; they do not give that colour. An annotated marker is a geometric point: it can be placed between cell centres. In our example, P lies at an intersection of their edges.

The photo, the plate, the marker and all the values in this scene are fictional. We already have the annotation and the dimensions; we are neither trying to detect the plate automatically nor to infer its actual size.

The rule to remember

State the origin, the direction of the axes and what the coordinates mean; divide the x-coordinate by the width and the y-coordinate by the height. Keep the dimensions so that you can return to pixels.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A position, from pixels to fractions of the frame

The fictional photo has dimensions W = 800 px and H = 600 px. Point P has coordinates x = 300 px and y = 150 px. The symbol px means pixel, our unit of length in the plane of this image. We want a pair of normalized coordinates, followed by a check using the reverse conversion.

  1. Set a convention before calculating

    Origin O, with coordinates (0 ; 0), is the outer top left corner of the frame. x is the horizontal coordinate, increasing to the right; y is the vertical coordinate, increasing downwards. W denotes the width and H the height, both strictly positive, in pixels. The coordinates of P are continuous: a fraction of a pixel is allowed.

    To relate this geometry to the grid, let c denote the integer index of a column, counted from 0, and r that of a row, also counted from 0. The cell extends from c to c + 1 horizontally and from r to r + 1 vertically, in pixels. Its centre C therefore has x-coordinate c + 0.5 and y-coordinate r + 0.5. The letters c and r denote indices without units; here their values count steps of 1 px.

    The last cells have indices W − 1 = 799 and H − 1 = 599. The last centre is at (799.5 ; 599.5) px, inside the frame. A program may choose another origin, such as the centre of the first pixel: you must then convert from its convention before combining its coordinates with ours.

  2. Choose the reference for each quotient

    Let u denote the normalized horizontal coordinate and v the normalized vertical coordinate. Here, normalizing means expressing each position as a fraction of the corresponding dimension. u and v have no units, since each divides a length in pixels by a length in pixels.

    u=xW;v=yH

    For a point inside the frame, including its edges, 0 ≤ x ≤ W and 0 ≤ y ≤ H give 0 ≤ u ≤ 1 and 0 ≤ v ≤ 1. The right and bottom edges correspond to 1; they are not the centres of the last cells. Coordinates alone do not say which cell a boundary belongs to: an indexing rule is still needed.

    We divide by W and H, according to the stated convention. Dividing centre indices by W − 1 and H − 1 to map the outermost centres to 0 and 1 would be another convention. Here, normalizing coordinates also does not mean dividing a vector by its norm to give it unit length.

  3. Calculate the horizontal fraction

    The horizontal distance is measured from the left edge. The distance x must therefore be compared with the full width W, rather than the height. Keep the exact fraction, then give a decimal representation for reading.

    u=300800=38=0.375

    P is therefore at 3/8 of the width from the left. The quotient 0.375 is exact and has no units; it is not a length of 0.375 px.

  4. Calculate the vertical fraction

    The vertical distance is measured from the top. Its denominator is the height H. Since the axis points downwards, a larger y-coordinate means a point lower in our image.

    v=150600=14=0.25

    The normalized position is therefore (0.375 ; 0.25), without units, in horizontal then vertical order. The quotients have neither changed the photo nor moved P: they have changed how its position is described.

  5. Return to pixels and look for a plausible error

    The reverse check undoes each division by multiplying by the same dimension. W and H are still essential.

    x=uW=38×800=300 px
    y=vH=14×600=150 px

    We recover P. An independent check: 3 parts of the width out of 8 give 300 px; the vertical fraction 1/4 of 600 px gives 150 px. The values lie within the frame and the vertical axis points in the same direction as in the drawing.

    Swapping the denominators would give 300/600 = 0.50 and 150/800 = 0.1875. These values also lie between 0 and 1: checking only the interval would not detect the error. The choice of reference and the recovery of the coordinates must also be checked.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading the quotients, explain why each axis has its own denominator. Then use the close view to show the difference between the cell index, its centre and P. What information must you keep to make the conversion reversible?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The first coordinate is the row, since I go through the rows of the array first.”

    The order in an array and the order for a point are different conventions. We write (x ; y), horizontal then vertical. To find the cell in an array organized by rows, look up row r, then column c. Write the name of each axis and its dimension before any division.

    Set out the axes and references again

  • “The pixel number directly gives the coordinates of its centre.”

    In our coordinate system based at the outer edge, index c locates the left edge of the cell, rather than its centre. Add 0.5 px in each direction to reach the centre. In the close view, P touches a corner of the cell; replacing it with the centre would move the annotation.

    Compare C and P in the close view

  • “Once normalized, the position is a measurement in centimetres.”

    The pixel units cancel in the quotient: u and v have no units. They describe a position relative to the frame. Without physical information and an established relationship between the object and the image, this pair cannot tell us any actual length of the plate.

Exercise 1 · Read the grid

Find the centre of the neighbouring pixel

In the close view of the same photo, take the cell in column c = 299 and row r = 149. Give the coordinates of its centre C in pixels in our coordinate system. Are they the coordinates of P? Justify your answer using the edges of the cell.

Hint 1

A cell covers a step of 1 px on each axis. Its centre divides this step into equal parts.

Hint 2

The right edge is at 300 px and the bottom edge at 150 px. The midpoint is on neither of these edges.

Compare with the solution

The centre is at x = 299 + 0.5 = 299.5 px and y = 149 + 0.5 = 149.5 px. Use the image axes, without swapping row and column.

Check: 299 < 299.5 < 300 and 149 < 149.5 < 150. The centre is indeed inside the pixel. P = (300 ; 150) px touches its bottom right corner; C and P are therefore different positions.

Check your reasoning

Exercise 2 · Change dimensions

Place P on an enlarged copy

The same photo is represented in a frame of W′ = 1200 px and H′ = 900 px, without cropping, rotation or shifting of the content. The prime mark denotes the copy. The position of P relative to the edges is preserved. Calculate x′ and y′, then check that the normalized coordinates have not changed.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The copy represents the same fraction of the frame on each axis. Start again from u and v, then apply the dimensions of the copy.

Hint 2

Also check W′/W and H′/H. In this exercise, these ratios are equal.

Compare with the solution

The horizontal factor is 1200/800 = 1.5; the vertical factor is 900/600 = 1.5. The assumption of the same frame without cropping allows us to preserve u = 3/8 and v = 1/4.

This gives x′ = 3/8 × 1200 = 450 px and y′ = 1/4 × 900 = 225 px. Another approach: multiplying x and y directly by 1.5 gives the same results.

Check: 450/1200 = 3/8 and 225/900 = 1/4. P keeps its relative position. We are transferring a geometric annotation; we are neither calculating the new colours nor how software resamples the pixel centres.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the dimensions of the copy when returning to pixels.
  • Obtain (450 ; 225) px and check each quotient.
  • State the assumption that the frame is preserved; it would not apply in the same way after cropping.

Exercise 3 · Defend a conclusion

The dimensions have disappeared

An export of annotation P keeps only u = 0.375 and v = 0.25, in our convention. The image dimensions are no longer given. Someone claims to recover the exact pixel coordinates, then the actual size of the plate. Respond using the original photo and its enlarged copy as counterexamples.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The same normalized pair appears in the calculations for the photo and its copy, with different pixel results.

Hint 2

Even if the image dimensions were recovered, have we provided a physical scale for the plate?

Compare with the solution

For 800 × 600 px, the pair corresponds to (300 ; 150) px. For 1200 × 900 px, it corresponds to (450 ; 225) px. The quotients are identical. Normalized coordinates alone therefore do not select a unique position in pixels.

You must recover the dimensions of the image that the export refers to, along with its convention and any transformations of the frame. The display dimensions of another copy are not enough to identify the source dimensions.

Once the pixels are recovered, the physical size is still unknown: our scene has provided no scale in centimetres. The justified conclusion concerns the relative position of P. It assigns neither an actual size nor a physical interpretation to the plate.

Check your reasoning

  • Present the different pixel positions that share the same normalized pair.
  • Name the missing dimensions and convention, rather than choosing a copy arbitrarily.
  • Distinguish returning to pixels from making a physical measurement.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What convention must you specify before communicating the coordinates of P?

The origin, the direction of the axes, the horizontal then vertical order, the unit and the geometric reference for the coordinates. Here the origin is the outer top left edge; x increases to the right and y downwards.

Recall 2In our convention, what is the difference between cell (c ; r) and its centre?

c and r are integer column and row indices. The cell occupies a step of 1 px on each axis; its centre has coordinates (c + 0.5 ; r + 0.5) px from the outer edges. An annotated marker can occupy a different position.

Recall 3How do you normalize the position, and what units should you give the result?

Calculate u = x/W and v = y/H with W and H strictly positive. The coordinates have no units: the pixels cancel. The choice of W and H is part of the convention; normalization by W − 1 and H − 1 cannot silently replace it.

Recall 4What must you know to return to pixels, and how can you check an enlarged copy?

Know the dimensions and convention of the image concerned; calculate x = uW and y = vH. On a copy that preserves the same frame, check that x′/W′ = u and y′/H′ = v. A normalized coordinate alone does not fix a distance in pixels.

Recall 5Can a normalized pair be used to measure the plate in centimetres?

It locates P relative to the frame. A physical measurement requires additional information about the scale and the relationship between the plate and its photo. Dividing by the image dimensions does not provide this information.
