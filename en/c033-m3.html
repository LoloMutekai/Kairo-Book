Level 1 · C033·M3

Exp and log

Your goalDifferentiate composite exponential and natural logarithm functions, then interpret their sensitivities in the correct units.

Before you start
c031-m5 : asymptotes · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c033-m1 : derivative · c033-m2 : composition

✿01 See the idea

A lamp dims in front of a stationary sensor. Its illuminance starts at 80 lux, then reaches 40 lux. As less light remains, the absolute decrease becomes slower. We want to find this local rate, then reverse the question: how much does the time to reach a target change with the requested light level?

The lux, written lx, measures the illuminance received by the sensor here. The scene, values and chosen law are fictional. We assume an ideal sensor, a fixed position and a smooth decay, with no added light. We are studying the model; we are not describing measurements of an actual lamp.

The exponential function turns a duration into a multiplicative factor that is always positive. The natural logarithm recovers the duration from this factor. They undo each other, but their derivatives do not express the same thing: lux per minute in one direction, minutes per lux in the other.

Prerequisite reminder: a derivative is the limit of a rate of change, so it is a local sensitivity. For a composite function, multiply the derivative of the outer function by the derivative of its input. Here we will apply this chain rule to exp and ln. On this page, “log” always means the natural logarithm, written ln.

The rule to remember

Differentiating exp keeps exp; differentiating ln gives the reciprocal of its positive input. For a composite function, also multiply by the derivative of the input.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Sensitivity when reaching 40 lux

Starting from the model E(t) = E₀ exp(−kt), with E₀ = 80 lx and k = 0.25 per minute, let us find the time t* when E equals 40 lx, the slope E′(t*) and the slope of the time to reach the target T′(40). Expressions containing ln will be kept exact.

  1. Define the symbols, units and domain

    t is the elapsed time in minutes; E(t) is the illuminance in lux; E0 is its initial value, which is constant and strictly positive. The subscript in E0 indicates the start. k is a positive constant in inverse minutes. Thus kt has no unit and can be an input to exp. The prime in E′ denotes the derivative with respect to time, in lx/min.

    The physical model in this exercise applies to t ≥ 0. It reaches targets q such that 0 < q ≤ E0. T(q) will denote the time to reach the target, in minutes. For derivative calculations inside the domain, we will take 0 < q < E0; the starting point will be treated as a limit from the right in time.

    E(t)=E0exp(−kt)

  2. Understand the two differentiation rules

    For a real number x with no unit, exp(x) is always positive and its derivative is exp(x). This is the defining property of the natural exponential function, with exp(0) = 1. ln is its inverse: exp(ln x) = x for x > 0, and ln(exp x) = x for every real x. In particular, ln(1) = 0.

    dexp(x)dx=exp(x);dln(x)dx=1x pour x>0

    The notation d/dx means “derivative with respect to x”. To recover the rule for ln, differentiate the identity exp(ln x) = x using the chain rule: exp(ln x) × (ln x)′ = 1. The first factor equals x; dividing by positive x gives (ln x)′ = 1/x. Here we use the differentiability of the inverse of exp, whose derivative is positive everywhere.

    For differentiable inputs u and v, with v strictly positive, the rules for composite functions are as follows. Every prime refers to the same input variable.

    (exp(u))′=u′exp(u);(ln(v))′=v′v

  3. Differentiate the light level, keeping the inner derivative

    Take u(t) = −kt. Its derivative is −k, since k is constant. E0 is also constant: it multiplies the derivative without being differentiated again. The chain rule therefore gives:

    E′(t)=−kE0exp(−kt)=−kE(t)

    This formula explains the scene: the relative rate E′(t)/E(t) always equals −k, while the decrease in lux per minute depends on the remaining light. The sign is negative because k and E(t) are positive.

  4. Recover the time using the logarithm of a ratio

    To reach the target q = 40 lx, divide E(t*) = q by E0: the ratio equals 40/80 = 1/2 and has no unit. The star in t* labels the time we are looking for; it is not multiplication.

    Applying ln to both sides of exp(−kt*) = q/E0 gives −kt* = ln(q/E0). Dividing by −k isolates the time. To write the logarithm of a number greater than 1, use ln(1/x) = −ln(x), for x > 0.

    t*=−1kln(qE0)=4ln(2) min

    This time is positive: 2 > 1 implies ln(2) > 0. Exact check in the model: exp(−k t*) = exp(−ln(2)) = 1/2; E(t*) = 80 × 1/2 = 40 lx. No rounding of ln is needed.

  5. Calculate the slope when reaching the target

    At the point being studied, E(t*) is already known: it equals q. Replacing E(t*) directly with 40 lx avoids recalculating the exponential. The product kq equals 1/4 × 40 = 10 lx/min; the negative sign still needs to be applied.

    E′(t*)=−1/4×40=-10 lx/min

    The slope is -10 lx/min at this instant. This is a local sensitivity, not the exact amount lost during the next minute. This check on the meaning is essential: a positive derivative would contradict the decay in our model.

  6. Differentiate the time to reach a target with respect to that target

    For every admissible target, T(q) = −(1/k) ln(q/E0). Here q is the variable and E0 remains constant. Let v(q) = q/E0; its derivative is 1/E0. The derivative of the composite logarithm is v′/v:

    dln(q/E0)dq=1/E0q/E0=1q

    At 40 lx, this intermediate derivative equals 1/40 per lux. The outer factor −1/k equals -4 min. Their product gives the sensitivity of the time:

    T′(q)=−1kq;T′(40)=-440=-1/10 min/lx

    Written as a decimal for readability, T′(40) = -0.10 min/lx. Its negative sign says that a higher target is reached sooner. It does not mean that the time T(q) is negative.

  7. Check using inverse composition and the boundaries of the domain

    Since T(E(t)) = t, its derivative equals 1. The chain rule requires T′(E(t)) × E′(t) = 1. At our target: (-1/10 min/lx) × (-10 lx/min) = 1, with no unit. This is another way to recover the slope in the return direction.

    As q approaches 0 through positive values, ln(q/E0) tends to negative infinity: T(q) grows without bound. The model never reaches 0 illuminance in a finite time. At q = E0, the ratio equals 1, so T(E0) = 0 min. A target greater than E0 would give a negative mathematical time, outside our observation after the start.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations. Recover the inner factor in each derivative, explain why the logarithm takes a ratio with no unit, and justify why the sensitivities in the forward and return directions have a product equal to 1.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The derivative of exp(−kt) is still just exp(−kt).”

    Identify the input u(t) = −kt. Its derivative is −k: it multiplies exp(−kt). Without it, the sign and unit of the slope would be wrong. Work through the calculation E′(t) = −kE(t) again.

    Find the inner derivative in the example

  • “The derivative of ln(q/E₀) is E₀/q.”

    E₀/q is the reciprocal of the input, but the input’s derivative 1/E₀ is missing. Their product equals 1/q. The complete formula is v′/v, with v positive. Do not replace ln(q/E₀) with the logarithm of an illuminance that has a unit either.

    Work through the differentiation of the time to reach a target again

  • “The minus sign gives a negative time; a 0 target should still work.”

    For a target below E₀, ln(q/E₀) is negative: the outer sign makes T positive. A negative derivative describes the direction of change of T. At a 0 target, ln is undefined and the time to reach it is not finite.

    Check the signs and the boundaries of the domain

Compare with the solution

At the boundary q = E0, admissible targets approach from below: T′(E0) = -4/80 = -1/20 min/lx, in the sense of this derivative from the left. The light level slope and the time slope are negative; this does not prevent T(E0) = 0 min.

Exercise 2 · Inverse calculation and check

Wait until 20 lux

With the same lamp and the same k, find the time to reach 20 lx in exact form. Then calculate E′ at that instant and T′ at that target. Check the result using the product of the sensitivities.

Hint 1

Divide the new target by E₀ before applying ln.

Hint 2

Use E′ = −kq at the required instant; the exact time does not need to be rounded.

Compare with the solution

The ratio is 20/80 = 1/4. The time is −4 ln(1/4) = 4 ln(4) min. Since 4 = 2 × 2, ln(4) = 2 ln(2). The time is therefore 2t*: the same additional duration divides the light level by 2 again.

The light level slope is -1/4 × 20 = -5 lx/min. The time is more sensitive to the target: T′(20) = -4/20 = -1/5 min/lx = -0.20 min/lx.

Check: (-5 lx/min) × (-1/5 min/lx) = 1. Returning through exp gives 80 × 1/4 = 20 lx.

  • Keep the symbolic logarithm exact.

Exercise 3 · Unit transfer and domain

The sensor displays seconds

The same experiment is now timed in seconds, with 60 s per minute. At the target 40 lx, find the decay coefficient in inverse seconds, the light level slope in lx/s and the slope of the time to reach the target in s/lx. Then say whether this change of unit allows 0 lx to be reached in a finite time.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The same minute contains 60 seconds: the decrease per second is smaller, and the time expressed in seconds is larger.

Hint 2

Check the product of the slopes again. The domain of ln does not depend on the clock.

Compare with the solution

Let s denote time counted in seconds and t time counted in minutes. Their numerical values satisfy s = 60t. The coefficient becomes ks = 1/4/60 = 1/240 per second to keep the same exponent with no unit.

The light level slope becomes -10/60 = -1/6 lx/s. The time slope becomes -1/10 × 60 = -6 s/lx. Check: -1/6 × -6 = 1. Dividing both slopes by the same factor would fail this check.

The exponential remains strictly positive for every finite time. A 0 ratio is still excluded from ln: changing units creates no finite time at which the light goes out in this model.

Check your reasoning

  • Convert the slopes in reciprocal directions and obtain a product equal to 1.
  • Retain the limitation of the model at a 0 target.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the derivative of exp(u(t)), and which factor should you look for first?

u′(t) exp(u(t)). Identify the input u and its derivative before keeping the exponential. For the lamp, u = −kt and u′ = −k.

Recall 2What is the derivative of ln(v(q)), and under what condition?

v′(q)/v(q), with v differentiable and strictly positive. The input to ln has no unit. For v = q/E₀, this gives 1/q.

Recall 3For the lamp, what do E′ and T′ measure, and how can they be checked together?

E′ measures lux per minute; T′ measures minutes per lux. At corresponding points, T′(E(t)) × E′(t) = 1, because T(E(t)) = t.

Recall 4Why can the time to reach a 0 target not be obtained by substituting into ln?

ln is defined only for a strictly positive input. As the target approaches 0, the time grows without bound; the exponential never reaches 0 in a finite time.

Recall 5How can a positive time have a negative derivative?

The value T(q) indicates a time; its derivative indicates how that time changes with the target. A higher target is reached sooner, so T′ is negative even if T is positive.
