Level 1 · C055·M5

Confidence interval

Your goalConstruct an interval for a mean and explain what its confidence level applies to, under explicit assumptions.

Before you start
c035-m5 : finite covariance and conditioning · c024-m5 : measurement quality · c055-m1 : sampling distribution · c055-m2 : law of large numbers · c055-m3 : introduction to the central limit theorem · c055-m4 : standard error

✿01 See the idea

A machine fills bags of rice. You want to know the mean mass of its output at its current setting, but you cannot weigh every bag. A random sample of 100 bags gives a mean of 502 g. Another sample would generally give a different mean: the observed figure is a useful centre, accompanied by sampling uncertainty.

The idea is to place a margin on each side of this centre. The resulting interval targets the process mean, which is unknown and assumed stable. Its width must account for the variability of means between samples: this is the role of the standard error, covered in the previous unit.

The level of approximately 95 % describes the construction rule. If we repeated the same sampling and calculation under the same assumptions, approximately 95 % of the intervals produced would contain the true mean. This mean does not move to follow the samples; the intervals change.

The setting and all its values are fictional. Here we assume independent masses with the same normal distribution and a known population standard deviation of 10 g. The weighing results accurately represent the masses. These assumptions help isolate the idea of an interval; they are not findings about a real machine.

The rule to remember

Construct centre ± margin, then interpret the coverage of the procedure under its assumptions. The true mean is the fixed target; sampling makes the bounds vary.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From weighing bags to an interval for the mean

The fictional sample contains n = 100 bags; the sum of their masses is 50200 g. The known population standard deviation is σ = 10 g. Let us construct a two-sided interval at a confidence level of approximately 95 %: “two-sided” means keeping a margin on each side of the centre.

  1. Define the target and the conditions for the calculation

    μ, the Greek letter mu, denotes the unknown mean mass of the process in grams. It remains fixed during the study. Xᵢ is the random mass of the bag at index i, before weighing; xᵢ is the value obtained. i is an index and n is a number of bags, with no physical unit. X̄, read “X bar”, is the random sample mean; x̄ is its value after weighing. All these masses and means are in grams.

    σ, read “sigma”, is the population standard deviation of individual masses, in grams. We assume it is known independently of the sample. Bags are sampled without favouring particular masses; their masses are independent and follow the same normal distribution with mean μ and standard deviation σ. The setting remains stable and weighing introduces no bias. A bias would be a systematic shift in the values used.

    Independence and stability cannot be inferred from the number of bags alone. Normality gives the exact distribution of the mean here. For a non-normal population, using a central limit approximation would require justifying its conditions; we do not automatically assume it is valid.

  2. Obtain the centre from the data

    The mean gives the mass per bag. We therefore divide the sum of the masses by the number of bags. The symbol ∑ means adding the masses xᵢ, from the first index to index n.

    x¯=∑i=1nxin=50200100=502 g

    To check the centre, multiply back: 502 g per bag × 100 bags = 50200 g. This recovers the stated sum, without turning this observed mean into a known true mean.

  3. Move from the spread of masses to the spread of means

    Let e denote the standard error of X̄, that is, the standard deviation of the means across repeated samples. Under the preceding assumptions, e = σ/√n. The square root √n has no unit: dividing by it preserves grams.

    e=σn=10100=1010=1 g

    Check the square root: 10 × 10 = 100. The bags have a standard deviation σ of 10 g; the means of these samples have a standard deviation e of 1 g. e does not denote the actual error made by our sample, since μ remains unknown.

  4. Choose the multiplier before looking at the result

    Z = (X̄ − μ)/e is the error of the mean measured in standard errors. Z has no unit. Under our normal model, its distribution has mean 0 and standard deviation 1: this is the standard normal distribution.

    To retain approximately 95 % of this distribution in the centre, choose z = 1.96, with no unit. This number is the usual normal quantile rounded to the nearest hundredth: here a quantile is a bound chosen to retain a given probability. It is not calculated from the observed mean. P denotes a probability in this model.

    P(−z≤Z≤z)≈0.95

    The complement is nominally 0.05, or 5 %, split between the two tails of the distribution. The level and the sign ≈ express an approximation, because z is rounded. The following operations with z = 49/25, however, are arithmetically exact.

  5. Turn the multiplier into a margin

    Let m denote the margin, in grams. The multiplier z indicates how many standard errors we retain on each side. We therefore multiply it by e.

    m=ze=1.96×1=1.96 g

    The margin is the distance from the centre to a bound. The full width runs from one bound to the other and is therefore 2 times this margin.

  6. Construct the bounds and recover the meaning of the method

    Let L denote the lower bound, U the upper bound and I the closed interval, which includes its bounds. L and U are in grams. Subtract m from the centre for L, then add it for U.

    I=[L;U]=[x¯−m;x¯+m]

    Lower bound: 502 − 1.96 = 500.04 g. Upper bound: 502 + 1.96 = 503.96 g. We obtain I = [500.04 g ; 503.96 g].

    Why does this interval target μ? Since e is positive, the inequality −z ≤ (X̄ − μ)/e ≤ z means that μ lies between X̄ − ze and X̄ + ze. We have simply rewritten the same event: the coverage level of the normal distribution becomes that of the interval constructed around X̄. After observation, we replace X̄ with x̄ to obtain the displayed bounds.

  7. Check the calculation and state a limited conclusion

    Width check: 503.96 − 500.04 = 3.92 g, or 2 × 1.96 g. Midpoint check: (500.04 + 503.96)/2 = 1004/2 = 502 g. The bounds do surround the observed mean and have the correct unit.

    Conclusion: for the mean mass of the process, the normal method with known standard deviation gives [500.04 g ; 503.96 g] at a confidence level of approximately 95 %. Once these bounds have been observed, μ is either inside or outside this interval. In this interpretation, we do not assign it a probability of 95 % of being in this particular interval.

    The guarantee concerns repetitions of the procedure. It does not promise that 95 % of bags have masses between these bounds, or that the result of this particular sample is correct. If σ must be estimated from the same weighing results, this procedure with known σ no longer applies as it stands; the choice of multiplier must be reconsidered.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “There is a 95 % chance that the true mean is in this interval that has already been calculated.”

    In the frequentist model used here, μ is fixed. Before sampling, the interval is random; afterwards, its bounds are fixed. Rephrase: approximately 95 % of the intervals constructed by this procedure would cover μ over many repetitions that satisfy the assumptions.

    Review the fixed target and the varying intervals

  • “These bounds must contain 95 % of the bags.”

    The target is the process mean. Here the spread of individual masses is σ = 10 g, whereas the standard error of the means is 1 g. The calculation uses the latter. Describing the masses of individual bags would require a different interval and a different objective.

    Review the move from σ to the standard error

  • “A narrower interval is enough to prove that my sample represents production better.”

    The calculated width does not check how the bags were selected. A choice favouring the heaviest bags can give a shifted centre, even with a large sample size. Fix the selection and check the stability of the process before applying the calculation.

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Distinguish margin from width

For the bags in the example, start again from x̄ = 502 g, e = 1 g and z = 1.96. Calculate the margin, the two bounds and the width. Explain why the margin is not the total width.

Hint 1

Multiply the multiplier, which has no unit, by the standard error in grams.

Hint 2

Place this same distance on each side of 502 g; the width combines both sides.

Compare with the solution

The margin is m = 1.96 × 1 = 1.96 g. The lower bound is 502 − 1.96 = 500.04 g; the upper bound is 502 + 1.96 = 503.96 g.

The width is U − L = 503.96 − 500.04 = 3.92 g. It combines a margin on the left and a margin on the right, so it equals 2m. The midpoint remains 502 g.

Check your reasoning

  • The margin is 1.96 g and the width is 3.92 g.
  • The bounds are symmetric around 502 g.
  • Each mass, margin and width carries the unit g; z has no unit.

Exercise 2 · Independent calculation and comparison

Sample more bags

Another fictional sample from the same stable production process contains 400 bags with a total mass of 200800 g. Keep σ = 10 g, the same model and z = 1.96. Predict the direction of the change in width, then calculate the mean, standard error and interval. By what factor has the margin been divided?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The sample size is multiplied by 4. Compare the square roots of the two sample sizes before dividing σ.

Hint 2

Check that 20 × 20 = 400, then calculate the margin from the new standard error.

Compare with the solution

The mean is 200800/400 = 502 g. Its equality with the previous mean is given by the data in this exercise; increasing the sample size does not guarantee the same observed mean.

The standard error becomes 10/√400 = 10/20 = 0.50 g. The margin is 1.96 × 0.50 = 0.98 g.

The interval is [501.02 g ; 502.98 g], obtained from 502 ± 0.98 g. Its width is 1.96 g. The ratio of the margins is 1.96/0.98 = 2: multiplying the sample size by 4 has divided the margin by 2, under the same assumptions.

The requested confidence level remains approximately 95 %. The precision of the calculation for the mean changes with sample size; the spread σ of individual masses has not been divided.

Check your reasoning

  • Predict a smaller width with σ and the level fixed.
  • Divide σ by the square root of the sample size, rather than directly by the sample size.
  • Recover the new margin and both bounds, with their units.
  • Distinguish the mean kept the same here by the data from the level kept the same by the choice of method.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

A correct calculation on a poor sample

The person weighing the bags now chooses those that look heaviest. They obtain the same sum and sample size as in the larger sample, recalculate the bounds and announce: “My interval is narrow, so it covers the mean of all production at the stated level.” Explain what the calculation checks, which assumption has changed and how to fix the approach.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Reconstruct how the bags were selected before looking at the bounds.

Hint 2

Ask whether the mean of what is selected still targets the mean of all production.

Compare with the solution

The arithmetic can still give [501.02 g ; 502.98 g]. This checks the application of a formula to the stated numbers. Selecting bags by mass, however, changes the observed distribution and favours high values.

Sampling no longer follows the mechanism assumed to represent all production. Our calculation therefore does not justify coverage of approximately 95 % for its mean. A larger sample size does not fix this selection shift.

Define a sampling procedure that does not favour particular masses, cover the intended production with a stable setting, then take a new sample and check the other assumptions. If σ is not actually known, state this and choose an appropriate method before publishing a confidence level.

Check your reasoning

  • Recognise that exact operations can accompany an invalid interpretation.
  • Identify selection bias and the population actually targeted.
  • Propose a new sample consistent with the model, followed by a check of its assumptions.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which quantity does our interval aim to cover?

μ, the unknown and stable mean mass of the process. The interval does not describe the distribution of the masses of individual bags and does not target the next individual mass.

Recall 2How can you reconstruct the interval from the sum of the weighing results, n and σ?

Calculate x̄ = sum/n, then e = σ/√n and m = ze. The bounds are x̄ − m and x̄ + m. σ is known here; independence, the same normal distribution and stability are part of the model. z is chosen from the target level, before using the observed mean.

Recall 3What does the level of approximately 95 % mean when sampling is repeated?

Under the stated assumptions, approximately 95 % of the intervals constructed by the same procedure would contain μ. The intervals vary between samples; μ remains fixed. The multiplier 1.96 is rounded, hence “approximately”.

Recall 4Why does multiplying the sample size by 4 divide the margin by 2?

With σ and the level fixed, the margin is proportional to the inverse of √n. Multiplying n by 4 multiplies √n by 2. This reduces sampling uncertainty in the mean, without correcting selection bias.

Recall 5Is a narrow interval enough to justify its confidence level?

No. Check the target and sampling mechanism, stability, independence, the distribution model and the knowledge of σ used by the method. Bags chosen by mass can produce narrow bounds without the stated coverage.
