Level 2 · C046·M3

Taylor

Your goalConstruct a Taylor polynomial from the derivatives at the centre, then check an approximation of the time needed to fill a tank.

Before you start
c031-m5 : asymptotes · c045-m5 : introductory quadrature · c046-m1 : series tests · c046-m2 : power series

✿01 See the idea

A pump fills a tank holding 100 L. At a constant flow rate of 10 L/min, filling takes 10 min. If the flow rate increases to 11 L/min, the time decreases. Before calculating, predict: can a rule that always subtracts the same amount of time for the same increase in flow rate follow this decrease exactly?

The relationship between time and flow rate is curved: time is volume divided by flow rate. Taylor constructs a small polynomial around a known situation. It preserves the value at the centre, then the slope, then the change in that slope. This last contribution curves the polynomial instead of leaving it as a straight line.

Let us compare flow rates by their change relative to the initial flow rate. We will call this change divided by 10 L/min x. It is 0 at the centre and 0.10 at the target flow rate. Time, divided by the initial 10 min, becomes a dimensionless quantity: this is the simplified function we will approximate.

The scene and all its data are fictional. The volume is assumed to be known exactly, and the flow rate is positive and constant during each filling, with no leaks or start-up phase. Taylor will approximate this mathematical relationship; it will not check these assumptions for a real pump.

The rule to remember

Choose the centre, match the derivatives at the centre, evaluate the polynomial near that centre, then distinguish this value from the value of the function.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Moving from 10 to 11 L/min without confusing the function and the polynomial

The volume V is 100 L. The reference flow rate b is 10 L/min and the target flow rate q is 11 L/min. Let us construct the Taylor polynomial of degree 2 at the centre of the relative change, then compare its time with the exact quotient. The tolerance chosen for this exercise is 0.01 min.

  1. Separate the time and the dimensionless function

    T(q) denotes the time in minutes at flow rate q, expressed in litres per minute. The letter b denotes the reference flow rate and τ, read as "tau", the time at flow rate b. We define x = (q − b)/b: this is a dimensionless relative change, positive if the flow rate increases. The function f gives the ratio T(q)/τ, which is also dimensionless.

    T(q)=Vq;τ=Vb=10 min
    f(x)=11+x;T(q)=τf(x)

    Indeed, q = b(1 + x), so V/q = (V/b)/(1 + x). The physical domain is x > −1, because the flow rate must remain positive. Here, the change in flow rate is 11 − 10 = 1 L/min, giving x = 1/10 = 0.10. Using 1 directly in f would omit the division by the reference flow rate.

  2. Construct the coefficients by matching the derivatives

    Let a denote the centre and h the change from that centre, both dimensionless. A derivative f′ describes the slope; the second derivative f″ describes how that slope changes. We seek a polynomial P of the form A + Bh + C × (h squared). The letters A, B and C are its coefficients, dimensionless in our normalized function. At the centre h = 0, its value is A, its derivative is B and its second derivative is 2C. To recover the same information as f at the centre a, we therefore need A = f(a), B = f′(a) and C = f″(a)/2.

    P2(a+h)=f(a)+f′(a)h+f″(a)2h2

    This is the Taylor polynomial of degree at most 2 at the centre a. It is not a fit to measurements: the derivatives determine the coefficients. For the normalized tank function, we choose a = 0, so h = x.

  3. Place the general formula in context without moving to an infinite sum

    The same reasoning gives a finite sum, if the derivatives used exist at the centre. n is the chosen maximum degree, a non-negative integer; k ranges over the orders of differentiation. These indices are dimensionless. f⁽ᵏ⁾ is the derivative taken k times; by convention, the derivative of order 0 is f itself. The factorial k! multiplies the integers from 1 to k when k is positive, and 0! is 1. Differentiating h to the power k, k times, produces the factor k!: dividing by it in the coefficient recovers exactly the desired derivative at the centre.

    Pn(a+h)=∑k=0nf(k)(a)k!hk

    For n = 2, if f has continuous derivatives up to order 2 in a neighbourhood of a, Taylor gives a local approximation. Let R(h) denote the dimensionless difference between f(a + h) and its polynomial of degree 2: the quotient of R(h) by h squared tends to 0 as h tends to 0, with h nonzero. This explains the word "local", without yet giving a bound at a fixed distance. The next unit will study the remainder. This property does not state that f is equal to an infinite Taylor series: such an equality requires an additional proof.

  4. Calculate the derivatives of our function at the centre

    Let us write f as a power of (1 + x). The power rule for differentiation gives the following expressions; the derivative of (1 + x) is 1. The prime means "derivative with respect to x". All these derivatives remain dimensionless because x and f are dimensionless.

    f′(x)=−1(1+x)2
    f″(x)=2(1+x)3

    At the centre: f(0) = 1, f′(0) = -1, f″(0) = 2. The quadratic coefficient is 2/2 = 1. The slope is negative and the curvature positive: time decreases, but its decrease slows as the flow rate increases.

    P2(x)=1−x+x2

  5. Evaluate the contributions, then return to minutes

    At x = 0.10, the square is 1/10 × 1/10 = 1/100. The linear term is -1 × 1/10 = -1/10. The quadratic term is 1 × 1/100 = 1/100. Let us add them in this order to distinguish their roles.

    P2(0.10)=1−0.10+0.01=0.91

    The value 0.91 is a ratio of times. To recover minutes, multiply by τ: 10 min × 0.91 = 9.10 min. With the slope alone, we would have obtained 10 min × 0.90 = 9 min. The quadratic term therefore adjusts the linear approximation upwards; it does not change the physical model.

  6. Check against the exact quotient

    In this example, the original function is easy to evaluate: the check uses the volume and flow rate directly, without using the polynomial again. The exact time is 100/11 = 100/11 min, or approximately 9.0909 min. Multiplying the exact time by the flow rate does indeed recover the 100 L in the tank.

    The polynomial overestimates the time here. The absolute error is 91/10 − 100/11 = 1/110 min, or approximately 0.0091 min. For the straight line, the error was 100/11 − 9 = 1/11 min. We can therefore confirm, for this specific target, that the quadratic term brings the result closer to the exact time.

    Let us compare the fractions before rounding: the tolerance 1/100 min minus the error 1/110 min is 1/1100 min, a positive quantity. The chosen criterion is met. This check at a single point guarantees neither the same accuracy at all flow rates nor that the actual time for a pump follows V/q exactly.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The coefficient of the squared term is the second derivative.”

    Differentiating the squared term twice produces the factor 2. For the second derivative of the polynomial to be 2, its coefficient must therefore be 2/2 = 1. Reconstruct this coefficient from the derivatives rather than memorizing it on its own.

    Revisit the construction of the coefficients

  • “The change in flow rate is 1, so I substitute 1 for x.”

    This change is in litres per minute. x is the ratio of this change to the reference flow rate: 1/10 = 0.10, dimensionless. Start again from the physical quantities and also check the conversion from the ratio of times back to minutes.

    Revisit the change of variable and the units

  • “The derivatives match at the centre, so the polynomial is exact everywhere.”

    The match concerns the local information retained. At a flow rate of 11 L/min, the difference is already nonzero: 1/110 min. Then try 20 L/min to see why a distant target requires another check.

Exercise 1 · Reconstruction

Recover the missing coefficient

For the same normalized function, at the centre 0, the value is 1, the slope -1 and the second derivative 2. Reconstruct A, B and C in A + Bx + C × (x squared). Justify C by differentiation, then calculate the value at a flow rate of 11 L/min.

Hint 1

At the centre, the constant term gives the value and the coefficient of x gives the slope.

Hint 2

The second derivative of C × (x squared) is 2C. Finish by calculating the relative change in flow rate.

Compare with the solution

We need A = 1 and B = -1. Then 2C = 2, giving C = 1. The division comes from differentiating the squared term; it does not change the required second derivative.

At a flow rate of 11 L/min, x = (11 − 10)/10 = 0.10. The polynomial is 1 − 0.10 + 0.01 = 0.91, dimensionless. The approximate time is therefore 10 × 0.91 = 9.10 min.

Exercise 2 · Application and checking

The flow rate decreases slightly

The same pump now delivers 9 L/min. Keep the centre b = 10 L/min. Calculate x, the polynomial of degree 2, then the approximate time. Compare with the exact quotient: does the error still meet the tolerance of 0.01 min?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The relative change is negative; its square remains positive.

Hint 2

Return to minutes before subtracting the times. The exact time is the volume divided by 9 L/min.

Compare with the solution

The change in flow rate is 9 − 10 = -1 L/min, so x = -1/10 = -0.10. The linear term is -1 × -1/10 = 1/10 and the square is 1/100. The polynomial gives 1 + 1/10 + 1/100 = 1.11.

The approximate time is 10 × 1.11 = 11.10 min. The exact time is 100/9 = 100/9 min, approximately 11.1111 min. Here the polynomial underestimates the time; the absolute error is 100/9 − 111/10 = 1/90 min, approximately 0.0111 min.

The fraction 1/90 is greater than 1/100: the criterion is no longer met. The same distance from the centre, on the other side, does not necessarily give the same error. The result remains greater than the initial time of 10 min, which is consistent with a lower flow rate.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the sign of x and a positive square.
  • Compare times in minutes and state whether the time is overestimated or underestimated.
  • Reject the tolerance by comparing the exact fractions.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and repair

Can a local approximation be used far from the centre?

Someone keeps the same polynomial at the centre b = 10 L/min for a flow rate of 20 L/min. They claim that matching the derivatives guarantees the time everywhere. Calculate their result, compare it with the exact time and propose a reasonable next step for this tank.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate (20 − 10)/10. Is the relative change still small?

Hint 2

The volume and flow rate allow us to calculate the exact time directly here. A polynomial formula can still be evaluated even when it approximates the function poorly.

Compare with the solution

The change in flow rate is 10 L/min, so x = 1. The polynomial is 1 + (-1) + 1 = 1. The stated time is 10 × 1 = 10 min.

The exact quotient is 100/20 = 5 min. The error is 10 − 5 = 5 min, well beyond 0.01 min. The flow rate has increased, but the polynomial gives the initial time here: this check already signals a need to verify the calculation.

The derivatives match at the centre, without imposing equality elsewhere. For this target, let us use the exact quotient, available in the scene. If an approximation were needed, we could choose a closer centre, reconstruct the coefficients and check the new difference. Using Taylor far from the centre is not prohibited; its accuracy is not guaranteed by the local construction.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover the approximate 10 min and the exact 5 min.
  • Refute the global guarantee using the difference of 5 min.
  • Propose the exact quotient or a new local construction with a check.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the centre in the tank problem, and why is the flow rate not used directly as the input to f?

The reference flow rate is b = 10 L/min; the centre of f is x = 0. The argument x = (q − b)/b is a dimensionless relative change. The time is τf(x), with τ = 10 min.

Recall 2How can you reconstruct the polynomial of degree 2 without reciting a list of coefficients?

Write A + Bh + C × (h squared) around the centre a. Require the same value A = f(a), the same slope B = f′(a), then the same second derivative 2C = f″(a). This gives C = f″(a)/2.

Recall 3Why does the general Taylor formula divide the derivative of order k by k!?

The derivative taken k times of the term h to the power k is k!. The division compensates for this factor so that the derivative of order k of the polynomial at the centre is that of f. The sum stops at the chosen degree n.

Recall 4What have we established at a flow rate of 11 L/min, and which conclusion does not follow from this check?

The polynomial gives 9.10 min compared with exactly 100/11 min. The error 1/110 min is less than 1/100 min at this target. This guarantees neither this accuracy at all flow rates nor that the model is appropriate for a real pump.

Recall 5Is a Taylor polynomial an equality with the function or a promise about an infinite sum?

It is a finite sum constructed to match the derivatives at the centre up to the chosen degree. Under the stated smoothness assumptions, it approximates the function locally. Equality with an infinite sum requires further justification.
