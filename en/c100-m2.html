Ridge

Your goalBuild a ridge regression, calculate its slope and explain how the penalty and the scale of the variable affect it.

Before you start
c067-m5 : pipeline · c059-m6 : deterministic discrete Bellman · c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c100-m1 : predictive regression

✿01 See the idea

In a workshop, you want to predict the drying time of sheets from the dryer load. The fictional observations pair load indices -1, 0 and 1 with drying times 8, 10 and 12 min. The index expresses a load difference from the mean, divided by a common reference mass: it is dimensionless.

The line that fits these observations exactly increases by 2 min per index unit. But fitting a few observations very closely can make the coefficients sensitive to small changes in the data. Ridge regression adds a cost for large slopes. It then accepts differences from the observations to reduce the magnitude of the coefficient.

Imagine a restraint that pulls the slope towards 0 while leaving the mean height free. The strength of this restraint is set by a parameter called lambda, written λ. With our setting, the slope becomes 1 min per index unit; the line still passes through the mean drying time 10 min at the mean load.

These data are entirely fictional. The drying times deliberately lie on a line to make the tradeoff visible in a calculation by hand. The example shows what ridge optimizes; it does not demonstrate better predictions for new loads. The choice of λ must be evaluated on observations set aside for validation.

The rule to remember

Add λ times the squared slope to the residual cost; keep the intercept outside the penalty and state the scale of the variables.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Regularize the dryer slope

We keep the 3 fictional pairs (-1 ; 8 min), (0 ; 10 min), (1 ; 12 min). Each observation has the same weight. Set λ = 2 to understand the calculation, without presenting this choice as optimal for a real dryer.

  1. Define the model and its units

    For observation i, xᵢ is the dimensionless centered load and yᵢ is the observed drying time in min. The model predicts ŷᵢ = b + βxᵢ. The hat indicates a prediction; b is the intercept, in min; β is the slope, in min per index unit. The number of observations is n = 3; i runs through them from 1 to n.

    The bar denotes a training mean. Here x̄ = (-1 + 0 + 1)/3 = 0. For the drying times, 8 + 10 = 18 min, then 18 + 12 = 30 min; so ȳ = 30/3 = 10 min. This centering is kept when predicting new loads.

    We seek a line by minimizing a quadratic cost. The observations and the load index are given; the linear relationship is our modeling choice. The algebraic solution below provides neither a prediction interval nor a causal interpretation of the slope.

  2. Write the exact cost being used

    The residual is yᵢ − ŷᵢ, in min. Squaring it prevents a positive difference from canceling a negative difference in the cost. Ridge adds λ times the square of β: a large slope must reduce the squared differences enough to offset this penalty.

    Jλ(b,β)=∑i=1n(yi−b−βxi)2+λβ2

    Jλ is in min². With our dimensionless index, λ is dimensionless and nonnegative, and the square of β is in min². We use a sum of squares, without division by n. Another convention would change the numerical value of λ that gives the same fit; the second exercise asks you to check this.

    The intercept b remains free and is not penalized. For any fixed slope, minimizing with respect to b requires the residuals to sum to 0, so b = ȳ − βx̄. Since x̄ = 0, b is 10 min for all the slopes being compared. The penalty pulls towards a constant drying time equal to the mean, rather than a drying time of 0.

  3. Summarize the data with two useful sums

    Write Sxx for the sum of the squares of xᵢ and Sxy = ∑ xᵢ(yᵢ − ȳ), where ∑ sums over the n observations. Sxx is dimensionless; Sxy is in min. The first measures the spread of the centered load; the second links changes in load and drying time.

    The drying time differences are -2 min, 0 min and 2 min. The extreme loads each have a square of 1; the central load contributes 0. Thus Sxx = 1 + 0 + 1 = 2.

    For Sxy, the first product is (-1) × (-2) = 2 min. The central product is 0. The last is 1 × 2 = 2 min. Their sum gives Sxy = 4 min. Both extreme observations support a positive slope here.

  4. Find the slope that minimizes the cost

    After setting b = ȳ, Jλ depends only on β. Its derivative, written Jλ′, describes how the cost varies as the slope changes. Differentiating each square and then collecting terms gives:

    Jλ′(β)=2[(Sxx+λ)β−Sxy]

    The derivative is 0 when (Sxx + λ)β = Sxy. The second derivative is 2(Sxx + λ), strictly positive whenever Sxx + λ is positive. We then have a unique minimum, rather than merely a slope at which the cost momentarily stops changing.

    β^λ=SxySxx+λ=42+2=1 min

    The denominator is 4. Without a penalty, λ = 0, least squares gives β̂MC = Sxy/Sxx = 2 min. MC is the French abbreviation for least squares. Ridge multiplies this slope by Sxx/(Sxx + λ), here 2/4. With fixed data in this single-variable model, increasing λ moves the slope towards 0.

  5. Return to a prediction in minutes

    For the load with index x = 1, the slope gives an offset of β̂λx = 1 × 1 = 1 min. Add it to b, since b already represents the drying time at the mean load.

    y^=b+β^λx=10+1×1=11 min

    For the training loads, the ridge predictions are therefore 9, 10 and 11 min. The unpenalized line predicts 8, 10 and 12 min. Ridge has kept the center and reduced the differences between the predictions and this center.

  6. Check the residuals and the full cost

    The ridge residuals are 8 − 9 = −1 min, 10 − 10 = 0 min and 12 − 11 = 1 min. Their sum is 0, which checks the fit of b. The sum of their squares is 2 min². We also obtain it by multiplying Sxx by the squared difference between the slopes: this difference is 1 min, its square is 1 min², and then 2 × 1 = 2 min².

    The square of the ridge slope is 1 min²; its penalty is 2 × 1 = 2 min². The full cost is therefore Jλ = 2 + 2 = 4 min². For the least squares slope, the residuals are all 0, but its square is 4 min²: the same cost Jλ is 0 + 2 × 4 = 8 min².

    An independent check is to expand the cost for our dryer directly, then complete the square. For any slope β in min, with b = 10 min:

    Jλ(β)=4(β−1 min)2+4 min²

    The square cannot be negative: the minimum cost is indeed 4 min² at slope 1 min. Yet the training error alone has risen from 0 to 2 min². A decrease in the penalized cost therefore provides no proof of lower error on future data.

    (XᵀX+λI)β^=Xᵀyc

    This system is the matrix version of (Sxx + λ)β = Sxy; it is solved with a suitable numerical method. For strictly positive λ, adding λI makes the solution unique even if some columns are redundant. In coordinates made dimensionless, the coefficients remain in min and λ remains dimensionless. With several related variables, each coefficient depends on the others: the scalar shrinkage factor in our example does not apply separately to each column.

    Comparing squared coefficients requires chosen scales. For each variable, learn the centering and, if necessary, division by a nonzero standard deviation from the training data only; then apply the same transformations to validation data and new loads. ✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Ridge must reduce the error on the training data.”

    It minimizes the sum of squared residuals plus the penalty. For our dryer, the sum of squared residuals increases from 0 to 2 min², while the full cost at the same λ falls from 8 to 4 min². A separate evaluation is still needed to assess new predictions.

    Check both costs again

  • “I also penalize the baseline drying time and use the same λ in every convention.”

    In the model defined here, only the slope enters the penalty; the free intercept keeps b = ȳ. The value of λ also depends on the choice between the sum and the mean of squares. Rewrite the exact cost before comparing settings or software.

    Reread the definition of the cost and the intercept

  • “Changing the unit of load makes no difference to ridge.”

    The same line can have a different numerical slope after rescaling. Its square then receives a different penalty if λ stays unchanged. Set the transformations using the training data and check their effect on the objective.

    Solve the dryer rescaling exercise

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Tighten the restraint

Keep exactly the same dryer observations and replace λ = 2 with λ = 6. Before calculating, predict the direction of the change in slope. Then calculate the slope, b and the prediction at x = 1. Give the units.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The data have not changed: keep Sxx and Sxy, then change only the denominator.

Hint 2

The load is centered and b is not penalized: its value remains ȳ.

Compare with the solution

The positive slope must decrease, since its denominator increases: Sxx + λ = 2 + 6 = 8. It is 4/8 = 1/2 min per index unit.

The intercept remains b = 10 min. At x = 1, ŷ = 10 + (1/2) × 1 = 21/2 min, or 10.5 min. The prediction is closer to the mean than 11 min; its quality for a future load still needs to be evaluated.

Check your reasoning

  • Predict a decrease in the slope before calculating and link it to the denominator.
  • Obtain 1/2 min per index unit and keep b = 10 min.
  • Give 10.5 min without turning this shrinkage into proof of a better prediction.

Exercise 2 · Compare two formulations

A sum or a mean?

A colleague defines Jm as the mean of squared residuals plus λm times the square of β, with n = 3. Which λm gives exactly the same fit as our sum with λ = 2? What result does the colleague obtain by mistakenly keeping λm = 2?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Multiplying the entire cost by n does not move its minimum.

Hint 2

After this multiplication, the coefficient of the squared slope becomes nλm.

Compare with the solution

λm is the penalty parameter for the mean convention. For nJm to be exactly our Jλ, we need nλm = λ, so λm = 2/3 = 2/3. The slope remains 1 min and the prediction at x = 1 remains 11 min.

If the colleague keeps λm = 2, the cost multiplied by n has a penalty parameter of 3 × 2 = 6. This gives the result of the previous exercise: slope 1/2 min and prediction 10.5 min. The difference comes from the convention, even though the observations are identical.

Check your reasoning

  • Write nλm = λ and obtain λm = 2/3.
  • Explain why multiplying the entire cost by a positive number preserves its minimum.
  • Identify the equivalent penalty parameter 6 when the same number is mistakenly kept.

Exercise 3 · Reasoned transfer

Recode the same load

The colleague recodes the index as x′ = 2x, without changing any drying time or the sum convention. The prime marks the new coordinate. Find Sxx′ and Sxy′. With λ unchanged, calculate the new slope β′ and the prediction for the same physical load, now x′ = 2. Which λ′ restores our original ridge line exactly?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Each squared load is multiplied by the square of the factor; each load–drying time product receives only the factor.

Hint 2

Predicting the same drying time requires β′x′ = βx. For the same line, β′ must therefore be divided by the factor.

Hint 3

Express the square of β using β = 2β′, then find the coefficient of the square of β′ in the penalty.

Compare with the solution

The square of the factor is 4. Thus Sxx′ = 4 × 2 = 8 and Sxy′ = 2 × 4 = 8 min. With λ unchanged, the denominator is 8 + 2 = 10, and β′ = 8/10 = 4/5 min per unit of the new index.

At x′ = 2, the offset is (4/5) × 2 = 8/5 min. The prediction is 10 + 8/5 = 58/5 min, or 11.6 min. It differs from 11 min: recoding has changed the effective strength of the penalty.

To keep the same line, β = 2β′. Its original penalty is therefore λ × 4 times the square of β′: we need λ′ = 4 × 2 = 8. The new denominator becomes 8 + 8 = 16; β′ = 8/16 = 1/2 min. The offset is 1 min and the prediction is 11 min: this recovers the original fit for the same physical load.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish the factor 2 from its square 4 in the two sums.
  • Evaluate the load at the new index 2, rather than at the same number as before.
  • Justify λ′ = 8 by showing that the penalties for the same line are identical.
  • Recover the prediction 11 min after compensation.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which cost does ridge minimize, and what role does λ play?

The sum of squared residuals plus λ times the sum of squared slopes. λ sets the cost of coefficients with large magnitudes. In our convention, the intercept is not penalized.

Recall 2How can you recover the dryer ridge slope from the centered data?

Form Sxx, the sum of the squares of xᵢ, and Sxy = ∑ xᵢ(yᵢ − ȳ), then solve (Sxx + λ)β = Sxy. The slope is Sxy/(Sxx + λ), with b = ȳ. The positive denominator guarantees a unique minimum.

Recall 3Why does a decrease in the penalized cost fail to prove a better prediction?

This cost can decrease in exchange for a larger training error. New predictions must be evaluated separately; tuning λ on validation data and then consulting a final test kept aside answers a different question from minimizing the training cost.

Recall 4What information must be fixed before comparing two values of λ?

The choice between the sum and the mean of squares, the penalized variables and their scale transformations. The transformations are learned from the training data and then kept. The same number can correspond to different effective penalties.

Recall 5Does ridge require variables to be removed by setting their coefficients to 0?

The quadratic penalty reduces the magnitude of coefficients without requiring them to be exactly 0. In our scalar formula, with nonzero Sxy, the slope remains nonzero for any finite penalty. A coefficient can still be 0 when the data and the model lead to that value.
