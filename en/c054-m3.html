Level 1 · C054·M3

Convexity

Your goalRecognize a convex function by its chords, justify its convexity and explain what it allows us to conclude about a minimum.

Before you start
c033-m5 : affine approximation · c014-m5 : invariants · c054-m1 : extrema · c054-m2 : mean value theorem

✿01 See the idea

You adjust a stop that brings a small part to a halt on a rail. The target position is 3 cm. To compare settings, we define a penalty: it is 2 points at the target position, then increases with the square of the deviation. The positions, coefficients and penalty points are fictional; no manufacturing measurements are reported.

Imagine the curve of this penalty and stretch a straight thread between two points on it. This segment is called a chord. A convex curve stays below each of its chords, allowing contact. At the midpoint of two settings, the penalty for the intermediate setting therefore does not exceed the average of the penalties at the endpoints.

The diagram shows the settings 1 cm and 5 cm. Their penalties are equal, but the intermediate position is better: it lies below the thread. Convexity requires this comparison to hold for all endpoints and all intermediate positions in the interval being studied. A diagram or a few trials cannot prove it.

Also observe the slope: it goes from negative to 0, then positive. It increases all along the rail, even though the penalty initially decreases. Convex describes how the slope changes; increasing describes the direction in which the value changes.

The rule to remember

A convex function stays below all its chords. If it is differentiable, its tangents stay below its curve; a 0 slope then certifies a global minimum on the interval.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The intermediate setting and the chord

The stop can be adjusted continuously between 0 cm and 6 cm. The target is h = 3 cm. The penalty model adds c = 2 points to k = 1 point per square centimetre multiplied by the square of the deviation from h. Let us compare the settings a = 1 cm and b = 5 cm, then prove the property for all permitted settings.

  1. Write the model and its units

    x is the chosen position and h the target position, both in centimetres. I denotes the closed interval of permitted positions, including its endpoints. J(x) is the penalty in points; c is its base value and k its deviation coefficient. A point here is a conventional unit of penalty, with no measured physical meaning.

    I=[0;6] cm
    J(x)=c+k(x−h)2

    The deviation is in centimetres; its square is in square centimetres. Multiplying it by k, expressed in points per square centimetre, gives points, which we can add to c. We assume this model is exact within the exercise, with strictly positive k and continuous positions. This does not validate the model for a real workshop.

  2. Express “below all the chords” mathematically

    Choose a and b in I. The dimensionless quantity t indicates the fraction of the way from a to b: it lies between 0 and 1. The position z = (1 − t)a + tb still belongs to I because I is an interval. With the same weights, the values J(a) and J(b) give the height of the straight segment.

    z=(1−t)a+tb
    J(z)≤(1−t)J(a)+tJ(b)

    J is convex on I if this inequality holds for all permitted a, b and t. The left side is the penalty for the intermediate setting; the right side is a weighted average of penalties. These are different operations.

  3. Calculate the penalties at the chosen endpoints

    At a = 1 cm, the deviation is 1 − 3 = -2 cm. Its square is 4 square centimetres; the contribution from the deviation is 1 × 4 = 4 points. Therefore J(a) = 2 + 4 = 6 points.

    At b = 5 cm, the deviation is 5 − 3 = 2 cm. Its square is 4 square centimetres, and the contribution is 4 points. Therefore J(b) = 2 + 4 = 6 points. Check: the deviations are opposites and have the same square; the penalties must be equal.

  4. Compare the curve and the chord at the midpoint

    Take t = 1/2, or 0.50 with no unit: the two positions have equal weight. The midpoint is (1 + 5)/2 = 6/2 = 3 cm. Its deviation from the target is 0 cm, its square 0 square centimetre and its penalty contribution 0 point. J at the midpoint is therefore 2 + 0 = 2 points.

    The height of the chord is calculated from the endpoint penalties: (6 + 6)/2 = 12/2 = 6 points. The vertical gap is 6 − 2 = 4 points. Thus 2 ≤ 6: the curve lies below this chord. We have checked one case, not yet the whole definition.

  5. Prove the property throughout the interval

    Call the height of the chord minus J(z), in points, D. Replace each J with c + k times the square of the deviation. The c terms cancel because the weights sum to 1; after expanding the squares, the terms containing h also cancel. This leaves:

    D=k[(1−t)a2+tb2−z2]
    D=kt(1−t)(b−a)2≥0

    To recover the last equality, expand z squared: the coefficients of a squared and b squared both become t(1 − t), and the cross term is −2t(1 − t)ab. We recognize the square of b − a. All the factors in D are non-negative: convexity is proved for every permitted pair and weight.

    With our endpoints, b − a = 4 cm; at the midpoint, k × t × (1 − t) × (b − a) squared gives 4 points again. If a and b are distinct and 0 < t < 1, D is strictly positive: J is strictly convex, meaning strictly below the chord except at its endpoints.

  6. Relate the shape to the second derivative

    J′ denotes the derivative of J, hence its slope, in points per centimetre. J″ is the derivative of J′: it measures the change in this slope, in points per square centimetre. Our polynomial is twice differentiable on the whole real line.

    J′(x)=2k(x−h);J″(x)=2k=2

    A useful criterion: for a function twice differentiable on an open interval containing I, a non-negative second derivative on that interval ensures convexity on I. A strictly positive second derivative ensures strict convexity. By the mean value theorem, this sign makes the first derivative non-decreasing; the chord slopes then have the order required for convexity.

    Here J″ = 2 points per square centimetre everywhere, so the criterion confirms the algebraic proof. The slopes at positions 1, 3 and 5 cm are -4, 0 and 4 points per centimetre. A negative slope on the left is entirely compatible with a derivative that increases.

  7. Certify the minimum and its uniqueness

    At h = 3 cm, J′ is 0 point per centimetre. For a differentiable convex function on an interval, a 0 derivative at an interior point certifies a global minimum: no other position in the interval gives a lower value. Convexity alone does not require a minimum to exist or to be unique.

    J(x)−J(h)=k(x−h)2≥0

    Here this direct check establishes more than a search for a 0 slope: J(x) never falls below J(h) = 2 points. The setting h is permitted, so the minimum is attained. Since k is positive, equality requires x = h: the minimum is unique. More generally, a strictly convex function has at most one minimizer; we must still establish that the minimum is attained.

  8. Give the tangent a precise role

    For a convex differentiable function, the tangent at x provides a lower bound for every other position y in the same interval. y is in centimetres; J′(x)(y − x) is in points. The affine approximation thus becomes an inequality valid throughout the interval:

    J(y)≥J(x)+J′(x)(y−x)

    In our model, subtracting the right side from the left gives exactly k times the square of y − x. This is non-negative. If J′(x) is 0, the bound becomes J(y) ≥ J(x), which explains why it certifies the minimum. A tangent taken elsewhere may give a bound below the penalty actually attained: the tangent exercise distinguishes these two values.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Convex means that the penalty increases.”

    To the left of the target, J′ is negative: the penalty decreases. Convexity requires a non-decreasing derivative, meaning a slope that becomes less negative, then positive here. Compare the slopes in the diagram with the formula for J″.

    Reread the slope diagram

  • “The comparison works at the midpoint, so it is proved everywhere.”

    The midpoint calculation checks a single position on a single chord. The definition requires all pairs and all weights. Return to the identity D = kt(1 − t)(b − a) squared: it justifies the sign without listing the settings.

    Revisit the proof for all chords

  • “As soon as the derivative is 0, I have the unique global minimum.”

    A 0 slope alone does not give this conclusion. On an interval, convexity allows us to certify the global minimum; strict convexity gives its uniqueness if it exists. The constant penalty in the last exercise separates these claims.

Exercise 1 · Numerical application

Move away from the midpoint of the chord

Keep a = 1 cm and b = 5 cm. Take t = 1/4, or 0.25 with no unit. Calculate z, J(z), the height of the chord and their difference. Does this difference suffice to prove convexity everywhere? Write an attempt before consulting a hint.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

t gives the weight of b; the weight of a is 1 − t. Use the same weights for the chord, but apply J after calculating z for the curve.

Hint 2

J(a) and J(b) are already known. To check the difference, use k × t × (1 − t) × (b − a) squared.

Compare with the solution

The weight of a is 3/4. The contributions to the position are 3/4 × 1 = 3/4 cm and 1/4 × 5 = 5/4 cm. Their sum gives z = 2 cm.

The deviation z − h is -1 cm. Its square is 1 square centimetre; the penalty contribution is 1 point. Therefore J(z) = 2 + 1 = 3 points.

On the chord, the contributions are 3/4 × 6 = 9/2 points and 1/4 × 6 = 3/2 points. Their sum is 6 points. The chord therefore exceeds the curve by 6 − 3 = 3 points.

Independent check: 1 × 1/4 × 3/4 × (4 squared) gives 3 points again. This numerical check still concerns only one weight; the general proof is the identity valid for all choices.

Check your reasoning

  • You keep the weight t on b without swapping the two positions.
  • You find 2 cm, 3 points and 6 points using distinct operations.
  • You check the gap of 3 points and correctly limit the scope of the calculation.

Exercise 2 · Reasoning and checking

A tangent that does not give the exact value

At x = 2 cm, calculate J′(x). Use the tangent at this point to give a lower bound for J(h), with h = 3 cm. Compare the bound with the true value in the model. Explain why the negative slope does not invalidate convexity.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The slope is 2k(x − h). The change in position from x to h is a distance: multiply it by the slope, then add J(x).

Hint 2

The difference between the curve and this tangent is k times the square of h − x. A lower bound is not necessarily attained.

Compare with the solution

The slope is 2 × -1 = -2 points per centimetre. The displacement to h is 3 − 2 = 1 cm. The change term on the tangent is -2 × 1 = -2 points.

The tangent gives J(h) ≥ 3 + (-2) = 1 point. The model, however, gives J(h) = 2 points. The gap comes from the square of the displacement: k × (h − x) squared is 1 point. The bound is correct, but it is not the exact value.

J decreases at x because its slope there is negative. J remains convex because this slope increases: J″ is 2 points per square centimetre everywhere. To obtain a horizontal tangent that certifies the minimum, take it at h, where J′ is 0.

Check your reasoning

  • You give the slope -2 in points per centimetre.
  • You distinguish the bound 1 point from the value 2 points.
  • You justify convexity by how the slope changes, rather than by the sign of J′.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same model

If deviation no longer costs anything

In the same scene, replace only k with 0 point per square centimetre. Someone says: “The penalty remains convex, so the setting 3 cm is still the unique minimum.” Examine convexity, existence and uniqueness separately. Give a different setting that settles the objection.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start by simplifying J(x) for every permitted position. What happens to the curve, its chords and its tangents?

Hint 2

A function can coincide with its chord: the definition of convex uses ≤. Strict convexity would require < between two distinct points.

Compare with the solution

With k = 0, the penalty becomes constant: J(x) = 2 points throughout I. For all permitted a, b and t, the chord also has value 2 points. The convexity inequality is therefore an equality: the function is convex, but it is no longer strictly convex.

A minimum exists and equals 2 points. It is attained at every position in I. For example, the setting 1 cm has the same penalty as 3 cm: the claimed uniqueness is false. A derivative that is 0 everywhere does not single out any position.

We must therefore ask what has been proved: convexity, the existence of a point that attains the smallest value, then possibly uniqueness through strict convexity. The initial model had all these properties; removing its deviation penalty preserves only some of them.

Check your reasoning

  • You prove convexity through equality with the chords for all weights.
  • You identify an attained minimum without claiming uniqueness.
  • You refute the objection with a permitted position different from the target.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must we compare to define convexity on an interval?

For all a and b in the interval and every t between 0 and 1, compare J((1 − t)a + tb) with (1 − t)J(a) + tJ(b). The first value must be less than or equal to the second: the curve stays below the chord.

Recall 2Does a negative slope prevent convexity? What sign of the second derivative is sufficient?

No. A convex differentiable function has a non-decreasing derivative, even if it is negative at some points. For a function twice differentiable on an open interval containing the interval under study, J″ being non-negative everywhere suffices to establish convexity.

Recall 3When does a 0 slope certify a global minimum, and what does strict convexity add?

For a differentiable convex function on an interval, a 0 slope at an interior point certifies a global minimum through the tangent bound. Strict convexity makes a minimum unique if it is attained; it does not prove existence on its own.

Recall 4Where do a chord and a tangent of a convex function lie?

Between its endpoints, the chord is above the curve, with equality allowed. If the function is differentiable, every tangent stays below the curve on the interval. The tangent provides a lower bound, not necessarily the exact value at another position.

Recall 5Why do calculations at a few positions fail to prove convexity throughout the interval?

The definition covers all pairs and all weights. A few calculations check particular cases. In the stop model, the identity chord minus curve = kt(1 − t)(b − a) squared provides the general proof because all its factors are non-negative.
