Level 4 · C236·M1

Integration by parts

Your goalTransfer the derivative of a function of the reading to a Gaussian weight, justifying the scale factor, the domains and the boundary terms.

Before you start
c235-m5 : divergence · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c231-m5 : geodesics

✿01 See the idea

A stationary sensor measures a position offset X. In our fictional test setup, this offset consists entirely of centered Gaussian noise with standard deviation σ = 2 mm. We apply a function φ to the reading: here, its cube, which preserves the sign of the offset. We want the mean of the slopes of this function at the positions that the noise can produce.

A slope is calculated by differentiating φ. Integration by parts offers another route: keep φ without differentiating it, but multiply its value by a signed weight that depends on the noise. The mean stays the same under specific assumptions. The weight is not a probability: a negative reading gives it a negative sign.

Both routes use the same distribution. They do not give the same contribution for each reading: for the fictional reading X = 4 mm, we will obtain 48 and 64 square millimetres. Yet their theoretical means are both 12 square millimetres.

The aim is to explain this transfer using the duality between the Malliavin derivative and divergence covered in the prerequisite. We stay within a single Gaussian mode. No diffusion path, conditional representation or conclusion about the regularity of a distribution will be needed. The parameters describe an exercise, with no real reading or performance comparison.

The rule to remember

Choose a direction whose dot product with DX equals unity; check that it belongs to the domain of divergence; then transfer the derivative under the expectation.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The mean slope of a cubic sensor response

The fictional model is X = σZ, where Z has a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 1, with no units. With σ = 2 mm and φ(x) = x to the power 3, let us calculate S = E[φ′(X)] using the derivative, then using a weight. E denotes expectation under this distribution, never the mean of an isolated reading.

  1. Define the space, symbols and units

    We work on the Gaussian space generated by Z. H is the real line equipped with a direction h of norm 1. The dot product satisfies ⟨ah, bh⟩H = ab for real coefficients a and b. D is the Malliavin derivative: for a smooth function g of Z, Dg(Z) = g′(Z)h. The prime denotes the ordinary derivative. Thus DZ = h and DX = σh.

    The letter x is an argument of φ, expressed in millimetres; φ(x) is in cubic millimetres. The slope φ′(x), and hence S, are in square millimetres. The variables Z and h have no units. We assume that σ is strictly positive and known. The test functions used are smooth, and both they and their derivatives grow at most polynomially; all their relevant Gaussian moments are therefore finite.

    δ is divergence, the adjoint of D for the dot product defined by expectation. Its domain contains the square-integrable fields u for which G ↦ E[⟨DG, u⟩H] is bounded by a constant times the square root of E[G squared]. In this case, this map can be written as E[Gδ(u)]. Here G denotes a square-integrable test variable with a square-integrable derivative. This reminder specifies the domain and sign of δ.

  2. Construct the direction that removes the derivative

    The chain rule gives Dφ(X) = φ′(X)DX. We seek a field u whose dot product with DX equals 1. Since DX = σh and ⟨h, h⟩H = 1, we choose u = h/σ. Its unit is the reciprocal millimetre.

    ⟨DX,u⟩H=σσ⟨h,h⟩H=1

    The divergence of a deterministic direction h is Z; linearity with respect to deterministic coefficients gives δ(u) = Z/σ. This weight is square-integrable: E[(Z/σ) squared] = 1/4 per square millimetre. Gaussian duality and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality then provide the bound required for the domain of δ. For this constant direction, we are therefore entitled to use duality; simply writing down an arbitrary field would not establish this.

    E[φ′(X)]=E[⟨Dφ(X),u⟩H]=E[φ(X)Zσ]

    The weight also equals X divided by the square of σ. We will keep the notation Z/σ. The product φ(X)δ(u) has the correct unit: cubic millimetres multiplied by reciprocal millimetres, giving square millimetres.

  3. Check the transfer using an ordinary integral

    Let p denote the standard normal density and t its real integration variable, with no units. When we differentiate its exponential factor, whose exponent is −t squared divided by 2, the normalization factor stays constant. The derivative of the density therefore satisfies:

    p′(t)=−tp(t)

    Set b(t) = φ(σt). Its derivative is b′(t) = σφ′(σt): this is precisely why division by σ is needed. For positive R with no units, first integrate over the finite interval from −R to R:

    ∫−RRφ′(σt)p(t)dt=[b(t)p(t)]−RRσ+1σ∫−RRtb(t)p(t)dt

    The bracket notation means the value at R minus the value at −R. The positive sign comes from combining the minus sign in integration by parts with p′(t) = −tp(t). As R tends to infinity, each boundary value tends to 0: a polynomial multiplied by the Gaussian density vanishes at both ends. The integrands are absolutely integrable. Taking the limit gives exactly E[φ′(X)] = E[φ(X)Z/σ]. We have checked the sign, factor and boundaries separately.

  4. Calculate using the derivative of the cube

    The standard normal distribution is centered and has variance 1: E[Z] = 0 and E[Z squared] = 1. Its symmetry also gives E[Z to the power 3] = 0. Thus E[X squared] = σ squared multiplied by E[Z squared] = 2 × 2 × 1 = 4 square millimetres.

    S=E[3X2]=3×4=12

    The ordinary derivative of φ(x) = x to the power 3 is 3 times x squared. We can take the deterministic coefficient outside the expectation. S therefore equals 12 square millimetres; its dimension is that of a change in cubic response divided by a change in position.

  5. Recalculate without differentiating the function of the reading

    In the weighted expression, φ(σZ) = σ to the power 3 multiplied by Z to the power 3. The weight Z/σ adds a factor Z and removes a factor σ. The remaining coefficient is 8 × 1/2 = 4 square millimetres; it multiplies the moment E[Z to the power 4].

    To find this moment, apply standard integration by parts to the function t ↦ t to the power 3. The same boundary checks apply to this polynomial. This gives E[Z to the power 4] = 3 E[Z squared] = 3. This moment calculation checks the example exactly; it assumes no numerical sampling.

    E[φ(X)Zσ]=σ2E[Z4]=4×3=12

    Numerical check: 12 − 12 = 0 square millimetres. Both routes give the same expectation. The proof of the identity uses duality or integration; the agreement of these numbers checks only this application.

  6. Return to the isolated reading and check another interpretation

    For z = 2, X equals 2 × 2 = 4 mm. Its cubic response equals 4 × 4 × 4 = 64 cubic millimetres. The weight equals 2/2 = 1 per millimetre. The weighted product therefore equals 64 square millimetres. The slope at the same point equals 3 × 4 × 4 = 48 square millimetres. Their difference of 16 square millimetres does not contradict the equality of expectations.

    Another check of S: add a deterministic shift a, in millimetres, to the same sensor. Expanding the cube and using the vanishing odd moments gives E[φ(X+a)] = a to the power 3 + 3 a times σ squared. The derivative of this expression with respect to a, evaluated at a = 0, equals 3 σ squared = 12 square millimetres. This is indeed the expected mean slope. The last exercise will distinguish this derivative from a quotient for a finite shift.

    We have checked a mathematical identity in the fictional model. The signed weight establishes neither the accuracy of a real sensor nor a reduction in the spread of an estimator constructed from readings.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The weight is Z, whatever the scale of the sensor.”

    The composition b(t) = φ(σt) introduces σ when differentiated. To isolate φ′, we must divide by σ. The dot product of σh and h/σ equals 1; that of σh and h would equal σ. The unit of the weight must be the reciprocal millimetre.

    Recover the direction and scale factor

  • “I can replace the slope by the weighted value for each reading.”

    The contributions for the reading X = 4 mm equal 48 and 64 square millimetres. The identity concerns their expectations under the same Gaussian distribution. It does not promise pointwise equality.

    Compare the diagram of the reading with the diagram of the means

  • “The formula holds for any function because the noise is Gaussian.”

    This proof uses a smooth function with polynomial growth, the required integrability conditions and vanishing boundary terms. An indicator function, which jumps between 0 and 1, does not satisfy these differentiation assumptions. We cannot conclude that its ordinary derivative, which vanishes away from the jump, gives a vanishing sensitivity. An approximation argument and its passage to the limit would require an additional proof.

    Reread the assumptions and boundary terms

Hint 2

The divergence of h is Z; the reciprocal of σ is a deterministic coefficient.

Compare with the solution

We need u = h/σ, because ⟨σh, h/σ⟩H = 1. By linearity with respect to deterministic coefficients, δ(u) = Z/σ, with units of reciprocal millimetres. Its coefficient equals 1/2 per millimetre and, for z = 2, its value equals 1 per millimetre.

Exercise 2 · Application with a different function

The square on the same sensor

The response of the same sensor becomes ψ(x) = x squared, in square millimetres. Calculate E[ψ′(X)] and E[ψ(X)Z/σ]. Explain why a negative weighted value is possible even though ψ is nonnegative.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The sensor is centered and its distribution is symmetric.

Hint 2

The product X squared multiplied by Z/σ equals σ times Z to the power 3.

Compare with the solution

The derivative equals 2X. Its expectation is 2 E[X] = 0 mm, because E[X] = σE[Z] = 0 mm.

On the other side, ψ(X)Z/σ = σ times Z to the power 3. The odd moment vanishes by symmetry, so this side equals 2 × 0 = 0 mm. Both expectations agree, including their units.

A negative reading gives a negative Z and a negative weight. Multiplying the positive square by this weight gives a negative contribution. The contributions of opposite readings cancel; the weight is not a probability.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate both sides and express them in millimetres.
  • Justify the vanishing moments using Gaussian centering and symmetry.
  • Explain the sign of the contribution without assigning a negative sign to the square.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and exact check

Is a finite shift a derivative?

Return to the cubic response and the same noise. Add a = 1 mm to the reading. Calculate the change E[φ(X+a)] − E[φ(X)] exactly, then divide it by a. Must the quotient equal S? Justify your answer by expanding the cube and identify the term that disappears as a tends to 0.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Expand (X+a) to the power 3 before taking the expectation. The odd moments of X vanish.

Hint 2

The change is 3 aE[X squared] + a to the power 3. Divide each term by a.

Compare with the solution

The expansion is X to the power 3 + 3 a times X squared + 3 a squared multiplied by X + a to the power 3. Here E[X] and E[X to the power 3] equal 0 in their respective units. This leaves 3 a times σ squared + a to the power 3.

With a = 1 mm, the linear part equals 12 cubic millimetres and the cube of the shift equals 1 cubic millimetre. The shifted expectation equals 13 cubic millimetres, compared with 0 before the shift. The change equals 13 cubic millimetres.

After division by 1 mm, the quotient equals 13 square millimetres. It exceeds S = 12 square millimetres by 1 square millimetre. For any nonzero a, the quotient is 3 σ squared + a squared. The term a squared tends to 0: only the limit of the quotient gives S. The integration by parts weight calculates this derivative at 0 shift, not every finite change.

Check your reasoning

  • Expand the cube and justify the terms with vanishing expectation.
  • Distinguish the units of the change from those of the quotient.
  • Identify the remainder a squared and the limit that defines the derivative.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What dot product condition must u satisfy to transfer φ′(X)?

We need ⟨DX, u⟩H = 1, with u in the domain of δ and φ(X) in the domain of D. The chain rule and duality then give E[φ′(X)] = E[φ(X)δ(u)].

Recall 2For X = σZ, why is the weight Z/σ?

DX = σh and ⟨h, h⟩H = 1. Choose u = h/σ. The coefficient is deterministic and δ(h) = Z, so δ(u) = Z/σ. The unit of the weight is the reciprocal of the unit of X.

Recall 3What checks allow the calculation using the density?

The chain rule introduces σ; p′(t) = −tp(t) determines the sign; the product φ(σt)p(t) must vanish at both infinities and the integrands must be integrable. The smoothness and polynomial growth assumptions on this page guarantee these conditions.

Recall 4Must the two contributions coincide for each reading?

No. For the reading X = 4 mm, they equal 48 and 64 square millimetres. The identity concerns their expectations, which equal 12 square millimetres under the model.

Recall 5Why does a quotient for a finite shift not necessarily give S?

For the cube, the quotient equals 3 σ squared + a squared. S is its limit as a tends to 0, namely 3 σ squared. The term a squared remains for a finite shift.
