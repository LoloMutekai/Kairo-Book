Level 1 · C037·M1

Tables

Your goalBuild and read a table of weighing records while keeping each crate linked to its readings and their units.

Before you start
c036-m5 : annotated graphs · c035-m5 : finite covariance and conditioning

✿01 See the idea

At an orchard’s receiving area, each crate of apples arrives with a weighing ticket. For crate B, the ticket shows 900 g including the packaging and 200 g for the empty packaging. Copying all the masses into a long list would lose a crucial piece of information: which mass belongs to which crate?

A data table keeps these links. Here, a row describes a crate received; a column always describes the same property. The Crate column names the object, Gross gives its mass including the packaging, and Tare gives the mass of the empty packaging. A cell is the value at the intersection of a row and a column. A field is a named property; all the fields in a row form a record.

Our scene and all its values are fictional. The tickets show: A, gross 1250 g, tare 250 g; B, gross 900 g, tare 200 g; C, gross 1500 g, tare 300 g. Each crate is weighed only once in this dataset. We treat the recorded masses as exact for the exercise, without studying errors in the scale.

The letters A, B and C are identifiers: labels that unambiguously identify the crates in this dataset. B appears in position 2, but its position can change. If you move its entire row, its readings must move with it. Identity comes from the label, rather than its height on the page.

The rule to remember

A row keeps together everything about the same crate; a column keeps the same meaning, value type and unit.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the table to the contents of crate B

What is the mass of the apples in crate B? Let us build a dataset that matches the tickets, select the relevant cells, then calculate the net mass in grams and kilograms. Here, net mass means the apples alone.

  1. State what a row represents

    The basic observation is a crate when it is received. There are 3 crates, so there are 3 data rows. A row represents neither a gram nor a column. The header describes the fields: it does not count as an additional crate.

    We choose 3 fields: caisse, brute_g and tare_g. The names brute_g and tare_g make the unit, grams, explicit. A table’s schema describes its columns and the expected rules, independently of their current values. Here, caisse contains nonempty text; brute_g and tare_g contain nonnegative integers in grams. The identifier caisse is unique in this dataset.

    The table contains 3 × 3 = 9 data cells. This count includes the labels A, B and C; it does not mean that all cells are numbers. In each row, the gross mass must be at least as large as the tare. These rules help detect inconsistencies; they do not replace the source ticket.

  2. Find a value by its meaning

    Find the row whose caisse field is B, then the brute_g column: we read 900 g. In that same row, the tare_g column gives 200 g. These values both belong to B. Reading a column from A and a column from B would produce a calculation that does not refer to a single, consistent object.

    An empty cell here would mean “mass not recorded”. It would not mean 0 g. Our table is complete; if B’s tare were missing, we would leave its net mass undetermined and return to the ticket, without inventing a mass to make subtraction possible.

  3. Define the calculation for each row

    For any crate in the dataset, i denotes its identifier, A, B or C; it has no unit. Mᵢ denotes its gross mass, Tᵢ its tare and Nᵢ its net mass, all in grams. The small i written below is a subscript: it specifies which crate we mean. Thus, N with subscript B denotes the net mass of crate B.

    We assume that the gross weighing includes exactly the apples and the packaging whose tare is known, with no change of packaging or loss between readings. The gross mass then consists of the mass of the apples and the mass of the packaging: Mᵢ = Nᵢ + Tᵢ. Subtracting the tare gives the net mass.

    Ni=Mi−Ti

    This calculation uses fields from the same row. If we added a net_g column, it would contain a derived value, meaning a value calculated from the readings rather than a new weighing. The schema would gain this column; each result would have to retain its crate’s identifier.

  4. Subtract, then express the result

    For B, the masses use the same unit. Subtract the 200 g of packaging from the 900 g weighed:

    NB=900−200=700 g

    Since 1 kg contains 1000 g, we divide the number of grams by 1000 to read the same mass in kilograms. The exact result is 7/10 kg, or 0.70 kg. The comma is part of French decimal notation; it does not change the mass. The original cells remain in grams: writing this result in a column named brute_g would change both its meaning and its unit.

  5. Check the calculation and its link to the crate

    Arithmetic check: the contents and packaging must reproduce B’s ticket. We obtain 700 + 200 = 900 g, equal to its gross mass. The net mass is positive and does not exceed 900 g, as expected under our assumptions.

    Association check: also reread the identifier B and its values on the ticket. The inverse addition checks the subtraction, but on its own it would not detect a mass copied from another crate. Finally, displaying the rows in the order B, C, A preserves B’s 900 g, 200 g and therefore 700 g of apples. We have changed the reading order, rather than the records.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “B is simply the row in position 2.”

    That position describes only the display A, B, C. In B, C, A, B comes in position 1. Find the label B and move its entire row: its masses remain 900 g and 200 g.

    Review moving the entire row

  • “The tare is blank, so I can enter 0.”

    Missing information does not measure packaging with no mass. Leave the tare missing and the net mass undetermined, then consult the ticket or ask for a reading. Here, numerical subtraction requires two known masses in the same unit.

    Review choosing cells and their units

  • “If the types are right and the subtraction is correct, the table is correct.”

    Integers in grams can belong to the wrong crates. Type rules and the inverse calculation do not prove the association with the correct ticket. Also check the identifier and that each record stays intact.

    Examine what happens when a column moves on its own

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis with reasons

A column moved on its own

The identifiers remain A, B, C and the tares remain 250, 200, 300 g. Someone reorders only brute_g, which now displays 900, 1250, 1500 g. They then calculate a net mass of 1050 g for the row labelled B. The inverse addition gives the value in the new brute_g cell. Can this result be accepted? Identify the broken link and propose a repair based on the tickets.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare the mass now placed beside B with the mass on its original ticket.

Hint 2

A calculation can be consistent with a cell that has been linked to the wrong crate.

Compare with the solution

The row labelled B now contains a gross mass of 1250 g, which belonged to A, and a tare of 200 g, which belongs to B. The subtraction 1250 − 200 = 1050 g is arithmetically correct, but it combines readings from different crates.

Its check gives 1050 + 200 = 1250 g. It reproduces the moved cell, rather than B’s ticket. That ticket showed 900 g: the difference is 1250 − 900 = 350 g. The values are still positive integers and the gross masses remain greater than the tares, so these checks are insufficient.

Restore the associations from the tickets: A with 1250 g and 250 g, B with 900 g and 200 g, C with 1500 g and 300 g. B’s net mass returns to 700 g. To change the display afterwards, move complete rows. Without tickets or another reliable record, you would have to report that the association is uncertain, rather than guess the lost order.

Check your reasoning

  • Show which reading from A was associated with B’s tare.
  • Explain why the inverse check can succeed despite this broken link.
  • Repair the table using the tickets and obtain 700 g for B.
  • Propose moving entire records and recognise the limitation when no reliable source is available.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In the crate dataset, what do a row, a column and a cell represent?

A row describes a crate received; a column always describes the same field; a cell gives the value of that field for that crate. The header names the fields and their units.

Recall 2What must be specified before filling in our table?

What a row represents, the expected fields, their types and units, the unique identifier for the dataset and the consistency rules. Here: caisse is nonempty text; brute_g and tare_g are nonnegative integers in grams; gross mass is at least as large as tare.

Recall 3Why look for B by its identifier rather than its position?

The display can reorder the rows. The label B continues to identify the same crate; its readings must move with it. Its position does not define its identity.

Recall 4If a tare is not recorded, can we calculate the net mass by using 0 g?

No. Missing information is not a mass measured as 0. The net mass remains undetermined until a reliable tare is obtained.

Recall 5What does the inverse addition of net mass and tare prove, and what still needs to be checked?

It checks that the subtraction reproduces the gross mass used. We still need to check the source to establish that gross mass and tare belong to the identified crate; moving a column on its own can preserve the arithmetic while breaking this link.
