Level 2 · C101·M1

AR and MA

Your goalDistinguish memory of observations from memory of innovations, then calculate forecasts from AR and MA models with a single lag.

Before you start
c090-m5 : stationarity · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional workshop, a thermometer provides a temperature every 1 hour. The assumed mean baseline is 20 °C; the latest reading is 22 °C. Should we retain part of the deviation from the baseline, or part of what has just surprised us? These questions lead to two different kinds of memory.

The autoregressive model, abbreviated AR, carries forward part of the latest centered observation: the difference between the temperature reading and the baseline. A new surprise changes the observation; that observation in turn carries forward part of its deviation. The effect can therefore continue through successive steps.

The moving average model, abbreviated MA, carries forward part of a past surprise, called an innovation. This surprise is the difference between what happens and what the model forecast just beforehand. A temperature above the baseline is not necessarily a surprise if this warming was already expected.

We compare the versions with a single lag, AR(1) and MA(1). Their coefficients are given, both equal to 0.50, with no units. The temperatures, coefficients and innovations are fictional. Here we are neither choosing the best model for the workshop nor estimating its parameters.

The rule to remember

First ask what is carried forward: an observed deviation from the baseline in AR, an innovation in MA. Forecasting future noise with a mean of 0 does not mean that this noise will be 0.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The same reading, two kinds of memory

At time t, the workshop thermometer shows yₜ = 22 °C. We set μ = 20 °C, φ = 1/2 and θ = 1/2. For MA, the previous innovation ε at time t − 1 is supplied: 2 °C. Let us calculate what each model forecasts for the next reading.

  1. Define the baseline, time and noise

    t is an hour index with no units; t − 1 denotes the previous reading and t + 1 the next one. yt is the observed temperature, in °C. μ, the letter mu, is the assumed constant mean of the model, in °C; it is given, rather than estimated from these few readings. Set xt = yt − μ: xt is a temperature deviation, also in °C.

    ε, the letter epsilon, denotes the new noise term in the model at each time. We assume that these noise terms have the same distribution, are independent from one hour to the next and of the initial state, and have mean 0 °C and a finite constant variance. No normal distribution is needed for the mean forecasts calculated here. The noise sequences belonging to the AR and MA models are not assumed to be identical.

    A stationary series in the sense used here maintains a constant mean and variance, with covariances depending only on the time lag. For AR, we will use the solution constructed from past noise terms with |φ| < 1, which makes this memory stable. The notation | | indicates absolute value; the fictional thermometer does not prove this assumption.

    A hat indicates a mean forecast. In the subscript “t + 1 | t”, the left part names the target time; the bar means “with the information available up to t”. The forecast value and the temperature that will occur are distinct.

  2. AR: start from the latest centered observation

    φ, the letter phi, is the autoregressive coefficient, with no units. The AR(1) model multiplies the latest observed deviation by φ, then adds a new noise term. The single lag refers to the equation; through successive steps, a surprise can still influence more distant hours.

    xt=φxt−1+εt

    At the next hour, the mean of the still unknown noise term is 0. The forecast is therefore the baseline plus the part carried forward from xt.

    y^t+1|t=μ+φ(yt−μ)

    First subtract the baseline: 22 − 20 = 2 °C. Then carry forward: 0.50 × 2 = 1 °C. Finally add the baseline back: 20 + 1 = 21 °C. Each term added is indeed a temperature or a deviation in °C.

  3. MA: recover the surprise before carrying it forward

    θ, the letter theta, is the coefficient controlling memory of noise terms, with no units. With the plus sign convention chosen here, MA(1) is written as:

    xt=εt+θεt−1

    The forecast made before the current reading used the supplied previous innovation: θ × previous ε = 0.50 × 2 = 1 °C. It was therefore 20 + 1 = 21 °C.

    The current innovation is what must be added to this forecast to recover the reading: εt = 22 − 21 = 1 °C. Check: 21 + 1 = 22 °C. This is not the deviation from the baseline, which is 2 °C.

    In this exercise, the initial state and model parameters are given, so this innovation can be calculated exactly. When fitting data, the noise terms are not observed directly; their reconstruction depends on the parameters and initialization. A residual calculated with estimated parameters does not prove that the true generating noise term is known.

  4. Forecast with the appropriate memory, then compare

    In the MA equation at the next hour, εt is now known in our supplied state, while the next noise term remains unknown and has a mean of 0. The remaining contribution is therefore θ εt:

    y^t+1|t=μ+θεt

    Carry forward: 1/2 × 1 = 1/2 °C, or 0.50 °C. Add the baseline: 20 + 1/2 = 41/2 °C, or 20.50 °C.

    AR gives 21 °C; MA gives 20.50 °C. Their difference is 1/2 °C, or 0.50 °C. The equal coefficients multiply different inputs: 2 °C of observed deviation versus 1 °C of surprise. Without the next observation, these forecasts do not allow us to declare a winner.

  5. Check AR by returning to the raw temperature

    Expand μ + φ(yt − μ): this gives φ yt + (1 − φ)μ. Call the additive constant c, in °C. For this stationary AR model centered around μ, c and μ are related; they do not denote the same quantity.

    c=(1−φ)μ=10 °C

    Check using another form: c + φ yt = 10 + 0.50 × 22 = 10 + 11 = 21 °C. This gives exactly the centered forecast. Adding μ directly to φ yt would count part of the baseline twice.

    Check the assumptions: |φ| = 0.50 < 1. This is consistent with the stable AR solution described above. MA, a finite sum of noise terms with the same distribution and finite variance, is stationary without requiring this condition on θ. Recovering its noise terms stably from observations is a separate question: for MA(1), |θ| < 1 ensures that the effect of an initialization error in this reconstruction diminishes. Our θ satisfies this condition; we have also supplied the state needed for the exact calculation.

  6. Explain what order means, then stop at the mechanism

    In AR(p), p is the number of observation lags included; in MA(q), q is the number of noise lags included, in addition to the current noise term. p and q are integers with no units; lags are counted in sampling steps. We have covered p = q = 1. The word “average” in MA requires neither division nor weights that sum to 1.

    To advance further without a new reading, AR reuses its forecast in place of the still unknown observation. MA uses the mean of 0 for the still future innovation. These rules give the forecasts at 2 steps in the exercise; they do not assert that the actual paths will follow these means.

    The coefficients and mean have remained fixed. Their estimation, the identification of an order and residual diagnostics belong to the rest of the chapter. An exact calculation in this scene measures no forecasting performance in the real workshop.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations and reconstruct both kinds of memory. Why does 22 °C become a deviation of 2 °C in AR, but a surprise of 1 °C in MA? Also explain why no realized future noise term has been set to 0.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “MA is the average of the latest temperatures.”

    A rolling average of observations smooths the series. The MA model describes a weighted sum of present and past noise terms. Identify what θ multiplies before using the same expression “moving average”.

    Compare smoothing with the model in the first exercise

  • “The innovation is always the reading minus the mean μ.”

    Subtract the forecast that was available just before this reading. Here, part of the warming was already forecast. Recover the innovation, then add it to that forecast to check the reconstructed reading.

    Return to the step “MA: recover the surprise”

  • “Forecasting future noise as 0 means that nothing will happen.”

    The mean of future surprises is 0 under our assumptions. Their realization can be positive or negative. At the next reading, recalculate the MA innovation and the AR deviation; stop using an outdated forecast as though no reading had arrived.

    Incorporate the new reading in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Recognize and justify

Is the name enough?

In the same workshop, a colleague proposes smoothing the readings 20 °C and 22 °C using their arithmetic mean. Calculate it. Is this the calculation made by the MA(1) model on this page? Name the inputs used by each.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

An arithmetic mean adds the readings and then divides by their number. The model on this page starts from the innovation.

Compare with the solution

The smoothed value is (20 + 22)/2 = 42/2 = 21 °C. It combines observations directly, without reconstructing their surprise.

The MA model calculates μ + θ εt = 20.50 °C. Its inputs are the baseline, the coefficient θ and the current innovation. Smoothing and the model give different results here. The smoothed value coincides with the AR forecast in the example, but this numerical equality does not change its definition.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain the mean in °C and show the division.
  • Distinguish observations from innovations, without relying only on the name “moving average”.

Exercise 2 · Propagate without an observation

The next hour is still unknown

Stay at time t, without receiving a new reading. Forecast the temperature at t + 2 with each model. For AR, start from the forecast 21 °C; for MA, distinguish the known current innovation from the noise term that will arrive at t + 1.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For AR, center the forecast from the first step, multiply by φ again, then add μ.

Hint 2

At t + 2, the MA equation uses the noise terms at t + 2 and t + 1. At time t, both have a mean of 0.

Compare with the solution

AR: the forecast deviation at the first step is 21 − 20 = 1 °C. At the next step, 1/2 × 1 = 1/2 °C. The forecast is 20 + 1/2 = 41/2 °C, or 20.50 °C.

y^t+2|t=μ+φ2(yt−μ)

Check: φ squared is 1/4; 1/4 × 2 = 1/2 °C. Carrying the deviation forward in AR prolongs it while reducing it with this positive coefficient less than 1.

MA: the known innovation εt no longer appears directly at t + 2 in this model with a single lag. Both noise terms are still in the future and have a mean of 0, so the forecast is 20 °C. The actual temperature can differ from it; the model has not eliminated the noise terms. The equality between this AR forecast at 2 steps and the MA forecast at 1 step is specific to the chosen data.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the same forecast origin t: no future reading is available.
  • Recover the square of φ by carrying the deviation forward twice in succession.
  • Justify why the MA forecast returns to μ without claiming that the temperature is certain.

Exercise 3 · Revise with an observation

The thermometer differs from the forecast

This time, the hour actually advances and the reading at t + 1 is 19 °C. The coefficients remain fixed. Calculate the MA innovation for this reading, then the AR and MA forecasts at t + 2, with this new information. Compare them with those in the second exercise.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For MA, subtract the forecast 20.50 °C made before the new reading. For AR, subtract the baseline μ from this new reading.

Hint 2

The MA surprise is negative. Multiply it by θ before adding the baseline back.

Compare with the solution

MA had forecast 20.50 °C. The innovation observed in our supplied state is 19 − 20.50 = -3/2 °C, or -1.50 °C. Its sign indicates a colder reading than forecast.

The new part carried forward is 1/2 × (-3/2) = -3/4 °C. Adding the baseline gives 77/4 °C, or 19.25 °C. Check: the earlier forecast plus the new innovation gives 19 °C again.

AR uses the new observation: 19 − 20 = -1 °C; 1/2 × (-1) = -1/2 °C; 20 + (-1/2) = 39/2 °C, or 19.50 °C.

The results differ from the forecasts made without a new reading: AR forecast 20.50 °C and MA 20 °C from t. Here, the forecast origin has become t + 1. The additional information changes the forecast; it does not retroactively alter the one that was available before.

Check your reasoning

  • Subtract the correct MA forecast and keep the negative sign.
  • Start from the new observation for AR.
  • Give both temperatures in °C and state the change in the available information.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What memory does AR(1) use, and why can a shock last longer than a single lag?

AR carries forward φ times the latest centered observation. The new observation in turn carries forward part of the deviation: these successive steps prolong the effect of the shock.

Recall 2What memory does MA(1) use, and how can its current surprise be recovered?

MA carries θ times the current innovation forward to the next step. With the given parameters and past state, this innovation is the reading minus the forecast made just beforehand; it is not simply the reading minus μ.

Recall 3How are the baseline μ and the additive constant c of a stable AR(1) related?

Expanding μ + φ(y − μ) gives φy + c, with c = (1 − φ)μ. The coefficient has no units; μ and c are in °C in the workshop. The condition |φ| < 1 applies to the stable AR solution chosen.

Recall 4From the same forecast origin, what happens to the MA(1) memory at 2 steps?

The current innovation no longer appears in the equation at this horizon. The noise terms that appear there are still in the future and have a mean of 0: the forecast returns to μ. Their realization remains uncertain.

Recall 5What do these exact calculations and an answer obtained with a hint fail to prove?

The calculations prove neither that the models are appropriate for the real workshop nor that their forecasting performance is superior. An assisted answer does not demonstrate independent recall: reconstructing the mechanisms and their assumptions without support on another day is still necessary.
