Level 3 · C183·M4

Crank-Nicolson

Your goalConstruct a Crank-Nicolson step, handle its boundaries and check the resulting prices by substitution.

Before you start
c167-m5 : initial-boundary value problem · c051-m5 : error diagnostics · c175-m5 : model limitations · c050-m5 : conditioning · c183-m1 : grid · c183-m2 : explicit scheme · c183-m3 : implicit scheme

✿01 See the idea

On graph paper, we have plotted the value of a fictional contract against the price of its underlying asset. At expiry, the curve of values is known. As we work back to an earlier date, diffusion smooths its corners: the local curvature indicates how each value changes.

The explicit scheme evaluates the curvature at the start of the step; the implicit scheme evaluates it at the end. Crank-Nicolson gives equal weight to these two curvatures. Since the curvature at the end contains prices that are still unknown, we must find them together. We average the predicted changes, rather than the final solutions from the other two methods.

In the diagram, the endpoints have prescribed values. The two interior boxes become u and v. A new interior value depends on its neighbour at the new time: we cannot fill them in one after another by arbitrarily reusing the last value calculated.

All values are fictional. We retain only diffusion with a constant coefficient, with no transport or discounting. The boundaries are chosen for the exercise; the result will be a solution of this discrete problem, with no claim to represent a market price.

The rule to remember

Average the curvatures at the start and end, move the known boundary terms to the right-hand side, then solve for all unknowns at the new time level together.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A step backwards from expiry

The strike price is K = 100 €. The grid runs from 90 € to 120 €, with h = 10 €. At expiry, the contract pays the greater of x − K and 0 €. We will calculate the values at positions 100 € and 110 € after a step Δτ = 1 year.

  1. Define the problem before choosing the scheme

    x is the price of the underlying asset and V(x, τ) the contract value, both in euros. τ is the time remaining until expiry, in years: τ = 0 at expiry; increasing τ means moving backwards through calendar time. Our simplified model imposes the equation below. ∂ denotes a partial derivative, with the other variables held fixed.

    ∂V∂τ=a∂2V∂x2

    a = 50 square euros per year is constant and positive. The second spatial derivative measures curvature, in inverse euros; multiplying it by a gives euros per year, as for the time derivative. We are not applying the full Black-Scholes-Merton PDE here.

    At the initial time level, the values are (0, 0, 10, 20) €. At both times of the step, we impose V(90, τ) = 0 € and V(120, τ) = 20 €. These boundary conditions are choices for the truncated problem. They do not follow from the payoff at expiry alone.

  2. Put the average in the right place

    Uᵢⁿ approximates V at node i and time level n. i and n are dimensionless indices; i ± 1 denotes a neighbour, n + 1 the next time level. h is the spatial spacing in euros, Δτ the duration of a step in years. Define Dᵢⁿ as the curvature approximated by a centred difference, in inverse euros.

    Din=Ui−1n−2Uin+Ui+1nh2
    Uin+1−UinΔτ=a2(Din+Din+1)

    The trapezoidal rule in time approximates the evolution over the step using the average of the slopes at its endpoints. Here these slopes are aD. The new D depends on the new U: the step remains implicit.

  3. Gather the unknowns on the left

    Define μ, a dimensionless number, by dividing aΔτ by 2 times the square of h:

    μ=aΔτ2h2=50200=1/4

    Using the data: h squared = 100 square euros and aΔτ = 50 square euros; the units cancel in μ. Collecting the terms at the new time level gives:

    −μUi−1n+1+(1+2μ)Uin+1−μUi+1n+1=μUi−1n+(1−2μ)Uin+μUi+1n

    On the left, the diagonal entry is 1 + 1/2 = 3/2; the neighbours have coefficients −1/4. On the right, the central weight is 1 − 1/2 = 1/2. Each row involves only the node and its neighbours: on a large grid, the matrix is tridiagonal.

  4. Include the boundaries at both times

    Call the new value at x = 100 € u and the one at x = 110 € v. For the row for u, only the old neighbour on the right contributes a nonzero value: 1/4 × 10 = 5/2 €. The old and new left boundary values are both 0 €.

    For the row for v, the old central value contributes 1/2 × 10 = 5 €. The old boundary contributes μ × 20 €. The new boundary initially appears on the left with a minus sign; moving it to the right adds another μ × 20 €. Together: μ × (20 + 20) = 1/4 × 40 = 10 €.

    The right-hand side for the right interior node is therefore 5 + 10 = 15 €. Even when the boundary is constant, its contributions at both times are needed.

  5. Read and solve the coupled system

    The column on the right is in euros, the matrix is dimensionless and the column of unknowns contains u and v in euros. The two rows represent the two simultaneous equations:

    [3/2−1/4−1/43/2][uv]=[5/215]

    Multiply both equations by 4 to clear the denominators: 6u − v = 10 € and −u + 6v = 60 €. The first gives v = 6u − 10 €. Substitute this expression into the second:

    −u+6(6u−10 €)=60 €

  6. Finish with exact fractions

    Expanding gives: (36 − 1)u − 60 € = 60 €, so 35u = 120 €. Dividing by 35 gives u = 120/35 € = 24/7 €, approximately 3.4286 €.

    Return to the first equation: v = 6 × (24/7 €) − 10 € = 144/7 € − 10 € = 74/7 €, approximately 10.5714 €. Fractions are retained throughout the calculation; decimals are used only to display the result.

    For a large grid, we solve the tridiagonal system with a suitable solver; there is no need to form the inverse of its matrix. Elimination in our small system shows the mechanism without introducing the general algorithm.

  7. Check what has actually been obtained

    Substituting into the scaled equations: 6 × (24/7) − (74/7) = 10 and −(24/7) + 6 × (74/7) = 60. The residuals, obtained by subtracting each right-hand side, are therefore exactly 0 €. Here both new values stay between the boundaries 0 € and 20 €.

    We can also check the equation before collecting terms: for u, u − 0 € must equal μ times the sum of the spatial differences (0 − 2 × 0 + 10) € and (0 € − 2u + v). For v, use (0 − 2 × 10 + 20) € and (u − 2v + 20 €). This check recovers the average of the two curvatures, as well as checking the rearranged system.

    For a sufficiently smooth solution, the scheme has order 2 in time and space: the discretization error is of the order of the sum of Δτ squared and h squared, with fixed scales. Here the payoff has a corner at K; this smoothness is not assured at the start. A correctly solved step does not demonstrate convergence to an exact price. The next unit will examine convergence tests.

    For this linear diffusion problem with prescribed boundaries, time stability requires no bound on the step size. It guarantees neither freedom from oscillations nor accuracy with a large step. The final exercise separates these properties on the same grid.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I calculate an explicit step and an implicit step, then average their results.”

    The average applies to the two curvatures in a single step equation. The value at the end enters the curvature at the end. Write this equation before solving; averaging two separate solutions generally defines a different method.

    Review the average of the curvatures and the collection of terms

  • “The boundary is constant, so I add it only once.”

    A constant boundary enters each of the two curvatures. The new boundary moves from the left-hand side to the right-hand side; the old boundary was already there. Reconstruct the contribution of μ times their sum.

    Recover the boundary contributions at both times

  • “Stable means the price can no longer oscillate.”

    A perturbation can decrease in amplitude while changing sign at each step. Calculate the factor in the transfer exercise, then distinguish its sign from its absolute value.

    Test the alternating perturbation on the same grid

Exercise 3 · Transfer to the same grid

Decay while changing sign

Compare two calculations with the same boundaries. Their difference is 0 at the boundaries; suppose it is (ε, −ε) at the interior nodes, where ε is a small amplitude in euros. Only the step changes to Δτ = 4 years for this fictional test. The spatial operator multiplies this perturbation by λ = −3a/(h squared), in inverse years. With q = −λΔτ, the Crank-Nicolson amplitude factor is g = (1 − q/2)/(1 + q/2). Calculate q and g. Deduce whether the difference grows and whether its sign is preserved.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At the first node, the centred difference gives 0 − 2ε − ε = −3ε. At the second, it gives the opposite sign: the alternating profile keeps its shape.

Hint 2

First calculate q = 3 × 50 × 4/100. Then compare the absolute value of g with 1, and examine its sign.

Compare with the solution

The positive rate −λ is 150/100 = 3/2 per year. Thus q = 3/2 × 4 = 6, dimensionless. q/2 = 3, giving g = (1 − 3)/(1 + 3) = -2/4 = -1/2.

To recover the supplied formula, call the next amplitude ε′ and apply the time average: (ε′ − ε)/Δτ = λ(ε + ε′)/2. Collecting the terms in ε′ gives (1 + q/2)ε′ = (1 − q/2)ε. Thus ε′ = gε.

The absolute value of the factor is 1/2: the amplitude is divided by 2 at each step, but the sign reverses. The difference decays while oscillating. This is a perturbation between calculations, rather than a claim that the total prices become negative.

For positive q, the absolute value of the numerator does not exceed the denominator: the factor stays bounded. However, when q exceeds 2, it becomes negative. This illustrates precisely why stability and freedom from oscillations are separate requirements.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover the factor −3 for the alternating profile with 0 boundaries.
  • Obtain q = 6 and g = -1/2, both dimensionless.
  • Explain the decay in amplitude and the alternation of sign separately.
  • Distinguish the difference between solutions from the total contract price.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What exactly does Crank-Nicolson average?

The slopes of the evolution at the endpoints of the step, here the two curvatures multiplied by a, each with a weight of 1/2. The future curvature depends on the new values; we solve a single implicit equation.

Recall 2Why must we solve a system rather than fill in the new nodes separately?

The curvature at a node at the end also uses its neighbours’ values at the end. The unknowns therefore share equations. On our grid, u and v must satisfy both rows simultaneously.

Recall 3How does a prescribed boundary enter the right-hand side?

The old boundary is already on the known side; the new boundary leaves the side containing the unknowns and changes sign. Here their contribution is μ times the sum of the two boundary values.

Recall 4What does a 0 residual in the step equations prove?

The calculated pair satisfies these discrete equations. This demonstrates neither the validity of the continuous model nor the accuracy of the grid, and it does not establish that convergence has been checked.

Recall 5Why is a negative amplitude factor with absolute value less than 1 compatible with stability?

The perturbation decreases in size but changes sign at each step. Stability bounds its growth; it does not guarantee evolution without oscillations.
