Injection and surjection

Your goalDistinguish injection and surjection by counting preimages, then justify a verdict or refute it with a specific witness.

Before you start
c014-m5 : invariants · c012-m5 : counterexamples · c006-m5 : composition · c069-m1 : equivalence relations · c069-m2 : orders

✿01 See the idea

In the cloakroom at a workshop, Alix is assigned the Pine locker, Basil the Willow locker, Chloe the Willow locker and Doria the Beech locker. The names and assignments are fictional. This is a list to examine: it may assign the same locker to several people, even if sharing causes a practical problem.

Ask the following questions separately. Do distinct people end up at the same locker? Is any locker left without a person? In our list, Basil and Chloe share a locker, but every declared locker is reached. Avoiding collisions and covering the codomain are different requirements.

An assignment is injective if distinct people always receive distinct lockers. It is surjective if every locker in the declared codomain is assigned to at least 1 person. An unused locker prevents surjectivity; a shared locker prevents injectivity.

The first diagram shows the original list. The second adds Elm and moves Chloe there: the other assignments remain those of the same cloakroom. Look at the arrows before reading the counts; they point from people to lockers.

The rule to remember

Injection: at most 1 preimage for each element of the codomain. Surjection: at least 1 preimage for each element of the codomain. Always state the domain and codomain.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Examine the cloakroom list, then repair it

The original list assigns Alix to Pine, Basil to Willow, Chloe to Willow and Doria to Beech. Establish separately whether it is injective and whether it is surjective. Then try to satisfy both properties together by adding Elm.

  1. Specify the function and the sets

    Let A = {Alix, Basil, Chloe, Doria} and B = {Pine, Willow, Beech}. The notation f : A → B specifies a function from A to B: each person has exactly 1 image in B, their assigned locker. The fact that several people may have the same image does not prevent f from being a function.

    A preimage of locker b is a person a in A such that f(a) = b. The image f(A) is the set of lockers actually reached. It may be smaller than B, the declared codomain. The letters a and b denote objects; the locker labels are names.

    The sets here are finite and the list is complete. No person is left without an assignment. The notation |A| counts the elements of A: |A| = 4 people and |B| = 3 lockers. These cardinalities are counts, with no physical unit.

  2. Count the preimages, locker by locker

    Write n(b) for the number of people sent to locker b. Counting incoming arrows avoids confusing having a locker with having it to yourself. Pine receives Alix, Willow receives Basil and Chloe, and Beech receives Doria. The unchanged formula table below uses the French locker labels in this same order.

    bPinSauleHêtren(b)121

    Check the total: 1 + 2 = 3, then 3 + 1 = 4 people. This recovers |A|: each person is counted in their locker exactly 1 time.

  3. Test injectivity with a collision witness

    To be injective, f must give different images to all distinct inputs. An equivalent formulation starts with equal images and requires the inputs to be the same object. The symbol ∀ means “for all”; a′ is another arbitrary person in A, ∈ means “belongs to”, ⇒ means “implies” and ⇔ means “is equivalent to”.

    f injective⇔∀a,a′∈A,f(a)=f(a′)⇒a=a′

    Here, f(Basil) = f(Chloe) = Willow, while Basil ≠ Chloe; ≠ means “is different from”. This witness is enough to refute injectivity. In the table, n(Willow) = 2 > 1 reveals the same collision. Injectivity would require n(b) ≤ 1 for every b in B, where ≤ means “is less than or equal to”. A locker with no preimage would be allowed.

  4. Prove surjectivity by covering the codomain

    Surjectivity requires finding an input for every declared element of the codomain. The symbol ∃ means “there exists”. The order matters: for each locker b, we can choose a person a who depends on that locker.

    f surjective⇔∀b∈B,∃a∈A,f(a)=b

    For Pine, choose Alix; for Willow, choose Basil; for Beech, choose Doria. This list covers all of B and proves surjectivity. Chloe does not need to be chosen in this proof. Surjectivity allows several preimages.

    Each codomain entry has n(b) ≥ 1, where ≥ means “is greater than or equal to”. Counting the lockers reached gives 1 + 1 = 2, then 2 + 1 = 3 lockers. The number of unused lockers is therefore 3 − 3 = 0 lockers. Thus f(A) = B.

  5. Cross-check the counts and their limits

    The 4 people reach only 3 distinct lockers. The surplus 4 − 3 = 1 counts the people beyond the first in each occupied locker. Here it equals n(Willow) − 1. In general, this surplus is not the number of pairs of people involved in collisions.

    For these finite sets, an injection requires |A| ≤ |B|: people sent to distinct lockers occupy as many lockers as there are people. Here 4 > 3 therefore makes injectivity impossible while A and B stay the same, whatever the list.

    A surjection requires |A| ≥ |B|: covering every locker requires at least as many people. These inequalities are necessary conditions; on their own, they do not certify that a particular list has the property. The decisive check is still the preimage count for each element of the codomain.

  6. Repair the assignment and check both properties together

    Add Elm to the codomain: B′ = {Pine, Willow, Beech, Elm}. The prime distinguishes this new set; |B′| = 3 + 1 = 4 lockers. Define g : A → B′ by moving Chloe to Elm and keeping the other assignments.

    Willow goes from 2 to 2 − 1 = 1 person. Elm goes from 0 to 0 + 1 = 1 person. Pine and Beech each keep 1 person. There are now 3 + 1 = 4 lockers reached.

    Each locker has exactly 1 preimage: g is injective and surjective. We then say it is bijective. Independent check: the images Pine, Willow, Elm and Beech are distinct, and their list is all of B′. The number of unused lockers is 4 − 4 = 0, and the surplus of people is 4 − 4 = 0.

    A bijection combines the requirements “at most 1” and “at least 1”. Equal cardinalities |A| = |B′| would not replace these checks: the exercise with Elm left unused shows a failure despite this equality.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Each person has a locker, so the assignment is injective.”

    Having exactly one image per person defines a function. For injectivity, compare the images of distinct people: Basil and Chloe share Willow in the original list. Revisit the witness and the injectivity formula.

    Find the injectivity test in the example

  • “A surjection forbids shared lockers.”

    Surjectivity requires at least 1 preimage per locker; it imposes no upper bound. The original diagram covers Pine, Willow and Beech even though Willow is shared. State the question about collisions and the question about unused lockers separately.

    Review the original diagram and the separate questions

  • “Having as many people as lockers guarantees a bijection.”

    The cardinality does not determine the arrows. Add Elm without changing the list: Willow remains shared and Elm remains unused. The corresponding exercise asks you to name the witnesses to these failures.

    Examine Elm left unused in the exercises

Exercise 2 · Explained counterexample

Elm is added but stays unused

Use all the people in A and all the arrows of f, including Chloe to Willow. Change only the declared codomain to B′ = {Pine, Willow, Beech, Elm}. Are the cardinalities of the domain and codomain equal? Is this function injective, surjective, bijective? Provide a witness for each negative verdict.

Hint 1

Do not move Chloe to Elm: this exercise changes only the codomain.

Hint 2

The image is the list of lockers reached; the codomain also includes lockers that are declared but unused.

Compare with the solution

The cardinalities are equal: 4 people for 4 lockers. Yet Basil and Chloe, who are distinct, still have the same image Willow: the function is not injective. Elm has no preimage: the function is not surjective. It is therefore not bijective.

The image is still {Pine, Willow, Beech}, comprising 3 lockers; it does not contain Elm. The number of unused lockers is 4 − 3 = 1. Equal cardinalities are compatible with a collision and an unreached element of the codomain.

Check your reasoning

  • Give the equal cardinalities without inferring the properties from them.
  • Name Basil and Chloe to refute injectivity, and Elm to refute surjectivity.
  • Distinguish the image from the codomain B′.

Exercise 3 · Justification and repair

Can Elm be filled without bringing Chloe back?

Keep A′ = {Alix, Basil, Doria} and the codomain B′ = {Pine, Willow, Beech, Elm}. First assign Pine to Alix, Willow to Basil and Beech to Doria. Classify this function. Can it be made surjective by only reassigning these people’s lockers, with each person keeping exactly 1 locker? If Chloe returns, then propose a bijection with B′.

Hint 1

Compare the maximum number of distinct lockers that can be reached with the number of lockers in B′.

Hint 2

If you send Doria to Elm, which locker does she leave with no preimage?

Hint 3

For Chloe’s return, start from the arrows with no collision and look for the only locker still unused.

Compare with the solution

Alix, Basil and Doria have distinct images: the function is injective. Elm stays unused: it is not surjective. Injectivity can therefore hold without surjectivity, just as the original list gave surjectivity without injectivity.

A function on 3 people gives exactly 3 arrows. Each reaches 1 locker; we therefore cannot cover more than 3 distinct lockers. But B′ contains 4, and 3 < 4. No reassignment of these people alone can be surjective. Giving several lockers to a person would change the function rule being studied.

With Chloe back, keep Alix assigned to Pine, Basil to Willow and Doria to Beech, then assign Elm to Chloe. This list reaches each locker exactly 1 time: it is bijective. The argument combines an impossibility proved by cardinalities with a construction checked locker by locker.

Check your reasoning

  • Prove the initial injectivity and refute surjectivity using Elm.
  • Justify the impossibility for every reassignment, rather than only for a failed attempt.
  • Construct and check the bijection when Chloe returns.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you prove that a function is not injective?

Exhibit distinct inputs with the same image: here Basil and Chloe going to Willow. A proof of injectivity must instead rule out all such collisions.

Recall 2What must you provide to prove surjectivity?

A preimage for every element of the declared codomain. In B, Alix reaches Pine, Basil reaches Willow and Doria reaches Beech; this list exhausts B.

Recall 3Why does adding Elm to the codomain change the verdict without changing the arrows?

Surjectivity is judged against the whole codomain. Elm becomes a required element of the codomain with no preimage; the actual image is unchanged. The injectivity verdict, which depends on collisions, stays the same.

Recall 4What preimage count characterizes a bijection?

Exactly 1 preimage per element of the codomain. This is both at most 1, for injectivity, and at least 1, for surjectivity.

Recall 5For finite sets, which inequalities are necessary? Is equality enough?

Injection: |A| ≤ |B|; surjection: |A| ≥ |B|. Equality alone is not enough: with Elm added but unused, shared Willow makes the properties fail despite equal cardinalities.
