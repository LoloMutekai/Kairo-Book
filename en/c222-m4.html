Level 3 · C222·M4

Attrition

Your goalMeasure attrition by group, explain why it can weaken the difference between completers, and calculate a tipping threshold under a written assumption about missing participants.

Before you start
c074-m5 : identification assumptions · c145-m5 : policy evaluation · c073-m5 : interaction and repeated measurements · c081-m5 : experimental ethics · c222-m1 : protocol · c222-m2 : repeated measurements · c222-m3 : interaction

✿01 See the idea

A fictional team compares two groups of adults: attention training for 8 weeks, and a control task unrelated to attention. Participants are allocated at random, 40 per group. The outcome is a working memory score, from 0 to 100 points, measured at baseline and again at the end of the last week. All values on this page are fictional: they are for calculation, not for judging a real training programme.

At the final measurement, some participants are missing: 10 people in the training group and 4 in the control group have no final score. Attrition means this loss of allocated participants before the final measurement. It raises three separate questions: how many participants are missing, whether the loss differs between groups, and whether missing participants resemble those who remain.

Random allocation promised comparable groups at baseline, on average. This promise applies to the allocated participants. If we keep only the completers, meaning those with a final measurement, we compare two subgroups that nobody selected at random. When the reason for leaving is related to the score, the observed difference may depart from the difference we would have seen with everyone included.

We cannot measure the final scores of missing participants. We can, however, make a written assumption about them, then find the value that would overturn the conclusion. This tipping threshold does not tell us what happened: it tells us what assumption we would need to defend for the difference to disappear. For the same observed difference, the fewer participants are lost, the more extreme the required assumption.

The rule to remember

Count the participants lost in each group, compare the rates, then find what assumption about missing participants would make the difference between completers disappear: a difference is robust only if that assumption is hard to defend.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Who is missing at the final measurement?

In the fictional study, 40 participants are allocated to training and 40 to the control task. At the end, 30 completers remain in training, with a mean score of 66 points; 36 remain in the control group, with 60 points. Let us calculate the loss, then test what the missing participants could change.

  1. Name the quantities and assumptions

    For a group g, N is the number of allocated participants, C the number of completers and L the number lost, all measured in people. The attrition rate a expresses the loss as a proportion of the group; it has no unit, and multiplying it by 100 expresses it as a percentage. The mean ȳ is the mean final score of the completers, in points. The subscripts e and t denote training and the control task.

    Lg=Ng−Cg , ag=LgNg

    Assumptions in the scenario: random allocation took place; “lost” means having no final score, whatever the reason; the final score is the only outcome; none of the final scores for lost participants are known. We therefore do not try to guess them: we will test a written assumption about them.

  2. Count the loss by group

    A rate expresses lost participants as a proportion of those allocated to the same group: the same number lost has less weight in a large group than in a small one. We therefore start from N and C for each group.

    Le=40−30=10 ; ae=1040=0.25=25 %
    Lt=40−36=4 ; at=440=0.10=10 %

    The difference in rates is 25 − 10 = 15 percentage points: subtracting two percentages gives percentage points, not a percentage. When loss differs between groups in this way, it is called differential attrition.

  3. Interpret the difference between completers

    The available calculation covers only the completers, using their final means.

    D=y¯e−y¯t=66−60=6 points

    This difference describes the completers. It does not describe all allocated participants, since 10 final scores are missing on one side and 4 on the other. Random allocation guaranteed comparable groups at baseline; it does not guarantee that the completers remain comparable.

  4. Look for a clue about those who leave

    A baseline score was recorded for everyone, including the missing participants. In the training group, completers had a mean baseline score of 52 points and lost participants had 44.

    52−44=8 points

    This lower baseline suggests that loss may depend on score level. It gives neither their final scores nor the direction of any progress they may have made: it is a clue for choosing assumptions to test, not a measurement of what is missing.

  5. State the assumption about missing control participants

    Assumption A, written before calculating: the 4 lost control participants would have scored 60 points, the mean of their completers. A total score is a mean multiplied by a participant count; we add the total for completers to the total for lost participants, then divide by the number of all allocated participants.

    36×60+4×60=2160+240=2400 points
    240040=60 points

    Under assumption A, the mean for the 40 allocated control participants is 60 points.

  6. Solve for the tipping threshold

    Question: what mean s would the 10 lost training participants need to have had for the mean of the 40 participants allocated to this group to equal the control mean? We write that the completers’ total plus the lost participants’ total must equal 40 times the target mean.

    1980+10×s=2400
    s=2400−198010=42010=42 points

    If the lost training participants had scored 42 points on average, which is 24 points below their completers, the difference between the means for all allocated participants would be 0. This is the tipping threshold, valid under assumption A.

  7. Check the result and limit its interpretation

    Check by reconstruction: we work back using the threshold we found.

    10×42=420 ; 1980+420=2400 ; 240040=60

    The reconstructed mean is 60 points, the same as the control mean under assumption A. Another check: if the lost participants had had the mean of their completers, namely 66 points, the difference for all allocated participants would remain 6 points; the threshold therefore had to lie below 66, and it does.

    Limitation: these numbers are fictional. The threshold does not tell us what the missing participants scored; judging its plausibility would require the reasons for leaving and other data that the scenario does not provide.

Explain why a rate compares groups better than a count of lost participants, then why a tipping threshold does not mean that the missing participants actually scored that value.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “With few lost participants, attrition cannot be a problem.”

    A small count does not tell us who left: it only limits the weight of missing participants in the mean. Compare the rates by group, make a written assumption about missing participants and find the threshold, as in the example.

    Revisit the threshold calculation

  • “If both groups lose the same number of people, they remain comparable.”

    Two groups can lose the same number of people for opposite reasons. Random allocation protects the allocated participants, not the completers; also look at what is known about lost participants, such as their baseline scores.

    Reread what random allocation protects

  • “The tipping threshold gives the true score of missing participants.”

    It is a conditional value: it depends on the assumption about the other group and changes with it. It tells us what the missing participants would need to have scored to cancel the difference, never what they did score.

    Recalculate the threshold with another assumption

Exercise 1 · Explained application

A second cohort

A second cohort in the same fictional study allocates 50 people to each group. At the end, 40 completers remain in training and 45 in the control task. Calculate the numbers lost, the rates as percentages and the difference in rates, with their units.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A lost participant is an allocated participant with no final score: allocated participants minus completers.

Hint 2

Divide by the number allocated to the same group before multiplying by 100.

Compare with the solution

Lost: 50 − 40 = 10 in training; 50 − 45 = 5 in the control group. Rates: 10 / 50 = 0.20, or 20 %; 5 / 50 = 0.10, or 10 %. Difference: 20 − 10 = 10 percentage points.

This difference is smaller than in the example (15 percentage points), but it is not 0: attrition is still differential. No scores are given here, so no conclusion can be drawn about the effect of training.

Check your reasoning

  • Numbers lost calculated by group as allocated participants minus completers.
  • Rates calculated relative to the participants allocated to the same group.
  • Difference expressed in percentage points, not per cent.
  • No conclusion about the effect without a final score.

Exercise 2 · Numerical assumption

An unfavourable assumption about lost participants

Return to the example. Make the fictional assumption that the 10 lost participants in the training group would have scored 50 points on average, and that lost control participants would have scored 60 points. Calculate the mean for the 40 allocated training participants, then its difference from the mean for the 40 allocated control participants.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the completers’ total and the lost participants’ total before dividing by the number of all allocated participants.

Hint 2

Under this assumption, the control mean over all its allocated participants is already 60 points.

Compare with the solution

Lost participants’ total: 10 × 50 = 500. Group total: 1980 + 500 = 2480. Mean: 2480 / 40 = 62 points. Difference from the control group: 62 − 60 = 2 points.

Check: this mean lies between 50 and 66, closer to 66 because there are more completers. Under this fictional assumption, the difference goes from 6 to 2 points: attrition reduces it without cancelling it. This is not an estimate of what the missing participants scored.

Check your reasoning

  • Mean calculated over all allocated participants, not just the completers.
  • Total scores added before division.
  • Difference given in points, using the control mean under the stated assumption.
  • Assumption presented as fictional.

Exercise 3 · Critical reasoning

Change the assumption about the control group

A reviewer objects that lost control participants may not have the mean of their completers. Recalculate the tipping threshold under the fictional assumption that the 4 lost control participants would have scored 70 points. Compare it with the threshold in the example and explain what the change implies for the robustness of the observed difference.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First recalculate the control mean over its 40 allocated participants.

Hint 2

Then find the mean for lost training participants that brings their group to this value.

Hint 3

Compare the new threshold with 42: a threshold closer to 66 requires a less extreme assumption about lost participants.

Compare with the solution

Control total: 2160 + 4 × 70 = 2160 + 280 = 2440; mean: 2440 / 40 = 61 points. Target for training: 40 × 61 = 2440. Total to assign to lost participants: 2440 − 1980 = 460. Threshold: 460 / 10 = 46 points.

Check: 10 × 46 = 460, which, added to 1980, gives 2440 again. The threshold rises from 42 to 46, an increase of 4 points: if missing control participants are assumed to score higher, a slightly less severe assumption about missing training participants is enough to cancel the difference, making it less robust. Neither assumption is established; only the actual reasons for leaving could distinguish between them.

Check your reasoning

  • Control mean recalculated with the new assumption.
  • Threshold obtained by solving for the mean of lost training participants.
  • Comparison with the threshold in the example, including the direction of the change.
  • Conditional conclusion, without claiming what the missing participants scored.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is attrition in a study?

The loss of allocated participants before the final measurement. It is counted by group: the number lost equals the number allocated minus the number of completers.

Recall 2Why compare loss rates rather than counts of lost participants?

The rate expresses lost participants as a proportion of those allocated to the same group, so it remains interpretable when group sizes differ. A difference between two rates is expressed in percentage points.

Recall 3Why is the difference between completers not the difference between all allocated participants?

Random allocation applies to the allocated participants. Keeping only completers compares subgroups that were not selected at random; if leaving depends on the score, the difference can change.

Recall 4What does a tipping threshold tell us, and what does it not tell us?

It gives the mean that lost participants in one group would need to have had, under a written assumption about the other group, to cancel the difference. It does not tell us what they actually scored.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define allocated participants, completers and lost participants by group; calculate each group’s loss rate and the difference in percentage points; interpret the difference between completers as a comparison of subgroups; examine what is known about lost participants; make a written assumption about missing control participants; solve for the mean of missing training participants that would cancel the difference; check by reconstructing the mean; recall that the threshold is conditional and estimates nothing.
