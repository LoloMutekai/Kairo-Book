Level 2 · C061·M1

Likelihood

Your goalBuild the likelihood of an observed sequence, compare candidate parameters, and explain why this calculation does not give their probabilities.

Before you start
c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c059-m6 : deterministic discrete Bellman

✿01 See the idea

An opaque dispenser releases a red or blue token each time you press its button. You record red, red, blue, in that order. The setting is hidden. To examine it, first consider a probability of red equal to 1/2, then to 3/4. The question is: under each setting, how probable was this same sequence?

This way of examining an observation is called likelihood. The sequence stays fixed in the record; the setting is what we vary in the calculation. A setting that gives more weight to this sequence receives more support from it, under the chosen assumptions. This is not enough to say that the setting is true.

The entire scene and all its values are fictional. We assume that the setting stays constant and that successive colors are independent once this setting is fixed: knowing one color then does not change the probability of those that follow. The dispenser never runs out, and we plan to record exactly 3 button presses, whatever their outcomes.

Red attracts attention, but blue matters too. Favoring red makes the first outcomes more probable and the last less probable. We therefore need to evaluate the whole sequence, including all its colors, before comparing settings.

The rule to remember

Fix what was actually observed; vary the parameter within the same model; calculate the weight of this observation without reversing the question.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Examine the record red, red, blue

We recorded 3 outputs, including 2 red tokens and 1 blue token. Let us compare p = 0.50 and p = 0.75 for the exact sequence red, red, blue. These values are assumptions for the calculation, not frequencies measured on a real machine.

  1. Define the model and symbols

    p is the unknown parameter: the probability of red, with no units, between 0 and 1. The probability of blue is 1 − p, because red and blue are the only outcomes. n is the planned number of button presses, here 3; r is the number of red tokens, here 2; n − r counts the blue tokens, here 1. These are token counts, not durations.

    D denotes the observed ordered record, red, red, blue; it has no physical unit. Pp(D) is read as “the probability of record D under setting p.” L(p ; D) denotes the likelihood: the same expression, viewed as a function of p while keeping D fixed. The semicolon is a reminder that the data are fixed.

    L(p;D)=Pp(D)

    We retain conditional independence and the same p on every button press. A sequence selected by stopping at the first blue would require a different protocol to be specified; that is not the protocol for our record.

  2. Assign a factor to each observation

    Each red contributes p; the blue contributes 1 − p. Independence under p allows us to multiply these probabilities. Without it, we would need the probabilities of subsequent colors conditional on the preceding ones.

    L(p;D)=p×p×(1−p)=pr(1−p)n−r

    The exponent r counts the red factors, and n − r counts the blue factors. Here this gives p to the power 2, multiplied by 1 − p. We do not multiply by the number of possible orders: D already fixes the exact order.

  3. Evaluate setting A

    With p = 1/2, blue also has probability 1/2. The product of the red factors is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4; we then multiply it by the blue factor.

    L(1/2;D)=1/4×1/2=1/8=0.125

    Check by counting: under this setting, each of the 2 colors is equally likely on every button press. There are 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 sequences of length 3, all equally likely; only 1 matches our record. Its probability is therefore 1/8 = 1/8, as given by the product.

  4. Evaluate setting B and compare

    With p = 3/4, the probability of blue is 1 − 3/4 = 1/4. The reds give 9/16; the blue reduces their product to 9/64.

    L(3/4;D)=3/4×3/4×1/4=9/64=0.140625

    To compare settings on the same observation, divide the likelihood of B by that of A. This quotient, called the likelihood ratio, has no units. Its denominator is strictly positive here.

    L(3/4;D)L(1/2;D)=9/8=1.125

    The ratio is greater than 1: model B assigns this sequence 1.125 times the probability assigned by A. This support remains modest; it gives neither the probability that B is true nor the best setting among all values of p.

  5. Check the interpretation and limits

    Independence and a constant setting make the order of the factors irrelevant: red, blue, red would have the same product here. However, the event “exactly 2 red tokens” includes several sequences; its probability will differ, as you will check in the exercises.

    At the extremes p = 0 and p = 1, the likelihood of our record is 0: one rules out the observed reds, the other the observed blue. For candidate values in the interior, the factors and their products are between 0 and 1, with no units.

    L(p ; D) is not a distribution over the parameters. For example, L(1/2 ; D) + L(3/4 ; D) = 17/64, not 1. These terms belong to different models; no rule requires their sum to equal 1. The distribution over p after the observation is a separate construction, which will require us, among other things, to specify what we assign to the parameters before this observation.

    We remain within a model with discrete outcomes. For continuous readings, we would evaluate a density, meaning a probability per unit of measurement, rather than the probability of an exact value. The absence of units and the upper bound in our example therefore do not generalize to all likelihoods.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The likelihood of B is its probability after seeing the record.”

    The calculation asks for the probability of the record under B. It does not reverse the question. Their sum 17/64 does not form a distribution over A and B; obtaining a distribution over the parameters requires an additional construction and assumptions about them.

    Reread the definition and the interpretation check

  • “You must always multiply by the number of possible orders.”

    First ask which event was observed. The ordered record D contains only one sequence. The count “exactly 2 red tokens” includes the sequences red-red-blue, red-blue-red, and blue-red-red. The extra factor depends on this distinction.

    Compare the record and the count

  • “Every line received provides a new independent factor.”

    A copy of the same record is not a new series of button presses. To multiply marginal probabilities, independence under the setting must be justified. Check the provenance before counting an additional observation.

    Distinguish a copy from a new button press

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · exact calculation

A setting with less red

Keep the same record red, red, blue. Evaluate a setting C with p = 1/4. Write each factor, then compare its likelihood with that of A without turning the result into a probability of the setting.

Hint 1

The reds contribute p, but the blue contributes 1 − p.

Hint 2

Start by multiplying the red factors, then include the blue factor.

Compare with the solution

The blue factor is 3/4. The red factors give 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16. Thus L(1/4 ; D) = 1/16 × 3/4 = 3/64, or 0.046875, with no units.

This value is less than L(1/2 ; D) = 1/8. The record therefore provides more support for A than for C in this model. This does not assign C a probability 3/64 of being the true setting.

Check your reasoning

  • Assign 2 red factors and 1 blue factor to the sequence.
  • Obtain 3/64 by exact calculation, with a blue factor 3/4.
  • State the comparison in terms of data conditional on a parameter.

Exercise 2 · Application · change the information retained

The record loses its order

The record has retained only “exactly 2 red tokens in 3 button presses.” List the compatible sequences. Calculate the probability of this count under A and B, then their ratio. Why is the ratio preserved even though the probabilities change?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Place the blue in each position in turn, without adding another button press.

Hint 2

These sequences are mutually exclusive and have the same product under a fixed p: add their probabilities.

Compare with the solution

The sequences are red-red-blue, red-blue-red, and blue-red-red. The count includes them without overlap: their probabilities must be added. Each is p × p × (1 − p), because the button presses remain independent with the same parameter.

Under A, the probability of the count is 3 × 1/8 = 3/8, or 0.375. Under B, it is 3 × 9/64 = 27/64, or 0.421875. These are also the likelihoods of these parameters for the observed count.

The quotient is 27/64 ÷ 3/8 = 9/8, as before. The same nonzero factor 3, independent of p, cancels. Proportional likelihoods preserve their ratio; this does not make the probabilities of two different events identical.

Check your reasoning

  • List all compatible sequences without duplicates.
  • Justify addition by the mutual exclusivity of the sequences.
  • Obtain 3/8 and 27/64, then explain why the common factor cancels.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · provenance and assumptions

A copy or a new button press?

The system displays a second copy of the record red, red, blue. Someone multiplies the likelihood of the record by itself. Discuss this calculation. Then suppose that an actual new independent button press gives red: calculate the likelihoods of the extended sequence and the new ratio of B to A.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A copy that is certain once the record is known contributes no new color.

Hint 2

A new button press giving red contributes a single factor p to the likelihood of the initial record.

Compare with the solution

The copy is not independent. Once D is known, its faithful reproduction is certain: it contributes the factor 1, and the likelihoods remain 1/8 and 9/64. Squaring would artificially give 1/64 and 81/4096, with a ratio 81/64; that calculation would describe two identical independent series, not the stated copy.

With a new button press giving red, the sequence becomes red, red, blue, red. Under A: 1/8 × 1/2 = 1/16, or 0.0625. Under B: 9/64 × 3/4 = 27/256, or 0.105469. The setting must have stayed constant, and the new button press must be independent of the preceding ones under p.

The new ratio is 27/256 ÷ 1/16 = 27/16, or 1.6875. Check it using the old ratio multiplied by the ratio of the factors for the new red: 9/8 × (3/4 ÷ 1/2). The ratio increases here; a new event must never be replaced by a duplicate document.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the initial likelihoods for a faithful copy.
  • Add only the factor for the actual new button press.
  • Obtain 1/16, 27/256, and 27/16, stating the assumptions.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In L(p ; D), what do we fix and what do we vary?

We fix the record D that was actually observed and vary the parameter p. We evaluate the probability of D in each candidate model; this is not a probability assigned to p.

Recall 2Why does the record red, red, blue contribute p × p × (1 − p)?

Each red contributes p, and the blue contributes 1 − p. Independence of the button presses under the same setting p allows us to multiply these factors. Without this assumption, the preceding colors must be taken into account.

Recall 3Why do “exactly 2 red tokens” and the record red, red, blue not give the same probability?

The count includes 3 distinct sequences, each with the same probability under the model. The ordered record specifies only one. The factor 3 changes the probability but cancels in a ratio between the same candidates.

Recall 4What can we claim from the ratio 9/8 in favor of B?

B assigns the same record 1.125 times the probability assigned by A, under the assumptions. This number gives neither the probability that B is true nor a guarantee that the model works correctly.

Recall 5What should you check before multiplying by a new contribution?

Check that there is a new observation and that independence under p is justified. A faithful copy of the record already known adds no information: its conditional contribution is 1.
