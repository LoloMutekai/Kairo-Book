Level 2 · C074·M2

Randomization

Your goalConstruct a random assignment with fixed group sizes, calculate a difference in means, and explain what its average over the possible draws guarantees.

Before you start
c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c073-m5 : interaction and repeated measures · c009-m5 : reading notes · c074-m1 : causal effect

✿01 See the idea

A teacher prepares an illustrated vocabulary worksheet. To compare it with her usual worksheet, she has 4 students, labelled A, B, C, and D. If she gives the illustrations to the students who are already most comfortable with the vocabulary, a higher score could reflect their starting level. She must decide who receives which worksheet before looking at the result.

She writes the labels on identical slips of paper, mixes them in an opaque box, then draws 2 without replacing them. The students drawn receive the illustrated worksheet; the others receive the usual worksheet. Randomization is this assignment through a random mechanism announced in advance. It does not mean choosing the students who seem to suit each group.

Chance does not create identical groups. It prevents assignment from being systematically guided by the results the students could achieve. A draw can still put students who find the vocabulary easier together in the same group. Mentally repeating all the possible draws will help us understand the guarantee.

The entire situation and all its scores are fictional. The points were constructed to explain a mechanism, without measuring the actual effectiveness of illustrations. We study only assignment and the difference in means; statistical tests and generalization to other students would require further work.

The rule to remember

Fix the protocol, assign the worksheets at random before the results, then compare the groups as drawn. Unbiased on average does not mean exact on every draw.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What drawing lots for the worksheets allows us to conclude

The protocol keeps N = 4 students, n_F = 2 illustrated worksheets, and n_H = 2 usual worksheets. Scores range from 0 to 20 points, using a common marking scheme. The draw shown gives the illustrated worksheet to B and D. Let us calculate its difference, then check it against all the possible draws.

  1. Describe the lottery precisely

    N is the total number of students; n_F and n_H are the sizes of the illustrated and usual groups. We draw without replacement, so a student cannot receive worksheets from opposing groups. This leaves N − n_F = 4 − 2 = 2 students in the usual group.

    We assume uniform mixing: the pairs AB, AC, AD, BC, BD, and CD are the 6 possibilities, each with probability 1/6. AB and BA refer to the same group, because the order of drawing does not change the worksheet received. This enumeration checks the denominator.

    For a given student, the probability of receiving the illustrated worksheet is n_F/N = 2/4 = 1/2 = 0.50, with no unit. It is fixed without using the student’s level or possible outcomes. However, students’ assignments are not independent of one another: the number of places in the group is fixed.

  2. Fix the possible outcomes before the draw

    i is a student’s label. Yᵢ(0) denotes their score with the usual worksheet, and Yᵢ(1) their score with the illustrated worksheet, in points. These are their potential outcomes: the scores they would have in each situation. The parentheses indicate the situation, rather than multiplication. We assume these scores remain fixed during the lottery.

    Each difference Yᵢ(1) − Yᵢ(0) is 2 points in this constructed table. Their mean, denoted τ and read as “tau”, is therefore also 2 points. This is the average causal effect for these students. Identical individual effects simplify the example; randomization does not require them to be identical.

    This complete table is a fictional tool for reasoning. A real experiment reveals only the score corresponding to the worksheet each student actually received. Changing worksheets after the test does not reveal the score the student would have obtained at the same time in the other situation.

  3. State the conditions for the calculation

    Zᵢ is the assignment of student i, with no unit: Zᵢ = 1 for the illustrated worksheet, Zᵢ = 0 for the usual one. The observed score Yᵢ is the score in the assigned situation. Everyone must actually receive their assigned worksheet, the study time and test must be shared, and all scores must be available.

    We also assume that no student passes their worksheet or provides help to another: a potential score depends on the student’s own worksheet, rather than those of their neighbours. The marking scheme and marking procedure are fixed before the draw. Without these conditions, the calculation below no longer necessarily represents the effect of the illustrations alone.

    The lottery takes place after the student list is fixed and before the worksheets are distributed. Its outcome is neither chosen nor cancelled according to which students are drawn. Recruiting students at random from a population would be a different action: assigning worksheets at random does not make this small class representative of all classes.

  4. Calculate the observed difference for BD

    Ȳ_F denotes the observed mean of the illustrated group, and Ȳ_H that of the usual group. The bar above Y means “mean”. The estimator τ̂, tau with a hat, is the quantity calculated to estimate τ: τ̂ = Ȳ_F − Ȳ_H, in points. The hat does not guarantee that the estimate equals the true effect.

    B and D receive the illustrations. Their total is 8 + 12 = 20 points. We divide by their group size to compare a mean score rather than a total. A and C keep the usual worksheet; their total is 4 + 8 = 12 points.

    Y¯F=202=10 points
    Y¯H=122=6 points
    τ^=10−6=4 points

    Check by another route: the group sizes here are equal, so we can first subtract the totals, then divide. (20 − 12)/2 = 8/2 = 4 points. This simplification would need to change if the group sizes differed.

  5. Calculate the other outcomes of the same mechanism

    Keep the potential outcomes table fixed and change only the labels drawn. The indicated pair receives the illustrated worksheet, and its complement receives the usual worksheet. We always calculate illustrated minus usual, with the same denominator because n_F = n_H.

    AB: (6 + 8 − 8 − 10)/2 = (14 − 18)/2 = -2 points. AC: (6 + 10 − 6 − 10)/2 = (16 − 16)/2 = 0 point.

    AD: (6 + 12 − 6 − 8)/2 = (18 − 14)/2 = 2 points. BC: (8 + 10 − 4 − 10)/2 = (18 − 14)/2 = 2 points.

    BD again gives 4 points. CD: (10 + 12 − 4 − 6)/2 = (22 − 10)/2 = 6 points. A negative result can therefore arise from a fair draw, even in this table where every individual effect is positive.

  6. Understand the guarantee on average

    The expectation over randomization, denoted E with a subscript Z, is the mean of the results calculated over the draws, weighted by their probabilities. It is in points, like τ̂. Here, all draws have probability 1/6; their mean is therefore their sum divided by 6.

    Add in pairs to check the signs: -2 + 0 = -2; 2 + 2 = 4; 4 + 6 = 10 points. Then -2 + 4 = 2 and 2 + 10 = 12 points.

    EZ(τ^)=126=2 points=τ

    This result is called unbiased: its mean over assignments recovers the average effect. The general idea is that each student has the same chance of contributing their illustrated score to the illustrated group, and the same chance of contributing their usual score to the usual group. Averaged over the draw, the group means therefore recover the corresponding means for the entire list.

    Symmetry check in our table: AB and CD, AC and BD, and AD and BC each have their midpoint at 2 points. This symmetry of the values uses the constant individual effect here; the design’s lack of bias does not require constant effects. We have not repeated the experiment on the same students: all the rows describe alternative possibilities for the same experiment.

  7. Draw a conclusion supported by the evidence

    The BD draw gives an observed difference of 4 points, whereas the teaching table fixes the true effect at 2 points. This difference does not invalidate the lottery. It shows the variability of an estimator across draws: exact on average does not mean exact in this realization.

    In a real comparison, the complete table would remain unknown. We would report the protocol, the assignment, and the observed difference, then study its uncertainty with a method appropriate to the design. This unit calculates neither a decision threshold nor an interval; it does not allow us to claim established effectiveness from this isolated difference.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results. Explain how the BD difference can exceed the true effect without the draw being unfair. Then reconstruct the possible pairs and what their mean means, specifying which scores would remain unknown in a real experiment.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Drawing lots guarantees that the groups have the same level.”

    The group sizes are equal by construction, but the potential scores are not. Compare outcomes AB and CD: chance can bring together the students who find the vocabulary hardest or easiest. The guarantee concerns the average over all permitted draws.

    Review the outcomes and their mean

  • “A negative difference proves that the draw was rigged.”

    The sign of the difference does not verify the assignment mechanism. Outcome AB gives -2 points despite the positive individual effect constructed here. Check the protocol before judging its fairness from the result.

    Repeat the AB draw

  • “Shuffling the scores after the test makes the experiment randomized.”

    Assignment must precede the worksheets and their effects. Shuffling values already observed may serve an analysis; it does not change how the students received their worksheets.

    Distinguish assignment from shuffling in the code

Exercise 1 · Identify the mechanism

A pair or a student?

Without rereading the list, enumerate the pairs that can receive the illustrated worksheet. What is the probability of pair BD? What is the probability that B receives this worksheet? Explain why these probabilities differ and why tossing a coin separately for each student does not guarantee our design with fixed group sizes.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with the pairs containing A, then avoid counting a pair in opposite orders.

Hint 2

For B, count all pairs containing B; for BD, one specific pair is enough.

Compare with the solution

The possibilities are AB, AC, AD, BC, BD, and CD. There are 6 of them, each with the same probability: BD therefore has probability 1/6. B appears in AB, BC, and BD; by symmetry, B’s probability is n_F/N = 2/4 = 1/2 = 0.50.

A particular pair and the set of pairs containing B are different events. With separate coin tosses, all students could receive the illustrated worksheet. Our box instead imposes exactly 2 students in this group: it links the assignments to one another. Separate tosses would define a different design, which would need to be announced and analysed as such.

Check your reasoning

  • List all pairs, without duplicates caused by their order.
  • Distinguish the probability of a complete group from the probability for a student.
  • Explain how fixed group sizes affect dependence between assignments.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and interpretation

The illustrations seem to lose

This time, the same lottery assigns the illustrated worksheet to A and B. Their illustrated scores are 6 and 8 points; the usual scores of C and D are 8 and 10 points. Calculate the difference, illustrated minus usual. Does this result contradict randomization or the positive effect in our fictional table?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the scores in each group, then divide by the group size.

Hint 2

Compare different students in the observed difference, then situations for the same student in the individual effect.

Compare with the solution

The illustrated total is 6 + 8 = 14 points; the usual total is 8 + 10 = 18 points. Since the group sizes are equal, the difference is (14 − 18)/2 = -4/2 = -2 points.

The negative sign here compares students A and B with students C and D. It does not compare each student with themselves under the other worksheet. It remains compatible with the fictional individual effect of 2 points and with a uniform draw. The mean over draws, rather than each difference, recovers this effect.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the direction illustrated minus usual and retain points as the unit.
  • Justify the common denominator using the equal group sizes.
  • Distinguish a negative observed difference from evidence of a negative individual effect.

Exercise 3 · Critique and repair a protocol

The draw has been replaced by a choice

Before distributing the worksheets, the teacher finally decides that C and D will have the illustrations because their potential usual scores are the highest in the fictional table. She obtains the CD difference. Calculate this difference and its difference from the constructed true effect. Can she invoke the average over uniform draws? Propose a repair before distribution, then say what would remain unknown if the scores had already been collected this way.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

With CD, use the illustrated scores of C and D and the usual scores of A and B.

Hint 2

Ask yourself whether AB, AC, and the other groups still have any chance of being assigned by this rule.

Compare with the solution

The difference is (10 + 12 − 4 − 6)/2 = (22 − 10)/2 = 6 points. It exceeds the fictional true effect by 6 − 2 = 4 points.

The rule selects CD; the other pairs are no longer equally possible. We therefore cannot give it the guarantee demonstrated for the uniform lottery. Before distribution, restore the complete list and the announced draw, keep its outcome without selecting a preferred one, then give everyone the same test.

If the scores have already been collected using this choice, shuffling their labels afterwards does not repair the past assignment. In reality, the scores under the other worksheet would remain unknown. The 4 points of overestimation can be calculated here only thanks to the invented table; we could not automatically subtract them from real results. A different causal justification would require additional assumptions and information.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the difference and its difference from the fictional effect without confusing them.
  • Identify precisely the loss of the uniform mechanism.
  • Repair before distribution and refuse to rewrite the history of the draw afterwards.
  • Distinguish the check provided by the fictional table from what would be observable.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be drawn at random, and when?

The assignment of worksheets to students, according to the fixed design, before distribution and results. Shuffling the scores afterwards does not change the past assignment.

Recall 2Why does the probability of BD differ from the probability that B receives the illustrated worksheet?

BD is a specific pair among 6, with probability 1/6. B belongs to several pairs; B’s probability of being drawn is 1/2. Assignments are linked because the illustrated group size is fixed at 2 students.

Recall 3What is averaged in “unbiased through randomization”?

The possible assignments, weighted by the design’s probabilities, with potential outcomes fixed. The mean of the differences recovers the average effect; a particular difference can depart from it, even change sign.

Recall 4What conditions accompany our calculation, beyond the draw?

The worksheets are actually received, the time and test are shared, all scores are available, and students do not pass worksheets or help one another. Assignment is not repeated according to its outcome.

Recall 5Why do the complete table and a conclusion for all classes remain beyond the reach of a simple draw?

Each student reveals only the score under the worksheet received. Assigning worksheets at random does not recruit students at random from a population; the average effect targeted here concerns the fixed list.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the protocol with a box and the students’ labels, then recover the permitted pairs and their probabilities. Distinguish potential outcomes, observed scores, and the difference in means. Explain why the mean over draws recovers the average causal effect under the stated conditions, whereas an isolated draw can depart from it. Finish with what no shuffling of scores after the test can repair.
