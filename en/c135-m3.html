Level 2 · C135·M3

False memories

Your goalMeasure, in a fictional recognition test, the proportion of “yes” responses to a word that was never presented, compare it with a baseline, and distinguish it from the feeling of remembering.

Before you start
c083-m5 : transfer · c086-m5 : emotion and cognition · c056-m5 : effect size · c135-m1 : faces · c135-m2 : language

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional workshop, 40 people read a list of 12 words: bed, night, rest, dream, pillow, duvet, pyjamas, yawn, nap, snore, wakefulness, doze. One word is missing from the list, “sleep”, although all the others suggest it. The data in this scene are fictional: they are used to teach a measurement, rather than describe a real experiment.

After a delay, each person judges words and responds “yes” if they think they read the word in the list, and “no” otherwise. The test mixes three kinds of words: the 12 studied words, the lure “sleep”, and 3 neutral words unrelated to the list (screwdriver, cactus, slate). The lure and the neutral words were never presented.

A false memory occurs when a person treats something that was never presented as something they experienced. Our measurement does not observe the memory: it observes only a “yes” response to an absent word, which we call false acceptance.

Why is the lure tempting? A common explanation, which we treat as a working hypothesis, is that remembering is not replaying a recording: we retain a general meaning and reconstruct. A word strongly associated with the others seems familiar, and the person cannot always identify where that familiarity comes from. The calculations in this unit do not measure this mechanism; they count responses.

A “yes” to the lure is not enough, because someone can respond yes through distraction or by chance. The neutral words therefore provide a baseline: the rate of “yes” responses to an absent word unrelated to the list. What we attribute to association is what remains above this baseline, with the qualification that the baseline is only a reference point.

A proportion is a number of responses divided by the number of possible judgments; it has no unit. The difference between two proportions is expressed in proportion points.

The rule to remember

Count each “yes” relative to its possible judgments, subtract the baseline for neutral words, then keep the feeling of remembering separate from whether the word is correctly recognized.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A word never read is accepted by part of the group

Return to the scene: 40 people, 12 studied words, one lure, and 3 neutral words. The “yes” counts are fictional: 270 for studied words, 18 for the lure, and 12 for neutral words. Let us measure what the lure adds above the baseline.

  1. Define the setup, symbols, and assumptions

    Each person judges each word once and responds yes or no. Let n be the number of people (40), m the number of studied words (12), and k the number of neutral words (3). A judgment is a pair (person, word) together with its response.

    V, L, and N are counts of “yes” responses: V for studied words (270), L for the lure (18), and N for neutral words (12, summed across all neutral words). All three are counts, with no unit other than “responses”.

    Assumptions: each person responds alone; each word is judged once per person; the instructions are the same for all words; the groups compared later took the same test. None of this is verified here, because the scene is fictional.

  2. Count the possible judgments

    A rate needs its denominator: the number of judgments for which a “yes” response was possible. The lure is judged once per person, so n times. The studied words give n × m judgments, and the neutral words n × k.

    JV=n×m=40×12=480
    JN=n×k=40×3=120

    Dividing N by n instead of n × k would multiply the rate for neutral words by k. The basis for comparison would then be wrong before any calculation.

  3. Calculate the lure rate and the baseline

    The lure rate is the proportion of people who accepted a word they had never read. The baseline is the proportion of “yes” judgments for words that were absent and unrelated to the list.

    rL=Ln=1840=0.45
    rN=NJN=12120=0.10

    These rates are proportions, with no unit. They are fictional, like the counts they come from.

  4. Subtract the baseline to obtain the excess

    A “yes” to an absent word can result from distraction, chance, or response bias, even without an associated word. The neutral words estimate this proportion. We subtract it and call the remainder the lure excess.

    Δ=rL−rN=0.45−0.10=0.35

    Δ is expressed in proportion points. It is a raw effect size, a difference in proportions, as described in the unit on effect size. It tells us neither its precision nor its cause: with only one group of 40 people, we do not know whether another group would give the same difference, or why the lure attracts “yes” responses.

  5. Compare the lure with words actually read

    To compare like with like, let us calculate the average rate per judgment for studied words, then the ratio of the lure rate to that rate.

    rV=VJV=270480=0.5625
    ρ=rLrV=0.450.5625=0.80

    In these fictional data, the word never read is accepted almost as often as a read word is on average. This ratio compares two rates; it does not prove that the two responses rely on the same process, and an average rate hides differences between words.

  6. Break down all the “yes” responses

    Combine all the “yes” responses: O = V + L + N. Of these, F = L + N concern an absent word. The proportions calculated using O add up to one.

    O=270+18+12=300,F=18+12=30

    The proportion of correct “yes” responses is 0.90, and the proportion of “yes” responses to an absent word is 0.10. The first figure is reassuring, but it hides a fact: among the 30 responses to an absent word, a proportion of 0.60 concerns the lure alone. False acceptances are therefore not distributed randomly among the absent words.

  7. Keep the feeling of remembering separate from accuracy

    For each “yes”, people also indicate whether they feel they remember reading the word. Let S be the number of these responses: 189 among the 270 “yes” responses to studied words, and 12 among the 18 “yes” responses to the lure.

    cV=SVV=0.70,cL=SLL=0.67

    The feeling accompanies “yes” responses to the lure almost as often as “yes” responses to read words: the difference is about 0.033. It rests on only 18 responses, too few to interpret it. The confidence rating and accuracy are two distinct measurements.

  8. Check the results

    The proportions add up: 0.90 + 0.10 = 1. The rates also reproduce the total number of “yes” responses: 0.5625 × 480 + 0.45 × 40 + 0.10 × 120 = 300.

    Boundary case: if the neutral words received as many “yes” responses as the lure, the excess would be 0, and nothing would be attributable to the associated list. Our excess of 0.35 is positive, but only in this fictional setup.

    Units: rates, the excess, and ratios have no unit; only the counts are numbers of people or responses.

Explain it in your own words

Explain in your own words why the neutral-word rate is calculated over 120 judgments rather than 40, then what the excess of 0.35 allows you to assert and what it leaves open.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A “yes” to the absent word proves that a false memory has formed.”

    A “yes” response measures acceptance, rather than a memory. It can result from familiarity, chance, or distraction. Always compare the lure with the baseline for neutral words, then report the excess as a difference in rates for a group, rather than an individual diagnosis.

    Review the calculation of the excess

  • “The more vivid a memory seems, the more accurate it is.”

    The feeling of remembering is a subjective report. In the example, it accompanies “yes” responses to the lure almost as often as “yes” responses to read words. Keep the confidence rating and accuracy separate, then measure both.

    Redo the exercise on the feeling of remembering

  • “The group with the most “yes” responses to the lure has the strongest effect.”

    A count depends on group size, and a raw rate ignores the baseline. Divide by the possible judgments, subtract the rate for neutral words, then compare the excesses.

    Redo the comparison of two groups

Exercise 1 · Guided application

A shorter list

A second fictional group of 40 people studies a shorter list of 6 words associated with “sleep”. In the test, with the same 3 neutral words, there are 10 “yes” responses to the lure and 12 “yes” responses to neutral words. Calculate the lure rate, the neutral-word rate, and the excess, then compare this excess with the one for the long list.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Use the same denominator for neutral words as in the example.

Hint 2

Calculate the excess for the second group before comparing the two groups.

Compare with the solution

Lure: 10 / 40 = 0.25. Neutral words: 12 / 120 = 0.10, because there are 120 possible judgments. Excess: 0.25 − 0.10 = 0.15.

This excess is 0.20 lower than the one for the long list, 0.35. The difference describes these two fictional groups; without repetition or a measure of precision, it does not demonstrate that list length caused it.

Check your reasoning

  • Divide the lure count by the number of people.
  • Divide the neutral-word count by people × neutral words.
  • Compare excesses rather than raw counts.
  • Describe the difference without asserting its cause.

Exercise 2 · Interpretation

The feeling of remembering

In the group from the example, 189 of the 270 “yes” responses to studied words and 12 of the 18 “yes” responses to the lure are accompanied by “I remember reading it”. Calculate the two proportions. Does the feeling distinguish the read words from the lure? What more would be needed before drawing a conclusion?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Divide each count of reported feelings by the number of “yes” responses for the same type of word.

Hint 2

Compare the difference in proportions with the number of responses it rests on.

Compare with the solution

Studied words: 189 / 270 = 0.70. Lure: 12 / 18 = 0.67. The difference is about 0.033.

In these data, the feeling barely distinguishes the read words from the lure. But the difference rests on 18 responses for the lure: it is fragile. A larger group, a protocol fixed before observation, and a measure of the precision of the difference would be needed. A shared feeling also does not establish that the two responses have the same origin.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate each proportion using its own denominator.
  • Do not confuse the confidence rating with accuracy.
  • Point out the fragility of a difference based on few responses.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

Which group has the larger excess?

A fictional group of 24 people takes the same test with 3 neutral words. There are 12 “yes” responses to the lure and 18 to neutral words. A hasty reader compares 18 and 12 and concludes that the group from the example has more false memories. Compare the counts, then the raw rates, then the excesses. What do you conclude?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The neutral words in this group are judged by 24 people, each judging 3 words.

Hint 2

Calculate three comparisons in order: count, raw lure rate, excess.

Compare with the solution

Counts: 18 versus 12, giving 6 more for the group from the example. Raw rates: 12 / 24 = 0.50 versus 0.45, giving 0.05 more for the new group.

Neutral words: 18 / 72 = 0.25. Excess: 0.50 − 0.25 = 0.25, versus 0.35 for the group from the example, giving 0.10 less.

The ranking changes at each step. Only the excess accounts for both group size and the baseline. It remains descriptive: it does not say whether this difference would recur in other groups.

Check your reasoning

  • Divide each count by its number of possible judgments.
  • Subtract the baseline specific to each group.
  • Explain why the ranking changes.
  • Keep claims about the scope of the difference descriptive.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What counts as false acceptance in the test used in this lesson?

A “yes” response to a word that was never presented. It records acceptance, rather than the memory itself.

Recall 2Why add neutral words to the test?

They give the rate of “yes” responses to an absent word unrelated to the list. The lure excess is measured above this reference point.

Recall 3What do we divide “yes” responses to neutral words by?

By the number of possible judgments: people × neutral words, rather than just the number of people.

Recall 4Does the feeling of remembering prove that the word was read?

No. It is a confidence rating; it can accompany a “yes” to an absent word and must be compared with accuracy.

Recall 5What does the lure excess not tell us?

It tells us neither its precision, nor its cause, nor what happens for a particular person: it describes a group, a protocol, and its data.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define studied words, the lure, and neutral words; count the possible judgments for each; calculate rates per judgment; subtract the baseline for neutral words to obtain the lure excess; compare it with the rate for read words; break down the “yes” responses; keep the confidence rating separate from accuracy; state the limits: fictional data, a single protocol, no demonstrated cause.
