Emotion and cognition

Your goalExplain how the emotional meaning of an alert can direct attention, then distinguish a time difference, an interpretation and evidence.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You copy an application code shown on a screen. A “Submission rejected” notification appears. You may know how to read the code yet still look back at the alert: if this submission matters to you, its possible failure demands your attention. The difficulty therefore goes beyond knowing or not knowing.

Cognition here includes perceiving, selecting information, holding it briefly and choosing a response. Attention selects what you process first. Working memory temporarily holds and uses relevant information, such as the code while you copy it; its capacity is limited.

An emotion combines an appraisal of what matters in the situation, a feeling and bodily changes or tendencies to act. These components are not always all visible. Valence refers to how pleasant or unpleasant something is; arousal refers to the degree of mobilization. On their own, they do not tell us which emotion is experienced or whether a response will be correct.

The link works in both directions. Interpreting the alert as a threat to your application may cause worry; this worry may encourage you to monitor the submission at the expense of copying. Learning that the screen is part of a simulation may change the interpretation. This reappraisal means changing the meaning assigned to the situation; it does not guarantee that the feeling will disappear.

Emotional information can also help: if your goal is to check the submission, looking at the alert directly serves the task. The effect therefore depends on what takes priority and on the goal being pursued. The diagrams describe possible functional links, rather than separate brain areas or a demonstrated neural circuit.

The entire scene and all its times are fictional. We will compare responses to the same instruction with an informational notification or an alert. These labels describe the screens; they do not measure the person’s emotional state.

The rule to remember

Follow situation → assigned meaning → attentional priority → response, then separate what is observed from what is merely proposed as an explanation.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Copying a code during a notification

A person completes 3 trials with “Submission recorded” (condition N, information), then 3 trials with “Submission rejected” (condition A, alert). In our fictional account, the person reports worrying about the failed submission. Each time runs from the appearance of the code and notification to confirmation of the copied code. All trials are correct, with 0 errors in each condition.

  1. Define what is being measured

    The letters N and A name the conditions. The letters a, b and c identify the trials in order. t denotes a time in milliseconds, written ms; for example, t with the subscript N,a is the time for the first trial with information. n = 3 is the number of trials per condition, a count with no physical unit. A bar over t denotes the mean time.

    The N times are 600 ms, 640 ms and 620 ms; the A times are 740 ms, 700 ms and 720 ms. We use the same timing rule and the same copying instruction. Calculating a mean summarizes these trials; it assumes no universal law of emotion.

  2. State the possible mechanism

    The notification may attract attention because it concerns an important goal: successfully submitting the application. Looking back at it may interrupt holding the code in mind: you then need to read it again or resume copying. This is a possible explanation for the delay, rather than an event that the timer records directly.

    Response time also includes reading, choosing and the action of confirming. The reported feeling tells us about the reported experience; it does not let us divide the time among these operations. A bodily reaction alone would not tell us the exact content of the emotion either.

  3. Summarize the trials with information

    Adding the times gives the cumulative duration T with the subscript N. First combine the initial trials: 600 + 640 = 1240 ms; then 1240 + 620 = 1860 ms. Dividing by the number of trials gives the mean time, without favoring a condition that might contain more responses.

    tN¯=TNn=18603=620 ms

    Check: the deviations from 620 ms balance out, and this mean remains between the shortest and longest times in the condition.

  4. Apply the same operation to the alerts

    The total T with the subscript A is first 740 + 700 = 1440 ms, then 1440 + 720 = 2160 ms. The same number of trials must be used as the divisor.

    tA¯=TAn=21603=720 ms

    Reverse check: 3 × 720 = 2160 ms. The mean does not describe each individual trial: the A responses did not all take this amount of time.

  5. Choose the direction of the difference

    Δ, the letter delta, here denotes the mean for A minus the mean for N. Its unit remains the millisecond. With this convention, a positive sign means later confirmation in A; a negative sign would mean faster confirmation.

    Δ=tA¯−tN¯=720−620=100 ms

    The observation concerns these trials: the mean confirmation time in A exceeds that in N by 100 ms. Δ is neither an emotional intensity nor a loss of cognitive capacity.

  6. Check by another method

    The conditions have the same number of trials. Pair their trials by position in the sequence to check the arithmetic: 740 − 600 = 140 ms; 700 − 640 = 60 ms; 720 − 620 = 100 ms.

    The sum of these differences is 140 + 60 = 200 ms, then 200 + 100 = 300 ms. Their mean is 300 ÷ 3 = 100 ms: we recover Δ. This is the difference between the totals divided by their common trial count.

    Pairing by position serves to check the calculation; it does not make the paired trials equivalent and does not constitute random assignment of conditions.

  7. Limit the conclusion to the available result

    In these fictional trials, all copies are correct and A is slower on average. The hypothesis that the alert takes priority is compatible with this result. Duration alone demonstrates neither attentional capture nor an emotional cause for it.

    All N information trials came before the A alerts: fatigue, practice and trial order may play a role. The content and appearance of the notifications may also require different reading. To test the role of assigned meaning, we would need to compare similar presentations, vary the order and collect suitable observations. This experimental work remains to be done.

    The contrast therefore does not justify saying “an emotion always interferes.” In the same scene, checking the submission rather than copying the code changes what counts as useful attention.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Emotion prevents clear thinking.”

    An emotional priority may draw you away from copying or help you detect a rejected submission. Start again from the task’s goal: interfering with an operation does not imply impairing all cognition.

    Review the role of the goal being pursued

  • “These 100 ms measure worry.”

    The difference measures confirmation times. The feeling is reported in the account; attention, reading, decision and action remain combined in the duration. Emotion has no unit in milliseconds.

    Review the measurement and its scope

  • “The program gives an emotional label, so it knows the feeling.”

    A label depends on a representation of the text and comparison rules. Its presence proves neither that the person feels this emotion nor that the proposed adjustment will be useful to them.

    Read the evidence and its limit

Compare with the solution

“Submission rejected” is information from the screen. “I am worried” reports a feeling. Reading the notification again is behavior directed toward the submission; it does not directly reveal all mental activity.

If reading it again interrupts holding the code in mind, resuming the copy may require looking back at the screen. This is a possible mechanism. With the goal of checking the submission, reading it again is relevant: its value depends on the task.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer within the same scene

Faster, but with an error

Another fictional variant: after being told that the alert is part of a simulation, the person confirms an incorrect copy in 300 ms, then correct copies in 740 ms and 720 ms. Compare the mean of all responses and the mean of correct responses only with N. Can we claim that the person is performing better and that reappraisal caused this change?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate the mean of all the times provided, keeping track of the errors.

Hint 2

Then remove the time for the incorrect copy and divide by the number of correct copies, which has changed.

Compare with the solution

The sum of all the times is 300 + 740 = 1040 ms, then 1040 + 720 = 1760 ms. Their exact mean is 1760 ÷ 3 = (1760/3) ms, or approximately 586.67 ms. The difference from N is (-100/3) ms, or approximately -33.33 ms: across all responses, the mean is shorter.

But the reference had 0 errors, and this variant has 1. There are 3 − 1 = 2 correct copies left, totaling 740 + 720 = 1460 ms. Their mean is 1460 ÷ 2 = 730 ms, which is 110 ms more than the N reference. The fast, incorrect confirmation therefore lowered the overall mean; the correct copies here are slower on average.

Comparing only correct copies does not remove every difficulty: we are selecting different trials. Times, response counts and errors must be kept together. These results are not enough to declare better performance.

The information “this is a simulation” offers another meaning for the alert. It may be relevant to reappraisal, but learning the task, order or a change in caution remain possible explanations. An organized comparison would be needed to distinguish these explanations; no general benefit has been demonstrated.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain the exact mean (1760/3) ms and the difference (-100/3) ms; round only for readability.
  • Recalculate the divisor 2 and obtain 730 ms for correct copies only, keeping 1 error visible.
  • Explain the difference between the means and name an explanation other than reappraisal.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can the meaning assigned to the alert and emotion influence each other?

Appraising what is at stake may cause worry; this may direct attention toward the submission. Another interpretation may change this priority. None of these links is an automatic guarantee for an individual.

Recall 2Why can reading the same alert again interfere or help?

It may interrupt holding the code in mind when it must be copied, but provide useful information when the submission must be checked. Priority is assessed relative to the goal.

Recall 3What does Δ measure, and what does it not measure?

Δ = mean A minus mean N, in milliseconds. It summarizes a difference in confirmation times; it measures neither feelings, nor an overall capacity, nor an emotional cause.

Recall 4What does reappraising the situation mean in our scene?

Changing the meaning assigned to the alert, for example by learning that it is a simulation. This may change the reaction, without guaranteeing its effect or proving that this information explains a faster response.

Recall 5Why does a score calculated from a text not prove an experienced emotion?

The score depends on an encoding and chosen references. It describes a model output. Further evidence is needed to establish its validity for people, its actual use and the usefulness of modulation.
