Level 4 · C269·M2

Study selection

Your goalBuild and check the selection account for a review: write ordered criteria before screening, count each removal with its reason, and state what this account does not demonstrate.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a fictional review of a learning question: in adults, does spaced study repetition improve recall measured at least 7 days later, compared with massed repetition in a single session? This entire lesson stays within this scenario. The databases, records and counts are fictional: no real study is described and no scientific finding is claimed.

The search has already been completed; that is the subject of C269·M1. It returned 220 records, each an entry with a title, an abstract and a reference. Selecting studies means deciding which will contribute to the review using a rule that another person can read and apply. If you rely on instinct to keep work you like, the conclusion reflects your preferences rather than the question.

A useful picture is a funnel with successive gates. At each gate, a record passes through or is removed; if it is removed, the page records the reason. Nothing disappears without a line in the account. This account checks counts: it does not judge the quality of the studies retained; it only shows how the sample was formed.

Keep these two words distinct. A record is an entry found in a source; a study is research conducted with participants. Several records may describe the same study. After removing duplicates, we assume here that there is one record per study; without this assumption, we would need two accounts, one counting entries and the other counting studies.

The rule to remember

Write the criteria and their order before screening; remove each record only once, recording the first criterion it fails; check that the number found minus the number included equals the sum of all removals; never interpret this check as proof of quality.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the search account to the included studies

In the fictional scenario, the 3 sources yielded 120, 85 and 15 records, including 40 duplicates. Title and abstract screening removes 140 records, 2 full texts cannot be retrieved, and 27 exclusions follow full text reading. Let us build the account, check it in two ways and interpret it cautiously.

  1. Define the quantities and assumptions

    Let T be the number of records found in database A, database B and reference lists, and D the number of duplicates removed. U = T − D is the number of unique records to screen. Title and abstract screening excludes es records; this leaves L = U − es full texts sought for retrieval. R texts cannot be retrieved, and A = L − R texts are assessed. All these numbers are whole-number counts, expressed in records.

    At full text assessment, there are 4 possible reasons for exclusion: ep for population, em for comparison, et for the delay and ex for unusable recall data. Let E be their sum and n = A − E the number of included studies.

    Assumptions: the criteria are written before reading; each excluded record receives a single reason, the first criterion it fails in the fixed order; after deduplication, one record corresponds to one study; title and abstract screening removes only records clearly outside the question; the account is kept by someone who can justify each line. If any of these assumptions fails, the account must be reexamined.

  2. Set the selection rule before screening

    The rule consists of 4 observable criteria, applied in this order: adult population; explicit comparison between spaced repetition and massed repetition; recall measured at least 7 days after studying; usable recall data in the text. A criterion is observable when you can check it by reading the document, without guessing its authors' intentions.

    The order matters: it determines the reason recorded. A study that fails several criteria is counted only once, at its first failure. This avoids double counting but hides later failures: a recorded reason does not describe the whole study.

    The rule is set before reading the study results. If it changes afterwards, you can choose your conclusions by choosing the studies: any change must be disclosed with its reason, and the account must be redone under both the old rule and the new one.

  3. Remove duplicates, then screen titles and abstracts

    The sources initially yield 120, 85 and 15 records. We add them because each source is a separate entry point:

    T=120+85+15=220

    The same study may appear in several sources; counting it twice would inflate the sample. We therefore remove the 40 duplicates:

    U=T−D=220−40=180

    Title and abstract screening removes only records clearly outside the question, such as a study explicitly involving children. Uncertainty is resolved by reading the full text. Here es = 140:

    L=U−es=180−140=40

    Texts that cannot be retrieved are also removed, with their reason: we cannot assess them, which does not mean they would be excluded. Here R = 2:

    A=L−R=40−2=38

  4. Apply the criteria to the full texts

    Each assessed text goes through the criteria in order. The recorded reasons are: population 6, comparison 9, delay 8, usable data 4. Their sum E gives the number of removals at this stage:

    E=ep+em+et+ex=6+9+8+4=27

    What remains is included:

    n=A−E=38−27=11

    The 11 included studies are those that met every criterion. They are not selected for confirming an expected answer: the rule disregards the direction of the results.

  5. Check by a second route

    First check: follow the counts criterion by criterion, as shown in the second diagram. The final number must equal n:

    38→32→23→15→11

    This gives 11, matching n = 11. Second check: each record found but not included was removed exactly once, so

    T−n=D+es+R+E

    Substituting the numbers, the left side equals:

    220−11=209

    And the right side:

    40+140+2+27=209

    The two sides, 209 and 209, agree. This check catches an arithmetic or copying error in the account. It detects neither a misclassified record nor a study missing from the search. For n to provide a meaningful check, also recount the included studies from their reference list.

  6. Interpret the result without overstating it

    p=nT=11220=0.05

    The account retains 11 studies from 220 records found, a unitless proportion of 0.05. Among full text exclusions, the comparison criterion accounts for 1/3 (9 out of 27). This share does not mean the criterion is poorly defined: it indicates where to reread the texts if there is doubt.

    This proportion is fictional; it is neither a norm nor an expected rate. The account does not tell us whether the search found all useful studies, whether the retained studies were well conducted, or how large an effect is. These questions are addressed in C269·M3 and the following units of the chapter.

Explain it in your own words

Why can an account in which every equality holds still be based on poor selection? Name two possible causes, then state what an independent second reading of borderline cases might detect.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If the counts in the account agree, the selection is correct.”

    Cross-checking verifies the bookkeeping. It checks neither the choice of criteria, the completeness of the search nor the correct classification of each record: a misclassified record leaves every equality intact. Recount the included studies from their reference list and have the borderline cases reread.

    Review the check by a second route

  • “I can relax a criterion when I see the results, since the studies stay the same.”

    Changing the rule after reading the results lets you choose studies that suit you. If a change is justified, disclose it with its reason and report the account under both the old and the new rule, as in the exercise on relaxing a criterion.

    Redo the exercise on relaxing a criterion

  • “A record is a study: the number of entries is the number of studies.”

    Several entries can describe the same study, such as an article and a conference abstract. The account initially counts records; the assumption of one record per study is made only after deduplication. If it is false, keep a second account counting studies.

    Reread the distinction between a record and a study

Exercise 1 · Explained application

Rerun a database search

In the same fictional scenario, database B is searched again with a revised query: it returns 5 additional records, of which 2 are already on your list. How many unique records are now waiting to be screened? Give a second route that yields the same count.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with what the repeated search actually adds to the list of unique records.

Hint 2

For the second route, recount the records found and the duplicates for the entire search, then subtract.

Compare with the solution

Only new records enter the unique list: 5 − 2 = 3. The list grows from 180 to 180 + 3 = 183.

Second route: the number found becomes 220 + 5 = 225, and duplicates become 40 + 2 = 42. Then 225 − 42 = 183, the same count.

The new records have not yet been screened: the subsequent counts, texts sought and studies included, change only after those records have been read.

Check your reasoning

  • Do not add duplicates to the list of unique records.
  • Provide a second route that yields the same count.
  • Do not change downstream counts before screening the new records.

Exercise 2 · Guided reasoning

Relax a criterion

A reviewer proposes reducing the minimum delay from 7 days to 1 day. In the fictional account, 8 studies are excluded for this reason. Between which values would the number of included studies lie, and why can you not give an exact value? What must you disclose?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Which criteria have these studies already met, given the order of the rule?

Hint 2

Which criterion has not yet been examined for them?

Compare with the solution

In the fixed order, these 8 studies met the population and comparison criteria. They failed the delay criterion, so the usable data criterion was not examined for them. At a minimum, none is added: 11 studies remain included. At a maximum, all are added:

11≤n′≤11+8=19

The exact value requires rereading these texts against the last criterion: some may have unusable data. This calculation also assumes that title and abstract screening excluded no records because of the delay. The change and its reason must be disclosed, and the account must be reported under both rules.

Check your reasoning

  • Give the lower and upper bounds, justified by the order of the criteria.
  • Explain why the exact value is unknown.
  • Require disclosure of the change and both accounts.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis

An account whose counts do not agree

A draft reports 38 texts assessed, 27 excluded at full text assessment and 12 studies included. Check the account, measure the discrepancy and state what you request before correcting a number.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the included and excluded counts, then compare the sum with the number of assessed texts.

Hint 2

Three numbers are linked: which can you recount directly from a list?

Compare with the solution

The included and excluded studies should account for exactly all assessed texts: 12 + 27 = 39, whereas 38 texts were assessed. The discrepancy is 1.

If the other two numbers are correct, n = 38 − 27 = 11, and the draft counts 1 study too many. However, this conclusion assumes that those two numbers are correct. Without a list identifying each study, we cannot tell which number is wrong: an extra included study, an omitted exclusion or a miscounted assessed text.

Recount the included studies from their list, then the reasons for exclusion, and change a number only after finding the line responsible.

Check your reasoning

  • Measure the discrepancy with an explicit addition or subtraction.
  • Do not decide which number is wrong without the list identifying each study.
  • Specify what must be recounted before any correction.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must a selection rule contain before the first screening?

Observable criteria, their order of application and the reason recorded when a record is removed, all written before reading the study results.

Recall 2Which equality links the records found to the included studies?

The number found minus the number included equals the sum of all removals: duplicates, title and abstract exclusions, texts that cannot be retrieved and full text exclusions, each counted only once.

Recall 3Does a selection account whose counts agree prove that selection was sound?

No. It checks the counts. It proves neither the completeness of the search, the correct classification of each record nor the quality of the studies.

Recall 4If you relax a criterion afterwards, what do you bound and what do you disclose?

You bound the number included using the studies excluded for that reason, since subsequent criteria have not been examined for them; you disclose the change and its reason, and report both accounts.

Recall 5What is the difference between a record and a study?

A record is an entry in a source; a study is research conducted with participants. Several entries may describe the same study, which is why deduplication is needed.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Pose the question, write observable criteria in order, count the records found, remove duplicates, screen titles and abstracts, assess the texts, record a single reason for each exclusion, calculate the number included, check both the sequence of criteria and the sum of all removals, then state what this account does not prove.
