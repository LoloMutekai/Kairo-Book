Level 2 · C100·M4

Bias–variance

Your goalDecompose the expected squared prediction error into irreducible noise, squared bias and variance, then explain why a ridge penalty can lower the error by accepting some bias.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A fictional delivery service wants to predict the duration of a 3 km trip. It has observed only 2 training trips, of 1 km and 2 km, and its rule is simple: duration is proportional to distance, with a coefficient in minutes per kilometre. All these values are fictional; they support a calculation rather than a field measurement.

Imagine repeating the training several times, with the same distances but different random traffic effects. The fitted coefficient changes from one training run to the next, so the prediction changes too. Two separate questions arise. Where is the mean of these predictions relative to the target duration? This systematic difference is called bias. And how spread out are the predictions around their own mean? This is the variance.

A third component is beyond the control of any model: the new trip also has its own random effect, independent of the training. This irreducible noise remains even if we know the true coefficient.

The ridge penalty, introduced in the unit on ridge, moves the coefficient towards 0. It brings the predictions closer together across training runs, reducing variance, but it also pulls their mean below the target duration, creating bias. Regularization means choosing a point in this trade-off; the example calculates it in full.

This decomposition is a theoretical calculation under a model whose truth we know because the scenario is invented. With real data, we have only one training run and cannot calculate bias and variance directly: we measure error on data held aside.

The rule to remember

Expected error = irreducible noise + bias² + variance. A penalty improves prediction only if the variance it removes exceeds the bias² it adds.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Predict a 3 km trip after 2 training trips

In a fictional scenario, the true relation is y = θx + ε, with θ = 2 min/km, unknown to the model. The random effect ε is +5 min or −5 min with equal probability, independently from one trip to another. The two training trips have x₁ = 1 km and x₂ = 2 km; we predict at x₀ = 3 km. Compare the expected squared error for λ = 0 km² and for λ = 5 km².

  1. Define the symbols and assumptions

    x is distance in kilometres and y is duration in minutes. The true model is as follows, with i equal to 1 or 2. θ is the unknown coefficient, in minutes per kilometre; εᵢ is the random effect for trip i, in minutes.

    yi=θxi+εi

    Assumptions. Each random effect has 0 mean and variance σ² = s², here 5 × 5 = 25 min². The random effects for the trips are independent of one another and independent of the random effect ε0 for the trip to be predicted. The distances are fixed in advance: only the duration is random. The fitted model has the same form as the true relation, with no intercept; its only possible weakness will therefore come from the penalty.

    The target duration at x0 is θx0 = 2 × 3 = 6 min. The model does not know it; we know it because the scenario is invented, and because we want to decompose the error rather than estimate anything.

  2. Write the ridge estimator and the prediction

    Ridge seeks the θ̂ that minimizes the sum of squared training residuals plus λθ². The setting λ is expressed in km², so λθ² is in min², like the squared residuals. Setting the derivative with respect to θ to 0 gives θ̂(S + λ) = Σ xᵢyᵢ, where S is the sum of squared distances.

    S=∑ixi2

    Here S = 1 × 1 + 2 × 2 = 1 + 4 = 5 km².

    θ^λ=∑ixiyiS+λ

    Replace yᵢ with θxᵢ + εᵢ. The numerator becomes Sθ + g, where g, in km·min, is the sum of the xᵢεᵢ terms. The prediction at x0 can then be written using r = S / (S + λ), a dimensionless shrinkage factor between 0 and one.

    g=∑ixiεi
    r=SS+λ
    f^(x0)=θ^λx0=r(θx0+x0gS)

    The term x0g/S is the shift due to the training noise; r multiplies both the target duration and this shift.

  3. Repeat the training without a penalty

    For λ = 0 km², r = 1. Each random effect is ±5 min: the 4 combinations are equally likely, giving our 4 training runs. If the two random effects have the same sign, g = ±(1 + 2) × 5 = ±15 km·min. If they have opposite signs, g = ±(2 − 1) × 5 = ±5 km·min.

    The shift x0g/S uses x0/S = 3 / 5 = 0.6 per kilometre, so its product with g remains in minutes. It therefore equals ±9 min or ±3 min. The 4 predictions are 6 ± 9 min and 6 ± 3 min.

    Their mean remains 6 min: the bias is 0. Their variance is the mean of the squared deviations from this mean: (9² + 9² + 3² + 3²) / 4 = 45 min².

    Check using the formula σ²x0²/S:

    Var=25×95=45 min²

    The product 25 × 9 is 225, then 225 / 5 gives 45 min². The two methods agree. Adding the noise of the new trip, the expected error without a penalty is 0 + 45 + 25 = 70 min².

  4. Add the penalty

    Take λ = 5 km². Then S + λ = 5 + 5 = 10 km² and r = 5 / 10 = 0.5.

    Each prediction is multiplied by r: the mean becomes 0.5 × 6 = 3 min. The bias is therefore 3 − 6 = -3 min, giving bias² of 9 min². The negative sign means that the model underestimates on average.

    The deviations around the mean are multiplied by r, so their squares are multiplied by r² = 0.25:

    Var=0.25×45=11.25 min²

    The predictions are closer together, but they are no longer centred on the target duration.

  5. Justify the decomposition

    Let m = E[f̂(x0)] be the mean of the predictions across training runs. For a new trip, y0 = θx0 + ε0. The difference splits into three parts:

    y0−f^(x0)=ε0+(θx0−m)+(m−f^(x0))

    Square this expression and take the mean. The three squares give σ², (θx0 − m)² and the variance of f̂. Three cross terms remain. The expected cross product of ε0 with either of the other two parts is 0: ε0 has 0 mean and is independent of the training. The expected cross product of the constant θx0 − m with m − f̂ is also 0, since m − f̂ has 0 mean by the definition of m.

    E[(y0−f^(x0))2]=σ2+(E[f^(x0)]−θx0)2+Var[f^(x0)]

    For this model, the previous calculations give the two components in the following form:

    E[f^(x0)]=rθx0
    Var[f^(x0)]=r2σ2x02S

    These formulas assume random effects with 0 mean and equal variance, independent of one another and of the new trip. Without these assumptions, cross terms or a variance that depends on distance would appear.

  6. Compare the errors and check the units

    Without a penalty: bias² = 0, variance = 45, noise = 25, total = 70 min². With λ = 5 km²:

    9+11.25+25=45.25 min²

    Variance falls from 45 to 11.25 min², while bias² rises from 0 to 9 min²: total error falls from 70 to 45.25 min². The noise of 25 min² is identical in both cases; no penalty removes it.

    Units: bias is in minutes before it is squared; variance and noise are already in square minutes. Consistency check with the penalty: each deviation is multiplied by 0.5, so each square is multiplied by 0.25, giving 11.25 min² again.

    These numerical results depend on a fictional scenario, a model of the correct form and a known truth. They illustrate a mechanism; they do not tell us which penalty to choose for real data.

Explain it in your own words

Why does the bias become nonzero with the penalty even though the model has the correct form? Then explain why the irreducible noise does not change when λ changes.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “An unbiased estimator must be the best.”

    Bias is only one of the three terms. Without a penalty it is 0, but the expected error is 70 min², compared with 45.25 min² with penalty 5 km². Compare the bars in the second diagram and revisit the comparison in the example.

    Review the error comparison

  • “The stronger the penalty, the lower the error.”

    Beyond a certain point, bias² grows faster than variance falls: for λ = 20 km², the expected error is 49.84 min², above the 45.25 min² obtained for λ = 5 km². In the limit, the prediction tends to 0 and bias² tends to 36 min².

    Redo the exercise on three settings

  • “Bias and variance can be measured using a single real dataset.”

    Bias and variance are averages across repeated training runs, and bias requires knowledge of the truth. With a single training run, we estimate error on data held aside. The milestone shows an example of this kind of split in code.

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

A stronger penalty

In the same fictional scenario, take λ = 20 km². Calculate r, the mean of the predictions, bias, bias², variance, reducible error and then expected error including the noise of 25 min². Compare with λ = 5 km², then check your result against the third bar in the second diagram.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Use S = 5 km² and r = S / (S + λ) again.

Hint 2

Bias is the mean of the predictions minus the target duration of 6 min; variance is r² × 45 min².

Compare with the solution

S + λ = 5 + 20 = 25 km², so r = 5 / 25 = 0.2.

Mean: 0.2 × 6 = 1.2 min. Bias: 1.2 − 6 = -4.8 min, so bias² = 23.04 min².

Variance: r² = 0.04, then 0.04 × 45 = 1.8 min². Reducible error: 23.04 + 1.8 = 24.84 min². Including the noise: 24.84 + 25 = 49.84 min².

This is more than 45.25 min² for λ = 5 km²: variance has fallen from 11.25 to 1.8 min², but bias² has risen from 9 to 23.04 min².

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate r before any other term.
  • Keep the units: minutes for bias, square minutes for the three error terms.
  • Conclude by comparing total errors rather than variances alone.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis with explanation

Rank three settings

A colleague looks only at variance and concludes that λ = 20 km² is the best setting for the fictional scenario. Rank λ = 0, 5 and 20 km² by expected error, then reply to her. Finally, consider a penalty so large that the prediction falls almost to 0: what are the variance, bias² and reducible error then?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare total errors; the noise of 25 min² is shared and does not change the ranking.

Hint 2

When the prediction is almost 0, the bias is almost the negative of the target duration, 6 min.

Compare with the solution

Expected errors: 45.25 min² for λ = 5 km², 49.84 min² for λ = 20 km², 70 min² for λ = 0 km². The best of the three is therefore λ = 5 km², followed by λ = 20 km², then λ = 0 km².

Variance alone overlooks bias²: it is smallest for λ = 20 km² (1.8 min²), but this reduction comes at the cost of bias² of 23.04 min².

In the limit of an enormous penalty, variance tends to 0, bias² to 6² = 36 min², and therefore reducible error to 36 min². This is less than the 45 min² without a penalty, but more than the 20.25 min² of the intermediate setting. The best setting is intermediate, and this conclusion applies to the fictional scenario, whose truth is known.

Check your reasoning

  • Compare total errors expressed in the same unit.
  • Name the bias² overlooked by the argument based on variance alone.
  • Handle the limit without concluding that more penalty is always better or always worse.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

Noise with 2 times the amplitude

Trips become more irregular: the random effect is now ±10 min instead of ±5 min, with no other change. Calculate variance, bias² and reducible error for λ = 0 and λ = 5 km². How does the benefit of the penalty change, and why?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Bias does not depend on the random effect: it comes only from r and the target duration.

Hint 2

Without a penalty, variance is σ²x₀²/S; with a penalty, multiply it by r².

Compare with the solution

σ² = 10 × 10 = 100 min². Variance without a penalty: 100 × 9 = 900, then 900 / 5 = 180 min². Reducible error without a penalty is therefore 180 min², since bias is 0.

With λ = 5 km²: variance = 0.25 × 180 = 45 min². Bias² is unchanged, 9 min². Reducible error is 9 + 45 = 54 min².

The ranking is the same, but the gap has widened: bias² stays fixed while variance follows σ². The larger the random effect, the more variance a penalty removes for the same added bias. This applies to this fictional scenario; it is not a general rate of improvement.

Check your reasoning

  • State that bias² remains identical.
  • Recalculate variance with the new σ² before multiplying it by r².
  • Connect the wider gap to the role of variance without generalizing beyond the scenario.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How is the expected squared prediction error decomposed?

Into irreducible noise, plus squared bias, plus the variance of predictions across repeated training runs, under random effects with 0 mean that are independent of the training.

Recall 2What does bias measure, and in what unit?

The difference between the mean of the predictions across repeated training runs and the target value, in the unit of the target; it is squared so it can be added to the other terms.

Recall 3What does an increasing ridge penalty do to bias and variance?

It shrinks the predictions: variance falls and bias moves away from 0. Total error falls only if the variance removed exceeds the bias² added.

Recall 4Can bias and variance be calculated using a single real dataset?

No: they require repeated training runs and a known truth. In practice, prediction error is estimated on data held aside.

Reconstruct the reasoning

State the model, the fictional truth and the assumptions about random effects; write the penalized estimator and its shrinkage factor r; repeat the training to obtain the mean and spread of predictions; calculate bias², variance and noise; check that they add up; compare several penalties while remembering that the truth is known only in the invented scenario.
