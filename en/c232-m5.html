Level 4 · C232·M5

Small-time limits

Your goalExtract the logarithmic rate of a signal at small times, check its prefactor, and specify what stays fixed in the limit.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A pulse of tracer is deposited at the center of an ideal straight channel with no current. A detector waits 2 cm from the deposit. Taking a reading very early means looking for tracer far from the region where most of it has spread: the signal becomes extremely small, while remaining positive in our diffusion model.

The detector also takes time to start up: its sensitivity grows in proportion to the elapsed time. The received signal therefore combines diffusion to the detector with this sensitivity. As the elapsed time decreases, these effects do not vanish at the same rate.

The idea is to take the logarithm of the signal, then multiply it by a dimensionless time. This operation reveals the dominant cost of the distance. It can remove the startup effect in the limit, even when that effect remains decisive for the signal’s value.

The scene and all its parameters are fictional. We adopt an ideal Gaussian profile and a prescribed detector response, without presenting them as readings. This unit studies their logarithmic limit; it does not prove a general law for all channels or a pricing formula at short maturities.

The rule to remember

Before taking a small-time limit: state the time scale, keep the other parameters explicitly fixed, then separate the dominant exponent from the prefactor.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Reading the same detector at progressively earlier times

Keep L = 2 cm, D = 1 cm²/s and τ = 1 s. Compare elapsed times t = 1/4 s and t = 1/8 s, then calculate the logarithmic limit of the received signal as t approaches 0 through positive values.

  1. Define the profile and units

    t is the elapsed time, in seconds; x is a distance from the deposit, in centimeters; L is the fixed position of the detector. D is the diffusivity, in cm²/s. We assume that D and L are strictly positive constants, and that the channel is homogeneous, with no current or boundary effects. The chosen profile is Gaussian: its ratio to the concentration at the center, at the same time, is G(t,x). G is dimensionless and positive; it is neither an absolute concentration nor the probability of hitting a specific point.

    G(t,x)=exp(−x24Dt)

    exp denotes the exponential function and ln its inverse, the natural logarithm. The quotient in the exponential is dimensionless: x squared and Dt are both in cm². Normalization by the center has removed the profile’s common amplitude; a limit of G therefore does not directly give the limit of an absolute concentration.

  2. Introduce the small parameter

    τ is a fixed, strictly positive reference time, in seconds. Set ε = t/τ, which is dimensionless; ε approaches 0 if and only if t does. We study 0 < ε ≤ 1. The dimensionless rate I and the ideal signal Gε at the detector are:

    I=L24Dτ;Gε=G(τε,L)=exp(−Iε)

    By assumption, the detector’s sensitivity equals ε during startup. Its normalized response is Sε = εGε. This multiplicative factor, called the prefactor, is dimensionless and strictly positive for positive t. The relation is a fictional detector rule, with no added noise.

    Calculate I: L squared is 2 × 2 = 4 cm². The product 4D is 4 cm²/s; multiplying it by τ gives 4 cm². Thus I = 4/4 = 1, which is dimensionless.

  3. Work through the two exact readings

    At the first elapsed time, ε is (1/4 s)/(1 s) = 1/4. The magnitude of the exponent is I/ε = 1/(1/4) = 4. Hence Gε = exp(−4) and Sε = (1/4) exp(−4).

    At the next elapsed time, ε = 1/8, I/ε = 8, so Gε = exp(−8) and Sε = (1/8) exp(−8). The exponentials remain in exact form: rounding to 0 would destroy the logarithm we need.

    Independent check: the ratio of the elapsed times is 1/2, the ratio of the exponent magnitudes is 2, and the ratio of the prefactors is 1/2. The difference between the exponent magnitudes is 8 − 4 = 4. The earlier response divided by the later response is therefore (1/2) exp(−4): the prefactor and the exponential both reduce the earlier response.

  4. Extract the rate while retaining the prefactor

    Let Hε denote the dimensionless logarithmic diagnostic −ε ln Sε. Since the signal is positive, we can take its logarithm. The logarithm turns a product into a sum; then multiplying by −ε isolates the rate:

    Hε=−εln(εexp(−Iε))=I−εlnε

    For ε = 1/4, Hε = 1 + (ln 4)/4. For ε = 1/8, Hε = 1 + (ln 8)/8. These expressions are exact; at positive times they differ from I. The logarithm of ε is negative for ε below 1: Hε is therefore greater than I here.

  5. Justify taking the limit

    We still need to prove that −ε ln ε approaches 0, rather than simply state that ε is small. Set y = 1/ε; y is dimensionless and tends to infinity. Then −ε ln ε = (ln y)/y. For u between 1 and y, 1/u ≤ 1/√u; u is a dimensionless integration variable. Integrating this inequality gives ln y ≤ 2(√y − 1).

    0≤lnyy≤2(y−1)y→0

    The bounds prove the limit of the correction term. Thus −ε ln Sε approaches I = 1. Since I is strictly positive, Gε and Sε both approach 0. The limit of the signal and the limit of the logarithmic diagnostic are different objects.

  6. State exactly what the limit preserves

    More generally, Aε is a positive, dimensionless prefactor. For Sε = Aε exp(−I/ε), the same calculation gives Hε = I − ε ln Aε. The rate stays I if ε ln Aε approaches 0. This condition is called “subexponential on the inverse-ε scale”; it does not require Aε to approach 1.

    Our Aε = ε approaches 0 while satisfying this condition. Gε and Sε therefore have the same rate, but Sε/Gε = ε approaches 0. Writing Sε ∼ Gε would be false: here the symbol ∼ means that the ratio approaches 1. A logarithmic limit is not a relative asymptotic equivalence and provides no relative error bound at a fixed time.

  7. Specify the regime before generalizing

    We have kept L, D and τ fixed. At a fixed nonzero distance, L squared divided by Dt becomes large as t decreases. If the detector approaches the deposit at the same time, this quotient can instead remain bounded: the calculation must be repeated, as in the last exercise.

    Here the Gaussian profile allows an exact calculation followed by a proved limit. It gives a concrete interpretation of the exponential scale encountered in large deviations. Neither Varadhan’s lemma alone nor this example justifies replacing every model with a Gaussian formula, moving the detector without checking the consequences, or drawing a conclusion about a crossing probability.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The rate is I, so the signal is asymptotically equivalent to exp(−I/ε).”

    Calculate the ratio: in our scene, it equals ε and approaches 0, instead of 1. The rate describes only what remains after taking the logarithm and multiplying by ε. The prefactor can still change the amplitude substantially.

    Review what the limit preserves

  • “Anything multiplying the exponential can be neglected.”

    Test ε ln Aε. Its limit must be 0 to preserve I. If Aε itself contains exp(−κ/ε), with positive κ, it adds κ to the rate; the second exercise checks this.

    Check the detector’s prefactor

  • “A small-time limit remains valid if I also move the detector.”

    Return to x squared divided by Dt. At a fixed distance it diverges; at a distance proportional to √t it can remain constant. The two paths through parameter space give different limits, without any contradiction.

Exercise 1 · Exact application

Taking a reading even earlier

At the same fixed detector, take t = 1/16 s, keeping D, L and τ unchanged. Calculate ε, the exponent magnitude I/ε, the response Sε and the diagnostic Hε. Keep the logarithm and exponential in exact form; state what is dimensionless.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First divide the elapsed time by τ. L and D do not change, so I remains the rate from the example.

Hint 2

For the diagnostic, take the logarithm of the product ε exp(−I/ε), then multiply the entire sum by −ε.

Compare with the solution

ε = (1/16 s)/(1 s) = 1/16. The rate remains I = 1. Dividing I/ε gives 16: the exponent is therefore −16.

Sε=116exp(−16);Hε=1+ln1616

ε, I/ε, Sε and Hε are all dimensionless; only the given elapsed time is in seconds. Hε is greater than 1 at this positive time, even though its limit is 1. Check: (1/16) × 16 = 1; reversing the division recovers the rate.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the dimensionless elapsed time and retain the rate fixed by L, D and τ.
  • Keep the negative sign of the exponent and the prefactor ε in Sε.
  • Obtain Hε by taking the logarithm of the entire signal, then check the units and reverse the division by multiplication.

Exercise 2 · Checking an assumption

A setting that changes the rate

Modify the startup of the same detector: the prefactor becomes Aε = exp(−κ/ε), with dimensionless κ = 1/2. The new response is Tε = AεGε. Determine its logarithmic rate and write Tε at elapsed time t = 1/8 s. Explain why treating Aε as a negligible prefactor would fail.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The name “prefactor” says only that Aε multiplies Gε. Calculate ε ln Aε to determine its effect.

Hint 2

The product of two exponentials is the exponential of the sum of their exponents.

Compare with the solution

Here, ε ln Aε = −κ = −1/2, which does not approach 0. The condition for preserving the rate fails. Since Tε = exp(−(I + κ)/ε), we have exactly −ε ln Tε = I + κ = 3/2, for every positive ε.

At elapsed time 1/8 s, ε = 1/8. The magnitude of the exponent is (3/2)/(1/8) = 12, hence Tε = exp(−12). Reversing the division gives (1/8) × 12 = 3/2.

The prefactor approaches 0, just like ε in the original response. This limit alone therefore cannot decide the issue: the limit of ε ln Aε distinguishes the two settings. The new setting adds an exponential cost κ.

Check your reasoning

  • Explicitly test ε ln Aε rather than only the limit of Aε.
  • Add the rates in the product of exponentials.
  • Give an exact value for the exponent at the requested elapsed time and check it by reversing the calculation.

Exercise 3 · Transfer to a different regime

A detector that follows the spreading tracer

Return to the original sensitivity ε, but now move the same detector to position x(t) = √(4Dt). The constants D and τ stay fixed. Calculate G(t,x(t)), the response εG(t,x(t)) and its logarithmic rate as t approaches 0. At elapsed time 1/8 s, also give x(t) squared in cm². Why does the fixed detector’s rate I no longer apply?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Replace x squared with 4Dt in the profile before taking the limit.

Hint 2

The geometric rate x(t) squared divided by 4Dτ now depends on time. Do not fix it at the value calculated using L.

Compare with the solution

The position is chosen so that x(t) squared = 4Dt. The quotient of x(t) squared by 4Dt therefore equals 1. Thus G(t,x(t)) = exp(−1), a constant, and the detector response equals ε exp(−1).

−εln(εexp(−1))=ε−εlnε→0

The ideal signal does not approach 0 along this path; the detector response does, because of its sensitivity. Its logarithmic rate is 0, because ε and −ε ln ε both approach 0.

At elapsed time 1/8 s, x(t) squared is 4 cm²/s × 1/8 s = 1/2 cm², or 0.50 cm². The instantaneous geometric rate is 1/2/4 = 1/8; dividing it by ε = 1/8 then recovers the exponent 1. At this elapsed time the diagnostic is (1/8) + (ln 8)/8.

Here the position approaches the deposit in proportion to the square root of time. The assumption of a “fixed nonzero distance L” has changed. A limit calculated for each fixed position does not automatically describe a path of time-dependent positions: there is no contradiction with the first calculation.

Check your reasoning

  • Substitute the time-dependent position before evaluating the exponent.
  • Distinguish the limit of the ideal signal, the limit of the response, and the limit of the logarithmic diagnostic.
  • Name the changed assumption and check the squared position with its units.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why use ε = t/τ before taking the logarithm of the signal?

τ is a positive, fixed reference time. ε is dimensionless, as is the normalized signal; the logarithms and rate I are then dimensionless. Letting ε approach 0 is equivalent to letting t approach 0 while keeping τ fixed.

Recall 2Which operation recovers the rate in Sε = Aε exp(−I/ε)?

Taking −ε ln Sε gives I − ε ln Aε. Its limit is I when Aε is positive and ε ln Aε approaches 0. Positivity allows us to take the logarithm.

Recall 3Why does the same logarithmic rate not guarantee Sε ∼ Gε?

Relative asymptotic equivalence requires Sε/Gε → 1. In our scene, Sε/Gε = ε → 0, whereas both rates equal I = 1. Multiplying the logarithm by ε can remove a factor that still matters in the ratio.

Recall 4How can you decide whether a multiplicative factor changes the rate?

Calculate the limit of ε ln Aε. If it is 0, the rate is preserved. For Aε = exp(−κ/ε), it is −κ and the rate becomes I + κ. The limit of Aε alone is not enough.

Recall 5What changes between a fixed distance and x(t) = √(4Dt)?

At a fixed nonzero distance, x squared divided by 4Dt diverges. Along the path x(t) = √(4Dt), this quotient equals 1, the ideal signal stays exp(−1), and the logarithmic rate of the response ε exp(−1) is 0. You must state what stays fixed.

Optional milestone · identify where time appears in an exponential

What you understand
An exponential alone does not identify a small-time regime. In the course, the exponent contains the inverse of the dimensionless elapsed time at a fixed distance. A factor whose exponent is proportional to the duration behaves differently: it approaches unity as that duration approaches 0, with the rate fixed.
