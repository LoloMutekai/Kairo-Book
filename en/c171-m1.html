Level 3 · C171·M1

Adapted integrands

Your goalDecide whether a coefficient uses only the information available, then construct a step integrand known before each Brownian increment.

Before you start
c170-m5 : Strong Markov property · c057-m5 : Hessian · c165-m5 : duality

✿01 See the idea

On a fictional test bench, a sensor receives a fluctuating signal. Before each interval, you set the gain by which the next change will be multiplied. You can look at what has just happened; you cannot wait until the end of the interval and then claim to have chosen the gain before it.

The model idealizes the signal as Brownian motion B. The readings used in the calculation will be B(0) = 0, B(1) = 2 and B(2) = 1. These values are fictional: they are neither actual readings nor computer-generated samples, and they do not describe the path between readings. The diagram shows, after the event, what was revealed in sequence.

The sensor rule is: after a reading, choose the gain 1 + that reading, then keep it until the next reading. The initial gain is therefore 1; the next will be 3. This second gain is random before the intermediate reading, but becomes known when that reading arrives. Adapted therefore does not mean fixed in advance.

Here an integrand is the process H that supplies the noise multiplier over time. Saying it is adapted means that its value at time t can be determined from the information available at t. Our step construction also requires each multiplier to be known at the start of the interval it weights.

The rule to remember

Establish when the coefficient is chosen before multiplying: information revealed later remains unavailable for that choice, even if the whole path is now drawn.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Construct the sensor gain without looking at the end

Normalized time runs from 0 to T = 2. The reference physical duration is τ = 1 s: t is the elapsed duration divided by τ. The signal B, the gains and the weighted output S are dimensionless. At the readings, B takes the successive values 0, 2, 1. We will set the coefficients in time, calculate S and check the square-integrability condition separately.

  1. State exactly what is observable

    An outcome ω describes one possible history of the signal; Ω is the set of these outcomes and P is the probability distribution of the model. The sigma-algebra ℱ_t collects the events observable up to t. The family (ℱ_t) is a filtration: if s ≤ t, then ℱ_s is contained in ℱ_t. Here we use the information generated by the values of B already observed, without information about its future.

    H_t is a real random variable, meaning a value that depends on the outcome ω. It is ℱ_t-measurable when every question “H_t ≤ a?”, for a real threshold a, can be decided from ℱ_t. The process H is adapted when this property holds for every t. The letters ω, Ω, P, s and a describe the model; they have no physical units.

    H adapté⇔∀t,∀a∈ℝ,{Ht≤a}∈ℱt

    B is assumed to be Brownian motion relative to this filtration: it is adapted, starts at 0, has continuous paths, and B_t − B_s, for s < t, is independent of ℱ_s and has a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance t − s. E will denote expectation under P. The choice of filtration is essential: giving the final value in advance would destroy this independence.

  2. Construct a step integrand known at the left endpoint

    On a deterministic partition 0 = t_départ < … < t_n = T, i is the index of an interval and n is the number of intervals. ξ_i, read “xi subscript i”, is the coefficient chosen at time t_i. The condition is that ξ_i be measurable with respect to ℱ at time t_i. It may depend on the entire observed past, but not on the next increment.

    Ht=∑i=0n−1ξi1(ti,ti+1](t)

    The indicator of an interval equals 1 when t belongs to it, and 0 otherwise: it activates only that interval’s coefficient. The intervals are open on the left and closed on the right; at a transition point, H keeps the value from the interval that is ending, and the new coefficient applies immediately afterwards. At the initial time, H is set separately to ξ_départ. This convention does not change the products with increments that we will calculate.

    Such a process is adapted and predictable: in this step form, the coefficient is already known before the increment it will multiply. For a general process, adaptation alone does not guarantee the required measurability in time and randomness. We restrict ourselves to these step processes, with the square-integrability condition checked below; no general construction of the integral is assumed.

  3. Set the gain before each new change

    In our partition, the times are 0, 1 and 2. The normalized durations are 1 − 0 = 1 and 2 − 1 = 1. The chosen rule is ξ_i = 1 + B(t_i). Since B(t_i) is observable at t_i, this sum is an admissible coefficient in terms of information.

    Initially: ξ_départ = 1 + 0 = 1. After the intermediate reading: ξ_suite = 1 + 2 = 3. This last coefficient will remain 3, whatever the final reading. Knowing the current signal does not mean predicting its next change.

    Ht=ξdépart1(0,1](t)+ξsuite1(1,2](t)

  4. Apply the coefficients to the increments

    ΔB_i, read “delta B subscript i”, denotes the reading at the end of interval i minus the reading at its start. The letter Δ means a difference, not a derivative. On our fictional path, the changes are 2 − 0 = 2 followed by 1 − 2 = -1. The product ξ_i ΔB_i is this interval’s contribution to the output S.

    ΔBi=B(ti+1)−B(ti)
    S=ξdépart(B(1)−B(0))+ξsuite(B(2)−B(1))

    First contribution: 1 × 2 = 2. Second: 3 × (-1) = -3. The second is negative because the signal fell while the gain was positive. Thus S = 2 + (-3) = -1, dimensionless. This is a finite weighted sum, the starting point for the later construction of the integral; we do not take a limit here.

  5. Check by a different calculation

    Expand the rule ξ_i = 1 + B(t_i). The constant part gives the total change B(2) − B(0) = 1 because the intermediate reading cancels. The variable part gives B(0)ΔB_départ + B(1)ΔB_suite. Its first term is 0; the second is 2 × (-1) = -2.

    This independent regrouping gives 1 + (-2) = -1, equal to S. It checks both the signs and the use of the reading at the start of each interval. Simply multiplying the final gain by the total change would not reproduce this calculation.

  6. Check square integrability in the model

    Non-anticipation governs which information can be used; it does not guarantee that the quantities are integrable. For our construction in mean square, we also require the expected energy of the gain to be finite. The integral below is an ordinary integral with respect to time, rather than an integral with respect to Brownian motion at this stage.

    E[∫0TH2(t)dt]=E[ξdépart2]Δtdépart+E[ξsuite2]Δtsuite<∞

    Δt is the normalized duration of an interval. The initial coefficient is constantly 1, so the expectation of its square is 1. For the next one, E[B(1)] = 0 and E[B(1) squared] = 1, because Brownian motion is centered and has the stated variance. Expanding the square of 1 + B(1) therefore gives the expectation 1 + 2 × 0 + 1 = 2.

    The expected energy is 1 × 1 + 2 × 1 = 1 + 2 = 3, dimensionless and finite. This is an analytic calculation under the assumptions, rather than an average estimated from actual readings.

    On the numerical path, by comparison, the squared gain is 1 followed by 9: the energy on this path is 1 + 9 = 10. This single number is not the expectation 3 and is insufficient to prove that the expectation is finite. We have checked the available information and the second moment separately; the isometry belongs to the next unit.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results. Explain why the next gain is random before the intermediate reading and known immediately afterwards; then reconstruct the products in order. Why does a sum that can be calculated on a single path fail to prove square integrability?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The final value is on the diagram, so I can use it to choose the initial gain.”

    The diagram is viewed after the events. Return to the time of the choice: B(1) is not available at time 0. For an interval, use a reading already obtained at its start; the value at the end cannot set its gain retrospectively.

    Review the sequence of readings and settings

  • “An adapted integrand must be independent of Brownian motion.”

    Our coefficient 1 + B(t_i) depends on past Brownian motion and remains admissible at the left endpoint. It is the future increment that is independent of the information already available, under the Brownian assumption; the gain does not have to be independent of that information.

    Review the filtration and coefficients

  • “I calculated a finite output, so the process is integrable in the setting of this course.”

    One path provides neither a proof of adaptation nor a proof of a finite moment. Here the path energy is 10, whereas its expectation is 3. You must justify the availability of the coefficients and check the expectation condition in the model, as well as the measurable form of the process.

    Review the square-integrability check

Exercise 1 · Recognition with justification

Which coefficient is available at the start?

On the interval (0, 1], the sensor proposes either ξ = 1 + B(0) or η = 1 + B(1). The letter η denotes only the second proposal. Which is known at the left endpoint for the filtration used in this course? Give its value on our path and justify rejecting the other without using the retrospective diagram.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Place yourself at time 0: which reading has already taken place?

Hint 2

A future Brownian increment has strictly positive variance and remains independent of the past.

Compare with the solution

ξ is known: B(0) = 0, so ξ = 1. It is a function of information already available, and in this case is even a constant.

η uses B(1), which is not known at 0. The future increment is independent of ℱ at the start and has variance 1, which is strictly positive: it cannot be both determined by this past and nondegenerate. Knowing the value 2 after the event does not fix this defect.

Check your reasoning

  • Name the time of the choice and the filtration being considered.
  • Select ξ = 1 and reject dependence on a future reading.
  • Explain the rejection using the available information, rather than the numerical size of the coefficient.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and causality check

The same start, a different end

Keep B(0) = 0 and B(1) = 2, but replace only B(2) with -1. With the rule ξ_i = 1 + B(t_i), reconstruct the coefficients, contributions and output. Which quantity must the change to the future leave intact?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The gain for the final interval was chosen before its new reading.

Hint 2

Recalculate the difference between the end and start readings, then keep its sign in the product.

Compare with the solution

The coefficients remain 1 and 3: the readings that determined them have not changed. The first contribution remains 2.

The new final change is -1 − 2 = -3. Its contribution is 3 × (-3) = -9. The output becomes 2 + (-9) = -7, dimensionless.

The change affects the increment and output, while the coefficient already set remains unchanged. Algebraic check: the same expansion as in the example gives B(2) − B(0) + B(1)(B(2) − B(1)), which recovers -7 with the new final value.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the gains 1 and 3 despite the change to the final value.
  • Find -3, then -9, then -7, explaining the signs.
  • Check the output using the expansion rather than simply repeating the addition.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same sensor

Repair a rule that looks at the result

A colleague chooses ξ_i = 1 if ΔB_i ≥ 0, and ξ_i = 2 otherwise, then multiplies this increment by ξ_i. The gain is bounded: does that make it admissible at the left endpoint? Repair the rule using the sign of B(t_i). For the repaired rule, justify the available information and bound the expected energy up to T = 2. Finally, apply it to the original path.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Is the sign of the future change observable at the time of the choice? Boundedness answers a different question.

Hint 2

The sign of the current reading is known; if the gain never exceeds 2, its square never exceeds 4.

Compare with the solution

The original proposal anticipates: choosing the gain at the start would require knowing the sign of ΔB_i. But this future Brownian increment, independent of the past and with positive variance, can have either sign. A bounded gain does not provide information that is not yet available.

Repair: choose ξ_i = 1 if B(t_i) ≥ 0, and 2 otherwise. The events describing these choices are measurable with respect to ℱ at time t_i. The step form constructed in the example is therefore adapted and predictable.

The square of each gain is at most 2 × 2 = 4. The time integral of this square, and therefore its expectation, is at most 4 × 2 = 8, dimensionless. This bound proves finiteness; it is not the exact expected energy.

On the original path, the readings at the choice times are 0 followed by 2, both greater than or equal to 0. The repaired gains are therefore 1 and 1. The output is the sum of the changes, namely B(2) − B(0) = 1. This value, different from -1, does not demonstrate any superiority of the setting: we changed the rule rather than measuring performance.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish a bounded gain from the availability of information.
  • Replace the sign of the future increment with the sign of the reading at the left endpoint.
  • Justify measurability and the bound 8, specifying that it is an upper bound.
  • Find the output 1 without concluding that performance has improved.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does it mean for H to be adapted to a given filtration?

For every time t, H_t is measurable with respect to ℱ_t: its value can be determined from the information available at t. This property depends on the chosen filtration.

Recall 2What condition is imposed on the coefficient of an interval in a step process?

ξ_i must be measurable with respect to ℱ at time t_i, before the change that follows. It may depend on the Brownian past; it cannot be chosen from this future increment.

Recall 3Why can a random coefficient remain admissible?

Random does not mean future. The coefficient 1 + B(t_i) is unknown long before t_i, then becomes known at t_i through the reading; it can weight the next interval.

Recall 4Why are adaptation and a calculation on one path insufficient in the mean-square setting?

A suitable form of measurability and finite expected energy are also needed. Our step processes known at the left endpoint have the required form; the moment is checked separately. The path energy 10 is distinct from its expectation 3.

Recall 5What must a change to the future after the choice time preserve?

The coefficient already determined by the same past. The later change and the output may differ; the choice must not be rewritten based on their result.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without reopening the cards, reconstruct a chain: available information → coefficient measurable at the left endpoint → step gain → increment still unknown → product then sum. Then explain why the square-integrability condition requires a separate expectation calculation. What you understand
A coefficient chosen for a future change uses the past already observed. The useful analogy in a forecasting calculation is to separate what is passed in before the forecast from what will later be used to evaluate it; this does not turn that calculation into an Ito integral.
