Level 2 · C047·M4

Linear equation

Your goalSolve a first-order linear differential equation using an integrating factor, then check the solution and its initial condition.

Before you start
c045-m5 : Introductory quadrature · c046-m5 : Approximation error · c033-m5 : Affine approximation · c047-m1 : Direction field · c047-m2 : Existence and uniqueness · c047-m3 : Separation

✿01 See the idea

A tank contains 100 L of salt water. Water flows in and out at 20 L/min each way, so the volume stays constant. The water is stirred fast enough for the outflow to have the same concentration as the tank at every instant. We track the mass of salt rather than the volume of water.

The inflow gradually becomes saltier: its concentration starts at 1 g/L and increases by 0.1 g/L each minute. The more salt the tank contains, the more salt the outflow carries away. The change therefore combines a known input and a loss proportional to the mass present.

A first-order linear differential equation relates the derivative of a quantity to the quantity itself, with neither squared terms nor products of the quantity and its derivative. “Linear” does not mean that its solution is a straight line. The integrating factor is a multiplier chosen to combine the derivative and the loss into the derivative of a single product. This grouping then allows us to integrate.

All the data are fictional. We assume constant flow rates, an instantly uniform mixture, and fully dissolved salt, with no reaction or deposits. The inflow rule applies only from 0 min to 5 min. The model describes neither the actual stirring nor a salinity measurement.

The rule to remember

Start with the derivative alone as the leading term; choose a multiplier whose derivative reproduces the coefficient of the quantity; integrate the product; impose the initial value; check both conditions.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

An inflow with increasing concentration

At the initial time 0 min, the tank contains 10 g of salt. Let us determine its mass m(t) up to 5 min, then its exact value at the end. We will keep the exponential in symbolic form: no approximate decimal will be presented as an exact value.

  1. Express the balance with units

    t is time in minutes; m(t) is the mass of salt in grams; m′(t) is its net rate of change in g/min. V = 100 L is the volume and D = 20 L/min is the water flow rate through each pipe. The inflow concentration ce(t) = cᵢ + αt has parameters cᵢ = 1 g/L and α = 0.1 g/(L·min). cᵢ denotes the initial inflow concentration, rather than the initial concentration in the tank.

    The incoming salt flow rate is D ce(t). The outgoing salt flow rate is D m(t)/V. Conservation of salt therefore gives the derivative as inflow minus outflow. Each term is in g/min.

    m′(t)=D(ci+αt)−DVm(t)

  2. Recognize the normalized linear form

    Write p = D/V = 1/5 per minute, or 0.2 per minute; a = D cᵢ = 20 g/min; b = Dα = 2 g/(min·min). Then q(t) = a + bt is a known salt flow rate, in g/min. Moving the loss to the left gives:

    m′(t)+pm(t)=a+bt

    The general form is y′(t) + p(t)y(t) = q(t). y is the unknown quantity, p is its known coefficient, and q is the known right-hand side. In our scenario, y = m and p is constant. The power of y is 1; q can vary with t while the equation remains linear. A squared y term or a coefficient depending on y would change the situation.

    If the given equation starts with r(t)y′(t), first divide every term by the known coefficient r(t), on an interval where it does not vanish. For the method below, p and q are continuous on the same interval I containing the initial time t0; y0 is the value imposed at that instant. In the scenario, t0 = 0 min and y0 = 10 g.

  3. Construct the integrating factor

    μ(t), using the Greek letter mu, denotes a dimensionless auxiliary function. For μy′ + μpy to be the derivative of μy, the product rule requires μ′ = pμ. We choose the following strictly positive solution; s is simply the time variable of integration.

    μ(t)=exp(∫t0tp(s)ds)

    exp is the exponential function. The integral of p, in inverse time units, with respect to ds, in time units, is dimensionless. Thus μ(t0) = exp(0) = 1. In the tank, μ(t) = exp(pt), because p is constant and the starting time is 0 min.

    (μy)′=μy′+μ′y=μ(y′+py)=μq

    The sign in μ is positive when the equation is written with +py. The negative exponential will appear after division by μ, at a later step.

  4. Integrate the product, then isolate the quantity

    Integrate from t0 to t. The left-hand side becomes μ(t)y(t) − y0, since μ(t0) equals 1. Dividing by μ(t), which is always positive, gives:

    y(t)=y0+∫t0tμ(s)q(s)dsμ(t)

    The numerator adds the initial quantity to an integral of a weighted rate, which is a quantity with the same units. This formula constructs the solution. It also shows that, for fixed p, q and y0 under the stated assumptions, no free choice remains. We still need to calculate the integral for our varying inflow.

  5. Calculate an antiderivative of the weighted rate

    In our case, the weighted right-hand side is exp(pt)(a + bt). Look for an antiderivative of the form exp(pt)(A + Bt). A is a constant in g; B is a constant in g/min. The derivative of this expression is exp(pt)[B + pA + pBt]. Equality with the weighted rate requires pB = b and B + pA = a separately.

    Since our p is nonzero, B = b/p = 2 ÷ 1/5 = 10 g/min. Next, a − B = 20 − 10 = 10 g/min, so A = (a − B)/p = 50 g. This calculation of A and B uses the constant p in our example; the preceding integral formula also allows a varying p.

    ddt[exp(pt)(A+Bt)]=exp(pt)(a+bt)

    The integral from 0 to t therefore equals exp(pt)(A + Bt) − A. The term −A comes from the lower limit; it must be retained.

  6. Use the initial condition

    Substitute the integral into the formula and divide each term by exp(pt). This gives m(t) = A + Bt + C exp(−pt), where C = m(0) − A is a constant in g. Here C = 10 − 50 = -40 g.

    m(t)=A+Bt+Cexp(−pt)

    Since 1/p = 5 min and −C = 40 g, the solution can be written with explicit units:

    m(t)=50 g+(10 g/min)t−40 gexp(−t5 min)

    Initial check: exp(0) = 1, so m(0) = A + C = 10 g. The exponential term retains the information about the salt already present at the start. The expression A + Bt alone would solve the equation, but it would start at 50 g instead of 10 g.

  7. Give a final result and an independent initial check

    At t = 5 min, pt = 1, which is dimensionless; A + Bt = 50 + 50 = 100 g. The exact result is:

    m(5 min)=[100+(-40)exp(−1)] g

    Since exp(−1) is strictly between 0 and 1, the final mass is strictly between 60 g and 100 g. These are bounds, rather than an approximation presented as something else. The final inflow concentration is 1.5 g/L.

    Return separately to the initial physical balance: the inflow brings in 20 g/min, and the outflow carries away D m(0)/V = 2 g/min. The mass must therefore initially increase at 18 g/min. We will compare this slope with that of the solution, as well as checking its initial value.

  8. Check the whole equation as well as the initial value

    The derivative of the solution is m′(t) = B − pC exp(−pt). Here pC = -8 g/min, so the exponential coefficient in the derivative is 8 g/min. At the start, m′(0) = 10 − (-8) = 18 g/min: this recovers the initial balance.

    m′+pm=B−pCexp(−pt)+p(A+Bt+Cexp(−pt))=a+bt

    The exponential terms cancel for every t in the interval. The remaining terms give B + pA = a and pB = b, already checked when finding the antiderivative. The solution therefore satisfies the equation and the initial condition.

    Another way to interpret this: the difference w(t) = m(t) − A − Bt, in g, satisfies w′ = −pw. This equation is separable; it gives C exp(−pt) again. We first subtracted a particular solution A + Bt before separating. Dividing the original equation directly by m would have left time and mass mixed together on the right-hand side.

Hide the formulas. Why must μ′ equal pμ? Where does the initial value enter? Then reconstruct the tank balance and the cancellations that verify your solution.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If the inflow rate varies with time, the equation is no longer linear.”

    Look at how the unknown quantity appears: m and m′ remain raised to the power 1, with known coefficients. The right-hand side q(t) can vary. A curved solution can satisfy a linear equation.

    Reread the normalized form and the role of q

  • “I choose exp(−pt) as the integrating factor because the solution must contain that sign.”

    For m′ + pm = q, expand (μm)′. We need μ′ = pμ, so μ = exp(pt) in our scenario. Multiply q by μ as well. The negative sign appears when you divide the result by μ.

    Review the diagram showing how to combine the terms into a product

  • “My formula starts with the correct mass, so it is the solution.”

    A curve can pass through the initial point with an incorrect slope. Check its derivative in the equation throughout the interval, then check its initial value. The last exercise gives a formula that passes only the initial point check.

    Diagnose the proposed formula in the exercises

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and repair

Correct initial value, incorrect evolution

Return to the initial mass of 10 g. Someone proposes m̃(t) = A + Bt + C exp(+pt), using A, B, C and p from the example, then claims: “At t = 0, I get the correct mass, so that settles it.” Calculate its initial derivative, compare it with the balance, then repair and check the formula. The tilde simply distinguishes the proposed expression from the correct solution.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The derivative of exp(+pt) is +p exp(+pt). Keep the sign of C when differentiating.

Hint 2

Compare B + pC with the initial net flow rate required by the equation, then change only the sign in the exponential.

Compare with the solution

The proposed expression does give A + C = 10 g at the start. However, its initial derivative is B + pC = 10 + (-8) = 2 g/min. The balance requires 18 g/min: the residual, proposed derivative minus required derivative, is -16 g/min. A single contradiction is enough to reject the formula.

The repaired formula is m(t) = A + Bt + C exp(−pt), with C still equal to -40 g. Its derivative B − pC exp(−pt) recovers 18 g/min at the start. To validate the repair throughout the interval, we still need to write out m′ + pm: the exponential terms cancel, and B + pA + pBt = a + bt. The initial point remains correct.

The initial slope detected the error, but obtaining a correct slope at a single point would not by itself prove that the whole curve satisfies the equation. The identity throughout the interval completes the check.

Check your reasoning

  • I found 2 g/min for the proposed expression and explained its disagreement with 18 g/min.
  • I kept the constant C and repaired the sign of the exponential argument.
  • I checked the repaired formula throughout the interval and distinguished this check from the initial condition check.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you recognize a first-order linear differential equation, even when its input varies?

After normalization, it has the form y′ + p(t)y = q(t), with p and q known. The quantity y and its derivative appear raised to the power 1; q can depend on time. This does not require a straight-line solution.

Recall 2Which identity determines the choice of integrating factor?

The product rule gives (μy)′ = μy′ + μ′y. We therefore want μ′ = pμ to obtain (μy)′ = μq. Choosing μ(t) = exp of the integral of p from t₀ to t gives a positive factor equal to 1 at the start.

Recall 3How can you reconstruct the solution from the product and the initial value?

Integrate (μy)′ = μq from t₀ to t. Since μ(t₀) = 1, μ(t)y(t) equals y₀ plus the integral of μq. Then divide this entire result by μ(t), which does not vanish. p and q are assumed to be continuous on the interval.

Recall 4In the tank, what does the initial condition determine, and how can you check the answer?

In A + Bt + C exp(−pt), it determines C = m(0) − A. Check the initial value, then the identity m′ + pm = a + bt throughout the interval. A correct slope at a single instant does not replace this identity.

Recall 5Which units prevent confusion between salt loss, mass and the integrating factor?

m is in g, m′ and q are in g/min, and p is in inverse minutes. The product pt and the integral of p with respect to time are dimensionless, so μ is too. The outgoing salt flow rate is Dm/V, in g/min; D alone is a water flow rate in L/min.

Reconstruct the reasoning

On paper, reconstruct the following in this order: the inflow minus outflow balance; the form y′ + py = q and its assumptions; the choice μ′ = pμ; the derivative of the product; its integral with the initial value; division by μ.
