Level 2 · C066·M2

Collinearity

Your goalDetect redundant information in a regression and distinguish coefficient uniqueness, stability, and agreement of predictions.

Before you start
c065-m5 : prediction interval · c050-m5 : conditioning · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c066-m1 : matrix OLS

✿01 See the idea

A workshop wants to predict the time needed to prepare its parcels from their mass and volume. For the observed parcels, the volume is always 2 litres per kilogram. When mass changes, volume follows exactly: the table gives two columns, but no opportunity to see one vary independently of the other.

One calculation can attribute all the additional time to mass. Another can split this additional time between mass and volume. If the predictions are the same for every observed parcel, the times cannot distinguish between these explanations. This is the problem of exact collinearity: one regression column can be reconstructed from the others.

Near collinearity leaves a small difference between the columns. The coefficients can then be unique while changing substantially when a time changes slightly. We will compare these situations in the same workshop, without treating a good fit as evidence of a separate effect of mass or volume.

All masses, volumes, and times on this page are fictional. The workshop provides no actual readings. The variants will change a single reading in this same table at a time.

The rule to remember

Look for what the columns can actually distinguish. Agreement of predictions on the observed parcels guarantees neither unique coefficients nor reliable separate effects.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Predict preparation time without being able to separate its contributions

The fictional table contains 3 parcels. Parcel A weighs 1 kg, occupies 2 L, and takes 4 min; B: 2 kg, 4 L, 7 min; C: 3 kg, 6 L, 10 min. Let us fit a time using a constant, a mass term, and a volume term.

  1. Define the symbols, units, and problem

    For parcel i, xᵢ is its mass in kg, zᵢ its volume in L, yᵢ the observed time in min, and ŷᵢ its prediction in min. The symbol i identifies a parcel among the n = 3 rows. The additive model is ŷᵢ = a + bxᵢ + czᵢ: a is a baseline time in min, b a coefficient in min/kg, and c a coefficient in min/L. The values can be real numbers; no sign is imposed on the coefficients.

    X=[112124136],β=[abc],y=[4710]

    X is the matrix of explanatory variables: each row contains the constant 1, followed by mass and volume. β is the column of coefficients and y the column of times. In the matrices, we write the numerical values in the stated units. The constant column allows for a; it does not count kilograms.

    S(β)=∑i=1n(yi−a−bxi−czi)2

    S is the sum of squared residuals, in min²; a residual is the observed time minus its prediction. The symbol ∑ means to add over the parcels. Least squares seeks the coefficients that minimize S. We assume the inputs are fixed and this additive model has been chosen. No noise distribution is needed to show the algebraic redundancy; we calculate neither an interval nor a causal effect here.

  2. Show the dependence between columns

    Write k = 2 L/kg. For each row, zᵢ − kxᵢ equals 0, 0, then 0 L. The volume column is therefore k times the mass column. Rank is the number of independent columns: here 2, since the constant and a varying mass suffice, whereas X has 3 columns.

    X[0−21]=[000]

    The column on the left, denoted h, is a nonzero direction of numerical coefficients that changes no prediction: Xh is 0. The symbol ᵀ denotes transposition, which exchanges rows and columns. Then (XᵀX)h is also 0: XᵀX is not invertible. The least squares formula based on its inverse does not apply. This failure comes from the available information, even with exact arithmetic.

    More generally, dependence can involve a combination of several variables and the constant, without a simply proportional pair. Examining only pairwise correlations is therefore insufficient in a larger model.

  3. Calculate what remains identifiable

    Substitute kx for z in the prediction: a + bx + cz = a + (b + kc)x. Write θ = b + kc, in min/kg. The parcels allow us to estimate θ, the slope along their mass-volume relation; they do not tell us how to split it between b and c.

    θ=10−43−1=62=3 min/kg

    We divide the change in time by the change in mass, because only this direction is observed. Parcel A then gives a = 4 − 3 × 1 = 1 min. Check on B: a + θx = 1 + 3 × 2 = 7 min, exactly its time. Here the middle parcel confirms the line determined by the endpoints.

  4. Construct several solutions and check that they attain the minimum

    Allocation A chooses b = 3 min/kg and c = 0 min/L. For B, its mass contribution is 6 min, so its predicted time is 7 min. Allocation B chooses c = 1 min/L; to preserve θ, it takes b = 3 − 2 × 1 = 1 min/kg. For the same parcel, the contributions are 2 min and 4 min; including the baseline, 1 + 6 = 7 min.

    Both allocations predict 4, 7, and 10 min for parcels A, B, and C respectively. All their residuals equal 0 min, so S equals 0 min². A sum of squares cannot be negative: each allocation therefore attains a global minimum.

    a=1,b=θ−kt,c=t

    Here t is any real coefficient in min/L, a retains its unit min, and b its unit min/kg. This family preserves b + kc = θ. There are therefore infinitely many optimal coefficients, but a single column of predictions on the observations. Software can choose a member of this family by convention; that choice provides no new information about the physical role of the variables.

  5. Distinguish exact dependence from small separation

    Return to the table and change only the volume of B to 4.1 L. Its departure from the initial relation is δ = 0.1 L. The masses and the endpoint parcels remain the same. The matrix now has rank 3: the affine volume-mass line through the endpoints would require 4 L in the middle and cannot give 4.1 L.

    However, we have little information for separating the contributions. In this table with full rank, the 3 equations can be satisfied exactly by the 3 coefficients. The mass of parcel B is the mean of the endpoint masses. Subtracting the mean of the endpoint equations from the middle equation cancels a and b. Define d as the difference between the middle time and this mean, in min. The same volume contrast equals δ, in L.

    d=yB−yA+yC2=cδ⇒c=dδ

    With the initial times, the mean of the endpoint times is (4 + 10)/2 = 7 min. Thus d = 7 − 7 = 0 min, c = 0 min/L, then b = 3 min/kg and a = 1 min. This solution is unique: there is now a small usable difference between the columns.

  6. Measure sensitivity by changing the middle time

    Keep the volume 4.1 L and increase only the time for B by 0.1 min: it becomes 7.1 min. The endpoints have not changed. The new contrast is d = 7.1 − 7 = 0.1 min.

    c=0.10.1=1 min/L,b=3−2×1=1 min/kg

    The baseline remains 1 min. Check on B: its mass contribution is 2 min and its volume contribution 4.1 min. Thus 1 + 6.1 = 7.1 min, exactly the modified time. At the endpoints, b + kc retains the value 3 min/kg: their predictions remain 4 and 10 min.

    The volume coefficient has changed from 0 to 1 min/L and the mass coefficient from 3 to 1 min/kg, for a change of 0.1 min. Division by δ explains this sensitivity: for a fixed change in time, a smaller separation would amplify it further. This is a conditioning problem, meaning sensitivity of the solution to the inputs, even before rounding errors.

    These coefficients are unique in each variant. Their sensitivity is therefore distinct from the non-uniqueness in the initial table. With 3 observations and 3 coefficients in these variants, the exact fit leaves no residual information for estimating noise dispersion: this calculation serves as an algebraic demonstration, rather than predictive validation.

  7. Check the scope of the predictions

    Return to the two allocations for the initial table. A hypothetical parcel of 2 kg and 5 L no longer satisfies z = kx. Allocation A still predicts 7 min. Allocation B gives 1 + 2 + 5 = 8 min, a difference of 1 min.

    This parcel remains within the separate observed ranges of mass and volume, but departs from their joint relation. The initial data cannot decide between these times. A prediction can remain stable along the observed line without being determined away from it. This agreement in the table also does not establish the true time for a future parcel.

  8. Choose a remedy that matches the question

    If volume merely repeats mass and the objective remains to predict times for comparable parcels, retaining one variable may suffice: ŷ = a + θx. We then report a combined slope, without relabelling it as the separate effect of mass. Checking new predictions would still require other parcels.

    If the objective is to separate the contributions, we need observations in which mass and volume vary sufficiently independently, together with a defensible model. Adding many parcels that follow exactly the same proportion does not restore rank. Changing units or centering the columns, meaning subtracting their means, does not create this missing variation. Robust numerical methods avoid certain calculation errors; they do not create the absent information.

    Even sufficient separation of the variables does not automatically make their coefficients causal. This unit stops at diagnosing collinearity and its consequences; indicator variables and uncertainty diagnostics follow later in the chapter.

Explain it in your own words

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “0 residuals prove that each coefficient has been estimated well.”

    Allocations A and B have the same 0 residuals and different coefficients. Check the relation b + kc = θ: only this combination is determined in the initial table. A solver convention does not make the parameters identifiable.

    Revisit the family of solutions and the check of the minimum

  • “If the columns are no longer exactly proportional, the problem has disappeared.”

    Distinguish uniqueness from stability. The contrast gives c = d/δ: a small separation δ makes a change in time highly influential. Recalculate the coefficients before and after the perturbation, keeping the same units.

    Repeat the sensitivity calculation in the exercises

  • “Changing units or adding similar parcels creates two separate effects.”

    Rescaling and repeating the same proportion create no independent variation. To separate the contributions, look for parcels that actually break the relation, then check whether the separation is sufficient for the desired accuracy.

    Review the common direction of the parcels

Hint 2

Compare with the solution

Duplicating proportional rows does not change the rank. Changing units does not remove the dependence either; the coefficients must be converted with them. Expressed in the initial units for comparison, allocations A and B predict 7 min and 8 min for the proposed parcel, a difference of 1 min.

This parcel departs from the observed relation. The table cannot decide between the predictions. ✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What information is missing when z = kx for every parcel?

Variation that would allow mass and volume to be separated is missing. The model distinguishes only the combination θ = b + kc; b and c remain non-unique.

Recall 2Why does the direction h for which Xh is 0 prevent XᵀX from being inverted?

h is nonzero, but XᵀX also maps it to the 0 column. An invertible matrix cannot map a nonzero vector to 0. Adding a multiple of h to the coefficients does not change the predictions.

Recall 3In the variant, why does c = d/δ reveal sensitivity?

d is the time contrast that eliminates the baseline and mass; δ is the volume contrast. A small separation δ can amplify a small change in time. Coefficient uniqueness therefore does not guarantee stability.

Recall 4Do identical predictions on the observed parcels guarantee identical predictions for every new combination?

No. The coefficients can compensate for each other along the observed relation, then yield different predictions as soon as we move away from it, even within the separate ranges of each variable.

Recall 5What should we choose depending on whether we want to predict times for comparable parcels or separate the contributions?

To predict along the same relation, one variable can carry the combined slope, to be checked on new parcels. Separating the contributions requires distinct and sufficient variation. Rescaling does not create this information.
