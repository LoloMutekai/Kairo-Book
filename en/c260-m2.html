Level 4 · C260·M2

Kernel

Your goalConstruct an event’s contribution from its age, then add these contributions to calculate a conditional intensity.

Before you start
c226-m5 : integration against jumps · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c259-m5 : simulation and limitations · c090-m5 : stationarity · c260-m1 : conditional intensity

✿01 See the idea

At a repair shop, repair tickets sometimes arrive in clusters. To predict the rate of new requests, we want to give more weight to a ticket just received than to an old ticket. The kernel is the rule that turns an event’s age into a contribution to the arrival rate.

Imagine that each ticket leaves behind a ramp that slopes down over time. At a given instant, we read the remaining height of each ramp, then add these heights to the background rate. The graph of a ramp describes the effect of a single ticket; the total rate also depends on the arrival times of all previous tickets.

The setting, arrival times and parameters are fictional. We choose a ramp that reaches 0 after 4 min, with an initial height of 0.50 ticket/min in the limit just after arrival. This shape allows calculations with pencil and paper. A Hawkes kernel need not be decreasing or have a finite duration; these properties belong to our chosen kernel.

The weight depends on the time elapsed since each arrival, rather than the absolute time of that arrival. Receiving the same ticket more recently changes its contribution. This dependence imposed by the model does not demonstrate that the tickets actually cause one another.

The rule to remember

Select events strictly before the current time; calculate their ages; apply the kernel with its boundaries; sum the contributions, then add the background rate.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From arrival times to the repair shop’s intensity

The history is empty when the shop opens, at t = 0 min. The only arrivals before t = 4 min are A at 1 min and B at 3 min. The background rate μ is 1/4 = 0.25 ticket/min; the height a is 1/2 = 0.50 ticket/min; the support duration L is 4 min. Let us calculate the rate at this instant using the triangular kernel.

  1. Define the kernel and its assumptions

    t is the instant at which we calculate the rate; tᵢ is the arrival time of ticket i; u = t − tᵢ is its age. t, tᵢ, u and L are expressed in minutes. h(u) is the contribution of an event of age u, in ticket/min. a, its limit from the right at age 0, and μ, the background rate, are also in ticket/min. The ratio u/L is dimensionless.

    h(u)={a(1−uL)si 0<u<L0sinon

    We choose a to be nonnegative and L to be strictly positive. The kernel is causal: contributions come only from events that have already occurred. It is nonnegative, bounded and integrable. All tickets have the same weight and are observed immediately, with no omissions or simultaneous arrivals. Over the time horizon studied, the history contains a finite number of events.

    We set h(0) = 0 for the convention of a predictable rate, calculated before the current arrival. The limit of h(u) as u approaches 0 through positive values is a: just after a new arrival, its contribution is almost a. The value at the single point 0 does not change an integral. At u = L, the ramp reaches 0 without a jump.

  2. Connect the kernel to the event history

    λ(t), lambda, denotes the conditional intensity introduced earlier, relative to the history available strictly before t. For a linear Hawkes process with a single event type, the model adds the same kernel to the background rate, shifted to each arrival time. The symbol Σ means to sum over all indices i whose arrival time tᵢ is strictly before t.

    λ(t)=μ+∑ti<th(t−ti)

    This sum is also the integration of the weight h(t − s) against the arrival count by summing over jumps: each unit jump at time s contributes a copy of the weight at that age. Here, s denotes a past time. The formula evaluates a rate for a given history; it does not provide a prediction of all future arrivals.

    The temporal support of our kernel is the range of ages over which it can contribute, between 0 and L. We therefore need only keep events with t − L < tᵢ < t; at the older boundary, the contribution is already 0. With no event in this range, λ(t) returns to μ. No assumption of stationarity is used for this calculation at a single instant; long-term stability will be studied in the next unit.

  3. Convert arrival times into ages

    For A: u = 4 − 1 = 3 min. For B: u = 4 − 3 = 1 min. These ages are strictly between 0 and 4 min: both contributions follow the sloping part of the kernel.

    Why subtract in this order? We want to find how much time has elapsed up to now. Substituting the arrival times 1 and 3 directly into h would reverse the tickets’ weights here. An event merely expected at 5 min is not in the past at 4 min: we exclude it before summing.

  4. Evaluate the contributions separately

    The age/duration ratio measures the fraction of the duration that has elapsed. For A, it is 3/4 = 3/4; the fraction of height remaining is 1 − 3/4 = 1/4. For B, these fractions are 1/4 and 3/4, respectively. Let us multiply each remaining fraction by the height a.

    h(3)=12×14=1/8 ticket/min
    h(1)=1/2×3/4=3/8 ticket/min

    A therefore contributes 0.125 ticket/min; B contributes 0.375 ticket/min. The kernel is neither the probability that a ticket causes a request nor a density whose integral must equal unity: its value has units of a rate. The decrease chosen here expresses an effect that weakens with age.

  5. Sum and add the background rate

    The contributions have the same units, so we can add them. Their sum is 1/8 + 3/8 = 1/2 ticket/min. This additional rate does not yet include the background rate. Let us add μ only once.

    λ(4)=1/4+1/8+3/8=3/4 ticket/min

    The result is 0.75 ticket/min. It describes the local rate that the model assigns to this history. It does not guarantee any number of tickets or, on its own, give an exact probability over the next minute, because the rate will change as events age and new arrivals occur.

  6. Check using another expression and the boundaries

    On the sloping part, h(u) = a − (a/L)u. The loss of height per minute of age is a/L = 1/8 ticket per minute per minute. For A: 1/2 − 1/8 × 3 = 1/8 ticket/min; for B: 1/2 − 1/8 × 1 = 3/8 ticket/min. We recover the results without using the fractions of duration remaining.

    Each contribution lies between 0 and a; the older ticket has the smaller contribution, as the graph shows. At age L = 4 min, the contribution becomes 0. Beyond this age, we keep 0 rather than extending the straight line to negative values. If the relevant history is empty, the sum vanishes and the rate remains μ = 0.25 ticket/min.

    Finally, shifting all arrival times and the observation time by the same duration preserves the differences t − tᵢ and therefore the contributions, provided that the background rate and the relative history remain unchanged. This check verifies that we used ages. It does not demonstrate stationarity of the process.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations and describe how a ticket leads to its contribution. Why should you neither apply h to its absolute arrival time nor add the background rate for each ticket? Also explain the difference between h at age 0 and its limit just afterwards.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I put the arrival time into the kernel.”

    First calculate the age t − tᵢ. At 4 min, the ticket that arrived at 1 min is 3 min old. h describes elapsed time, rather than the clock reading at arrival.

    Review the conversion from arrival times to ages

  • “I extend the straight line beyond L; the old ticket then reduces the rate.”

    The sloping formula has a domain. From age 4 min onwards, our kernel is 0. A negative contribution obtained by extending the line no longer matches the specified nonnegative kernel.

    Test ageing in the waiting exercise

  • “The same initial height means the same effect, whatever the delays.”

    The height a describes only a limit just after arrival. The duration L determines how long the effect persists and enters every weight. Compare durations of 4 min and 8 min for the same tickets.

    Review the ramp before comparing durations

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · read a kernel

A ticket halfway through the duration

In the same repair shop, with a = 0.50 ticket/min and L = 4 min, calculate the contribution of a ticket aged 2 min. Also give the contribution at age L and explain why the answer halfway through is not a.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Check the domain of the age before choosing a branch of the formula.

Hint 2

The age/duration ratio is 1/2. What fraction of the height remains?

Compare with the solution

The age 2 min is within the support duration. The ratio 2/4 is 1/2; the remaining fraction is 1 − 1/2 = 1/2. The contribution is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 = 0.25 ticket/min.

At age L, the contribution is 0 ticket/min. The height a is the initial limit, rather than a constant weight throughout the duration. Check: the value halfway through is the average of the limiting heights a and 0, because the segment is straight.

Check your reasoning

  • Choose the branch according to the age and obtain 1/4 ticket/min.
  • Distinguish the initial height, the remaining height and the boundary where the contribution reaches 0.

Exercise 2 · Application · recalculate the whole sum

Wait without any new arrivals

Start again from tickets A and B in the example. No other ticket arrives up to t = 6 min. Using the initial parameters, recalculate the ages, contributions and intensity. Explain why we cannot subtract a single slope from the total rate throughout the wait.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The ages become 6 − 1 and 6 − 3. One of them exceeds the support duration.

Hint 2

Once a contribution reaches 0, it stops decreasing. The background rate remains constant.

Compare with the solution

A is 5 min old, beyond L = 4 min: its contribution is 0 ticket/min. B is 3 min old; it has 1 min of its duration left, a fraction of 1/4. Its contribution is 1/2 × 1/4 = 1/8 ticket/min.

The total additional rate is 1/8 ticket/min. The rate is therefore 1/4 + 1/8 = 3/8 = 0.375 ticket/min. It is smaller than 0.75 ticket/min, but remains above the background rate because B still contributes.

Each active ramp loses 1/8 ticket/min with each minute of age. Before A’s contribution reaches 0, both ramps decrease; afterwards, only B’s ramp decreases. The slope of the sum changes when a ramp reaches 0. Extending the line for A would produce a negative contribution and an incorrect rate.

Check your reasoning

  • Recalculate each age and explicitly set A’s contribution to 0.
  • Obtain 3/8 ticket/min while retaining the background rate.
  • Explain the change in slope using the number of ramps still active.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · discuss a change of kernel

The same height, a longer memory

At t = 4 min, keep exactly the same tickets A and B, background rate μ and height a. A colleague replaces L = 4 min with L′ = 8 min and claims that the rate does not change because the initial height is the same. Predict the direction of the change, then calculate the contributions and the new rate. Can we conclude that this kernel is better for the repair shop?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The ages remain the same. The age/duration fraction becomes smaller at a fixed positive age.

Hint 2

You can calculate the remaining height using a × (L′ − u)/L′. The prime here denotes the new duration.

Compare with the solution

At a fixed positive age, increasing the duration while keeping a unchanged increases the remaining height. For A: (L′ − u)/L′ = 5/8 = 5/8; its contribution is 5/16 ticket/min. For B: 7/8 = 7/8; its contribution is 7/16 ticket/min.

The sum is 5/16 + 7/16 = 3/4 ticket/min. With the background rate, the rate is 1/4 + 3/4 = 1 ticket/min, instead of 3/4 ticket/min. The same initial height therefore does not mean the same contribution at a given age.

This result does not rank the kernels by their quality in practice. The same arrival times have been evaluated under different assumptions; no estimation or comparison with held-out observations has been performed. A higher rate is not a demonstrated improvement. The cumulative effect and its long-term consequences will be studied later.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the ages and change only the duration.
  • Obtain the contributions 5/16 and 7/16, then 1 ticket/min.
  • Distinguish the mathematical change in rate from the quality of the model for a real repair shop.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the kernel take as input and return for each ticket?

It takes the age u = t − tᵢ in minutes and returns the contribution h(u) to the rate, in ticket/min. Evaluate each event separately, then sum.

Recall 2Which boundaries should you check before using the sloping part?

The event must be strictly before t, and its age must be less than L. Our kernel is 0 for u ≤ 0 or u ≥ L. At age 0, the chosen value is 0, while the limit from the right is a.

Recall 3How do you go from the kernel to the intensity for the entire history?

Calculate h(t − tᵢ) for each past event, add the contributions, then add the background rate μ only once. The kernel is the rule for an individual event; λ is the total rate conditional on the history.

Recall 4Why is the initial height insufficient to describe our kernel?

The duration L also determines how long the effect lasts and how quickly it decreases. For fixed a, increasing L preserves more height at every positive age already within the support duration. A general kernel can have a shape other than this ramp.

Recall 5What does the rate calculated using the fictional tickets fail to prove?

It proves neither an exact probability over a minute, nor a guaranteed future count, nor actual causality between tickets, nor the quality or long-term stability of the model.
