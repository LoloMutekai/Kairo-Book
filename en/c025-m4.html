Cost recurrences

Your goalWrite the cost of a recursive process, expand it down to the base case, and check the total with a different count.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

An archive workshop prepares a batch of 8 index cards for filing. It follows a repeated rule: as long as a batch contains more than 1 card, mark each card, split the batch into 2 equal halves, then apply the same rule to each half. A single card receives its final mark, and processing of that batch stops. The scene and values are fictional.

Recursion means applying the same process to smaller batches. A call is an application of the rule to a batch. Its total cost includes everything its smaller batches will do; its local cost counts only the marks it adds itself. The marks made before splitting and the final mark are separate actions.

Counting only the divisions along the path of a single card leaves out the other cards. We must account for the costs of the 2 halves and include the cost of the pass through the current batch. A cost recurrence expresses this addition precisely: the cost of a problem is described in terms of the costs of smaller problems.

We count marks rather than seconds. In this model, a mark always has the same cost; moving and splitting the cards do not contribute to the chosen count. This gives an exact count of the marks without describing the entire time taken to file the cards. Batch sizes are obtained by repeatedly doubling 1, so that the halves always have whole numbers of cards.

The rule to remember

Define what is counted; write the base case; add the costs of all subproblems and the local work; check the total by grouping the actions differently.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

How many marks for 8 cards?

Each pass through a batch containing more than a single card adds c = 1 mark per card. Processing a single card costs b = 1 final mark. All smaller batches are processed completely, one after another. Let us calculate the total for the initial batch of 8 cards.

  1. Define the count and the stopping point

    n is the whole number of cards in the batch. T(n) denotes the total number of marks produced by processing it, including its smaller batches. The parentheses indicate the size supplied to the cost function T; they do not mean multiplication. b is the cost of the base case, in marks; c is the cost of a local pass, in marks per card. In this scene, b = 1 mark and c = 1 mark per card.

    A single card is not split and receives only its final mark. So T(1) = b = 1 mark. We do not model an empty batch. For the chosen sizes, every split strictly reduces the size until it reaches 1: the process terminates.

    T(1)=b

  2. Express what a call does

    For n greater than 1, the 2 halves each have n/2 cards. Processing each half costs T(n/2) marks. These 2 costs must be added, even though the calls use the same rule. Marking the current batch adds c × n marks; this local work is not included in the processing of the halves.

    The equality is exact for our count and for sizes that can be split this way down to 1. It measures neither the movement of cards, nor the time taken by a call, nor the memory used.

    T(n)=2T(n2)+cn pour n>1

  3. Solve the smallest split

    The sizes encountered are 8, 4, 2, then 1. Let us start at the stopping point and work back up: the batch of 2 cards makes calls for 2 single cards, whose cost is already known.

    Processing the single cards costs 2 × 1 = 2 marks. The local pass adds 1 × 2 = 2 marks. Thus T(2) = 2 + 2 = 4 marks.

    T(2)=2×T(1)+1×2=4 marques

  4. Work back up to the intermediate batch

    Each half of the batch of 4 cards is a batch of 2 cards. Its total cost is 4 marks, rather than just 2 marks. The 2 processes therefore cost 2 × 4 = 8 marks.

    The pass through the current batch also costs 1 × 4 = 4 marks. We obtain T(4) = 8 + 4 = 12 marks.

  5. Return to the initial batch

    The 2 halves cost 2 × 12 = 24 marks. The pass through the initial batch itself adds 1 × 8 = 8 marks. The required cost is therefore 24 + 8 = 32 marks.

    We have replaced each unknown cost with a cost for a smaller size until reaching a known value. Without the base value, the relation between sizes would not be enough to determine this total.

    T(8)=2×12+8=32 marques

  6. Check by grouping the actions differently

    Now let us count the actions by row in the diagram. The initial batch adds 8 marks. The next 2 batches add 2 × 4 = 8. The next 4 batches add 4 × 2 = 8. That gives 24 marks before the single cards.

    The 8 single cards add 8 × 1 = 8 final marks. The check gives 24 + 8 = 32 marks, matching the previous calculation working back up. Here we add the local costs of all rows; we never add the total cost of a call to those of its descendants.

  7. Express the same reasoning for a size obtained by repeated doubling

    k denotes the number of divisions by 2 needed to go from n to 1; it is a count with no unit. We write n = 2ᵏ, meaning that we multiply 1 by 2, k times. The case k = 0 directly represents a single card.

    In each row before the stopping point, the smaller batches together contain n cards: that row therefore adds c × n marks. There are k such rows, then n single cards costing b marks each. Hence T(n) = n × (c × k + b). This result is justified for these sizes and for the stated marking rule.

    n=2k, T(n)=n(ck+b)

    For our batch, k = 3. Each card receives 3 marks from the passes and 1 final mark, giving 4 marks. The total is 8 × 4 = 32 marks. With k = 0, the same formula gives b again; this check confirms that the base cost has not disappeared.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the calculations, explain why the factor 2 multiplies T, why the local term is added, and why the number of divisions is not enough to give the total cost.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “There are only 3 divisions, so the cost is 3 marks.”

    3 counts the splits along a path. The other smaller batches are also processed. Return to the rows: each contains all the batches at that depth and counts its own marks.

    Compare the splitting with the count by row

  • “The smaller batches account for everything: T(n) = 2 × T(n/2).”

    This expression leaves out the pass through the current batch. Note separately what it does itself: c × n marks, then add this term to the costs of the smaller batches. Identify the 8 local marks of the initial batch again.

    Review how a call is expressed as a recurrence

  • “Stopping means doing nothing: T(1) = 0.”

    The base case stops making calls but produces a final mark. It costs b = 1 mark. Forgetting these actions would leave out 8 marks from the total. The exercise on changing b shows where this cost contributes.

    Check the role of the cost of single cards

Hint 1

Count the smaller batches actually processed after each split.

Hint 2

For the smallest split, each smaller batch already has the size of the base case.

Compare with the solution

The base case is correct, but the cost of a half is missing. The relation becomes T(n) = 2 × T(n/2) + c × n for n greater than 1. The factor counts the smaller batches; the argument gives their size; the last term accounts for the pass through the current batch.

T(2) = 2 × 1 + 1 × 2 = 2 + 2 = 4 marks. This total includes the final marks and the pass before splitting.

Check your reasoning

  • Write the factor for the number of smaller batches and their size, distinguishing between them.
  • Keep the local cost and the base case.
  • Give the cost in marks and explain the actions counted.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and checking

Prepare a larger batch

The same workshop receives 16 cards with the same costs b and c. Calculate T(16) by reusing the result for 8 cards. Check it by counting the rows before the stopping point and the final marks.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The first split gives 2 batches of 8 cards; the cost of the pass through the large batch still needs to be included.

Hint 2

Add a division to those in the example to reach a single card.

Compare with the solution

The smaller batches cost 2 × 32 = 64 marks. The pass through the initial batch costs 1 × 16 = 16 marks. So T(16) = 64 + 16 = 80 marks.

The number of divisions is now 4. Each card receives 4 marks from the passes, then 1 final mark, giving 5 marks. Check: 16 × 5 = 80 marks. Doubling the size also adds a row of work, so the total is more than double the total in the example.

Check your reasoning

  • Reuse the total cost of the smaller batch without replacing it with its size.
  • Add the pass through the large batch.
  • Obtain the same total using the number of marks received by each card.

Exercise 3 · Variation and justification

Change only the final action

Return to the batch of 8 cards. Each single card now receives 3 final marks instead of 1; the passes before splitting stay the same. What is the new total cost? Explain why we do not multiply the entire old total by 3.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Separate the cost before reaching the single cards from the cost of their final actions.

Hint 2

The number of single cards stays the same; only their contribution increases.

Compare with the solution

The passes before the stopping point still cost 24 marks. The 8 single cards now cost 8 × 3 = 24 marks. The total becomes 24 + 24 = 48 marks.

Another check: each single card adds 3 − 1 = 2 marks compared with the old rule. The additional cost is 8 × 2 = 16 marks. Old total plus additional cost: 32 + 16 = 48 marks. Multiplying the entire cost would also change the passes, which the problem keeps the same.

The recurrence for sizes greater than 1 is unchanged; T(1), now equal to 3 marks, is what changes. The same relation between sizes can therefore lead to different costs if its base case changes.

Check your reasoning

  • Change only the base value and keep the local work.
  • Count all the single cards.
  • Check using the additional cost and explain why the other actions stay the same.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What information makes a cost recurrence usable?

The chosen count and its assumptions, the base case with its cost, the number and size of the subproblems, and the local work added to them. We must also check that the sizes reach the base case.

Recall 2In 2 × T(n/2), what do the two occurrences of 2 represent?

The outside factor counts the 2 smaller batches processed. The divisor in the argument describes the size of each. Knowing the reduction in size alone does not tell us how many subproblems are executed.

Recall 3How can we check the cost of the 8 cards without working back up through the T values again?

Count the local costs of all batches row by row: 8, then 8, then 8 marks, and finally 8 final marks. This gives 32 marks, counting each action exactly once.

Recall 4Why does the base case have a cost even though it stops the recursion?

Stopping the calls still allows a final action. Here each single card receives b final marks. This cost must be accounted for each single card; the relation for large batches alone does not determine it.

Recall 5What can we conclude from an exact total in marks?

It counts the actions defined by the model, for the stated sizes and rule. It does not give an actual duration: moving, splitting, and other costs have been excluded from the count.

What you understand
A recursive cost adds the work done by the call itself and by all its executed subproblems. The depth of a path is not enough to count the total work. The batch of cards provides a counting model.
