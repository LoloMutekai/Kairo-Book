Branching ratio

Your goalCalculate the branching ratio of a self-exciting process with a rectangular kernel, deduce the mean rate and mean cascade size, then state what the mean rate alone cannot identify.

Before you start
c226-m5 : integration with jumps · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c259-m5 : simulation and limitations · c090-m5 : stationarity · c260-m1 : conditional intensity · c260-m2 : kernel

✿01 See the idea

A fictional monitoring system produces alerts. Some appear with no visible cause in the log: we call these background alerts. Others follow a previous alert by a few minutes, because one incident leads to others. The values on this page are invented for the calculations; they do not describe any real system.

After each alert, the intensity of future alerts rises for a while, then falls back. The branching ratio answers a single question: on average, how many alerts does one alert trigger directly? As long as the answer stays below 1, each cascade dies out on average. When it reaches 1 or more, the mean no longer stabilizes.

The background therefore does not account for all the activity in the log: cascades multiply its contribution. With a background rate of 0.25 alerts per minute and a branching ratio of 0.6, we will see that the log contains more alerts than the background alone produces, even though the background has not changed.

To keep the calculations exact, the kernel here is a rectangle: after an alert, the intensity increases by a constant height h for a duration L, then this contribution stops. Other kernel shapes exist; the area under the curve always answers the question being asked.

The rule to remember

The branching ratio is the area under the kernel, a dimensionless number; if it stays strictly below 1, the mean rate equals the background rate divided by the quantity 1 minus this ratio.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A fictional alert log over four hours

The fictional log has a background rate μ = 0.25 alerts per minute and a rectangular kernel of height h = 0.05 alerts per minute for L = 12 minutes. We read the intensity at two times, calculate the branching ratio, the mean rate and the mean cascade size, then check each result.

  1. Define each quantity and its assumptions

    Time t is measured in minutes from the start of the log; past alerts occurred at times tk. The conditional intensity λ(t) is the intensity of alerts at time t, given the past; it is expressed in alerts per minute.

    The background rate μ is the intensity of alerts that do not come from any past alert: it is constant, strictly positive, and expressed in alerts per minute. The kernel φ(s) is the intensity added by a past alert, s minutes after it, in alerts per minute; it is never negative. Here φ(s) equals h if s is strictly positive and at most L, and 0 elsewhere. The model adds up the contributions of all past alerts:

    λ(t)=μ+∑tk<tφ(t−tk)

    Assumptions: a single kind of alert; contributions add up; the same μ and φ at all times; a stationary regime, specified in step five. No other assumptions are made about how the alerts are actually produced.

  2. Read the intensity from a history

    Suppose there are three past alerts, at times 0, 5 and 9 minutes. At time t = 10, the times since those alerts are 10, 5 and 1 minutes: all are at most L = 12, so each alert adds h. The intensity is μ + 3 × h = 0.25 + 0.15 = 0.40 alerts per minute.

    At time t = 14, the time since the first alert is 14 minutes, greater than L: its contribution is 0. Two contributions remain: μ + 2 × h = 0.25 + 0.10 = 0.35 alerts per minute. An alert therefore raises the intensity only for 12 minutes; this is the memory of the kernel.

  3. Calculate the area under the kernel

    An intensity multiplied by a duration gives a mean number of alerts. The total contribution of an alert is therefore the area under its kernel: this is the branching ratio n.

    n=∫0∞φ(s)ds=h×L

    Calculation: n = 0.05 × 12 = 0.6. Units: alerts per minute multiplied by minutes give alerts, here per parent alert; n is a dimensionless number. Interpretation: on average, one alert directly triggers 0.6 other alerts; this is neither a probability nor a guaranteed number of children.

    For an exponential kernel with amplitude a and decay rate b, the same area equals a divided by b. We keep the rectangle here, whose area can be read without finding an antiderivative.

  4. Examine the stability condition

    Calculate 1 − n = 1 − 0.6 = 0.4. This number is strictly positive. Each generation is, on average, smaller than the previous one by a factor n. If n were 1 or more, a generation would not be smaller than the previous one and the mean cascade would not die out; the required condition is therefore n < 1.

  5. Deduce the stationary mean rate

    Let Λ denote the mean alert rate, in alerts per minute, in a stationary regime: its value does not depend on the observation period. In this regime, the mean intensity equals Λ. The mean contribution of the kernels is the alert rate Λ multiplied by the area n of each kernel. Equating the means therefore gives:

    Λ=μ+nΛ⟹Λ=μ1−n

    Calculation: Λ = 0.25 ÷ 0.4 = 0.625 alerts per minute. Check: the mean contribution of the kernels is 0.6 × 0.625, and adding the background rate 0.25 gives exactly the rate 0.625. This derivation requires μ > 0, a positive kernel, stationarity and n < 1.

  6. Count over a window of four hours

    The window lasts 240 minutes. Expected background alerts: 0.25 × 240 = 60. Expected total alerts: 0.625 × 240 = 150. Triggered alerts: 150 − 60 = 90.

    Proportion of triggered alerts: 90 ÷ 150 = 0.6, exactly the value of n. This result is no coincidence: the background accounts for the fraction 1 − n of the mean rate.

  7. Follow the cascade generation by generation

    The 60 background alerts form generation 0. Each triggers n alerts on average: generation 1, 60 × 0.6 = 36; generation 2, 36 × 0.6 = 21.6; generation 3, 21.6 × 0.6 = 12.96. The sum of generations 0 through 2 is 117.60.

    From generation 3 onward, the sequence is geometric with common ratio n and first term 12.96, so its sum is 12.96 ÷ 0.4 = 32.40. The total 117.60 + 32.40 = 150 agrees with 150. In a single formula, the mean size of a cascade originating from a background alert is:

    1+n+n2+⋯=11−n

    Here it is 2.5 alerts; for 60 background alerts, 60 × 2.5 again gives 150.

  8. Check the results and state the limitations

    Three methods give 150 alerts: the mean rate multiplied by the duration, the background count multiplied by the mean cascade size, and the sum of the generations. They describe means under the model. A real window of 240 minutes would fluctuate around these means, and nothing indicates that a real system follows this kernel or this stationarity assumption. The calculation validates the arithmetic, not a model.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The branching ratio is a rate, in alerts per minute.”

    It is the area under the kernel: an intensity in alerts per minute multiplied by a duration in minutes. It counts triggered alerts per parent alert and is therefore dimensionless. Repeat the unit analysis for the area calculation.

    Review the area calculation

  • “A ratio below 1 means that an alert triggers at most one other alert.”

    The ratio is a mean. A given alert may trigger 0, 1 or several others; only the mean of these counts equals n. Reread the interpretation of the kernel in the intuition section.

    Reread the intuition

  • “The mean rate equals the background rate multiplied by the quantity 1 plus the ratio.”

    This formula counts only direct children: it gives 1.6 × 0.25 = 0.40 alerts per minute instead of 0.625. The children themselves have children, giving the sequence 1 + n + n² + … and the quotient 1 ÷ (1 − n).

    Test the formula in the exercises

Exercise 1 · Explained application

A lower kernel

In the same fictional setting, the kernel height becomes h′ as shown below; L = 12 minutes and μ = 0.25 alerts per minute remain unchanged. Calculate the branching ratio, the mean rate and the mean number of alerts over 240 minutes, then check using the proportion of triggered alerts.

h′=124 par minute

Hint 2

Then calculate one minus n and divide the background rate by this number.

Compare with the solution

Ratio: n = 12 ÷ 24 = 0.5. Then 1 − n = 0.5. Mean rate: 0.25 ÷ 0.5 = 0.5 alerts per minute.

Over 240 minutes: 0.5 × 240 = 120 alerts, including 60 background alerts and 60 triggered alerts. Check: the triggered proportion, 60 out of 120, does equal n′ = 0.5.

Check your reasoning

  • Area calculated using L and h′.
  • One minus n subtracted before division.
  • n is dimensionless, rates are in alerts per minute, and counts are in alerts.
  • Check using the proportion of triggered alerts.

Exercise 2 · Limiting case

When the cascade no longer dies out

Keep h = 0.05 alerts per minute and μ = 0.25. First extend L to 20 minutes, then to 24 minutes. Calculate n in each case. What happens to the equality Λ = μ + nΛ? What would the mean rate formula give for 24 minutes, and why is this result unacceptable?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate both areas, then one minus n in each case.

Hint 2

Can a rate of alerts per minute be negative?

Compare with the solution

For L = 20: n = 0.05 × 20 = 1 and 1 − n = 0. The equality becomes Λ = μ + Λ, so 0 = μ = 0.25, which is false: no finite mean rate satisfies it.

For L = 24: n = 1.2 and 1 − n = -0.2. The formula would give 0.25 ÷ (-0.2) = -1.25 alerts per minute, a negative and therefore impossible value. Conclusion: the formula holds only for n < 1. Beyond that, the stationary regime assumption fails, not the arithmetic.

Check your reasoning

  • Two correct areas with their durations.
  • Equality reduced to 0 = μ at the threshold.
  • Negative rate rejected because of its meaning, not because a calculation was missed.

Exercise 3 · Interpretation

Two explanations for the same mean rate

A fictional log, observed over a long time, has a mean rate of 0.5 alerts per minute. Explanation A: the background rate is 0.25 alerts per minute. Explanation B: the background rate is 0.125 alerts per minute. For each, deduce n from Λ = μ ÷ (1 − n), then the proportion of triggered alerts over 240 minutes. What can be concluded from the mean rate alone?

n=1−μΛ
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Isolate n in the mean rate equality.

Hint 2

For each explanation, compare the number of background alerts with the expected total.

Compare with the solution

A: this is the situation in the previous exercise, so n = 0.5. B: 0.125 ÷ 0.5 = 0.25, so n = 0.75.

The expected total is 120 alerts. The background provides 60 in A and 30 in B; triggered alerts number 60 in A and 90 in B, giving proportions of 0.5 and 0.75.

The two accounts have the same mean rate but different dynamics: in B, most alerts are triggered. With μ unknown, the mean rate leaves μ and n linked by a single relation; a parameter cannot be identified without further information, as in the unit on identifiability. We would need to use the way alerts cluster in time, which this unit does not model.

Check your reasoning

  • n isolated correctly.
  • The two values of n compared with the proportions of triggered alerts.
  • Identifiability conclusion limited to the mean rate alone, with no claim to an estimate.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the branching ratio n measure?

The mean number of events directly triggered by an event: the area under the kernel, which is dimensionless.

Recall 2What happens to the mean rate when n reaches 1?

With a strictly positive background rate, the equality Λ = μ + nΛ no longer has a finite solution: the stationary regime of the model does not exist. The formula μ ÷ (1 − n) holds only for n < 1.

Recall 3How is the stationary mean rate obtained, and under what assumptions?

By equating the mean intensity to μ + nΛ, giving Λ = μ ÷ (1 − n). This requires a positive background rate, a positive kernel, stationarity and n < 1.

Recall 4Does the mean rate alone determine n?

No: if the background rate is unknown, several background rate–branching ratio pairs give the same mean rate. Further information is needed.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define the background rate, kernel and intensity; calculate the area under the kernel to obtain dimensionless n; require n < 1; equate the mean intensity to μ + nΛ to obtain Λ = μ ÷ (1 − n); interpret 1 ÷ (1 − n) as the mean cascade size and n as the proportion of triggered alerts; do not deduce n from the mean rate alone.
