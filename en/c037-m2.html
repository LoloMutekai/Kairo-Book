Time indexes

Your goalFind readings by their time, put the rows in chronological order, and calculate the actual durations between them.

Before you start
c036-m5 : annotated graphs · c035-m5 : finite covariance and conditioning · c037-m1 : tables

✿01 See the idea

A greenhouse sensor sends its readings with a delay. You receive row A first, then B, C, and D. Yet B was measured before A. To describe how the temperature changes, should you follow the arrival of the messages or the time recorded on each one?

A table keeps the columns of each row together: here, an identifier, a timestamp, and a temperature. The timestamp is the date and time assigned to the reading. Our time index is the set of time labels associated with the rows, used to find them by time or by interval. It can be used to order them. Giving a column this role does not automatically sort it.

Throughout this example, the timestamps refer to measurement times on the same fictional day, in UTC, Coordinated Universal Time. We write them more simply as t, the number of minutes elapsed since 10 h 0 min UTC. Thus t = 5 min means 10 h 5 min UTC on that day. The temperature T is expressed in degrees Celsius, written °C. The letters A to D identify the readings and remain attached to them after sorting.

All values are fictional. The time labels are assumed to be valid and comparable; the clocks are assumed to be consistent. In a real file, you must specify the date, time zone, and meaning of the timestamp before comparing times. A local time alone can be ambiguous during a daylight saving time change. We do not cover conversion between time zones here.

The rule to remember

Define which event the timestamp dates, move whole rows when sorting, then use time differences to measure durations.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Find the readings in a time window

The log as received contains A: (5 min; 21 °C), B: (0 min; 20 °C), C: (20 min; 23 °C), D: (10 min; 22 °C). Let us find the readings from 5 min inclusive to 20 min exclusive, then check the durations and the associations.

  1. Define what the index means

    In a pair (t; T), t is the elapsed time in minutes since the common origin, and T is the temperature measured in °C. The semicolon separates the columns. The full timestamps are distinct in this initial log. Their t values are enough to compare them, since they share the same day, origin, and time zone.

    Position is a rank in the list, counted here from 1, with no unit of time. Identifier A remains A even if its position changes. The time label of A remains 5 min: sorting does not redefine the time origin.

  2. Sort the rows, rather than a single column

    Let us compare the times from earliest to latest. B comes before A, followed by D and finally C. We obtain B: (0 min; 20 °C), A: (5 min; 21 °C), D: (10 min; 22 °C), C: (20 min; 23 °C).

    tB<tA<tD<tC

    t with a letter as a subscript denotes the time of the reading identified by that letter. The sign < is read as “strictly less than”. Each temperature stays with its row. If we sorted the times while keeping the temperatures in arrival order, B would incorrectly carry the 21 °C belonging to A.

  3. Calculate the gaps between neighbors

    For readings u followed by v in time order, Δt denotes the duration elapsed from u to v. The letters u and v represent reading identifiers; Δ is read as “change”. Subtract the starting time from the ending time, using the same unit.

    Δt=tv−tu

    B to A: 5 − 0 = 5 min. A to D: 10 − 5 = 5 min. D to C: 20 − 10 = 10 min. There are 3 gaps for 4 readings. A series is regularly spaced when all its successive gaps equal the same strictly positive duration. That is not the case here: 10 ÷ 5 = 2, with no unit.

    Moving from A to B in arrival order would give 0 − 5 = -5 min. This negative sign shows a return to an earlier reading; it does not measure a reception delay. Reception times are not recorded in this log.

  4. Select a window using its bounds

    Let us write a window as [a, b): the square bracket on the left includes the start a, and the parenthesis on the right excludes the end b. Here a and b are elapsed times, in minutes, with the same origin as t. The sign ≤ means “less than or equal to”. A row belongs to the window exactly when:

    a≤t<b

    For [5, 20) min, B is too early because 0 < 5. A meets the starting bound, D is inside, and C reaches the excluded bound. The selection is therefore A followed by D, with 21 °C followed by 22 °C: 2 rows. The bounds were chosen explicitly; another convention could include C and would give a different answer.

  5. Separate duration from row count

    The window has duration b − a = 20 − 5 = 15 min. It contains 2 readings. These numbers describe different things: duration is measured in minutes, and the count is measured in rows. Any equality between them would be a numerical coincidence.

    An exact lookup at t = 15 min finds no row in the log. This does not mean that the temperature was 0 °C or that the sensor had stopped working. We only know that no reading from that time is present. Assigning a temperature to that time would require an additional rule; we do not invent one.

  6. Check without simply repeating the same calculation

    Count the identifiers before and after sorting: A, B, C, and D are all still there, giving 4 rows. Then check each time–temperature pair against the log as received. This check can detect a column sorted on its own, even if the time axis looks correct.

    Independent check of durations: the 3 gaps add up as 5 + 5 = 10 min, then 10 + 10 = 20 min. The gap between the endpoints directly gives 20 − 0 = 20 min. The results agree. Finally, test the window bounds: A is included, and C stays outside.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the sorted log. Reconstruct it from the arrival order, then explain why 2 selected rows do not mean 2 minutes, and why a missing reading does not mean a temperature of 0.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The last row received must be the most recent reading.”

    D arrives after C, but its time 10 min precedes 20 min. Choose the time described by the question: here, the measurement time. Sort whole rows by that time and keep the identifiers to check the associations.

    Review sorting and checking the pairs

  • “Once the index is sorted, each row represents the same duration.”

    Sorting only guarantees an order. Calculate the time differences: 5, 5, and 10 min are not all equal. Rows spaced equally on paper can represent different durations.

    Compare lengths on the time axis

  • “If a time is in the index, it must identify a single row.”

    Several readings can have the same timestamp. An exact lookup must then report multiple matches, unless an explicit rule specifies otherwise. The last exercise adds this case: keeping the 2 readings neither treats them as the same reading nor automatically chooses which one is correct.

    Try the case with repeated timestamps

Exercise 3 · Apply the method to the same log

A reading arrives late with a time already present

After D, you receive E, timestamped t = 10 min with 24 °C. Keep all rows. For equal timestamps, keep arrival order: this is our rule for breaking ties. Reconstruct the sorted order, the exact result at 10 min, then the selection [10, 20) min. What does the gap between D and E prove?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

E shares the time of D; arrival order breaks the tie without changing their timestamps.

Hint 2

Subtract the times of D and E. Equal times are not enough to establish that the two rows are the very same observation.

Compare with the solution

The order is B, A, D, E, C: 5 rows. An exact lookup at 10 min finds D at 22 °C and E at 24 °C. The time index is no longer unique: the same time identifies 2 rows. The rule for breaking ties specifies how to present them, without deciding which temperature is correct.

The window [10, 20) min contains D and E, giving 2 rows; its duration remains 10 min. Between D and E, the gap is 10 − 10 = 0 min. The time order is nondecreasing: it allows equality. It is no longer strictly increasing.

This 0 gap establishes that the timestamps are identical at the available precision. It does not prove that E should be deleted or that the temperatures should be merged. Deciding would require knowing the origins of the rows and the precision of the clock. Keep the 2 readings and report the ambiguity until an additional rule is established.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep D before E according to the stated rule, without losing their temperatures.
  • Return multiple matches for the exact lookup.
  • Find 0 min between D and E and distinguish nondecreasing order from strict order.
  • Separate the finding of equal timestamps from a decision to delete a row.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the difference between an identifier, a position, and a time index?

The identifier names the reading; the position is its rank in the current order; the time label gives its time according to a stated convention. Sorting can change the position while keeping the identifier, time, and temperature together.

Recall 2How can you check that a sorted index is also regularly spaced?

Subtract successive times using the same unit and reference. All gaps must equal a strictly positive duration. Sorting alone does not establish this.

Recall 3What does [a, b) mean, and why state the endpoint notation?

Keep the rows whose time t satisfies a ≤ t < b: start included, end excluded. The bounds must be comparable to the row times. Changing whether a bound is included can change the selection.

Recall 4Does an exact lookup with no match give a temperature of 0?

No. It establishes that no row for that time is present in the table. It does not determine the temperature; choosing a neighbor or making an estimate would require a separate rule.

Recall 5Do equal timestamps allow a row to be deleted automatically?

No. The index can identify several rows at the same time. You need to know their origins, the precision of the times, and an explicit rule to decide whether to delete a row. An order for breaking ties does not resolve this question.

Use the log to explain why order, regular spacing, and uniqueness must be checked separately.
