Level 2 · C060·M3

Generating functions

Your goalEncode a count distribution in a polynomial, find the distribution of an independent sum, and extract its expectation.

Before you start
c035-m5 : covariance and conditioning in finite settings · c058-m5 : change of variables · c046-m5 : approximation error · c060-m1 : transformations · c060-m2 : conditioning variables

✿01 See the idea

A workshop receives 2 batches of ceramics. It inspects a piece from each batch. Each piece may be accepted or need rework; we want to know the total number of pieces needing rework in this pair. All the probabilities in this scene are fictional: they support the reasoning and do not describe an observed workshop.

For each piece, the model assigns probability 2/3 to no rework and 1/3 to rework. Imagine a card with a box for each possible count. We will place the weight of each box in front of a power of the letter z: the weight of 0 in the constant term, and that of 1 in front of z. This letter serves as a label for organizing the counts.

Why use powers? When counts are added, their labels multiply: z to the power a, multiplied by z to the power b, gives z to the power a + b. To combine the pieces, we can therefore multiply their polynomial cards, provided their outcomes are independent. This assumption means that knowing the outcome for a piece does not change the probabilities assigned to the other.

In the diagram of outcome pairs, each box describes an outcome for piece A and an outcome for piece B. Boxes with the same total will need to be combined. Before reading the calculation, identify those that give exactly 1 piece needing rework.

The rule to remember

The coefficient gives the probability; the exponent gives the count. For a sum of independent counts, multiply the generating functions, then collect equal powers.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Predict rework for a pair

X counts the pieces needing rework in batch A, and Y those in batch B. Each equals 0 with probability 2/3, or 1 with probability 1/3. Assuming independence, let us find the entire distribution of S = X + Y, then its expectation.

  1. Define the generating function and its units

    For an integer count X between 0 and an integer bound N, let pk = P(X = k). P denotes a probability; k ranges over the possible counts. The probability generating function G_X is defined by:

    GX(z)=∑k=0Npkzk=E[zX]

    E denotes expectation: a weighted average under the distribution, rather than an average calculated from observations. Here X and Y are counts of pieces, recorded as dimensionless integers; we will use “pieces needing rework” to interpret their expectation. z is an auxiliary number with no unit; the coefficients and G_X(z) have no unit. In the finite sum, the term k = 0 is constant and carries the probability of a count of 0, including when z = 0.

    A distribution with finite support gives a polynomial defined for every real z. For an unbounded count, we replace the finite sum with a series: it converges absolutely at least for |z| ≤ 1. We will stay with the finite case; differentiating a polynomial then requires no interchange with an infinite sum.

  2. Write the card for each piece

    The probability of 0 pieces needing rework becomes the constant term. That of 1 piece needing rework becomes the coefficient of z. Since X and Y have identical distributions, their polynomials are also identical:

    GX(z)=GY(z)=23+13z

    Check the total probability: G_X(1) = 2/3 + 1/3 = 1. All probabilities have been preserved. G_X(0) = 2/3 reads only the box for a count of 0. A generating function therefore encodes the entire distribution; an expectation alone would retain only a summary.

  3. Justify the product using outcome pairs

    Let a be a possible outcome of X and b a possible outcome of Y. Independence gives P(X = a, Y = b) = P(X = a) × P(Y = b). The associated count is a + b and its label is z to the power a + b. Multiplying the terms of the polynomials performs exactly these operations.

    GS(z)=GX(z)GY(z)

    The pair (0, 0) contributes 2/3 × 2/3 = 4/9 to count 0. The pair (0, 1) contributes 2/3 × 1/3 = 2/9 to count 1, and (1, 0) contributes 1/3 × 2/3 = 2/9 to it. Finally, (1, 1) contributes 1/3 × 1/3 = 1/9 to count 2.

    Pairs that give the same total are mutually exclusive, so we add their probabilities. For count 1, this gives 2/9 + 2/9 = 4/9. This grouping of products is called convolution of distributions. It is already visible in the boxes of the diagram.

  4. Read the distribution from the resulting polynomial

    GS(z)=(23+13z)2=49+49z+19z2

    The constant coefficient gives P(S = 0) = 4/9. The coefficient of z gives P(S = 1) = 4/9. The coefficient of z squared gives P(S = 2) = 1/9. Other counts have probability 0 in this model.

    Check: the coefficients are positive and their sum is 4/9 + 4/9 + 1/9 = 1. This is also G_S(1) = G_X(1) × G_Y(1). This check detects lost probability mass; it does not justify independence on its own.

  5. Recover the expectation using the derivative

    The prime denotes the derivative with respect to z. Differentiating z to the power k brings down the factor k; evaluating at z = 1 then removes the remaining powers. For our finite distribution, G′_S(1) is therefore the sum of the counts multiplied by their probabilities: E[S]. The constant term disappears, just as a count of 0 contributes nothing to an average.

    GS′(z)=49+2×19z

    At z = 1, the contribution from count 2 is 2 × 1/9 = 2/9. We obtain E[S] = 4/9 + 2/9 = 2/3 pieces needing rework per pair, or approximately 0.67 pieces per pair. Rounding makes the value easier to read; the calculations remain in fractions.

    Check by another route: E[X] = 0 × 2/3 + 1 × 1/3 = 1/3; the same holds for Y. Linearity of expectation gives E[S] = E[X] + E[Y] = 2/3 pieces per pair. This equality of means remains true without independence, so it does not validate the product of generating functions.

  6. Distinguish evaluation from probability

    G_S(1) adds all the coefficients: its value 1 does not mean that S certainly equals 1. The probability of this count is the coefficient of z, namely 4/9. Evaluating the polynomial and reading a coefficient are different operations.

    To check the distribution without algebra, represent the outcome for a piece using 3 fictional, equally likely tickets: 2 carry count 0 and 1 carries count 1. An independent draw for each piece gives 9 equally likely pairs. There are 4 with total 0, 4 with total 1 and 1 with total 2. This recovers the calculated coefficients. These tickets make the fictional model tangible; they are not workshop observations.

  7. Connect the moment generating function

    The moment generating function M_X uses an exponential rather than a power of z. With t a real number with no unit and exp the exponential function, it is written M_X(t) = E[exp(tX)]. In our finite case, it exists for every real t. Since exp(tX) = exp(t) to the power X, we simply substitute z = exp(t) into G_X:

    MS(t)=GS(exp(t))=(23+13exp(t))2

    Since exp(0) = 1, M_S(0) = 1 and M′_S(0) = E[S] = 2/3. For M, the prime denotes a derivative with respect to t. More generally, a moment of order k is the expectation of X to the power k, with k a positive integer. Differentiating M that many times, then evaluating at 0, gives this moment. Successive derivatives of G do not follow the same rule. Beyond the finite case, specifying a moment generating function requires checking that this expectation is finite in an open interval around 0. Calculating limits is left for the next unit.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the formulas and reconstruct the polynomial, naming the outcome pairs for the pieces. Why do you multiply within a box, then add across certain boxes? Then explain why the derivative giving the mean is evaluated at 1 for G and at 0 for M.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I can leave out the constant term: no rework does not count.”

    A count of 0 is a possible outcome. Its weight 2/3 in G_X represents part of the distribution. Without it, G_X(1) would no longer total 1. Review the correspondence between each box and its term.

    Recover the complete card for a piece

  • “For a sum, I always multiply the generating functions.”

    You must justify independence to factor the probabilities of outcome pairs. If Y = X, the total is 2X: replace z with z to the power 2 in G_X. The final exercise keeps the individual distributions and changes their relationship.

    Test perfectly linked outcomes

  • “G_S(1) is the probability that S equals 1.”

    G_S(1) adds all the coefficients and equals 1. P(S = 1) is read from the coefficient of z and equals 4/9. An evaluation is an average of powers of z; it is generally not the probability of an event.

    Distinguish coefficient from evaluation

Exercise 1 · Reading a distribution

Predict at most a single piece needing rework

Return to the independent pair. Without evaluating G_S at an arbitrarily chosen number, find P(S ≤ 1). State the coefficients used, then check using the complement of the event S = 2.

Hint 1

Which counts of pieces satisfy S ≤ 1?

Hint 2

Counts 0 and 1 are mutually exclusive. Their probabilities add.

Compare with the solution

S ≤ 1 combines counts 0 and 1. The required probability is therefore 4/9 + 4/9 = 8/9. Read the constant term and the coefficient of z, without multiplying the second probability by a count.

The only excluded count is 2: 1 − 1/9 = 8/9. The direct and complementary approaches give the same fraction. G_S(1) would also include count 2 and would equal 1.

Check your reasoning

  • You identify the included counts before calculating.
  • You obtain 8/9 by addition and by taking the complement.
  • You explain why G_S(1) does not answer the question.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and interpretation

A generating function can exceed unity

Calculate G_S(2) using the product G_X(2) × G_Y(2), then using the expanded polynomial. Someone wants to report this result as P(S = 2): respond to this interpretation. Does G′_S(1) give the same quantity?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In G_X, replace only the letter z with 2: the constant term remains unchanged.

Hint 2

In the expanded polynomial, z squared equals 4. Then compare with the coefficient of this term.

Compare with the solution

For each piece: G_X(2) = 2/3 + 1/3 × 2 = 2/3 + 2/3 = 4/3. The product gives 4/3 × 4/3 = 16/9.

Using the expansion: 4/9 + 4/9 × 2 + 1/9 × 4 = 4/9 + 8/9 + 4/9 = 16/9. This is the mean of 2 to the power S, with no unit. Its value is greater than 1: it cannot be the proposed probability.

P(S = 2) is the coefficient of z squared, 1/9. The derivative at 1 gives a different quantity again: G′_S(1) = 2/3 pieces needing rework per pair. Name the operation and the resulting object before assigning meaning to a result.

Check your reasoning

  • You recover 16/9 using both forms of the same polynomial.
  • You distinguish evaluation, coefficient and expectation.
  • You keep 1/9 as the probability of count 2.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and challenge

The pieces share exactly the same outcome

Change just a single assumption in the fictional model: Y = X. The individual probabilities remain 2/3 for 0 and 1/3 for 1, but the pieces always have the same outcome. Construct the distribution of S and its generating function. Compare P(S = 1) and E[S] with the independent model. Does equality of the means prove that the product is appropriate?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

If X = 0, what is the pair (X, Y)? Ask the same question for X = 1.

Hint 2

Since S = 2X, each exponent in G_X is multiplied by 2. The individual probabilities are not squared.

Compare with the solution

The pair is (0, 0) with probability 2/3, or (1, 1) with probability 1/3. Thus P(S = 0) = 2/3, P(S = 1) = 0 and P(S = 2) = 1/3. The sum of the probabilities is still 1.

GS(z)=GX(z2)=23+13z2

The product of the individual generating functions would have assigned 4/9 to count 1, which is impossible here. The step P(X = a, Y = b) = P(X = a) × P(Y = b) is no longer justified.

The mean nevertheless equals 0 × 2/3 + 2 × 1/3 = 2/3 pieces per pair, matching 2/3 in the independent model. The distributions differ and their means coincide. To check the distribution, you must examine the events and dependence, rather than just this summary.

Check your reasoning

  • You exclude count 1 and justify why it is impossible.
  • You write G_X(z to the power 2) and explain why this is not the square of G_X(z).
  • You recover 2/3 and reject using this mean as proof of independence.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In G_X(z), what do the coefficient and exponent of a term represent?

The exponent k represents a possible count; its coefficient pₖ is the probability P(X = k). The constant term preserves the probability of a count of 0.

Recall 2Why does the sum of independent counts lead to a product of generating functions?

Independence multiplies the probabilities of outcome pairs. Powers of z multiply by adding their exponents. We then add the contributions from distinct pairs with the same total.

Recall 3How do you distinguish P(S = 1) from G_S(1) in the example?

P(S = 1) is the coefficient of z: 4/9. G_S(1) adds all the probabilities: 1. A function argument is not a count whose probability is being requested.

Recall 4Why does G′_S(1) give the expectation in our finite case?

The derivative brings down the exponents as factors. At 1, the remaining powers equal 1: this gives the sum of the counts weighted by their probabilities. Here it equals 2/3 pieces needing rework per pair.

Recall 5For a count with finite support, how do you connect M_X(t) and G_X(z), and where do you read the mean?

M_X(t) = E[exp(tX)] = G_X(exp(t)). The mean is read from M′_X(0), or from G′_X(1). Finite support justifies existence and differentiation here. For infinite support, the domains of existence must be checked.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the card for a piece, then the outcome pairs: justify independence, multiply the probabilities and group identical counts. Read the distribution from the coefficients; check the total probability at 1 and the mean using the derivative at 1. Finally, recover the case Y = X to explain what these checks are insufficient to prove, then connect G and M through z = exp(t).
