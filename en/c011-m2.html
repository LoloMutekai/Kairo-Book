Level 0 · C011·M2

Arithmetic sequences

Your goalCalculate the pages planned for a given day and their total from the start when the same increase is repeated each day.

Before you start
c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c011-m1 : summation notation

✿01 See the idea

You are preparing a short reading schedule: 4 pages on the first day, then 3 more pages than the day before on each following day. Picture the calendar boxes before looking at the diagram: the next box contains the previous day’s pages, with the same small group of pages added each time.

This gives 4, then 7, then 10, then 13 pages. This ordered list is a sequence. Each number is a term; its index indicates the day it belongs to. A sequence is arithmetic when the difference between each term and the preceding term stays the same. This difference is called the common difference.

Day 4 has 13 pages. The planned total from the start also includes the pages from the earlier days: it is 34 pages. The term and the total have the same unit, the page, but answer different questions.

All these values are fictional. They describe planned pages spread over consecutive days, with no days skipped and an unchanged increase throughout the period studied. The calculation does not prove that the pages were actually read and does not prescribe a study pace.

The rule to remember

Count the steps between days to find a term; add the relevant terms to find the total.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

That day, then from the start

The schedule starts with 4 pages and adds 3 pages each day. How many pages does it plan for day 4? What total does it plan from the first day up to and including that day?

  1. Define the symbols and check the rule

    n is an integer index with no unit, at least equal to 1; here, it corresponds to the day number. un denotes the pages planned for the single day at index n. u1, the first term, is 4 pages. The small subscript marks a position in the list, rather than multiplication. r is the common difference, in pages added with each step to the next day; its value is 3 pages.

    The symbol Sn denotes the sum of the pages from the first day up to and including day n, in pages. To state exactly which quantities we are adding, we write:

    Sn=u1+u2+⋯+un

    The dots mean that we continue through every day in between, leaving none out. For n = 1, the sum contains only u1. Let us check the differences in the schedule: 7 − 4 = 3, 10 − 7 = 3 and 13 − 10 = 3 pages. They are equal; the schedule is built using this same rule at each step. A few numbers alone would not be enough to determine a rule for days that are still unknown.

  2. Count the steps to reach the requested day

    On the first day, we already have the first term, before making any increase. Reaching day n therefore takes n − 1 steps. Each step adds r pages to the daily term:

    un=u1+(n−1)×r

    Here, the number of increases is 4 − 1 = 3. The increase from the starting value to the requested day is 3 × 3 = 9 pages. Adding the starting value gives 4 + 9 = 13 pages on day 4.

    Check using the schedule, without the direct formula: 4 → 7 → 10 → 13, with an increase of 3 pages at each arrow. The last term is indeed 13 pages.

  3. Add the days to obtain the total

    The total includes every term, including those before the last day. We add a day’s pages to the pages already planned before it: 4 + 7 = 11 pages, then 11 + 10 = 21 pages, then 21 + 13 = 34 pages.

    S4=4+7+10+13=34 pages

    Check that this makes sense: the total exceeds the last term because it also contains all the preceding terms, which are positive here. The answers are therefore 13 pages for that day and 34 pages for the whole period.

  4. Check by pairing the ends

    Pair the first day with the last: 4 + 13 = 17 pages. The remaining days give 7 + 10 = 17 pages. Moving forward a day from the start adds r; moving back a day from the end subtracts r. The sum of a pair therefore stays the same.

    There are 4 ÷ 2 = 2 pairs, covering each day once. They give 2 × 17 = 34 pages. This grouping gives the same total as successive additions.

  5. Write a formula that also works for an odd number of days

    To avoid leaving any day on its own, write the entire list forwards, then the same list backwards, aligning the terms. The first column pairs u1 with un, the next pairs u2 with the term before un, and so on. Each column has the same sum u1 + un: what is gained on one side is lost on the other.

    There are n columns. Their sums together equal n × (u1 + un). But each row equals Sn; by counting the 2 rows, we have counted the total 2 times. We must therefore divide by 2:

    2Sn=n×(u1+un);Sn=n×(u1+un)2

    For our schedule: 4 × (4 + 13) = 4 × 17 = 68 pages in the 2 rows; 68 ÷ 2 = 34 pages in a single row. The additions, the pairs and this formula agree.

    If n is odd, the middle term also appears in each row. It is counted 2 times, like all the others, so the division still works. This proof does not require an even number of days.

  6. Check the starting value and a 0 common difference

    At index 1, n − 1 equals 0: the term formula gives 4 pages again. The sum formula also gives this single term, since we add the starting value to itself, then divide by 2.

    If the increase in the same schedule became 0 pages, each day would keep 4 pages. Over 4 days, the total would be 4 × 4 = 16 pages. A 0 common difference makes the terms constant; it does not make them 0.

    In mathematics, a common difference can also be negative: a constant quantity is then subtracted. In a scenario involving planned pages, we must stop before getting negative numbers. The formulas always assume the same common difference and the same starting value at index 1; on their own, they do not determine what was actually read.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculation. Explain why the index counts the days while the number of increases counts the intervals between them. Then work out the requested term and the total again. Why does the proof of the sum using a reversed list require division?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The index is the number of increases.”

    Start again at the first box: it is given before any increase. To reach index 4, count the arrows, giving 3 increases. Counting 4 would take you to day 5, where the term is 16 pages.

    Recount the arrows between the boxes

  • “The last term gives all the pages from the start.”

    Write the questions before the numbers: “that day” gives 13 pages; “from the start” requires 4 + 7 + 10 + 13 = 34 pages. Having the same unit does not make these quantities interchangeable.

    Review the calculation of the total and its check

  • “The common difference is the number you multiply by.”

    In this arithmetic sequence, subtract adjacent terms: the difference is 3 pages. The day after the start has 4 + 3 = 7 pages. Multiplication would give 4 × 3 = 12 pages, which does not follow this schedule.

    Find the constant difference again

Exercise 3 · Transfer and justification

The middle day does not disappear

The schedule now lasts 5 days, with the same data. Someone says: “We can no longer use the sum formula: there is a day left in the middle.” Respond by calculating the total for these days. Justify the division by 2 using a list written forwards and backwards, then check by setting the middle day aside.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The last term is the one from the exercise on day 5. Write the lists one below the other in opposite directions.

Hint 2

Count the columns in the doubled lists. Then, in a single list, keep the middle day as well as the outer pairs.

Compare with the solution

The last term is 16 pages. The lists to align are 4, 7, 10, 13, 16, then 16, 13, 10, 7, 4. Each of the 5 columns gives 20 pages: the start and the end give 4 + 16, their neighbours give 7 + 13, and the middle gives 10 + 10.

These 5 columns count the 2 lists: 5 × 20 = 100 pages. The total for a single list is therefore 100 ÷ 2 = 50 pages. The middle day has been counted 2 times, just like the others.

Check in a single list: the outer pairs each give 20 pages, and the middle is 10 pages. This gives 2 × 20 + 10 = 50 pages. Another check: the total for the previous 4 days, 34 pages, increased by the 16 pages of the last day, also gives 50 pages.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain 50 pages and distinguish this total from the term 16.
  • You explain why the columns count the sum 2 times, including the middle term.
  • When checking with pairs in a single list, you keep the middle day once.
  • You address the objection by justifying the calculation, rather than simply quoting the formula.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you recognise an arithmetic sequence, and what does its common difference measure?

The difference between a term and the preceding term is constant throughout the sequence being studied. This difference is the common difference r; here, it counts the pages added to the previous day’s schedule. It is not a multiplier.

Recall 2With a starting value u₁, why does the term at index n use n − 1 increases?

The first term exists before any increase; each step to the next term adds r. So uₙ = u₁ + (n − 1) × r, for integer n at least equal to 1. Counting n increases leads to the next index.

Recall 3What is the difference between uₙ and Sₙ in the schedule?

uₙ counts the pages for day n alone; Sₙ adds all the days from the start up to and including n. On day 4, these give 13 and 34 pages respectively. The same unit does not mean the same quantity.

Recall 4Where does Sₙ = n × (u₁ + uₙ) ÷ 2 come from, even when n is odd?

Write the terms forwards and backwards in 2 rows. The n columns each equal u₁ + uₙ. They count the sum 2 times, including the middle term, so divide by 2. A constant common difference is required.

Recall 5If the common difference is 0, what happens to the terms and the sum?

Each term stays equal to the starting value u₁, and Sₙ = n × u₁. In our schedule, 4 days with 4 pages give 16 pages; the terms do not become 0.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking, redraw the schedule and write the formulas for the term and the sum. Explain the number of intervals, then why the ends form pairs with the same sum. Finish with the assumptions and the difference between planned pages and pages actually read.
