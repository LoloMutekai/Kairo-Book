Level 3 · C170·M1

Construction and properties

Your goalConstruct a random walk with steps ±h, justify the choice h = σ√Δt, then identify the properties of Brownian motion that follow in the limit: 0 mean, variance proportional to time, self-similarity, and an expected future position equal to the present position.

Before you start
c169-m5 : optional stopping under assumptions · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions

✿01 See the idea

A marker slides along a rail marked in centimetres. Every 0.25 s, a fair coin decides whether the marker takes a step to the right or to the left, always of the same length. The values in this scene are fictional: they are used for calculations and do not describe any real measurements.

We want to observe the marker for 1 s without the answer depending on how finely our clock divides time. If we divide time into shorter steps, how long should each step be so that the spread of the marker stays the same? This is the question behind constructing Brownian motion: we view it as the limit of such walks as the step duration tends to 0.

The spread is measured by the variance of the position, the mean of the squared deviations from the mean, expressed in cm². For independent steps, variances add: 4 steps of length h give 4 times h². A fixed step length therefore makes the variance grow without bound as we refine the time steps, while a step length proportional to the duration makes it shrink to 0. The right scale is the square root of the duration.

In the limit, the resulting process, denoted W, has properties that the example will recover: a start at 0, 0 mean, variance proportional to time, and an expected future position equal to the present position.

The rule to remember

To keep a variance of σ² per second when dividing the duration T into n steps, take independent steps of plus or minus h with h² = σ²·Δt; their sum has variance σ²·T, whatever n is.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A marker observed for one second

A marker starts at the 0 cm mark on a rail. Durations are in seconds, lengths in centimetres, and all values are fictional: the target dispersion is σ = 2 cm per square root of a second, giving a variance σ² = 4 cm² per second, for an observation duration T = 1 s. Let us construct walks with steps ±h, then identify what stays invariant as we refine the steps.

  1. Define the symbols and assumptions

    T is the observation duration, in s; n is the number of steps; Δt = T/n is the duration of a step, in s; h is the length of a step, in cm; σ² is the target variance per second, in cm²/s. At each step, the marker adds Sk, equal to +h or −h with the same probability, determined by tossing a fair coin. Starting from 0, the position after n steps is the sum of the steps.

    Xn=∑k=1nSk, avec Sk∈{−h,+h}

    Assumptions: the steps are independent and h does not depend on k. Each step then has 0 mean, since +h and −h are equally likely, and variance h², since its square is h² in both cases. Without independence, the variance of the sum would no longer be the sum of the variances.

  2. Choose h so that variance follows time

    For independent steps, the cross terms in the variance of the sum have 0 mean: the variance of the position is n·h². We want σ²·T regardless of the subdivision. Since n·Δt = T, we need h² = σ²·Δt, that is, h = σ√Δt.

    h=σΔt

    With n = 4 steps, Δt = 1 / 4 = 0.25 s. Then h² = σ²·Δt = 4 × 0.25 = 1 cm², so h = 1 cm. Check: h × h = 1 × 1 = 1 cm², which equals the target.

  3. Follow a path

    Suppose the coin selects up, up, down, up. Each position adds or subtracts 1 cm from the previous one: 1 cm, then 2 cm, then 1 cm, then 2 cm. This is the piecewise linear path in the first diagram; another sequence of tosses would have given another of the 16 possible paths.

    A path is one realization. To understand the process, we need to consider all the paths, and therefore means and variances.

  4. Add the variances

    A step is plus or minus 1 cm: its variance is 1 cm². For 4 independent steps, the variances add: 4 × 1 = 4 cm². The target σ²·T is 4 × 1 = 4 cm²: the two numbers agree.

    Var(Xn)=nh2=nσ2Δt=σ2T

  5. Check by counting

    We check this result another way. There are 16 equally likely sequences of 4 tosses. They end at −4 cm (one sequence), −2 cm (4 sequences), 0 cm (6 sequences), 2 cm (4 sequences) and 4 cm (one sequence). The mean is 0 by symmetry.

    The squared positions give, for each side, 1 × 16 + 4 × 4 = 32, giving 64 cm² in total. Dividing by 16 sequences gives 4 cm², the same result as additivity. The two approaches are independent of each other.

  6. Refine the steps without changing the dispersion

    Now take n = 16 steps. Δt = 1 / 16 = 0.0625 s, so h² = 4 × 0.0625 = 0.25 cm² and h = 0.5 cm. Check: 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 cm². The final variance is 16 × 0.25 = 4 cm², identical to that for 4 steps: only the step length has decreased.

    Two poor choices show why the square root is necessary. With 16 fixed steps of 1 cm, the variance is 16 × 1 = 16 cm² and grows with n. With a step length equal to the step duration multiplied by 1 cm/s, giving 0.0625 cm, the variance is 16 × 1/256 = 0.0625 cm²: it shrinks towards 0. The second diagram summarizes these four cases.

  7. Take the limit and name the result

    As n grows with h = σ√(T/n), the central limit theorem, covered in the unit on approximations, indicates that the final position approaches a normal distribution with 0 mean and variance σ²·T. Assumptions: independent steps, identically distributed for fixed n, with 0 mean and finite variance. Since h changes with n, we need the version of the theorem for steps whose distribution depends on n; the Lindeberg condition is satisfied here because the steps are bounded in magnitude by h, which tends to 0. A functional version of this result, often called Donsker’s theorem and accepted here without proof, states that the entire piecewise linear paths converge in distribution to a process with continuous paths.

    For σ = 1, this process is standard Brownian motion W. It is defined by three properties: W0 = 0; for s less than t, the increment Wt − Ws has a normal distribution with 0 mean and variance t − s, and is independent of everything that happened up to time s; the paths are continuous. The existence of such a process is accepted here. Our marker is σ·W.

    Wt−Ws∼N(0,t−s)

  8. Identify three properties of the marker

    Variance: σ·W at time t has variance σ²·t; at t = T, we recover 4 cm². Self-similarity: for c = 4, the variance at 4 s is 4 × 4 = 16 cm², giving 4 × 4 = 16 cm²: W at 4t has the same distribution as 2 times W at t, because 2 is the square root of 4.

    Wct a la même loi que cWt

    Expected future position: after 2 steps, at t = 0.50 s, the marker is at 2 cm. The 2 remaining steps are independent of the past and have 0 mean: the expected final position given the past is 2 cm, the present position. This is the martingale property, covered in the unit on filtrations. The variance of the remaining part is 2 × 1 = 2 cm², also equal to σ² × 0.50 = 2 cm², less than the initial 4 cm²: the past reduces uncertainty without removing it.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The more I refine the steps, the shorter they become, so making the step length proportional to the duration is enough.”

    This choice makes the dispersion shrink towards 0: with 16 steps of 0.0625 cm, the variance falls to 0.0625 cm² instead of 4 cm². Variance adds squares, so h² must follow Δt.

    Review the choice of step length and the second diagram

  • “Two variables with the same variance are equal: W at four t equals twice W at t.”

    Only the distributions agree. The position at 4 s depends on steps that are absent from the first second. Recalculate the two variances, 16 and 16 cm², then distinguish equality of distributions from equality of values.

    Return to the exercise on scaling

  • “A martingale is a process that stops moving.”

    The expected future position given the past is the present position, 2 cm in the example, but the uncertainty of the remaining part is still 2 cm² for the remaining 0.50 s. Having 0 mean does not mean being motionless.

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

Divide into 64 steps

The same fictional scene: σ² = 4 cm²/s, T = 1 s, starting at 0 cm. Divide the duration into 64 steps. Calculate Δt, the step length h, then the variance of the final position. Check that it does not depend on the subdivision.

Hint 1

Start with Δt = T/n, then find h such that h² = σ²·Δt; h is a simple number.

Hint 2

Then check n·h² and compare it with σ²·T.

Compare with the solution

Δt = 1 / 64 = 0.015625 s. Then h² = 4 × 0.015625 = 0.0625 cm², so h = 0.25 cm, because 0.25 × 0.25 = 0.0625 cm².

Variance: 64 × 0.0625 = 4 cm², equal to σ²·T = 4 cm². The dispersion is the same for 4, 16 and 64 steps; only the step length has changed.

Check your reasoning

  • Δt calculated in seconds before h.
  • h deduced from h² = σ²·Δt, without writing σ·Δt.
  • Final variance n·h² compared with σ²·T, with the unit cm².

Exercise 3 · Reasoning to justify

What the past tells us

In the path from the example, the marker is at 2 cm after 2 steps, at t = 0.50 s. What is the expected position at 1 s given this information? What is the conditional variance? Compare it with the initial variance and state what knowing the past has changed.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The remaining steps are independent of the past and have 0 mean.

Hint 2

For the variance, add the variances of the remaining steps, as in the example.

Compare with the solution

The final position is the present position plus the sum of the 2 remaining steps. This sum has 0 mean, so the conditional expectation is 2 cm: this is the martingale property.

The conditional variance is that of the remaining steps: 2 × 1 = 2 cm², or σ² × 0.50 = 2 cm². Initially it was 4 cm². The past has not shifted the expected future position; it has reduced uncertainty, because only 0.50 s remain. A martingale is therefore not a constant process.

Check your reasoning

  • Mean of the remaining steps 0, thanks to independence.
  • Variance of the remaining part calculated over the remaining duration.
  • Known position distinguished from the future that is still uncertain.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do we approximate Brownian motion with a walk?

Divide the duration into n steps, each equal to plus or minus h determined by independent tosses of a fair coin, and take h = σ√Δt. The limit as Δt tends to 0 is Brownian motion, under the assumptions of the central limit theorem and its functional version.

Recall 2Why is the step length proportional to the square root of Δt?

The variance of n independent steps is n·h². For it to stay at σ²·T as n grows, h² must equal σ²·Δt. A fixed step makes the variance grow without bound, while a step proportional to Δt makes it shrink to 0.

Recall 3Which properties define standard Brownian motion?

A start at 0, normally distributed increments with 0 mean and variance equal to the duration, independent of the past, and continuous paths. These imply a variance equal to t.

Recall 4What is the expected future position given the past?

The present position, because the following steps have 0 mean and are independent. Uncertainty nevertheless remains positive and proportional to the remaining duration.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define h, Δt, T and σ² with their units; take independent, equally likely steps of plus or minus h; add the variances to give n·h²; impose h² = σ²·Δt so that the variance stays at σ²·T whatever n is; check by counting; take the limit under the assumptions of the central limit theorem and its functional version; identify 0 mean, variance proportional to time, self-similarity and the martingale property.

Optional milestone · recognize how variances add

What you understand
The variances of independent steps add: n steps with variance q give n·q. For the total to be independent of how time is subdivided, the variance per step must be proportional to the step duration.
