Level 3 · C103·M2

Activations

Your goalCalculate an activation, explain why it changes the shape of a network, and trace its effect on the slope passed through it.

Before you start
c102-m5 : finite difference checking · c099-m5 : probability calibration · c067-m5 : pipeline · c103-m1 : initialization

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional greenhouse, a small network receives a heat indicator and produces a ventilation score. We want a contribution that stays at 0 when it is cool enough, then increases when the heat exceeds a threshold. A rule that always multiplies and adds in the same way cannot create this bend.

A neuron starts by weighting its input and adding a bias: this is its affine calculation. The activation then transforms this result. ReLU, or rectified linear unit, replaces a negative result with 0 and keeps a positive result. It resembles a ramp preceded by a floor.

With our settings, the indicator x = 2 gives the intermediate score z = 3. The ramp keeps it. With x = 0, z = -1: the floor blocks it. However, the final score keeps a baseline term even when this contribution becomes 0.

All the data and weights are fictional and chosen by hand. The indicator and scores are dimensionless; none measures a flow rate, an opening percentage, or a probability. We calculate a forward pass through the network without training its weights or controlling a fan.

The rule to remember

Calculate the affine score, apply the activation to that score, then continue. The shape of the activation also changes the slope propagated back through the network.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From heat signal to ventilation score

We fix w = 2, b = -1, v = 3, and c = 1. For x = 2, let us calculate z = wx + b, a = φ(z), then y = va + c with φ equal to ReLU. We will then compare other inputs to the same network without changing these weights.

  1. Define the network and what the activation adds

    x is the incoming indicator; w is its weight; b is the added bias; z is the score before activation, also called the preactivation. φ denotes the activation function, and a its output. v is the weight that multiplies a; c is the final bias; y is the final score. All quantities are real and dimensionless. The weights and biases stay fixed during our calculations.

    z=wx+b;a=φ(z);y=va+c

    If the activation were the identity, φ(z) = z, expanding would give y = v(wx + b) + c = (vw)x + vb + c. We would still have a single affine rule with a constant slope. With our settings, vw = 6, vb = -3, and vb + c = -2. Stacking affine transformations is therefore not enough to create a bend. The same conclusion holds for matrices: their composition remains affine.

  2. Apply the floor after the bias

    max means “take the largest of the values.” The ReLU rule is:

    φ(z)=max(0,z)={0si z≤0zsi z>0

    For the hot input, first apply the weight: wx = 2 × 2 = 4. Then add the bias: z = 4 + (-1) = 3. Since z is positive, a = z = 3. The sign to examine is that of z, after adding the bias; examining only x would give the wrong boundary.

  3. Continue to the final score and check

    The activated contribution is multiplied by v, then we add the baseline score c:

    y=3×3+1=9+1=10

    Reverse check: subtracting c from 10 gives 9 again; dividing by v gives a = 3 again. On this branch, replacing the activation with the identity also gives y = 6 × 2 + (-2) = 10. This local equality does not prove that the activation is unnecessary for all inputs.

  4. Find the threshold in the input space

    For x = 0, wx = 0, then z = -1. ReLU produces a = 0; the contribution va equals 0, and y = 1. The output of the neuron is 0, but the final bias remains.

    The boundary satisfies wx + b = 0. Since w is strictly positive, it lies at x⋆ = −b/w; the symbol ⋆ marks this boundary input. Here, −b = 1, so x⋆ = 1/2 = 0.50. Below it, y = c; above it, y = v(wx + b) + c. At the join, both expressions give c: the output is continuous.

  5. Prove the change in shape in the same greenhouse

    At the intermediate input x = 1, we obtain wx = 2, z = 1, a = 1, then y = 3 + 1 = 4. For the evenly spaced inputs 0, 1, 2, the outputs are therefore 1, 4, 10.

    The first output increase equals 4 − 1 = 3; the next equals 10 − 4 = 6. An affine rule would have equal increases for equal input intervals. They differ: our network is indeed nonaffine on this set. ReLU is piecewise affine, but a single straight line does not describe both branches together.

  6. Trace the slope through the activation

    Here, the derivative measures the local change in the score for a small change in its input. Away from the join, φ′(z) = 0 on the negative part and φ′(z) = 1 on the positive part. The prime symbol denotes the derivative. By the chain rule, keeping the weights fixed:

    dydx=dyda×dadz×dzdx=vφ′(z)w

    At the hot point, this gives 3 × 1 × 2 = 6. At the cool point, the ReLU factor is 0, so the slope passed back to x is 0. These slopes are dimensionless. ReLU does not bound positive values: a = 3 already exceeds 1.

    To propagate an error back to the weights, the same rule uses ∂a/∂w = φ′(z)x and ∂a/∂b = φ′(z), where ∂ means that only the named parameter varies. A negative preactivation therefore makes this derivative path 0 for this example. A single inactive example is not enough to say that the neuron will remain inactive for all inputs.

  7. Recognize the point where the derivative does not exist

    At z = 0, the value of ReLU is fully defined. However, the slope from the left equals 0 and the slope from the right equals 1: there is no unique derivative at the join. To calculate backpropagation, we can adopt the convention 0 at this point; this convention is not a classical derivative.

    In the greenhouse, the join is at x⋆ = 0.50. The slopes of y on either side are 0 and 6. A centered finite difference that crosses this join mixes the branches. The diagnostic exercise will show why it does not verify a derivative at the kink.

  8. Choose an activation according to the role of the output

    If the objective changes and we want a bounded output to represent the probability of a need for ventilation, we can apply a sigmoid to a dimensionless real score s. This score and the probability then refer to a defined event; they do not become physical measurements. The sigmoid σ is defined by:

    σ(s)=11+exp(−s);σ′(s)=σ(s)[1−σ(s)]

    exp is the exponential function: it is positive and exp(0) = 1. For every finite real s, the mathematical sigmoid is strictly between 0 and 1. For s = 0, its denominator equals 2, its output equals 0.50, and its slope equals 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25. These values and slopes are dimensionless.

    The sigmoid flattens as s becomes very positive or very negative: its slope approaches 0. This is saturation. Small derivative factors can weaken the signal propagated back through several activations. ReLU avoids this flattening on its positive branch, but its negative branch passes back a 0 slope.

    The choice therefore depends on the role: a ReLU can form an internal representation; a sigmoid can bound a binary output. An output in this interval is not, by itself, a calibrated probability: the stated probabilities must be compared with event frequencies on validation data. We do not claim that either choice improves this greenhouse without evaluation.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, explain why the network can be affine around the hot input while being nonaffine as a whole. Then distinguish the value at the join, its derivative, and a computational convention.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The activation checks whether the input x is positive.”

    It receives z = wx + b. Include the bias in the calculation before choosing the branch: the boundary for our indicator is x⋆ = 0.50, whereas the boundary of ReLU itself is z = 0.

    Find the threshold calculation again

  • “A 0 ReLU output means that the entire network is dead.”

    For this example alone, the contribution of the neuron and its derivative path become 0. The final bias remains, and other inputs can activate the neuron. To speak of persistent inactivity, we would need to examine the preactivations on the relevant training inputs.

    Recalculate the inactive branch

  • “A positive activation already provides a probability.”

    ReLU can exceed 1. The sigmoid bounds an output, but its interval does not prove calibration. Define the predicted event and distinguish the output constraint from empirical validity.

    Compare ReLU and sigmoid

Compare with the solution

On the left: x = 2/5 = 0.40, wx = 4/5, z = -1/5, so a = 0 and y = 1. On the right: x = 3/5 = 0.60, wx = 6/5, z = 1/5, so a = 1/5. The contribution equals 3/5, then y = 8/5 = 1.60. At the join, y(x⋆) = 1.

Left slope: (1 − 1)/1/10 = 0/1/10 = 0. Right slope: (8/5 − 1)/1/10 = 3/5/1/10 = 6. These slopes differ: there is no classical derivative at the join.

D=8/5−11/5=3/51/5=3

D is an average slope over an interval that crosses the bend. Even when ε is reduced while staying on either side of this join, it remains midway between the slopes of the branches; this does not make them equal. ✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why does stacking affine calculations without a nonlinear activation fail to create a bend?

The composition remains affine: v(wx + b) + c = (vw)x + vb + c. A nonaffine activation allows us to leave this family; its effect also depends on the weights and the inputs visited.

Recall 2To which quantity is ReLU applied, and what does it do?

To the score z = wx + b, after the bias. It gives max(0, z): a 0 floor for negative z, the identity for positive z. It does not bound positive values.

Recall 3How does ReLU affect the slope of y with respect to x?

dy/dx = vφ′(z)w. Away from the join, φ′ equals 0 on the negative branch and 1 on the positive branch. At the join, the value exists but the classical derivative does not; a computational convention must be distinguished.

Recall 4What does the sigmoid add to an output, and what evidence is still needed?

σ(s) = 1/(1 + exp(−s)) bounds the mathematical output between 0 and 1 for a finite real score. This allows a binary probabilistic representation, but does not prove calibration for the target event.

Recall 5Why can a centered finite difference be misleading at the bend?

It mixes the two branches. In the greenhouse, it equals 3, whereas the one-sided slopes equal 0 and 6. An average of the slopes does not demonstrate a derivative at the join.
