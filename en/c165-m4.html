Level 3 · C165·M4

Operators

Your goalDefine an operator on signals, prove its linearity and calculate its norm to control how a difference propagates.

Before you start
c164-m5 : Fubini–Tonelli · c040-m5 : least squares · c142-m5 : convergence criteria · c165-m1 : Lp spaces · c165-m2 : completeness · c165-m3 : Hilbert

✿01 See the idea

A recorder tracks a voltage for 2 s. It is 1 mV during the first half of the window, then 7 mV during the second half. You want to replace the whole curve with a single constant level that preserves its mean. Before calculating, mentally place this level between the two voltages.

Here the transformation takes a function of time and returns another function of time: a constant signal over the same window. This is called an operator. Its image is the function it produces; the number that sets the constant level is its mean.

Changing the input signal changes this level. But the rule stays fixed: the same window, the same durations and the same integral. We will ask whether it respects combinations of signals, then how much it can enlarge a difference measured by a norm.

The entire scene and all the values are fictional. The signal is a mathematical step function model, with no saturation, rounding or added noise. It is not a device reading or a claim about the performance of a real filter.

The rule to remember

A linear operator respects sums and changes of scale. Being bounded also requires a single amplification limit for every input in the domain.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

An averaging operator over a fixed window

Keep the window of duration τ = 2 s, with 1 s at 1 mV and 1 s at 7 mV. Let us calculate the image of the signal, its norm and the amplification limit valid for every admissible signal.

  1. Fix the space, norm and units

    H is the real space of functions whose square is integrable over [0, τ], identifying functions that are equal almost everywhere. Its notation is given below. “Almost everywhere” allows us to disregard a set of times of measure 0; changing only the value at the instant of the jump therefore does not change the element of H. t is time, in seconds; f(t) is voltage, in millivolts. τ remains strictly positive and fixed.

    H=L2([0,τ])

    We choose the normalized dot product below. g denotes another signal in H. The dot product is in mV² and the norm in mV; dividing the integral by τ removes the second introduced by integration. The fixed positive factor preserves the Hilbert space structure, that is, a space with a dot product that is complete in its norm.

    ⟨f,g⟩=1τ∫0τf(t)g(t) dt
    ∥f∥=1τ∫0τ|f(t)|2 dt

    This is a root mean square measure of size, rather than the largest instantaneous voltage. Every signal norm on this page refers to this choice.

  2. Define the operator and check its domain

    m(f) denotes the mean value associated with f. The operator T : H → H turns it into a constant signal. The arrow specifies that both its domain and its codomain are H. In the following integral, s is an integration time, in seconds; t indicates only the instant at which the output is read.

    m(f)=1τ∫0τf(s) ds;(Tf)(t)=m(f)

    On this finite window, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality ensures that the integral of |f| is finite: it is at most the square root of τ multiplied by the square root of the integral of the square of |f|. This inequality compares the absolute value of the dot product with the product of the norms; here it guarantees that m(f) exists for every f in H. A finite constant also belongs to H.

    ∫0τ|f(t)| dt≤τ∫0τ|f(t)|2 dt

    Representatives that are equal almost everywhere have the same integral. T is therefore well defined on the equivalence classes in H, without requiring a value at a particular instant. The height m(f) is in mV: the integral is in mV·s and is then divided by a duration in s.

  3. Prove linearity before substituting the data

    An operator is linear if it satisfies the following equality for all signals f and g in its domain and all dimensionless real numbers a and b. Integration respects sums and constant factors; the duration τ does not depend on the signal.

    T(af+bg)=aTf+bTg

    The property therefore holds here for all these inputs. It implies T(0) = 0. Testing only the constant levels of our example would not prove a universal equality. If the window were chosen according to the voltages, this proof would have to be reconsidered.

  4. Calculate the height of the image

    For a constant segment, integration amounts to multiplying its height by its duration. The first contributes 1 × 1 = 1 mV·s; the second contributes 7 × 1 = 7 mV·s. We add them because these intervals partition the whole window.

    m(f)=1+72=82=4 mV

    The result is Tf(t) = 4 mV almost everywhere. Check: a constant level of 4 mV for 2 s gives the area 8 mV·s, just as the original signal does. The arithmetic mean of the heights suffices only because the durations are equal.

  5. Measure what the operator preserves and removes

    For the input norm, square each height before weighting it by its duration. The squares are 1 and 49 mV²; the integral of the square is 50 mV²·s. After division by 2 s, the square of ∥f∥ is 25 mV². The positive square root is ∥f∥ = 5 mV, checked by 5 × 5 = 25.

    For the constant output, the square of ∥Tf∥ is 16 mV² and ∥Tf∥ = 4 mV. The ratio in this test is 4/5 = 0.80, with no unit. It does not yet measure the worst case.

    Call r = f − Tf the residual that was removed. Its constant levels are -3 and 3 mV, with mean 0 mV. Its mean square is (9 + 9)/2 = 9 mV², so ∥r∥ = 3 mV, since 3 × 3 = 9.

    Check with another decomposition: 16 + 9 = 25 mV², just as for the square of ∥f∥. This Pythagorean identity follows from orthogonality: for any constant signal of height c, ⟨r,c⟩ is c multiplied by the 0 mean of r. T is therefore the orthogonal projection onto the constant signals.

  6. Go from this test to a universal bound

    T is bounded if there is a finite, dimensionless real number C ≥ 0 such that ∥Th∥ ≤ C∥h∥ for every h in H. From now on, h denotes an arbitrary input, including signals that are not step functions. C must be the same for all of them. The operator norm is the supremum of the ratios: their least upper bound.

    ∥T∥=suph∈H,h≠0∥Th∥∥h∥

    To bound all the ratios from above, expand the square of h − m(h). Set μ = m(h), in mV, to simplify the notation. The cross term has mean −2μ × m(h) = −2μ × μ. This leaves the following identity; its left side is the mean of a square, so it is nonnegative.

    1τ∫0τ(h(t)−μ)2 dt=∥h∥2−μ2≥0

    The norm of Th is the absolute value of μ. The identity therefore gives ∥Th∥ ≤ ∥h∥. The constant C = 1 works for every input; the squared terms in the identity are in mV².

    To prove that no smaller constant works, take h = Tf, our nonzero constant signal at 4 mV. The mean of a constant is the constant itself: Th = h. The ratio is 1. The universal upper bound and this equality give exactly ∥T∥ = 1. The ratio 0.80 for the original input was just one particular ratio.

  7. Distinguish stability from preservation of information

    Applying T to the constant signal again changes nothing: T(Tf) = Tf. We also write T∘T = T, where the symbol ∘ denotes composition of the operator with itself. This property is called idempotence and defines an algebraic projection in our linear setting; orthogonality was justified separately.

    Our f and its constant image Tf are different, but they have the same image under T. The transformation is therefore not injective: an output does not allow us to recover a unique input. An operator norm equal to 1 does not promise to preserve any details of the signal.

  8. Deduce control of differences and continuity

    For two inputs f and g, linearity gives Tf − Tg = T(f − g). Apply the bound already proved to this new input:

    ∥Tf−Tg∥≤∥T∥∥f−g∥=∥f−g∥

    Both sides are in mV. If the norm of the input difference tends to 0, the norm of the output difference also tends to 0: this is continuity in this norm. This stability concerns root mean square differences; it does not bound instantaneous spikes or guarantee that a mean represents the signal well for every use.

    The word bounded concerns the output/input ratio. Multiplying a constant voltage by an arbitrarily large factor still gives an arbitrarily large output: ∥T∥ = 1 provides no absolute voltage limit. In infinite dimensions, linearity alone never removes the need to justify the bound.

Explain it in your own words

Why is the ratio calculated for the step signal insufficient to determine the norm of T? Reconstruct the proof in two stages: an upper bound for every input, then an input that attains that bound.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The norm of T is the ratio found in the example.”

    The ratio 0.80 concerns only one input. Revisit the bound for an arbitrary function, then test the constant signal Tf: its ratio 1 rules out any smaller bound.

    Revisit the universal bound and its equality case

  • “A bounded operator always keeps its output below a fixed voltage.”

    Write ∥Tf∥ ≤ C∥f∥: the input size remains on the right side. The bound limits relative amplification. A constant signal as large as you like passes through unchanged.

    Revisit control of differences and its units

  • “The mean of a step signal is always calculated by adding the heights and dividing by their number.”

    The definition adds areas, meaning voltages multiplied by their durations. The arithmetic mean of the heights equals the time average here because the durations are equal. If they change, retain the time weights.

Average again

Compare with the solution

Both constant levels are now 4 mV. Their sum is 8 mV; since the durations are equal, the mean is 8/2 = 4 mV. Another check: the integral of a constant c over the window is cτ, and division by τ then gives c again.

T(Tf) = Tf, rather than the original step signal. The two inputs f and Tf have the same image; the information about the jump has been removed. Stability of a transformation does not imply that it is reversible.

Exercise 2 · Control of a difference

Shift the whole recording

Add ε = 0.50 mV to the original signal everywhere, without changing the window. Call the resulting signal g. Calculate its mean, then ∥g − f∥ and ∥Tg − Tf∥. Is the stability bound attained?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The difference g − f is a constant function: its norm is the absolute value of its height.

Hint 2

Also use T(f + ε) = Tf + T(ε) to check the calculation of the constant levels.

Compare with the solution

The constant levels of g are 1.50 and 7.50 mV. Their sum is 9 mV; the time average is 9/2 = 4.50 mV, because the durations are equal.

The output difference is 4.50 − 4 = 0.50 mV. At the input, g − f is constant at ε, so ∥g − f∥ = 0.50 mV. At the output, ∥Tg − Tf∥ = 0.50 mV. Equality holds in the bound; T lets a constant offset pass through.

Independent check: T(ε) is the same constant ε. Linearity therefore requires m(g) = m(f) + ε, giving 4.50 mV again without recalculating the two areas.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the same duration and the same norm for both signals.
  • Calculate the difference of the functions before taking its norm.
  • Show equality of the two differences, with the unit mV, and check it using linearity.

Exercise 3 · General justification

Respond to a false conclusion

Someone claims: “The ratio is 0.80 for the recording, so ∥T∥ = 0.80 and all differences are strictly reduced.” Refute this conclusion, then reconstruct the exact value of ∥T∥ without restricting yourself to our two constant segments.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Expand the mean of the square of h − m(h) for an arbitrary signal h.

Hint 2

Then look for a nonzero constant difference; the previous exercise already provides this case.

Compare with the solution

The mean of the square of h − m(h) is nonnegative. Expanding it and using the definition of m(h) gives the square of ∥h∥ minus the square of m(h). Thus ∥Th∥ ≤ ∥h∥ for every h in H: the supremum of the ratios is at most 1.

For h = Tf, constant at 4 mV, Th = h and the ratio is 1. The supremum is therefore also at least 1, giving ∥T∥ = 1. This is the exact value; 0.80 was just one particular ratio.

The constant difference ε from the previous exercise is preserved. The operator does not increase any difference in this norm, but it does not strictly reduce them all. No success rate or improvement in a device is inferred from this mathematical property.

Check your reasoning

  • Give an upper bound valid for every function in H.
  • Exhibit a nonzero signal that attains the ratio 1.
  • Distinguish absence of amplification from strict reduction of every difference.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be fixed and checked to call T a linear operator?

Its domain, codomain and rule must be defined. We then need T(af + bg) = aTf + bTg for every input in the domain and all the scalars under consideration; our average uses a fixed window.

Recall 2What does bounded mean, and what is the unit of the norm of our operator?

A single finite constant C satisfies ∥Th∥ ≤ C∥h∥ for every input h. The input and output norms are both in mV: their ratio and the norm of T are dimensionless. This is not an absolute voltage limit.

Recall 3How can we prove that the norm of our operator is exactly 1?

The mean of the square of h − m(h) is nonnegative. Expanding it gives ∥Th∥ ≤ ∥h∥ for every input. A nonzero constant signal is unchanged and attains the ratio 1. Both arguments are needed, rather than a single test.

Recall 4How do we go from a bound on Th to a bound on differences between outputs?

By linearity, Tf − Tg = T(f − g), then ∥Tf − Tg∥ ≤ ∥T∥∥f − g∥. An input difference tending to 0 therefore leads to an output difference tending to 0 in the same norm.

Recall 5Why is averaging an orthogonal projection that loses information?

Its image is constant and a constant is preserved: T∘T = T. The residual f − Tf has 0 mean, so it is orthogonal to every constant signal. Different inputs have the same mean, which prevents their reconstruction from this output alone.
