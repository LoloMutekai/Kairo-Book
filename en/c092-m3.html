Density, CDF and quantile

Your goalRead a continuous forecast through its density, cumulative distribution function (CDF) and quantiles in a fictional example, then move between these descriptions with explicit units and assumptions.

✿01 See the idea

A probabilistic forecast does not give just one number: it distributes probability across all possible values. Our scene is entirely fictional. An overnight backup takes T minutes, and the forecast assigns it a duration between 0 and 8 minutes. We assume that this forecast was recorded before the observation; this rule belongs to the earlier units in the chapter and is not covered again here.

Three tools describe the same distribution from three angles. The density f answers the question "where is probability concentrated?": the higher the curve, the more plausible nearby durations are. The cumulative distribution function F, or CDF, answers "what is the probability of taking at most x minutes?": it accumulates probability from left to right. The quantile works in the opposite direction: we choose a probability p and read the duration below which that share of probability lies.

A density height is not a probability. A probability is an area under the curve, corresponding to an interval of durations. For a single exact duration, the area is 0, even if the curve has a height at that point. The units remind us of this: f is expressed in probability per minute, F has no unit, and a quantile is expressed in minutes.

We use a simple shape with a varying height: a symmetric triangle, defined by its width and peak. Its areas can be calculated as triangle areas, and all the results remain exact. This choice does not model real backups. The code cited at the end of the page uses a normal distribution, whose CDF uses the erf function; we retain only the three roles.

The rule to remember

The density is a height; the CDF is the accumulated area to the left of x; the quantile is the horizontal coordinate where this area reaches p; the probability of an interval is a difference of CDF values.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A fictional backup with a triangular forecast for its duration

Fictional values: the duration T of a backup is forecast to lie between 0 and 8 minutes, with the density peak at 4 minutes. We calculate the curve height, two cumulative probabilities and the probability of an interval, then read back from the levels to obtain quantiles.

  1. Define the symbols and assumptions

    T is the backup duration, in minutes, assumed to be continuous. L = 8 minutes is the width of the support: the density is 0 outside the interval from 0 to L. M = 4 minutes is the horizontal coordinate of the peak; we assume M = L / 2, which makes the triangle symmetric. K = L × M = 32 square minutes is a convenient constant.

    The density f(x) is expressed in probability per minute. The CDF F(x) = P(T ≤ x) is a probability, with no unit. A quantile q_p is a duration in minutes, associated with a level p between 0 and 1. Under these assumptions, the density to the left of the peak and then to the right is:

    f(x)=2xK
    f(x)=2(L−x)K

    The CDF is obtained by accumulating these triangular areas:

    F(x)=x2K
    F(x)=1−(L−x)2K

    The first formula applies to x between 0 and M, and the second to x between M and L. They are fictional, like the distribution.

  2. Check that the curve defines a probability distribution

    The peak height is 2 / L = 0.25 per minute. The triangle has a base L = 8 minutes and a height of 0.25 per minute, so its area is:

    aire=base×hauteur2

    The calculation gives 8 × 0.25 = 2, then 2 / 2 = 1. A total area equal to 1 is essential: otherwise the probabilities do not add up to the whole.

  3. Read the density and CDF at a

    Take a = 2 minutes, to the left of the peak. The density is 4 / 32 = 0.125 per minute: this is a height, not a probability. The CDF is a probability:

    F(2)=2232=432=0.125

    Geometric check: the shaded region in the first figure is a triangle with base a and height f(a). Its area is 2 × 0.125 / 2 = 0.125, identical to F(a). The formula and the geometry agree.

  4. Move to the right side and to an interval

    Take b = 6 minutes, to the right of the peak. The formula for the right side uses L − b = 2 minutes, then its square 4, divided by K = 32: the probability of exceeding b is 0.125. The CDF is its complement:

    F(6)=1−(8−6)232=1−0.125=0.875

    To obtain the probability that T falls between a and b, subtract the probability below a from the probability below b:

    P(a<T≤b)=F(b)−F(a)

    The result is 0.875 − 0.125 = 0.75. The two tails, below a and above b, each have probability 0.125, consistent with the symmetry of the triangle.

  5. Invert the CDF: find the quantiles

    A quantile answers the inverse question: for a given p, what duration q_p satisfies F(q_p) = p? Since F is 0.5 at the peak, a level p at most equal to 0.5 is read on the left branch, and a higher level on the right branch.

    qp=pK
    qp=L−(1−p)K

    Level p = 0.125, left branch: p × K = 4. The positive root is 2, since 2 × 2 = 4. The quantile is therefore 2 minutes, exactly a: going from the CDF to the quantile brings us back to the starting point.

    Level p = 0.875, right branch: (1 − p) × K = 0.125 × 32 = 4. The root is again 2, so q = 8 − 2 = 6 minutes, exactly b. Level p = 0.5: p × K = 16, and 4 × 4 = 16, so the median is at the peak, 4 minutes.

  6. Cross-check and interpret within the evidence

    The quantiles at levels 0.125 and 0.875, namely 2 and 6 minutes, bound a central interval of width 4 minutes. Its probability is 0.875 − 0.125 = 0.75, as in the previous step: the CDF, quantile and geometry all give the same result.

    This value describes the forecast, not the world. Whether the forecast is reliable is a separate claim: it requires comparing many recorded forecasts with their observed outcomes later.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading: why is the density height at a not the probability of taking exactly a minutes? How do the CDF values at b and a give the probability of the interval, and why does the quantile at level 0.125 lead back to a?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The density height is the probability of the value.”

    Density is expressed in probability per minute. A probability is an area: at the peak, the height is 0.25 per minute, whereas a single exact duration has 0 area. Only the area between a and b gives the probability 0.75.

    Review the step on density and the CDF

  • “F(x) is the probability of exceeding x.”

    F accumulates to the left: F(x) = P(T ≤ x). The probability of exceeding x is one minus F(x). At b, F is 0.875 and the probability of exceeding it is 0.125. The code examined in the milestone also calculates the probability of exceeding a threshold as one minus the CDF.

    See the threshold probability in the code

  • “An interval between two quantiles guarantees that the next duration will fall within it.”

    It has probability 0.75 according to the forecast, not certainty. Even a duration of 7 minutes, outside the interval, settles nothing by itself; the reliability of a forecast is judged over many observed outcomes.

    Return to the exercise on a fictional observation

Compare with the solution

Density: 2 × 3 = 6, then 6 / 32 = 0.1875 per minute. This number is a height.

CDF: 3 × 3 = 9, then 9 / 32 = 0.28125, with no unit. Probability of exceeding x: 1 − 0.28125 = 0.71875.

Lower: p × K = 0.03125 × 32 = 1. The positive root is 1, because 1 × 1 = 1. The quantile is 1 minute.

Upper: one minus p is 0.03125; multiplying by K gives 1, with root 1. The quantile is 8 − 1 = 7 minutes.

Exercise 3 · Critical reading

A fictional observation far from the centre

A fictional observation arrives after the forecast has been recorded: the backup took 7 minutes. Calculate the probability of exceeding 7 minutes according to the forecast and compare it with the probability of exceeding 6 minutes. Can we conclude that the forecast is poor? Explain.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

7 is to the right of the peak: use the square of L − x, divided by K.

Hint 2

Compare the two exceedance probabilities by taking their ratio, then consider how many forecasts with known outcomes would be enough to reach a conclusion.

Compare with the solution

Probability of exceeding 7 minutes: L − 7 = 1, square 1, divided by 32: 0.03125. For 6 minutes, it was 0.125, which is 4 times as high.

This observation was therefore unlikely according to the forecast, but not impossible. An isolated outcome can neither validate nor reject the forecast: we would need to compare many recorded forecasts with their outcomes, using a scoring rule. The next unit in the chapter introduces such rules.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the density value at x represent, and in what unit?

A curve height, in probability per minute in our scene. It is not the probability of the value: the probability of an interval is the area under the curve.

Recall 2What does F(x) measure, and in which direction does it accumulate?

The probability that T is less than or equal to x, accumulated from left to right, with no unit. The probability of exceeding x is one minus F(x).

Recall 3What does the quantile at level p do?

It inverts the CDF: it is the duration q such that F(q) = p, expressed in minutes; the median corresponds to p = one half.

Recall 4How do we obtain the probability that T falls between a and b?

By the difference F(b) − F(a). This probability describes the forecast; it does not guarantee an observation.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Describe the forecast by its density, a height in probability per unit; accumulate areas to obtain the CDF; read an interval probability as a difference of CDF values; invert the CDF to obtain a quantile in the units of the variable; check each result using an area or by going from one description to another and back; keep the forecast separate from its validation against observed outcomes.
