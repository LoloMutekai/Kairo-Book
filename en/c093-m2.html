Level 2 · C093·M2

Pairwise slopes and Theil–Sen

Your goalCalculate all the slopes between readings taken at recorded times, take their median, and explain why a disturbed reading leaves this estimate unchanged here.

Before you start
c090-m5 : stationarity · c056-m5 : effect size · c063-m5 : synthetic dataset and external validity · c025-m5 : O, Omega and Theta · c093-m1 : median, MAD and normal scaling

✿01 See the idea

A probe tracks the warming of a small enclosure. The first readings show a steady rise; the last is very high. If you connect only the first reading to the last, your entire estimate of the warming rate depends on that final reading.

Let us ask a different question: what rate does each pair of readings indicate? For each pair, divide the temperature change by the time that actually elapsed. Then sort all these slopes and choose their center, called the median. This slope estimate is called Theil–Sen.

The median depends on positions in the sorted list. A very large slope remains one entry in that list; its magnitude does not add directly to the central value. But the same reading belongs to several pairs, so a single reading can affect the method.

All the data for this enclosure are fictional. We deliberately raised the last value to test the calculation. In a real record, a high reading could be an error or a real change: the slope alone does not settle that question.

The rule to remember

Form each pair only once, divide the difference in readings by the difference in time, keep repeated slopes, sort, and take the median. Keep track of the unit and the limitation of this estimate.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A slope for the whole record

Time t is in minutes since the start of the record; reading y is in degrees Celsius. The pairs (t ; y) are A = (0 ; 20), B = (1 ; 22), C = (2 ; 24), D = (3 ; 26), E = (6 ; 60). Let us estimate the slope from these 5 readings without removing any of them.

  1. Define what is being estimated

    n is the number of readings and has no unit. Letters A through E name the observations in time order. Indices i and j identify two observations, with i before j; tᵢ and tⱼ are their times in minutes, and yᵢ and yⱼ are their readings in °C. bᵢⱼ denotes the slope of this pair, in °C/min. The hat on b̂ indicates the estimate obtained from the whole record.

    We use finite, comparable values expressed in the same units, with known, distinct times. We seek a single approximately affine trend over this interval: a change at an approximately constant rate. The calculation remains defined if the shape is not affine, but this interpretation may then be inappropriate.

    Neither a normal distribution nor independence of the readings is required to carry out the divisions and take the median. Here these operations provide neither a probability, nor a test for the presence of a trend, nor a guarantee about future temperatures.

  2. List the pairs without duplicates

    Pair A with B, C, D and E; pair B with C, D and E; pair C with D and E; pair D with E. The complete list is AB, AC, AD, AE, BC, BD, BE, CD, CE, DE.

    Why divide the count by 2? Choosing a starting observation and another observation counts both AB and BA; the slope does not change if both differences are reversed together. Each pair has therefore been counted 2 times. Pairing an observation with itself would give a time difference of 0 and does not define a slope.

    N=n(n−1)2=5×42=10

    N denotes the number of pairs and has no unit. Counting the list confirms that it has 10 entries.

  3. Turn a pair into a rate

    bij=yj−yitj−ti

    The numerator is a temperature change, not an initial temperature. The denominator is a strictly positive duration. The slope is positive if the reading rises. If the times coincided, division by 0 would be undefined: this pair could not be used.

    For AB: Δy = 22 − 20 = 2 °C; Δt = 1 − 0 = 1 min. So b_AB = 2/1 = 2 °C/min. Here the symbol Δ denotes the final value minus the starting value.

    For AE: Δy = 60 − 20 = 40 °C; Δt = 6 − 0 = 6 min. So b_AE = 40/6 = 20/3 °C/min, or approximately 6.67 °C/min. The fraction remains exact; only the last displayed value is rounded.

  4. Calculate the remaining slopes

    For AC: (24 − 20)/(2 − 0) = 4/2 = 2 °C/min. For AD: (26 − 20)/(3 − 0) = 6/3 = 2 °C/min.

    For BC: (24 − 22)/(2 − 1) = 2/1 = 2 °C/min. For BD: (26 − 22)/(3 − 1) = 4/2 = 2 °C/min.

    For BE: (60 − 22)/(6 − 1) = 38/5 = 38/5 °C/min. For CD: (26 − 24)/(3 − 2) = 2/1 = 2 °C/min.

    For CE: (60 − 24)/(6 − 2) = 36/4 = 9 °C/min. For DE: (60 − 26)/(6 − 3) = 34/3 = 34/3 °C/min.

    Including AB and AE, which were already calculated, we have 10 slopes. None has been removed for being large; identical values remain as many times as their pairs occur.

  5. Sort, then choose the center

    In increasing order, the slopes in °C/min are: 2 ; 2 ; 2 ; 2 ; 2 ; 2 ; 20/3 ; 38/5 ; 9 ; 34/3. Ties are displayed in letter order; this ordering of equal values does not change the result.

    The median is the central value of a sorted list of odd length. For an even length, we take the mean of the two central values. Here N = 10 is even: the central positions, at ranks 5 and 6, contain BD and CD, both equal to 2 °C/min.

    b^=médiane(bij)=2+22=42=2 °C/min

    The median notation in the formula uses all the pairs listed. Here Theil–Sen gives an estimated increase of 2 °C per minute over the interval studied. This result is a slope; it is not enough to determine the vertical position of a line.

  6. Check using how the record was constructed

    The first 4 readings lie on a straight line. The number of pairs among them is 4 × 3/2 = 12/2 = 6. They all give 2 °C/min. The other 10 − 6 = 4 pairs involve E; they all have a larger slope in this record.

    This gives another explanation of the center: the first 6 positions are occupied by 2. The large slopes come next; their magnitude does not shift the two central positions in this particular case.

    Check a division using the inverse operation: for BE, 22 °C + (38/5 °C/min) × 5 min = 22 °C + 38 °C = 60 °C. Multiplying the slope by the duration does recover the change. Adding the same constant to all temperatures would cancel that constant in every difference and leave the slopes unchanged.

  7. State what the result does not settle

    We know why the disturbance to E does not change this median. That does not mean that every erroneous reading will have no effect. One observation contributes to several slopes and can shift their center: the last exercise illustrates this with part of the same record.

    The result does not decide whether E is wrong or whether sudden warming really occurred. Do not remove E on the basis of the slope alone. Comparing shapes and residuals belongs to the next unit; full change point detection is beyond this page.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Connecting the start and the end must give Theil–Sen.”

    AE gives 20/3 °C/min; it is only one of the 10 slopes. Reconstruct the complete list and its center: here the median is 2 °C/min.

    Review the pair listing, then the median

  • “Each row in the record represents one more minute.”

    B was recorded at 1 min and E at 6 min: the duration between them is 5 min. The number of rows between readings does not replace measured time. Divide 38 °C by this duration to obtain 38/5 °C/min.

    Redo pair B–E and check its unit

  • “The median always protects against an incorrect reading.”

    One reading belongs to several pairs. In the subset A, B, E from the last exercise, E affects 2 of the 3 slopes; the center becomes 20/3 °C/min. The resistance observed in the whole record is not general immunity.

    Compare the whole record with its subset

The change is 60 − 22 = 38 °C. The duration is 6 − 1 = 5 min. Their quotient gives 38/5 = 38/5 °C/min, or 7.60 °C/min.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same record

When a reading affects the center

Suppose that only A, B and E from the same record had been kept, with their original times. Calculate the Theil–Sen slope for this subset. Someone claims: “A single disturbed reading can never change the median slope.” Respond using your calculation. This subset is a counterexample for teaching, not a recommendation to remove C and D.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

With A, B and E, the pairs are AB, AE and BE. Their durations still use the original times.

Hint 2

Sort these slopes: a list of odd length has a single central value.

Compare with the solution

There are 3 pairs left: AB is 2 °C/min, AE is 20/3 °C/min and BE is 38/5 °C/min. They are already in increasing order: 2 < 20/3 < 38/5.

The central value is therefore b̂ = 20/3 °C/min, or approximately 6.67 °C/min. It differs from 2 °C/min for the whole record. E affects 2 of the 3 pairs, and one of these slopes occupies the center.

The general claim is false. The median resists some disturbances, but the effect depends on how many pairs are affected and on their positions after sorting. This calculation does not say whether E represents an error or a real event.

Check your reasoning

  • The times of A, B and E are kept, without replacing them with their new ranks.
  • The median of the odd-length list is 20/3 °C/min; it is not the mean of the three slopes.
  • The refutation cites the 2 pairs affected by E and does not turn the slope into a fault diagnosis.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you calculate a slope between two readings, and what is its unit here?

Divide the temperature change yⱼ − yᵢ by the duration tⱼ − tᵢ, keeping the same direction in both differences. A change in °C divided by a duration in minutes gives °C/min.

Recall 2Why are there n(n − 1)/2 pairs for n distinct times?

Each observation can be paired with the n − 1 others. This counts each pair in both directions; dividing by 2 removes that duplication. No observation is paired with itself.

Recall 3What does Theil–Sen do after calculating the slopes? How do you handle an even length?

Sort all the slopes, keeping repeated values, and take their median. For an even length, take the mean of the two central values; for an odd length, take the value at the center.

Recall 4Why can you not replace a time with its rank in an irregular record?

Rank counts positions; the recorded time measures time. Differences in rank and in recorded time represent the same duration only if a known regular sampling interval allows a conversion. Two identical times do not allow a slope between those readings: the denominator would be 0.

Recall 5Why can a single disturbed reading still affect Theil–Sen?

It belongs to several pairs. These slopes can reach the central positions after sorting. In A, B, E, the 2 pairs containing E shift the center to 20/3 °C/min. Resistance observed in one example is not a universal guarantee.
