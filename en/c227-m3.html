Level 4 · C227·M3

General quadratic variation

Your goalDefine the quadratic variation of a process as the limit of sums of squared increments, decompose it into a continuous part and jumps, then calculate it for a fictional water level while distinguishing a sum on a grid from its limit.

Before you start
c226-m5 : integration with jumps · c165-m5 : duality · c171-m5 : the multidimensional case · c227-m1 : finite variation · c227-m2 : Doob-Meyer

✿01 See the idea

A fictional water level gauge records the water level X in a basin, in centimetres, over 4 minutes. We read it at the end of each minute, so the period contains 4 intervals of one minute. Between readings, the level has changed by an amount called an increment. How can we measure the fluctuations in this level without its slow rise or the net change from start to finish masking them?

The net change adds increments with different signs: rises and falls cancel out. If we square each increment before adding them, the signs disappear and each part contributes according to its size. Three behaviours then separate as the grid is refined. A steady displacement D, divided into n equal increments, gives a sum of squares equal to D² divided by n: it tends to 0. Fluctuations of diffusion type have increments whose squares are proportional to the interval duration; n intervals give a sum proportional to the total duration, whatever n is. An isolated jump of height J produces an increment whose square J² does not shrink as the grid is refined.

The limit of this sum as the mesh size tends to 0 is called quadratic variation and is written [X]. It is expressed in cm², the square of the unit of the level, just like a variance.

The values in this scene are fictional. The path is constructed by hand: a walk with a fixed step size imitates a diffusion, without representing either an actual recording or a sample of Brownian motion. The lesson does not validate any settings for a real basin.

The rule to remember

Partition, square each increment, add, then refine the grid: the steady drift vanishes, the diffusive part accumulates as intensity times duration, and each jump adds its square.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Four minutes of water levels, from the observed sum to quadratic variation

The level is modelled by X = drift + diffusive part + jump. The drift rises by 1 cm per minute. The diffusive part has intensity σ² = 1 cm² per minute; its observed increments on the grid of one minute are 1, 1, -1 and 1 cm. A jump of 3 cm occurs at time 2 min. Let us calculate the sum of squares on the grid and the quadratic variation of the model, then explain the difference.

  1. Set out the symbols, definition and assumptions

    Time t is measured in minutes and level X(t) in centimetres, on the interval from 0 to T with T = 4 min. A grid Π is a sequence of increasing times in this interval; its mesh size |Π| is the largest gap between consecutive times. The increment over interval k is ΔkX = X(tk) − X(tk-1), in cm. Here the grid contains n = 4 intervals, so Δt = 4 ÷ 4 = 1 min.

    S(Π)=∑k=1n(ΔkX)2

    S(Π) is the sum of squares on the grid, in cm². Definition accepted here: if X is a semimartingale, meaning the sum of a local martingale and a process of finite variation (the concept from c227-m1), then S(Π) converges in probability, as the mesh size tends to 0, to an increasing process denoted [X].

    [X]=lim|Π|→0S(Π) en probabilité

    Model assumptions: the drift is continuous and of finite variation; the diffusive part is a multiple of Brownian motion, whose quadratic variation equals intensity times duration (a result accepted here); the jump has a fixed size and time. The link with Doob-Meyer is also accepted here: for a local martingale M, M² − [M] is a local martingale; the predictable compensator ⟨M⟩, when it exists, differs from [M] by a local martingale, and the two processes coincide if M is continuous. Without the semimartingale assumption, the sum may fail to converge to any limit. The covariation of two processes follows by polarization; it is not used here.

  2. Read the increments on the grid

    Over each interval, the increment adds the drift aΔt, the step wk of the walk and jk, the jump contained in the interval: jk equals J if the interval contains it and 0 otherwise. Reading it this way separates the causes before combining them in the squares.

    ΔkX=aΔt+wk+jk

    The drift is 1 × 1 = 1 cm per interval. Without a jump: u1 = 1 + 1 = 2 cm, u2 = 1 + 1 = 2 cm, u3 = 1 + (-1) = 0 cm and u4 = 1 + 1 = 2 cm. The jump occurs in interval two, which ends at time 2 min: v2 = 2 + 3 = 5 cm. The observed increments are therefore 2, 5, 0 and 2 cm.

  3. Square and add on the grid

    Squaring removes the signs: a negative increment and a positive increment of the same size contribute equally. The squares are 4, 25, 0 and 4 cm². The running sums are 4 + 25 = 29, then 29 + 0 = 29, then 29 + 4 = 33 cm².

    4+25+0+4=33 cm²

    The interval over which the level stays flat contributes nothing, but it does not cancel anything either: terms in a sum of squares never cancel out.

  4. Predict the limit using the decomposition

    For a semimartingale, quadratic variation splits into two parts. X^c denotes the continuous martingale part; ΔX(s) = X(s) − X(s⁻) is the jump at time s, nonzero at at most countably many times. The following result is accepted here:

    [X]=[Xc]+∑s≤t(ΔX(s))2

    The continuous drift of finite variation does not appear; step six shows why. The continuous part equals intensity times duration, and the single jump adds its square, whatever its sign.

    [Xc]=σ2T=1×4=4 cm²
    [X]=4+32=4+9=13 cm²

  5. Explain the difference between the observed sum and the model

    The observed sum, 33 cm², exceeds the model value, 13 cm². This difference is not a contradiction: the definition requires a limit, rather than a single grid.

    33−13=20=4+16 cm²

    Expand the square of each increment: it contains squares of drift, walk and jump, plus cross products. The drift alone gives 4 × 1 = 4 cm². The walk alone gives 4 × 1 = 4 cm², equal to σ² × T = 4 cm², because each step satisfies pas² = σ² × Δt. The jump alone gives 9 cm². The cross products therefore equal 20 − 4 = 16 cm². They vanish in the limit: the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality bounds the drift–walk cross term by the square root of the product of the two sums of squares, and the sum for the drift tends to 0; the cross term with the jump involves only one interval, whose other increments tend to 0.

  6. Refine the grid: the drift vanishes

    Divide the interval duration by 4: the grid goes from 4 to 16 intervals. Each drift increment is divided by four, so its square is divided by sixteen, while the number of intervals is multiplied by four. The sum of squared drift increments is divided by 4.

    n(Dn)2=D2n

    In the example, 4 ÷ 4 = 1 cm². With D fixed, D²/n tends to 0 as n grows. The diffusive part keeps n × Δt equal to the total duration, and the jump keeps its square: this is why only these two parts survive.

  7. Check the result

    Units: all results are in cm², never in cm; adding an increment to a sum of squares would mix these units. Sign: replacing -1 by its opposite would not change its square, so it would not change the walk contribution. Limiting case: without diffusion or jumps, the model gives [X] = 0, while the sum on a grid retains a residual drift contribution, here 4 cm². Independent calculation: reconstructing the path at the grid times and then recalculating the differences gives the same increments and the same sum 33 cm².

    This result describes the fictional model and its path constructed by hand; it does not prove that a real water level follows this decomposition.

Explain it in your own words

Why does the sum over four intervals (33 cm²) exceed the model value (13 cm²) without contradicting the definition? Say what vanishes as the mesh size tends to 0 and what remains.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The sum of squares on my grid is the quadratic variation.”

    Quadratic variation is a limit as the mesh size tends to 0. On the grid in the example, the sum is 33 cm², while the model value is 13 cm²: cross products and a residual drift contribution still count at this mesh size.

    Review the difference between the sum and the limit

  • “Drift contributes to [X] as much as the fluctuations do.”

    For a displacement D divided into n equal increments, the sum of squares is D²/n and tends to 0. In the example, the drift gives 4 cm² with 4 intervals, then 1 cm² with 16 intervals.

    Review the refinement of the grid

  • “The square of the net change gives the quadratic variation.”

    The square of a sum contains cross products with varying signs; the sum of squares does not. Compare 81 cm², the square of the net change, with 33 cm², the sum of squares over four intervals.

    Repeat the grid comparison in exercise one

Exercise 1 · Explained application

A coarser grid

Use the observed increments from the example. Group them in pairs, into intervals of 2 minutes, then into a single interval of 4 minutes. Calculate the sum of squares on each grid and compare it with the sum over four intervals (33 cm²).

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First add the increments within each larger interval; then square the result.

Hint 2

For a single interval, the increment is the net change over the whole period.

Compare with the solution

First interval: 2 + 5 = 7 cm; second: 0 + 2 = 2 cm. Squares: 49 and 4; sum 53 cm². A single interval: 7 + 2 = 9 cm, with square 81 cm². The three sums 81, 53 and 33 differ: no single grid defines [X]. Here they all exceed 13 cm², but this describes this fictional path, rather than a general law.

Check your reasoning

  • Add the increments before squaring them.
  • Obtain three different sums and conclude that a single grid does not give [X].
  • Write the results in cm².

Exercise 2 · Transfer

Another basin, two jumps

A second fictional level has a diffusive part with intensity σ² = 3 cm² per minute over 4 minutes, an arbitrary continuous drift of finite variation, and two jumps: 3 cm followed by -1 cm, the latter being a fall. Calculate its quadratic variation at the time horizon.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The continuous part and the squared jumps add together; the drift does not contribute, and the sign of a jump has no effect.

Compare with the solution

Continuous part: 3 × 4 = 12 cm². Squared jumps: 3² = 9 and (-1)² = 1, giving 10 cm². Total: 12 + 10 = 22 cm². The fall contributes as much as a rise of the same size because we add squares.

Check your reasoning

  • Ignore the continuous drift of finite variation.
  • Square each jump, including the fall.
  • Give the final result in cm².

Exercise 3 · Analysis

Drift and jump only

Remove the diffusion from the example: only the drift of 1 cm per minute and the jump of 3 cm at time 2 min remain. Calculate the sum of squares on the grid of one minute, compare it with the sum of squared jumps, the value of [X] for this process of finite variation, then interpret the difference.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The increments are 1 cm, except in the interval containing the jump, where the jump is added.

Hint 2

The 3 intervals without a jump all have the same squared increment.

Compare with the solution

Interval containing the jump: 1 + 3 = 4 cm, with square 16 cm². The other 3 intervals give 4 − 1 = 3 cm². Sum: 3 + 16 = 19 cm². Value of [X]: the sum of squared jumps, 9 cm². Difference: 19 − 9 = 10 cm². It comes from the drift in the intervals and the product of drift and jump in the interval containing the jump; it vanishes as the mesh size tends to 0. With a continuous drift of finite variation, only the sum of squared jumps remains.

Check your reasoning

  • Separate the interval containing the jump from the other intervals.
  • Compare with the sum of squared jumps, without adding diffusion.
  • Explain the difference using the drift and its cross product with the jump.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the quadratic variation [X] of a semimartingale X?

The limit in probability of the sums of squared increments of X on grids whose mesh size tends to 0; it is expressed in the square of the unit of X.

Recall 2How does [X] decompose?

Into the quadratic variation of the continuous martingale part, plus the sum of squared jumps of X.

Recall 3Why does a continuous drift of finite variation not contribute to [X]?

For a displacement D divided into n equal increments, the sum of squares is D²/n, which tends to 0 as the mesh size tends to 0.

Recall 4Why does a sum on a single grid not give [X]?

[X] is a limit as the mesh size tends to 0; a fixed grid retains cross products and a residual drift contribution.

Recall 5What is the link between [M] and the compensator ⟨M⟩ of a local martingale M?

M² − [M] is a local martingale, and [M] differs from ⟨M⟩ by a local martingale; if M is continuous, [M] and ⟨M⟩ coincide.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Partition the period into intervals, square each increment, add, then refine the grid: the continuous drift of finite variation vanishes, the continuous part equals intensity times duration, and each jump adds its square; a fixed grid is only an approximation that retains cross products; everything is expressed in the square of the unit.
