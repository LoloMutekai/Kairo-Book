Motivation

Your goalExplain why a goal that matters may fail to trigger action, then separate the value of the goal, anticipated effort and observed success.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

Sam has put his study worksheet on the table. His goal is to solve the problem on that worksheet on his own by the end of the session. This goal matters to him, but finding the materials and getting started seem costly. He is still hesitating. Liking the idea of understanding is therefore not enough to describe what will get him working.

Here, motivation means the processes that direct action towards a goal, help initiate it and sustain effort over time. To examine it, ask: what goal is Sam working towards? What effort is he willing to make? Does he continue after a difficulty? Choice, starting and persistence are different observations.

Reward concerns what is sought or obtained; motivation concerns mobilizing effort to reach it. The pleasure felt while studying and the ability to solve the problem are separate questions again. Sam may want to understand without enjoying every minute of work; starting does not guarantee that he will be able to answer.

The same goal may be pursued out of an interest in understanding or for an external consequence, such as praise. This distinction between interest in the activity itself and an expected consequence does not require choosing a single reason: they can coexist within the same session.

We will represent Sam’s hesitation with a small model of choice. All its values and probabilities are fictional and fixed for the purpose of reasoning. The value points and effort units are conventions of the exercise, with no biological measurement or estimate of your level.

Scientific context: the experimental study of cognitive effort by Westbrook and colleagues, published in Science, links willingness to exert effort to the weighting of benefits and costs, and to striatal dopamine signaling. The striatum is a group of brain structures involved in action selection, among other functions; dopamine is a chemical messenger between neurons. This finding in a specific setting does not make dopamine either a universal motivation counter or a measure of Sam’s pleasure. The full reference is in rapport.md.

The rule to remember

To understand an action, separate the value of the goal, the perceived chance of reaching it and the cost of effort; then check what was actually attempted and achieved.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The goal matters, but starting has a cost

Sam compares undertaking the session with postponing it. Reaching the goal is worth V = 8 points; the assumed chance of reaching it is p = 3/4, or 0.75 with no unit. Anticipated effort is E = 4 effort units, weighted by k = 2 points per effort unit. Postponing serves as the reference at 0 points. Predict the choice before calculating.

  1. State what the model represents

    V is the value assigned to reaching the goal, in conventional points. p is the subjective probability of reaching it if Sam undertakes the session; it lies between 0 and 1, with no unit. It is given in the problem, rather than estimated from recorded observations. Reaching the goal yields V; failing to reach it yields 0 points in this simplified representation.

    E is the total anticipated effort of undertaking the session, in conventional effort units. k is the weight of this effort, strictly positive, in points per effort unit. C denotes its cost, in points; B the expected benefit, in points; S the net score of the session, in points. The letters denote neither a hormone nor measured brain activity.

    We assume that the cost C is incurred whether or not the goal is reached, that the parameters remain fixed during the choice, and that Sam can undertake the session. Here, postponing has a score fixed at 0; possible benefits of rest are omitted. The model does not describe a complete assessment of real-life trade-offs.

  2. Write an explicit comparison rule

    The expected benefit is a weighted average of the possible outcomes. Multiplying each value by the probability of its outcome accounts for uncertainty. The effort cost must be expressed in the same unit before it is subtracted.

    B=pV+(1−p)×0=pV
    C=kE;S=B−C=pV−kE

    The fictional rule chooses the session if S is greater than 0, and postponement if S is less than 0. At equality, it leaves the choice undetermined. This deterministic rule serves to analyze assumptions; it does not claim that a person always decides this way.

  3. Calculate the benefit without promising success

    The given probability is 3 ÷ 4 = 0.75. Its complement, for the goal not being reached, is 1 − 0.75 = 0.25. Their sum is 1: the outcomes cover our simplified description.

    B=0.75×8+0.25×0=6+0=6 points

    The 6 points are an expected value before action. They mean neither 6 problems solved nor an observed improvement in memory. In this particular session, the outcome remains unknown.

  4. Convert effort, then subtract

    The weight k converts effort to the points scale of V. The product gives C = 2 points per unit × 4 units = 8 points. Subtracting E directly from V would mix units and overlook uncertainty.

    S=6−8=-2 points<0

    The rule therefore selects postponement. Yet V remains positive: Sam can value the goal while anticipating effort that is too costly relative to its expected benefit. The sign of the score compares options; it makes no judgment about Sam.

  5. Make starting easier while keeping the same goal

    Compare a variant of the same scene: the materials are ready in advance and E becomes 2 units. We keep p, V and k unchanged to isolate this parameter. The expected benefit remains 6 points; the cost becomes 2 × 2 = 4 points.

    S=6−4=2 points>0

    The rule now favors the session. Preparation has added no value to the goal in the model; it has lowered its anticipated cost. We have observed neither an actual start, nor a correct answer, nor increased persistence.

  6. Check by another route and locate the boundary

    The effort removed is 4 − 2 = 2 units. Its cost is 2 × 2 = 4 points. The difference between the scores is also 2 − (-2) = 4 points. We find the same shift without recalculating the benefits.

    Equality with postponement occurs when kE = B: E = B ÷ k = 6 ÷ 2 = 3 units. At this boundary, C = 6 points and S = 0 points. The high effort is above it; the reduced effort is below it. This confirms the signs obtained.

    To test this explanation against the scene, we would need to ask Sam what he expects and what effort he anticipates, then observe his choice and attempt on the same worksheet. Difficulty understanding or unavailable materials can limit action. An isolated behavior does not allow its causes to be inferred with certainty.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results. Explain why postponement remains compatible with an important goal, then work out what preparing the materials changes and what it does not allow you to conclude. Define each letter with its unit before repeating the calculation.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If he does not start, the goal must not interest him.”

    In the scene, the value of the goal remains positive while its anticipated cost exceeds the expected benefit. Improve the explanation by also examining the perceived chance of success and the effort, then the actual ability to act. Refusal alone does not distinguish between these causes.

    Review the cost of starting and the checks on it

  • “A positive score means he has learned.”

    The score compares options before action. Starting, continuing and solving without support still need to be observed; even this immediate solution does not demonstrate lasting learning. Improve the explanation by naming the evidence needed for each step.

    Reread the path from choice to success

  • “Dopamine directly gives the number of motivation points.”

    The points are a calculation convention; no dopamine concentration is measured here. A study linking dopamine signaling and effort in a task does not turn our score into a biological quantity. Improve the explanation by distinguishing a fictional parameter, a task observation and a brain mechanism.

Compare with the solution

B = 0.50 × 8 = 4 points. C = 2 × 2 = 4 points. S = 4 − 4 = 0 points. The session and postponement have the same score: the rule does not distinguish between the options.

V has remained at 8 points. It is the perceived chance of reaching the goal, p, that has decreased. Concluding that the goal matters less would confuse distinct parameters; concluding that Sam will fail would confuse a forecast with an outcome.

  • Distinguish the value of the goal from the perceived chance of success.

Exercise 2 · Reasoning · find a boundary

The goal becomes more valuable

Return to the costly start: p = 0.75, E = 4 units and k = 2 points per unit. V increases to 12 points. Predict the direction of change, calculate the new score, then find the exact value of V that would make the session and postponement equivalent. What would still need to be observed?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At a fixed cost, increasing V increases pV because p is positive.

Hint 2

At equality, pV = C. Isolate V by dividing the cost by p.

Compare with the solution

The score increases. B = 0.75 × 12 = 9 points; C remains 8 points; S = 9 − 8 = 1 point. The rule favors the session.

At the boundary, V = C ÷ p = 8 ÷ 3/4 = 32/3 points, or approximately 10.67 points. Above this exact value, the score is positive; at equality, it is 0. Comparing V only with E would have overlooked the units and p.

A higher assigned value does not establish greater skill. Starting, sustained effort and solving the problem without support still need to be observed. None of these facts is provided by the calculation.

Check your reasoning

  • State the direction before the result and keep the high cost.
  • Find an exact boundary in points, without rounding during the calculation.
  • Separate predicted preference, engagement and outcome.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · critique an interpretation

The same hesitation, different explanations

Sam postpones his session. Compare the initial parameters p = 0.75, E = 4 with another hypothesis p = 0.25, E = 2. In the cases compared, V remains 8 points and k remains 2 points per unit. Show why postponement does not allow you to choose between these explanations. Propose a useful question and observation, without diagnosing Sam.

Hint 1

Calculate the scores separately: the benefit and cost can both decrease.

Hint 2

The same score can come from different parameters. Asking what Sam anticipates does not replace observing what he can do.

Compare with the solution

The initial hypothesis gives B = 6 and C = 8, so S = -2 points. The other gives B = 0.25 × 8 = 2 points and C = 2 × 2 = 4 points; S = 2 − 4 = -2 points. The scores are equal and negative: the rule predicts the same postponement.

Here, the observation does not even distinguish a lower perceived chance from greater anticipated effort. On its own, it therefore establishes neither a lack of interest, nor a level of brain activity, nor an inability.

Possible question: “What seems difficult about getting started, and do you think you could solve the problem if you began?” Possible observation: check that the materials are accessible, then observe a voluntary attempt on the same worksheet, distinguishing starting from answering. These elements help assess hypotheses; they do not directly measure a single quantity of motivation.

Check your reasoning

  • Find the same score using different parameters.
  • Explain why an isolated behavior does not identify its cause.
  • Propose a question about expectations and an observation of action, without labeling the person.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What are you trying to describe when you talk about motivation in Sam’s session?

Direction towards the goal, the initiation of action and the sustaining of effort. Choosing the session, starting it and continuing it require distinct observations.

Recall 2How can you reconstruct the fictional score, with its units and assumptions?

B = pV in points if reaching the goal is worth V and failing to reach it is worth 0; C = kE in points if k converts the effort units and the cost is incurred regardless of the outcome; S = B − C. p has no unit. Postponement is worth 0 by convention; equality does not distinguish between the options.

Recall 3Why can preparing the materials change the choice without changing the value of the goal in our calculation?

We reduced E while keeping p, V and k fixed. The cost decreased while the expected benefit remained the same. This is a comparison under assumptions, with no observed learning gain.

Recall 4Why is postponing the session not enough to conclude that Sam lacks interest?

Different perceived chances or costs can produce the same choice despite an identical value of the goal. Expectations, constraints and the ability to act must be examined; no fictional score directly measures dopamine.

Recall 5What is missing after a preference for studying, or after an answer obtained with a hint?

The preference proves neither starting nor success. An answer after a hint is an assisted answer. To prepare evidence of lasting understanding, reconstruct the explanation and calculation without support during delayed recall; reading alone does not establish an attained level.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the path from the goal to the choice, then to the observed attempt. Define p, V, E and k with their units; work out B, C and S; explain the change in choice with the goal unchanged. Finish by naming the missing evidence needed to speak of persistence, success and lasting understanding. An assisted answer remains an assisted answer.

What you understand
A valued goal is not enough to explain an action. A score can guide a selection; choosing, undertaking and succeeding remain distinct facts. Our fictional calculation is neither a person’s biological mechanism nor the equation of the cited module.
