Level 1 · C028·M1

Sum and product rules

Your goalCount plaque designs by separating alternatives from choices to combine, then justify each addition and multiplication.

Before you start
c014-m5 : invariants · c011-m5 : binomial

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional workshop, you order 1 door plaque design. You can choose a decorated oval plaque, Sun or Moon. Or you can put together your own plaque: choose its shape, Square or Round, then its motif, Leaf, Wave or Star. All these combinations are available.

A Square–Leaf plaque and a Square–Wave plaque are different designs. Making Square–Leaf again does not create a new design. We count the possible designs, regardless of how many copies are in stock.

For custom plaques, each shape allows the full list of motifs. The table shows the outcomes: each cell combines a shape and a motif. Counting only the names of shapes and motifs would leave the plaques themselves uncounted.

Ready-made plaques offer another ordering option. They are oval, whereas custom plaques are square or round: no design appears in both categories. The catalogue data are fictional; they do not describe a real workshop.

The rule to remember

Multiply to complete choices when each first choice allows the same number of subsequent choices; add to combine categories with no design in common that cover all allowed cases.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Count the workshop’s entire catalogue

The catalogue contains 2 ready-made designs, 2 shapes for custom plaques and 3 motifs per shape. How many plaque designs can you order? First count the combinations, then combine the categories.

  1. Define the outcome and its assumptions

    An outcome is a plaque fully defined by its shape and decoration. The letters p, f and m denote the numbers of ready-made designs, shapes for custom plaques and motifs available for each shape, respectively. Here, p = 2, f = 2 and m = 3. The letter c will denote the number of custom designs; t, the total number of designs.

    These letters represent whole numbers of possibilities, with no physical unit: p, c and t are counts of designs, f is a count of shapes and m is a count of motifs available per shape. The sign × indicates multiplication and the sign + indicates addition.

    Our lists are finite, their choices are distinct, each shape allows all motifs, and a shape–motif pair identifies exactly one custom design. The ready-made designs differ from the custom designs. Every allowed design belongs to one of these categories. Without these conditions, the following formula would not be justified.

  2. Fix a shape to see what is being counted

    With the Square shape fixed, the outcomes are Square–Leaf, Square–Wave and Square–Star: 3 designs. With Round fixed, they are Round–Leaf, Round–Wave and Round–Star: 3 designs.

    Each row of the table therefore contains 3 designs. The rows do not overlap: a plaque in the Square row cannot be a plaque in the Round row. We have classified complete outcomes, rather than just parts of an order.

  3. Apply the product rule to successive choices

    Each of the f shapes allows m possible motifs. Repeating the same number m of outcomes for each shape gives c = f × m. This is the product rule. The number of possible subsequent choices must be the same after each first choice; describing choices as “independent” without checking this condition is not enough.

    c=f×m=2×3=6

    We obtain 6 custom designs. The product counts pairs of choices. It represents neither an area nor a number of plaques manufactured. No probability assumption is needed.

  4. Apply the sum rule to the categories

    The order can now be for a ready-made design or a custom design. The categories cover all the designs on offer and share none, because their shapes differ. We can add their counts: this is the sum rule.

    t=p+c=2+6=8

    The catalogue therefore offers 8 designs. We multiplied within the custom category, then added across categories. The same question can therefore require both rules.

  5. Check with a complete list, then regroup

    Checklist: Oval–Sun; Oval–Moon; Square–Leaf; Square–Wave; Square–Star; Round–Leaf; Round–Wave; Round–Star. It contains 8 distinct designs, with no omissions or repetitions. Every custom design can be located in its cell using its shape and motif; every ready-made design can be located by its oval shape and decoration.

    Now regroup: the Leaf column contains 2 designs, as do Wave and Star. Counting by columns gives 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 custom designs. By rows, 3 + 3 = 6. Adding the 2 ready-made designs gives 8 again. These checks recover the result by looking at the same plaques in different ways.

    The essential check remains the correspondence between design and cell. If the same design had several descriptions, counting the descriptions could count it several times. If a combination became forbidden, its cell would have to disappear; we would then recount the allowed rows, as in the final exercise.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations. Explain why we multiply shapes by motifs, but add ready-made plaques to the result. Recover the assumptions by pointing to specific designs, then reconstruct the complete list.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The word “or” is always enough to justify addition.”

    The categories must also have no design in common and cover all the intended cases. Here, the ready-made plaques are oval: they do not appear among the square or round custom plaques. If catalogue labels placed the same design in several categories, we would first need to construct categories that do not overlap.

    Review the assumptions and the combination of categories

  • “I add the shapes and motifs to count the plaques.”

    A shape alone is not yet a decorated plaque. Its motif must be chosen. Each shape provides 3 complete designs; the 2 rows of the table therefore give 6 designs, by multiplication.

    Recount the designs in the table cells

  • “I can always multiply the lengths of the lists, even with restrictions.”

    Check which subsequent choices are actually allowed after each shape. If a shape loses a motif and the other keeps it, the rows no longer have the same length: the original product counts a forbidden cell. Count the outcomes in each allowed row, then add these separate rows.

    Handle the restriction in the final exercise

Exercise 2 · Combining the rules

The workshop also offers Triangle

Start from the original catalogue and add the Triangle shape to the custom plaques. It allows all the original motifs. The ready-made plaques remain the same. How many designs does this new catalogue offer? Check the result by counting only the new designs.

Hint 1

Count the shapes in the new catalogue before multiplying.

Hint 2

Triangle adds a complete new row; the other designs are kept.

Compare with the solution

The shapes are now Square, Round and Triangle: 2 + 1 = 3 shapes. Each allows 3 motifs, so 3 × 3 = 9 custom designs.

The ready-made designs, still oval, are distinct from these combinations. Add their count: 2 + 9 = 11 designs in the catalogue.

Check: the new designs are Triangle–Leaf, Triangle–Wave and Triangle–Star. There are 3 of them, giving 8 + 3 = 11 designs. Multiplication followed by addition gives the same total as adding this list.

  • Obtain 11 designs by justifying the product and then the sum.
  • Recover the increase of 3 designs by listing only the new combinations.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and repair

Star can no longer be engraved on Round

Start from the original catalogue, without Triangle. The workshop forbids only Round–Star. Someone keeps the calculation 2 + 2 × 3 = 8. Explain their mistake and recount the entire catalogue by adding categories with no design in common.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Do the Square and Round rows still have the same number of allowed outcomes?

Hint 2

Write down the motifs allowed on Round, then count the square, round and oval plaques separately.

Compare with the solution

The original product still counts Round–Star. The Square row keeps 3 designs; the Round row now allows only Leaf and Wave, giving 3 − 1 = 2 designs. These rows no longer have the same number of outcomes: the original product can no longer be applied to them.

The allowed custom designs are Square–Leaf, Square–Wave, Square–Star, Round–Leaf and Round–Wave. This gives 3 + 2 = 5 custom designs.

2+(3+2)=2+5=7

The ready-made oval plaques have not changed. They bring the total to 7 designs. Check against the original list: only Round–Star has disappeared; every other design is still counted once. Repairing the calculation therefore requires changing the allowed possibilities before choosing the operation.

Check your reasoning

  • Name Round–Star as the design counted in error.
  • Justify the separate counts 3 and 2 for the allowed rows.
  • Obtain 7 designs by adding the oval, square and round categories, without bringing back Triangle.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What exactly are we counting in the workshop?

Fully defined plaque designs. Making the same design again does not create an additional possibility; a shape alone is not a complete outcome.

Recall 2When can we add the numbers of designs in several categories?

When they cover all the intended designs with no design in common. Here, the ready-made designs are oval and the custom designs are square or round: each design belongs to a single category.

Recall 3What justifies multiplying shapes by motifs?

Each shape allows the same number of motifs, and each allowed pair corresponds to exactly one distinct design. The product counts the complete outcomes of these successive choices.

Recall 4How do we repair the count if Round–Star becomes forbidden?

Recount the allowed rows separately: 3 square designs and 2 round designs, then add the 2 ready-made oval designs. The total is 7; the original product no longer respects the allowed possibilities.

Recall 5How can we check that the calculation counts neither too many nor too few?

Match each outcome to a unique cell or category, then check that no allowed design is omitted. For this small catalogue, the complete list and counting by columns also check the count by rows.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without rereading: define what constitutes a complete design; check the subsequent choices allowed after each choice; multiply when their number is constant; combine categories that do not overlap by adding; check the designs for omissions and repetitions. Explain each step using the workshop’s plaques. Reading or an assisted answer does not establish an attained level.
