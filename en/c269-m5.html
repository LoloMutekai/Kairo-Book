Level 4 · C269·M5

Transfer and generalization

Your goalDistinguish what an average across studies says about the contexts observed from what it supports for an unobserved context, then check the prediction without leakage and state a conclusion within those limits.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A fictional review brings together 4 studies of the same learning method: testing yourself after a lesson rather than rereading it. Each study reports an effect: the average score of the group that tests itself minus that of the group that rereads, on a retention test marked out of 100 points. The effect is therefore expressed in score points. The sample sizes, contexts and effects on this page are invented for the course; they do not describe any real research or the effectiveness of a study method.

2 studies took place in a laboratory with student volunteers; 2 others took place in ordinary classrooms. A teaching team now wants to apply the method to a group that no study has measured, such as apprentices in a workshop. What number should it report?

Transfer asks this question: does the effect measured in one context stay the same in another? Generalization goes further: can we report it for contexts that the review has never observed? In this course, a context groups together what varies from one group to another: participants, duration, materials and assessor. The word is a convention for interpreting the studies; it does not refer to a measured factor.

A large number of participants does not answer the question. It reduces sampling uncertainty: variation in the estimate due to the selection of people within the same context. It says nothing about the gap between two contexts, which may stay the same with many more people.

The average across all studies mixes the contexts according to the number of studies found in each. It describes that mixture, which may not describe the target context. To test an extension fairly, we remove one context, estimate the effect using the others, then compare it with what was observed in the removed context. This held-out segment, excluded from estimation, is the idea that the milestone on this page will revisit in code.

The rule to remember

To claim that an effect generalizes, predict a context that the estimate has not seen, compare with what was observed, then report only the range for the contexts actually studied, with its limitations.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A fictional review of 4 studies in 2 contexts

Lab A and Lab B are laboratory studies; Class A and Class B are classroom studies. Each gives an effect in score points and a sample size in participants. We calculate an average per context and the gap between contexts, then check the prediction for the classroom based on the laboratory.

  1. Define the quantities and assumptions

    e denotes the effect of a study, in score points. n denotes its sample size, a unitless count of participants. For a context c, the weighted average by sample size is the sum of the products n × e divided by the sum of the sample sizes. The product n × e is the total number of points gained by the study participants; dividing by the sample size turns this total into an average.

    e¯c=∑i∈cniei∑i∈cni

    Assumptions: the score, marking scale and definition of the effect are identical in the four studies; each participant counts once. No uncertainty per study is provided: we will not be able to judge whether a gap exceeds what sampling chance alone could produce. Weighting by sample size is a simplification; other weighting methods exist and are not used here.

  2. Average for the laboratory context

    Lab A gives 14 points with 25 participants; Lab B gives 10 points with 75 participants. The products are 350 and 750, their sum is 1100, for 100 participants.

    e¯lab=14×25+10×7525+75=350+750100=1100100=11 points

    Check: 11 lies between 10 and 14, closer to 10 because Lab B accounts for 75 participants out of 100.

  3. Average for the classroom context

    Class A gives 2 points with 25 participants; Class B gives 6 points with 75 participants. The products are 50 and 450, their sum is 500, for 100 participants.

    e¯classe=2×25+6×7525+75=50+450100=500100=5 points

    Check: 5 lies between 2 and 6, closer to 6, the study with more participants. The sign is positive in both contexts.

  4. Compare the gap between contexts with the gap within a context

    The average across all studies is 1600 divided by 200, giving 8 points. The gap between contexts is 11 − 5 = 6 points. In the laboratory, the two studies differ by 14 − 10 = 4 points; in the classroom, by 6 − 2 = 4 points.

    The gap between contexts is 1.5 times the gap observed within the laboratory. A cautious interpretation: context seems to matter, because the difference between contexts exceeds the difference between studies within the same context. This is not a statistical test. With 2 studies per context and no uncertainty per study, the signal remains weak.

  5. Check the prediction without leakage

    We remove the classroom and predict its effect using only the laboratory: prediction 11, observation 5, error 11 − 5 = 6 points. If we instead use the average across all studies, which already includes the classroom, the apparent error falls to 8 − 5 = 3 points.

    63=2

    Leakage therefore divides the error by 2. The reason: the laboratory has a weight of 0.5 in the average across all studies, as does the classroom, so this average lies halfway between them and its error is half the gap. The world has not changed; only the check has used information it should have excluded.

  6. State a conclusion within the observed limits

    The classroom retains about 0.45 of the laboratory effect: 5 divided by 11. The sign is preserved, but the magnitude is not. A defensible statement: “In these fictional studies, the observed average effect ranges from 5 points in the classroom to 11 points in the laboratory.”

    For an unstudied context, such as apprentices, the review provides no measurement. The range of context averages is an observed range: it is neither a confidence interval nor a guarantee. If studies with no effect had not been published, these averages might be too high in both contexts; this point belongs to C269·M4 and is not assessed here.

Explain in your own words why the gap of 6 points can be read as a prediction error, and why the check with leakage halves it even though the studies have not changed.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “With many participants, the effect holds everywhere.”

    Sample size reduces sampling uncertainty within a context; it does not measure a missing context. Here the laboratory has 100 participants, yet it predicts the classroom with an error of 6 points.

    Revisit the intuition

  • “The average across all studies proves that the prediction holds.”

    This average already includes the context to be predicted. Remove it before estimating: the fair error is 6 points, rather than 3.

    Revisit the check without leakage

  • “The sign stays positive, so the effect is the same.”

    Sign and magnitude are judged separately. In the classroom the effect stays positive but is only 5 points compared with 11. Rephrase the conclusion using both numbers.

Exercise 1 · Explained application

A smaller classroom study

In the same fictional review, Class B now has only 25 participants, with the same effect of 6 points. Recalculate the classroom average, the average across all studies and the gap between contexts. What stays unchanged, and why does the average across all studies move?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First recalculate the product for Class B using its new sample size, then the total classroom sample size.

Hint 2

The laboratory has not changed: reuse its sum and sample size from the example.

Compare with the solution

Classroom: 50 + 150 = 200 total points for 50 participants, giving an average of 4 points. All studies: (1100 + 200) divided by (100 + 50) = 1300 divided by 150, giving about 8.67 points. Gap between contexts: 11 − 4 = 7 points.

The laboratory average does not change. The average across all studies changes because the composition of the set of studies has changed: the classroom has less weight. This tells us nothing new about the world. The gap between contexts goes from 6 to 7 points, because Class B, the more favorable study with 6 points, has less weight in the classroom average.

Check your reasoning

  • Recalculate the product using the new sample size.
  • Keep the units: points for effects, participants for sample sizes.
  • Explain the shift through the composition of the set of studies, rather than a change in the laboratory.

Exercise 2 · Transfer to a related situation

An unexpected context

A fictional study in a sports club, the only study in its context, reports an effect of 14 points with 100 participants. First predict this effect using the 4 initial studies, then using the 4 studies and the club. Which error should you report, and what do you not know?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A fair prediction does not use the club study; the average across all initial studies is 8 points.

Hint 2

For the check with leakage, add the club product to the sum and its sample size to the total sample size.

Compare with the solution

Fair check: prediction 8, observation 14, error 14 − 8 = 6 points, an underestimate. Check with leakage: club product 1400; sum (1600 + 1400) = 3000; sample size (200 + 100) = 300; average 10; apparent error 14 − 10 = 4 points.

You should report the fair error of 6 points. The apparent error is smaller because the club accounts for 100 participants out of 300 in the average used to predict it. Limitation: a single study confounds the club context with the particular features of that study; the review cannot separate them.

Check your reasoning

  • Exclude the club from the fair prediction.
  • Calculate the effect of leakage using the full sum and sample size.
  • Report the fair error and identify the confounding of context and study.

Exercise 3 · Critique of a claim

Reporting a number for apprentices

A note claims: “The method gains 8 points, so it will work for apprentices.” A fictional study of apprentices then gives 3 points with 50 participants, with no uncertainty provided. Compare this result with the observed range of context averages and with 8, then rewrite the claim within the limits of the evidence.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The observed context averages are 5 in the classroom and 11 in the laboratory: find the distance to the nearest boundary.

Hint 2

Divide 3 by 8 to assess the magnitude, then ask yourself what the sign alone allows you to conclude.

Hint 3

A conclusion within the limits of the evidence cites the contexts studied, the observed numbers and what has not been established.

Compare with the solution

The result for apprentices is 2 points below the lower boundary of the range: 5 − 3 = 2. Relative to the reported average, it is 0.375 times 8. The sign is preserved, but the magnitude falls outside the observed range: this range was therefore no guarantee.

Without uncertainty per study, we cannot say whether these 2 points exceed what sampling chance could produce. A statement within the limits of the evidence: “In the fictional studies, the observed average effect ranges from 3 to 11 points depending on the context; we do not generalize to other contexts.” The initial claim confuses the average of a mixture with a value for an unmeasured context.

Check your reasoning

  • Compare with the observed range without presenting it as a confidence interval.
  • Separate sign from magnitude.
  • Write a conclusion that cites the contexts and names what has not been established.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the question of transfer ask?

Whether the effect measured in one context stays the same in another context. Generalization extends this to contexts never observed.

Recall 2How can we fairly check a prediction for a different context?

Remove the target context before estimating, predict using the others, then compare with what was observed. Otherwise the average already includes the target.

Recall 3What does a large sample size reduce, and what does it leave unchanged?

It reduces sampling uncertainty within a context. It leaves the gap between contexts unchanged.

Recall 4What is the observed range of context averages not?

It is neither a confidence interval nor a guarantee for a new context: it describes only the contexts studied.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the approach: name the effect, its unit and its contexts; calculate a weighted average per context; measure the gap between contexts; remove a context, predict and compare with what was observed without leakage; state the observed range as a limit rather than a guarantee; name what is missing, such as uncertainty per study and publication bias.
