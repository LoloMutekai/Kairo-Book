Level 3 · C172·M4

CIR

Your goalRecognize the square root diffusion of the CIR model, check its boundary condition, and distinguish its non-negativity from that of an approximate calculation.

Before you start
c171-m5 : multidimensional case · c052-m5 : convergence protocol · c172-m1 : existence and uniqueness · c172-m2 : GBM · c172-m3 : Ornstein-Uhlenbeck

✿01 See the idea

On a fictional test bench, a continuous indicator describes the load on a machine. Its conventional unit is u. It is neither an integer number of tasks nor a proportion with an upper bound: the model allows any non-negative value. We want a level that returns toward 4 u while fluctuating irregularly.

The mean reversion resembles that of Ornstein-Uhlenbeck: below the level θ, it pushes upward; above it, downward. CIR, named after Cox–Ingersoll–Ross, changes the noise: its amplitude depends on the square root of the current load. Near 0, this amplitude decreases. A high state fluctuates more in absolute terms.

This picture is not enough to prove that the load stays positive. We must distinguish remaining non-negative, never reaching 0, and preserving these properties in an approximate calculation. The continuous model can satisfy the first two properties while a numerical step crosses 0.

The whole page uses this test bench and fictional parameters chosen to allow exact calculations by hand. It makes no claim about machine measurements or an experimental fit of the CIR model. The diagrams describe mechanisms and a calculation; they do not represent observations.

The rule to remember

The CIR model combines linear mean reversion toward θ with noise of amplitude σ√X; the Feller condition concerns whether the continuous process can reach 0, rather than the sign of an approximate step.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A load that returns toward its mean reversion level

Initially, the load is x = 1 u. Take κ = 2 per second, θ = 4 u, and σ = 2 √(u/s). Study the boundary, then a local calculation over a duration h = 1/16 s with the chosen normal shock Z = 1. This shock is prescribed for the exercise; it is neither sampled nor observed.

  1. Define an equation, a domain, and units

    X at time t is the random load in u; x is its known initial value. Time t is measured in seconds. W is a standard real Brownian motion starting at 0, whose increment over a duration h has 0 mean and variance h. In our unit convention, W and its increments are in √s. The equation is interpreted in the Ito sense, within the probabilistic framework of the prerequisites.

    dXt=κ(θ−Xt)dt+σXtdWt

    κ, which is strictly positive, controls the speed of mean reversion, in inverse seconds. θ, which is strictly positive, is the target level, in u. σ, which is strictly positive, controls the noise, in √(u/s). The parameters remain constant and the initial state x is strictly positive. The term κ(θ − X)dt is in u; so is σ√X dW. dX and dW are differential notation for stochastic integrals, rather than ordinary rates of change.

    The square root is not Lipschitz near 0. The usual Lipschitz theorem from the unit on existence therefore does not apply directly on the whole half-line. The result specific to the CIR model nevertheless gives a unique non-negative strong solution under these assumptions: a path is determined by the initial state and the chosen Brownian motion. This result is accepted here without proof; noise vanishing at 0 is not a proof of it.

  2. Examine mean reversion and local dispersion separately

    Write b(x) = κ(θ − x) for the drift, in u/s, and a(x) = σ√x for the diffusion coefficient, in u/√s. The drift describes the mean change over a very short time, rather than a certain displacement of a path. Initially, θ − x = 3 u, so b(x) = 2 × 3 = 6 u/s.

    The square root of the numerical value 1 is 1. The coefficient a(x) is therefore 2 × 1 = 2 u/√s. Its square is σ²x = 4 squared load units per second: this is the local variance rate. Over an infinitesimal duration, the variance of the increment divided by that duration tends to this rate.

    The square of σ is 4 u/s. Confusing σ² with σ²x would remove the state dependence. At the boundary X = 0 u, diffusion vanishes, while the mean reversion term is κθ = 8 u/s and pushes into the domain.

  3. Apply the Feller condition with its exact scope

    The CIR boundary theorem, accepted here without proof, makes the non-negativity result more precise: starting from strictly positive x, 0 is inaccessible if the following condition holds, including equality.

    2κθ≥σ2

    If this inequality fails, 0 becomes attainable; the CIR solution remains non-negative. With strictly positive θ, it is not permanently absorbed at 0. The Feller condition should therefore not be called a necessary condition for non-negativity alone.

    Here, both sides are in u/s: 2 × 2 × 4 = 16 u/s and σ² = 4 u/s. Their difference is 12 u/s, which is positive. The load in the continuous model therefore does not hit 0 over a finite horizon, almost surely, meaning with probability 1.

    To understand where the factor 2 comes from, apply Ito's formula to log(X/u) while X remains strictly positive. The logarithm acts on a dimensionless quantity; the Ito correction subtracts half of σ² from the coefficient of the singular term:

    dlog(Xt/u)=[κθ−σ2/2Xt−κ]dt+σXtdWt

    In our example, κθ − σ²/2 = 8 − 2 = 6 u/s. Its sign recovers the Feller threshold. This transformation helps explain the threshold; it does not by itself replace the proof of the boundary theorem.

  4. Solve for the mean while distinguishing it from a path

    Write m(t) for the expectation of X at time t, given the initial value x. For these parameters and a finite initial state, the required moments are finite over every finite horizon. The Ito integral has 0 expectation. Taking expectations in the equation, the affine drift gives m′(t) = κ(θ − m(t)), with m initially equal to x.

    Setting v(t) = m(t) − θ gives v′ = −κv. The derivative of exp(κt)v(t) is therefore 0; this quantity is constantly equal to x − θ. This gives the exact mean:

    m(t)=θ+(x−θ)exp(−κt)

    exp is the exponential function. κt is dimensionless, and m(t) is in u. At t = 0, the exponential is 1 and we recover x = 1 u; as t increases without bound, it tends to 0 and m tends to θ = 4 u. The characteristic mean reversion time is 1/κ = 1/2 s. An individual path nevertheless continues to fluctuate: it does not have to move closer to θ at every instant.

  5. Build a local diagnostic with frozen coefficients

    To see the effect of a noise increment, temporarily replace X in the coefficients with its initial value x. Write Y_h for the resulting approximate value; h is the duration, in s. Z has a standard normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 1, is dimensionless, and is independent of the initial information. We represent the Brownian increment by √h Z.

    Yh=x+κ(θ−x)h+σxhZ

    The square root of the step size is 1/4 √s: its square is 1/16 s = 1/16 s. With Z = 1, the chosen Brownian increment is ΔW = 1/4 √s. Δ means “increment over the step”. The factor √h, rather than h, comes from the Brownian variance h.

    This expression is a local calculation with frozen coefficients, rather than the exact CIR solution or an already validated simulation method. The next unit will study Euler–Maruyama and its limitations; here, we do not iterate any scheme or assign any order of convergence.

  6. Add the contributions and check the result

    Mean reversion adds b(x)h = 6 × 1/16 = 3/8 u. The chosen noise adds a(x)ΔW = 2 × 1/4 = 1/2 u. We add loads, never a load and a rate:

    Yh=1+3/8+1/2=15/8 u

    The result is 1.875 u. Check using a common denominator: 1 + 3/8 + 1/2 does give 15/8. Subtracting the noise 1/2 u, then the mean reversion contribution 3/8 u, recovers 1 u.

    Before choosing Z, the center of this approximate distribution is x + b(x)h = 11/8 u; its variance is σ²xh = 1/4 squared load unit. Squaring the shock amplitude, 1/2 u for a shock of value 1, recovers this variance. The center 1.375 u remains an approximation of m(h), whose exact expression retains an exponential.

    This positive result is not a test of the Feller condition. The normal distribution in the calculation with frozen coefficients also allows negative shocks of sufficiently large magnitude: the last exercise constructs one with the same parameters. Subsequently replacing a negative value with 0 would change the calculated distribution; this would prove neither the exact CIR distribution nor the convergence of a scheme.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why the noise depends on the square root of the load and the square root of the duration. Then separate the three statements: the CIR model remains non-negative, it does not hit 0 under the Feller condition, and the calculation with frozen coefficients gives a positive result here.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Without the Feller condition, the CIR model becomes negative.”

    Under our assumptions, the CIR model remains non-negative. The Feller condition distinguishes 0 being inaccessible from 0 being attainable from a positive state. Revisit the theorem statement, keeping the distinction between strictly positive and non-negative.

    Reread the boundary condition and its assumptions

  • “The noise coefficient is σx, as in GBM.”

    In the CIR model, the coefficient is σ√x. Its square is σ²x: the local variance grows linearly with x. Recalculate the coefficients at states 1 u and 9 u, using the same units.

    Compare the amplitudes in the first exercise

  • “The path stays positive, so all my steps will too.”

    The property concerns the continuous solution. The calculation with frozen coefficients has unbounded normal noise and can cross 0. Construct this case, then explain why bounding the result is not enough to recover the CIR distribution.

    Construct the counterexample in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Application and units

The load has increased

On the same fictional test bench, consider a current state of 9 u with the initial parameters. Calculate the drift, the diffusion coefficient, and the local variance rate. Compare them with those at state 1 u. Is a decrease at the next instant certain?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Find the positive square root of 9, then distinguish the diffusion coefficient from its square.

Hint 2

The drift gives the direction of mean reversion; Brownian motion can produce an increment of the opposite sign.

Compare with the solution

The difference from the mean reversion level is 4 − 9 = -5 u. The drift is 2 × -5 = -10 u/s: mean reversion pushes downward.

The square root of 9 is 3, because 3 × 3 = 9. The diffusion coefficient is 2 × 3 = 6 u/√s, compared with 2 u/√s initially. The variance rate is 6 × 6 = 36 squared load units per second, compared with 4 initially. We also recover it from σ²x = 4 × 9 = 36.

Negative mean reversion does not make a decrease certain. A positive fluctuation can dominate over a short interval. The increased coefficient also does not mean that the load must stay above θ.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify the square root by squaring it.
  • Distinguish drift, amplitude, and variance rate, including their units.
  • Separate the sign of mean reversion from the direction of a random increment.

Exercise 2 · Model diagnostic

More intense noise

Keep x, κ, and θ, but replace σ with 6 √(u/s). Does 0 remain inaccessible? Can we conclude that the exact process becomes negative? Justify both answers without using the approximate calculation.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare 2κθ with σ² again; both sides have the same unit.

Hint 2

Non-negativity and the impossibility of hitting 0 are two different properties.

Compare with the solution

The mean reversion side is unchanged: 2κθ = 16 u/s. The square of the new σ is 6 × 6 = 36 u/s. The margin becomes -20 u/s: the Feller condition fails.

0 is therefore attainable from the positive state. The CIR solution remains non-negative; θ is still strictly positive and the boundary is not absorbing. Failure of the Feller condition asserts neither that a given path has already hit 0 nor that all paths will hit it before a fixed time.

Check your reasoning

  • Recalculate the square of the new σ.
  • State that 0 is attainable, without claiming negative values for the exact process.
  • Do not turn a possibility into an observed event.

Exercise 3 · Explained counterexample

The Feller condition holds, but the calculation crosses 0

Return to the initial parameters and the step size h = 1/16 s. In the same calculation with frozen coefficients, change only the shock to Z = -3. Calculate Y_h, then find the condition on Z that makes Y_h negative. What would replacing the negative result with 0 u prove?

Yh=x+κ(θ−x)h+σxhZ
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Mean reversion and the center 11/8 u remain the same; only the Brownian increment changes.

Hint 2

Write Y_h = 11/8 + 1/2 Z, then isolate Z in the inequality Y_h < 0.

Compare with the solution

The chosen increment is 1/4 × (-3) = -3/4 √s. The noise is 2 × (-3/4) = -3/2 u. The mean reversion contribution remains 3/8 u. We obtain Y_h = 11/8 + (-3/2) = -1/8 u, or -0.125 u.

The form Y_h = 11/8 + 1/2 Z gives Y_h < 0 if and only if Z < -11/4. To check the threshold, 1/2 × (-11/4) = -11/8, which exactly cancels the center. Our shock -3 is below this threshold. The standard normal distribution allows such shocks; the probability of a single exact value is 0, but an interval of shocks below this threshold has strictly positive probability.

The Feller condition still holds for the continuous CIR process. This counterexample disproves automatic preservation of its positivity by the calculation with frozen coefficients. Setting the output to 0 u would impose a bound on this calculation and change its distribution, creating a probability mass at 0. This would demonstrate neither an exact CIR transition, nor convergence, nor correct use in practice.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the mean reversion contribution and change only the shock.
  • Produce a negative value and check the threshold by substitution.
  • Attribute the crossing to the approximate calculation.
  • State what bounding imposes and what it does not prove.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which terms distinguish the CIR model from Ornstein-Uhlenbeck mean reversion?

The drift remains κ(θ − X), but the diffusion becomes σ√X. The noise depends on the state and vanishes at 0; the parameters κ, θ, and σ are strictly positive here.

Recall 2What exactly does the Feller condition guarantee?

Starting from a strictly positive state, 2κθ ≥ σ² makes 0 inaccessible for the continuous CIR process. If the condition fails, 0 is attainable, but the solution remains non-negative under the assumptions on this page.

Recall 3What is the local variance rate at state x, and why?

It is σ²x, because it is the square of the diffusion coefficient σ√x. Over a step of duration h with frozen coefficients, the variance is σ²xh; the Brownian shock is proportional to √h.

Recall 4How can we recover the exact mean, and what does it not tell us?

The expectation of the Ito noise is 0: m′ = κ(θ − m), giving m(t) = θ + (x − θ)exp(−κt). This mean tends to θ; a path does not have to move closer to it at every instant.

Recall 5Why is the Feller condition insufficient to validate a numerical calculation?

It concerns the continuous process. The calculation with frozen coefficients has unbounded normal shocks and can become negative. Bounding its outputs changes their distribution and does not by itself prove convergence.
