Independence

Your goalDecide whether learning that a card has a star changes its probability of being red, then justify the conclusion with an exact calculation.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A deck contains 12 cards, including 6 red cards and 6 blue cards. Some have a star. You draw a card without seeing its face; someone checks only whether it has a star and tells you “it has a star”. Should this information change your chance of guessing its color?

In our deck, the 4 cards with a star include 2 red cards and 2 blue cards. Half the cards with a star are red, just as half the cards in the whole deck are red. The star therefore does not help you predict red: these events are independent for this draw.

The scene and all its counts are fictional. We know the full composition of the deck and assume that every card has exactly the same chance of being drawn. We draw only one card; color and the presence of a star are properties of that same card.

Independence concerns the information one event provides about another. It is determined by the distribution of cards and the rule for drawing, even if the properties have very different names. Simply feeling that they are “unrelated” is not enough.

The rule to remember

Check that the joint probability equals the product of the probabilities. When conditioning is defined, this is equivalent to checking that the information does not change the probability.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Does the star provide information about the color?

The deck still has its 12 equally likely cards: 2 red cards with a star, 4 red cards without a star, 2 blue cards with a star, and 4 blue cards without a star. We will decide whether “red” and “has a star” are independent, without assuming the conclusion in the calculation.

  1. Name the events and the calculation rule

    Ω denotes the set of possible cards. A is the event “the card drawn is red”; B is the event “the card drawn has a star”. A ∩ B, read “A and B”, contains the cards satisfying both properties. P(E) denotes the probability of any event E; the symbol P is therefore a rule that assigns a number to an event.

    Counts have cards as their unit. Probabilities have no unit and range from 0 to 1. Since the cards are equally likely, a probability here is the number of cards in the event divided by 12. This division using counts would not be valid if the cards had unequal chances.

  2. Calculate before conditioning

    For A, count all the red cards, with or without a star: 2 + 4 = 6 cards. For B, count all the cards with a star: 2 + 2 = 4 cards. The denominator refers to the same whole deck in both calculations.

    P(A)=612=1/2;P(B)=412=1/3

    The probability of red is therefore 0.50, with no unit. We keep the exact fraction 1/3 for the star rather than its infinite decimal expansion.

  3. Restrict the group when the star is known

    P(A | B), read “probability of A given B”, keeps only the cards with a star as possibilities. There are 4 of them, including 2 red cards. We divide by 4, because the information has changed the reference group. The vertical bar | means “given” and does not indicate division.

    P(A|B)=24=1/2=P(A)

    The probability of red remains 0.50, with no unit. Here, the star provides no information about red. This finding does not mean that all the cards are identical: the information received simply does not change this probability.

  4. Check the definition with another calculation

    By definition, events A and B are independent if the following equality holds. The sign × represents multiplication; it does not denote an intersection.

    P(A∩B)=P(A)×P(B)

    First calculate the left side by counting, without multiplying: 2 cards that are both red and marked with a star out of 12, giving 1/6. Then calculate the right side: (1/2) × (1/3) = 1/6. They agree exactly: A and B are independent.

    Check the result: 6 × (1/3) = 2 cards. Applying the proportion of stars in the deck to the red group gives the correct number of red cards with a star. We did not use multiplication to construct the joint probability before checking it.

  5. Connect the criteria and interpret the symmetry

    Conditioning always gives P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B)/P(B) when P(B) is strictly positive. If the definition of independence is satisfied, replace the numerator with P(A) × P(B); division by P(B) leaves P(A). Conversely, multiplying P(A | B) = P(A) by P(B) gives the definition again. The criteria are therefore equivalent under this condition.

    The product equality does not change when A and B are exchanged. Here P(A) is also positive: among the 6 red cards, 2 have a star. P(B | A) = 2/6 = 1/3 = P(B). The symmetry concerns the absence of change, rather than equality of the two conditional probabilities: 1/2 and 1/3 are different.

    If P(B) = 0, P(A | B) is not defined in the finite setting of this course: division by 0 would be impossible. The product definition remains usable; the intersection then has 0 probability, as does P(A) × P(B).

  6. Define the scope of the conclusion

    Independent does not mean mutually exclusive. Mutually exclusive events cannot occur together: their intersection is empty. Our events A and B occur together on 2 cards; this does not prevent them from being independent.

    Conversely, “red” and “blue” are mutually exclusive in this deck. Their intersection has probability 0, but the product of their probabilities is (1/2) × (1/2) = 1/4, which is strictly positive. They are therefore not independent. Two mutually exclusive events with positive probabilities are always dependent.

    Our equality is exact because the full composition and the rule for drawing make up the model. In an incomplete record of draws, an equality or a small difference between proportions would not by itself prove independence in the population. Nor does the calculation decide whether coloring a card can cause a change elsewhere. It describes the information in this specific model.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why the reference group changes after the star is announced, then why the probability of red stays the same. Reach the same conclusion using the product, and distinguish it from mutual exclusivity.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “To calculate “and”, I always multiply.”

    First count to find the joint probability. In general, if P(B) is positive, it equals P(A | B) × P(B). Replacing P(A | B) with P(A) requires precisely the independence that needs to be checked. In our deck, counting gives 1/6 before any multiplication.

    Review the separate calculations in the checking step

  • “If they are independent, they cannot happen together.”

    That is the definition of mutual exclusivity. Independence preserves a probability after information is received. The 2 red cards with a star show that the independent events in this lesson can occur together; red and blue, by contrast, show mutual exclusivity with dependence.

    Review the last step and its counterexamples

  • “Independence is symmetric, so P(A | B) = P(B | A).”

    Write the group below each fraction: among cards with a star, the red share is 2/4 = 1/2; among red cards, the share with a star is 2/6 = 1/3. Each value matches its own probability before information is received, without having to equal the other.

    Reread the groups and their totals in the table

Exercise 1 · Direct application

The opposite announcement

Keep the original deck. The person announces “the card has no star”. Calculate the probability that it is red. Is event A independent of “no star”? Justify your answer using the correct reference group.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Find the “No star” column: how many cards remain, and how many are red?

Hint 2

Compare the red proportion in this column with the proportion in the whole deck.

Compare with the solution

The event “no star” is the complement of B: it occurs exactly when B does not occur. Its probability is 1 − P(B). There are 8 cards without a star, including 4 red cards. The conditional probability of red is 4/8 = 1/2, or 0.50, with no unit.

It equals P(A) = 1/2, and the group we condition on is nonempty. A is therefore independent of the event “no star”. Dividing by 12 would have calculated the probability of being red and having no star, before any information was received, rather than the probability requested.

Check your reasoning

  • I justified the denominator 8 using the announcement.
  • I compared the exact fraction 1/2 with P(A).
  • My conclusion is tied to the model in which the cards in the deck are equally likely.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosing a change

Are the same totals enough?

Start again with the original deck. Move 1 star from a blue card with a star to a red card without a star. There are still 12 cards, 6 red cards, and 4 stars. Before calculating, predict the change in the red proportion among the cards with a star. Then check independence using conditioning and the product.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The probabilities P(A) and P(B) keep their values. The “red and has a star” cell gains 1 card.

Hint 2

Compare the new joint probability, counted in the deck, with the product of the unchanged probabilities.

Compare with the solution

The red share among cards with a star increases: the group gains a red card and loses a blue card without changing size. It now contains 3 red cards and 1 blue card, making 4 cards. P(A | B) is 3/4 = 3/4, or 0.75, compared with P(A) = 0.50. The events have become dependent.

Check using the product: P(A ∩ B) = 3/12 = 1/4. But P(A) × P(B) = (1/2) × (1/3) = 1/6. The fractions differ. The color and star totals are therefore not enough; their joint distribution also matters.

This move is a fictional change to the composition, rather than a repeated observation that would prove a general law about stars.

Check your reasoning

  • I stated the direction of the change before dividing.
  • I changed the intersection without changing the marginal probabilities, meaning the probabilities of each event taken separately.
  • Both checks conclude dependence and explicitly identify their reference groups.

Exercise 3 · Construction and impossibility

Can the stars be distributed differently?

The deck still has 12 equally likely cards, including 6 red cards. Erase all the stars, then try placing stars on exactly 3 distinct cards. Can you make “red” and “has a star” independent under these constraints? Calculate the number of red cards with a star that would be needed, then decide whether the construction is possible.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Independence would require the same red share among the 3 cards with a star as in the whole deck.

Hint 2

Multiply the required joint probability by 12 to return to a card count. A fraction of a card is not available.

Compare with the solution

The probability of red remains 1/2. The new probability of a star would be 3/12 = 1/4. Independence would therefore require P(A ∩ B) = (1/2) × (1/4) = 1/8.

Multiplying by the total count, the required number of red cards with a star would be (1/8) × 12 = 3/2, or 1.5 card. A count of whole cards cannot take this value. The construction is impossible under the given constraints.

Check without using the joint probability: half of the 3 cards with a star would have to be red, again giving 1.5 card. Rounding would give a different proportion and break the exact equality. Independence is therefore not a setting that can be imposed on any counts.

Check your reasoning

  • I stated a necessary equality before trying a distribution.
  • I converted the probability into a count and gave cards as the unit.
  • I justified the impossibility by the lack of a whole number count, without rounding.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What equality defines the independence of events A and B?

P(A ∩ B) = P(A) × P(B). The joint probability must be calculated or justified separately before using this product as a shortcut.

Recall 2How can independence be expressed in terms of information, and what condition is needed for conditioning?

If P(B) is strictly positive, P(A | B) = P(A): learning B does not change the probability of A. In our deck, the group we condition on contains 4 cards and the red share remains 1/2. If P(B) equals 0, this conditional probability is not defined.

Recall 3Why do mutually exclusive and independent not mean the same thing?

Mutually exclusive: impossible to occur together. Independent: the joint probability equals the product. In the deck, red and blue have an intersection with probability 0 but a product 1/4; they are mutually exclusive and dependent. Red and having a star are independent despite their 2 shared cards.

Recall 4What does the symmetry of independence mean when the probabilities are positive?

If learning B does not change P(A), learning A does not change P(B). This does not make the conditional probabilities equal to each other: here P(A | B) = 1/2 and P(B | A) = 1/3.

Recall 5Are the totals of red cards and cards with a star enough to guarantee independence?

No. The joint distribution is also needed. Moving a star from a blue card to a red card preserves the totals but changes the intersection: the red proportion among cards with a star changes from 1/2 to 3/4, which breaks independence.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct this sequence: name the events and the model for drawing; count their probabilities and their intersection separately; compare the latter with the product; reach the same conclusion by changing the reference group if conditioning is defined. Then explain the symmetry, the difference from mutual exclusivity, and what a change in the joint distribution can change.
