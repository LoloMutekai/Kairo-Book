Level 4 · C238·M1

Fractional covariance

Your goalEvaluate the covariance between the values of a fictional fractional signal at two times from the variances of its values and changes, without yet interpreting the parameter H.

Before you start
c235-m5 : divergence · c232-m5 : small-time limits · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a fictional signal, tracked over several days and expressed in arbitrary units denoted by u. We write it as B(t), where t is the number of days elapsed since the start. If B(s) is high on day s, does B(t) tend to be high on day t as well? Covariance answers this question: under the model, it is the mean of the product of the deviations of B(s) and B(t) from their means. Its unit is the product of their units, so u² here.

For standard Brownian motion, the answer is simple: the covariance between values at two times equals the smaller of the two times. Fractional Brownian motion keeps the idea of a centered signal starting at 0, but its covariance depends on a parameter H, a real number strictly between 0 and 1. This unit teaches you to evaluate this covariance. The interpretation of H, roughness, and memory are left to later units.

The guiding idea is to start with what the model says about the change B(t) − B(s) between two days rather than with the covariance. The variance of a difference is the sum of the two variances minus twice the covariance. When the two variances and the variance of the change are known, covariance is exactly what is missing to make the calculation balance.

All numerical values on this page are fictional. They support exact calculations, not a description of a real phenomenon or a validated setting. The times are chosen so that the powers can be calculated exactly by hand; this establishes nothing about observed data.

The rule to remember

Covariance of B(s) and B(t) = half of [variance at time s + variance at time t − variance of the change between s and t].

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Two days of a fictional fractional signal

A fictional signal B is centered, equals 0 on day 0, and follows normalized fractional Brownian motion with parameter H = 3/4. At times s = 9 d and t = 25 d, let us calculate the covariance of B(s) and B(t), then check it in two ways.

  1. Define the symbols and assumptions

    B(t) is the signal value on day t, in u. The times s and t are numbers of days with 0 ≤ s ≤ t, and d = t − s is their gap. Cov(X, Y) is the mean of the product of the centered deviations of X and Y, in u²; Var X = Cov(X, X) is a variance, also in u². H is the model parameter, fixed strictly between 0 and 1.

    Assumptions: B is centered and B(0) = 0. The variance at time t follows the first formula, with t expressed in days: the normalization gives 1 u² at t = 1 d. The change between two days follows the second formula, for every s ≤ t.

    Var(B(t))=t2H
    Var(B(t)−B(s))=|t−s|2H

    We assume that a Gaussian process satisfying these assumptions exists. This unit does not prove that the resulting covariance is positive definite for every H in the interval; without these assumptions, the following formula is not guaranteed.

  2. Recover the covariance from the variances

    The variance of a difference expands like the square of a difference: the sum of the two variances minus twice the product, which here becomes twice the covariance.

    Var(B(t)−B(s))=Var(B(t))+Var(B(s))−2Cov(B(s),B(t))

    We replace the three variances using the assumptions from the previous step, then isolate the covariance. The formula is symmetric: exchanging s and t leaves it unchanged because the time gap is taken in absolute value.

    Cov(B(s),B(t))=12(t2H+s2H−|t−s|2H)

  3. Choose times whose powers are exact

    Here 2H = 3/2. Raising x to the power 3/2 gives x × √x. To keep the calculation exact by hand, we choose times whose gap is also a perfect square: s = 9, t = 25, d = t − s = 16. Their square roots are 3, 5, and 4, because 3 × 3 = 9, 5 × 5 = 25, and 4 × 4 = 16.

    Powers: 9 × 3 = 27, then 25 × 5 = 125, then 16 × 4 = 64. These are variances in u²: Var B(s) = 27, Var B(t) = 125, and variance of the change = 64. These times are chosen for convenient calculation; the choice says nothing about the signal.

  4. Combine the terms to calculate covariance

    We apply the rule in order: add the two variances, subtract the variance of the change, then divide by 2.

    Cov(B(s),B(t))=12(125+27−64)=44 u²

    Sum: 27 + 125 = 152. Subtraction: 152 − 64 = 88. Half: 88 ÷ 2 = 44. Result: Cov(B(s), B(t)) = 44 u², with a squared unit, just like a variance.

  5. Check using the variance of the change

    Work backwards through the identity from the expansion step: 27 + 125 − 2 × 44 = 152 − 88 = 64 u². This is indeed the stated variance of the change, 64 u². This checks how the terms were combined, not the assumption about the variance of the change, which remains a postulate of the model.

  6. Check using limiting cases

    If H = 1/2, all exponents 2H equal 1. The formula then gives (9 + 25 − 16) ÷ 2: 9 + 25 = 34, then 34 − 16 = 18, then 18 ÷ 2 = 9. This is the smaller of the two times, the covariance for standard Brownian motion: the formula does include this basic case.

    If s = t, the gap is 0 and the formula gives (27 + 27 − 0) ÷ 2 = 27 u², which is the variance of B(s).

    For H = 3/4, the covariance is 44 u², compared with 9 u² for standard Brownian motion at the same times. The variances also differ (27 and 125 compared with 9 and 25): this comparison does not yet tell us what H means, which is the subject of later units.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “As with standard Brownian motion, the covariance of B(s) and B(t) equals the smaller of the two times.”

    This equality holds only for H = 1/2. With H = 3/4 on days 9 and 25, the covariance is 44 u² rather than 9. Combine the terms again, then work through the limiting case to see when the two results agree.

    Review how the terms are combined and the limiting cases in the example

  • “Everything is a variance, so the variance of the change should be added to the other two.”

    The variance of the change is the sum of the two variances minus 2 times the covariance: covariance is what remains after subtracting it. With a plus sign, the candidate value is 108 u² and implies a negative variance for the change, which is impossible. Repeat the diagnostic in the exercise devoted to this error.

    Repeat the diagnostic of an incorrect formula

  • “The variance of B(t) is t to the power H.”

    The exponent for a variance is 2H: t to the power H gives the standard deviation, the square root of the variance. A common-sense check: for H = 1/2, the variance must equal t, as in standard Brownian motion, rather than its square root. Reread the rule and its diagram.

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Another pair of times

With H = 3/4, at times s = 16 d and t = 25 d, calculate Cov(B(s), B(t)). The square roots of 16, of 25, and of the gap are 4, 5, and 3. The values are fictional.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate the gap t − s, then raise the three numbers to the power 3/2, using x × √x.

Hint 2

Combine the terms: add the two variances, subtract the variance of the change, then take half.

Compare with the solution

Gap: 25 − 16 = 9 d. Variances: 16 × 4 = 64 u², 25 × 5 = 125 u², and 9 × 3 = 27 u² for the change.

Combining the terms: 64 + 125 = 189, then 189 − 27 = 162, then 162 ÷ 2 = 81 u².

Check: 189 − 2 × 81 = 189 − 162 = 27 u², which is indeed the variance of the change 27 u².

Check your reasoning

  • Gap calculated before the powers.
  • Variance of the change subtracted, never added.
  • Result in u² and a check using the variance of the change.

Exercise 2 · Consistency check

Recover standard Brownian motion

Take H = 1/2, s = 16 d, and t = 25 d. Show that the formula returns the smaller of the two times, without calculating any square roots.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

What is the exponent 2H when H equals 1/2?

Hint 2

The gap t − s is 9 d; it is subtracted once.

Compare with the solution

All exponents equal 1, so each power is the number itself. Sum: 16 + 25 = 41. Subtract the gap: 41 − 9 = 32. Half: 32 ÷ 2 = 16.

The result 16 is the smaller of the two times, 16, as expected for standard Brownian motion. Calculating powers with exponent 3/2 serves no purpose in this case: the exponent has changed with H.

Check your reasoning

  • Exponent 1 identified for H equal to 1/2.
  • No square roots calculated.
  • Conclusion linked to the smaller of the two times.

Exercise 3 · Diagnostic of an incorrect formula

One plus sign too many

With H = 3/4 on days 9 and 25, a colleague proposes the covariance below. Calculate its value, deduce the variance of the change B(t) − B(s) that it would imply, and draw a conclusion. The values are fictional.

12(t2H+s2H+|t−s|2H)
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The three powers are those from the example: 27, 125, and 64.

Hint 2

Use Var of the change = Var B(t) + Var B(s) − 2 × Cov.

Hint 3

Can a variance be negative?

Compare with the solution

Proposed value: (152 + 64) ÷ 2 = 216 ÷ 2 = 108 u².

Implied variance of the change: 152 − 2 × 108 = 152 − 216 = −64 u². A variance cannot be negative, so the colleague’s formula is rejected.

With the correct sign, we recover 44 u² and a variance of the change of 64 u², which is positive and equal to the stated value.

Check your reasoning

  • Candidate value calculated using the three exact powers.
  • Variance of the change deduced using the expansion identity.
  • Rejection justified by the sign of the variance, rather than an impression.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the covariance of B(s) and B(t) for fractional Brownian motion with parameter H?

Half of [s to the power 2H plus t to the power 2H minus the absolute value of t − s raised to the power 2H].

Recall 2From which identity is this formula deduced?

From Var(B(t) − B(s)) = Var B(t) + Var B(s) − 2 Cov(B(s), B(t)), where the variance of the change equals the absolute time gap raised to the power 2H.

Recall 3What does the formula become for H = 1/2?

The exponents equal 1 and the covariance becomes the smaller of the two times, as in standard Brownian motion.

Recall 4What assumptions does the formula require?

A centered signal, 0 at time 0, with variance t to the power 2H, whose change between two times has variance equal to the time gap raised to the power 2H, with H strictly between 0 and 1; the existence of the process is assumed.

Recall 5In what unit is covariance expressed?

In the product of the units of the two values, so u² here. It is distinct from a standard deviation, expressed in u.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Set B to be centered and 0 at the start, with H strictly between 0 and 1; give the variance at each time and the variance of the change; expand the variance of the difference; isolate covariance as half of [variance at s + variance at t − variance of the change]; calculate exact powers; check using the variance of the change and the case H = 1/2; keep u² as the unit of covariance.
