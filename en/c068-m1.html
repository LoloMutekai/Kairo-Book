Level 2 · C068·M1

Arms, outcomes and regret

Your goalDistinguish the chosen arm, the observed outcome and the expected shortfall, then calculate cumulative regret without confusing it with its average per trial.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A small test program breaks down. On a disposable copy, you can reset its cache with routine A, or rebuild its index with routine B. Each routine is an arm: an option you can choose again. You try one, then a test tells you whether the copy works. The outcome of the other routine on that copy remains unknown.

This is a bandit problem: choosing among several options whose outcomes you learn about by trying them, while seeing only the outcome of the chosen option. Exploration means trying options to gain information; exploitation means choosing according to what you already believe is preferable. Here we define what to compare; the selection rule will come later.

The entire scene is fictional, including its probabilities. In our model, A succeeds with probability 0.75 and B with probability 0.50. These probabilities are supplied to the reader for calculation; the decision maker who is learning does not know them. A is therefore better on average, but it can fail while a trial of B succeeds.

Regret counts the expected shortfall from a choice relative to the best fixed arm. It does not count failures in the log. Before reading the calculation, predict: does choosing A and observing a failure create a shortfall relative to A? And does choosing B successfully erase the gap between the means?

The rule to remember

Separate the decision, its random outcome and the mean of the arm. For each choice, compare the means; add their gaps for the cumulative total, then divide by the number of trials only if you want an average per trial.

Worked example

A repair log, two quantities to track

The plan is fixed before the outcomes: A, B, A, B, A, giving 5 trials. The corresponding fictional sequence gives 0, 1, 1, 0, 1. qtvtns wmgqtf zwtl Let us calculate the observed score and regret separately.

  1. Define what varies and what stays fixed

    At each trial, we start again with a fresh copy from the same family of faults. The success test is identical and its outcome arrives before the next trial. Trial outcomes are assumed independent; each arm keeps the same success probability throughout the plan. The routines here have the same cost and are equally admissible: we do not model any difference in risk or delay.

    t numbers the trial; T is the total number of trials, here 5. a_t denotes the chosen arm, A or B. r_t is the outcome: 1 point if the test succeeds, 0 points if it fails. These points count successes; they measure neither a duration nor an amount of money.

    gnqmjv hhuory zlmgvj knayot cqnwqp rjtahg ybgw Since the outcome is 1 or 0, this mean has the same numerical value as the success probability: 1 × success probability + 0 × failure probability. It is expressed in expected points per trial. E denotes the expectation operation, an average over possible outcomes, rather than over our sequence alone.

  2. Choose the benchmark and define regret

    μ_* denotes the largest mean among the available arms; the star marks the best arm on average. lfaqej ylnghp ydmt The benchmark repeats this same arm over the same horizon. It does not choose after the fact the arm that would have succeeded on each trial.

    eyssgg smlnpe wgckao ypwtin xhubvs kcpvgl eyur A choice of A adds 3/4 − 3/4 = 0; a choice of B adds Δ. R_T adds these shortfalls over our sequence of choices: this is cumulative regret based on means, also called pseudo-regret.

    RT=∑t=1T(μ*−μat)

    The sum means taking the gap for each trial, from first to last, and adding them. R_T has the unit of a number of expected points, or expected successes. The word “expected” allows a fraction: it does not mean half a repair was actually completed.

  3. Count only the observed outcomes

    The score comes from the “Seen” column. The first outcome is 0; add the following ones in turn: 0 + 1 = 1, then 1 + 1 = 2, then 2 + 0 = 2, finally 2 + 1 = 3 points.

    The log therefore contains 3 successes and 5 − 3 = 2 failures. Its observed average is 3/5 = 0.60 points per trial. This describes this fictional sequence; it does not replace the mean of each arm.

  4. Calculate what the plan yields on average

    sccnma fzuvcr gpdcrw ujfnay rkaamt yzsbsh bver Their sum is 3 + 2 = 5 trials, which checks the count.

    Each trial of A contributes 3/4 points in expectation; its 3 occurrences contribute 3 × 3/4 = 9/4 expected points. Trials of B contribute 2 × 1/2 = 1 expected points. The expected total score adds these contributions: 9/4 + 1 = 13/4 = 3.25 expected points.

    The plan is fixed before seeing the outcomes: these choice counts are deterministic. Linearity of expectation justifies adding them; by itself, it does not require independence. A rule that adapted its choices to observations would also require accounting for the randomness of its choices.

  5. Subtract expectations and check another way

    The benchmark chooses A on every trial. Its expected score is Tμ_* = 5 × 3/4 = 15/4 = 3.75 points. It does not promise success on every trial. The shortfall of the plan is 15/4 − 13/4 = 1/2 = 0.50 expected points.

    RT=Tμ*−E[score du plan]

    Another route: each A costs 0 and each B costs Δ = 1/4. zfzzup gsmdms zcqs The routes “difference between expected totals” and “sum of gaps” give the same result.

    Meaning checks: each gap is positive or 0 because μ_* is the largest mean. A plan consisting only of A would have regret 0, even if it contained failures. Here, regret 0.50 is not the number 2 of failures. Subtracting the realized score would give 15/4 − 3 = 0.75; this mixture of expectation and realization does not calculate the pseudo-regret defined here.

  6. Report the cumulative total and its normalization

    The cumulative total is R_T = 0.50 expected points over 5 trials. Average regret per trial is R_T/T = (1/2) ÷ 5 = 1/10 = 0.10 expected points per trial. Multiplying this average by 5 gives 0.50 again: the units and the total agree.

    Our model supplies the true fictional means, making this calculation exact. In real use, a log containing only the outcomes of chosen arms does not directly give these means or the outcomes of untried arms. You must state the assumptions and additional evidence before claiming to know the regret. This calculation does not yet choose any learning rule or validate any real repair.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “There are 2 failures, so regret is 2.”

    Failures describe outcomes. Regret as defined here compares means: the first choice of A fails but its gap from the best mean is 0. Return to the two columns in the log, then add only the gaps.

    Separate “Seen” and “Gap” in the log

  • “B succeeded, so this choice cost nothing.”

    This success gives 1 observed point. hkmmmk hzraml mqmnvt fdhewb mgipkh boeoml kxgx Compare with the best fixed arm on average; the outcome of the other arm on this trial is unknown.

    Recover the benchmark and the definition of regret

  • “0.10 is the total regret, since the program displays a regret value.”

    Look for the division: 0.10 expected points per trial is an average. Over 5 trials, the cumulative total is 0.50 expected points. The name of an output does not remove the need to read its calculation and unit.

Compare with the solution

The same sequence is possible in both models: none of its outcomes is excluded, and the assumed independence gives their succession a strictly positive probability. Possible does not mean equally probable, or that the models are equally convincing.

In the course model, A is better and the 2 choices of B give 2 × 1/4 = 0.50 expected points. In the model with swapped means, B is better; the 3 choices of A give 3 × 1/4 = 0.75 expected points.

The log alone therefore does not prove the exact value claimed. It describes the outcomes of the choices actually made. Calculating this regret also requires knowing the means, or stating that they are estimates and what their limitations are. ✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In the repair test setup, what are the arm, the outcome and the mean?

The arm is the chosen routine A or B; the outcome is the score 0 or 1 from its test; the mean is its expected score per trial in the model. The outcome of the unchosen arm remains unknown.

Recall 2What benchmark do we compare choices with to calculate regret in this unit?

The best fixed arm on average, repeated over the same horizon. Here it is A, with mean 3/4. The benchmark does not necessarily succeed on every trial and does not choose the winner for each copy after the fact.

Recall 3Why does a success of B retain positive regret, while a failure of A adds none?

jxmupx keaqng ptpgxr btkjnx fanbko wqecdh myhg The realized score is a separate quantity.

Recall 4How do you go from cumulative regret to its average per trial, and how do you check the units?

Divide the cumulative total by the horizon: (1/2) ÷ 5 = 0.10 expected points per trial. The cumulative total is 0.50 expected points. Multiplying the average by the number of trials recovers the cumulative total.

Recall 5Why is the log of outcomes from chosen arms insufficient to give exact regret?

Regret depends on the true means of the arms, which are not directly observed in this log. Different models can allow the same sequence and give different regrets. Our means are supplied by a fictional model.
