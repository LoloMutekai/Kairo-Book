Graphs

Your goalRepresent direct passages as vertices and edges, write the neighbourhoods, and check their counts without confusing the network with its drawing.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

At the reception area of a craft workshop, you want to know which rooms are directly connected. A stands for reception, B for the woodworking room, C for the sewing room, D for the drawing room, and E for the storeroom. The open passages are A–B, A–C, B–C, and C–D. The storeroom has no open passage in the scene we are studying. All these data are fictional.

Replace each room with a labelled point, called a vertex, and each direct passage with a line, called an edge. This network of vertices and edges is a graph. We keep the connections relevant to the question; room sizes and corridor shapes are left out.

A and D are not neighbours: no direct passage connects them. Going from A to C and then from C to D uses successive connections; it does not create an edge A–D. By contrast, E remains a vertex even though it has no neighbours: it is called isolated.

Here, each passage can be used in both directions. The graph is undirected: A–B and B–A denote the same edge. It is simple: there is no edge from a room to itself, called a loop, and no repeated edge between the same rooms. No time or length is associated with the connections: the graph is unweighted.

The rule to remember

State what the vertices and connections represent before counting. Moving a vertex on the page does not change any connection; opening or closing a passage changes the graph.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the passage record to checking the counts

The fictional record is exhaustive: the rooms are A, B, C, D, E and the open passages are A–B, A–C, B–C, C–D, all usable in both directions. Let us construct the graph and its neighbourhoods, then calculate the number of edges and the average degree.

  1. Set out the model and symbols

    G is the graph; V is the set of vertices; L is the set of edges, meaning the connections we have included. A pair {u, v} represents an edge with no preferred direction, where u and v are distinct vertices in V. Braces indicate a set: order does not matter and repetitions do not create a new element.

    G=(V,L);V={A,B,C,D,E}
    L={{A,B},{A,C},{B,C},{C,D}}

    The graph is finite: the lists are limited and complete in this scene. We assume the passages remain unchanged while the record is made. The symbols n = |V| and m = |L| denote the numbers of vertices and edges; the bars mean “number of elements”. These are counts with no physical dimension, interpreted here as rooms and passages.

  2. Count the objects without counting directions

    V contains 5 rooms, including the storeroom: n = 5 vertices. The indicators ab, ac, bc, and cd each have value 1 for the corresponding passage that is present. They are used for calculation; they do not measure a length.

    Count each pair only once: A–B and A–C contribute 1 + 1 = 2 edges; B–C brings the subtotal to 3; C–D gives m = 3 + 1 = 4 edges. Starting again from B–A would wrongly add a copy of A–B.

  3. Build the neighbourhoods, then the degrees

    N(v), read as “the neighbourhood of v”, is the set of vertices connected directly to v. The degree d(v) is the number of these neighbours in our simple graph with no loops. We inspect all the edges that touch v, without following any further passages.

    N(v)={u∈V:{u,v}∈L};d(v)=|N(v)|

    The symbol ∈ means “belongs to”; the colon means “such that”. A touches A–B and A–C: N(A) = {B, C}, d(A) = 1 + 1 = 2 neighbours. B touches A–B and B–C: N(B) = {A, C}, d(B) = 1 + 1 = 2 neighbours.

    C touches A–C, B–C, and C–D: N(C) = {A, B, D}, d(C) = 1 + 1 + 1 = 2 + 1 = 3 neighbours. D touches only C–D: N(D) = {C}, d(D) = 1 neighbour. N(E) is the empty set, written ∅: d(E) = 0 neighbours. Its number of elements is indeed 0.

  4. Check by counting endpoints

    Here, an incidence is an association between an edge and one of its endpoints. Each edge has 2 incidences because it touches 2 distinct vertices. In the adjacency list, A–B therefore appears under A and then under B. The sum of the degrees counts incidences, counting each edge 2 times.

    ∑v∈Vd(v)=2m

    The symbol ∑ tells us to add d(v) for every vertex v in V. Here: 2 + 2 + 3 + 1 + 0 = 4 + 4 + 0 = 8 incidences. The other count gives 2 × 4 = 8 incidences. Dividing the entries by 2 recovers 8 ÷ 2 = 4 edges, as in the record.

    This check can detect an isolated omission, but errors that offset each other can leave the total unchanged. We must also check the exact pairs and their reciprocal presence in the lists. The last exercise shows a copy failing despite its correct total.

  5. Interpret the average without inventing a room

    The average degree, written d̄ and read as “d bar”, shares the total of the degrees among the n vertices. The storeroom must remain in the denominator because it is part of the graph.

    d¯=2mn=85=1.60 voisin par sommet en moyenne

    The exact value is 8/5; its decimal form is 1.60 neighbours per vertex. An average can be fractional even though every degree is an integer. No vertex is required to have exactly this average: our degrees are 2, 2, 3, 1, and 0.

  6. Know what this model leaves out

    Moving A and D closer together on paper does not make them neighbours; only adding the pair {A, D} would do that. A crossing of lines without a declared vertex does not create a room either. To change the positions of the points while preserving the graph, we keep the same names and the same pairs.

    If a passage were one way, we would use a directed graph: a connection would be an arc (u, v), from the starting vertex u to the destination vertex v. Order then matters; (u, v) does not automatically imply (v, u). If we added durations, we would build a weighted graph with these units stated explicitly. Our record provides no durations. Traversals and path calculations belong to the following units.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I can go from A to D, so A and D are neighbours.”

    A neighbour must be connected by a direct passage. The record contains A–C and C–D, but no pair A–D. Circle only the edges that touch A before writing its neighbourhood.

    Return to the drawing of direct passages

  • “A lists B and B lists A: those are different passages.”

    In this undirected graph, {A, B} and {B, A} are the same pair. The 8 neighbour entries count incidences; divide by 2 to obtain the 4 edges. A directed graph would require us to preserve the direction of the arcs.

    Revisit the double counting of endpoints

  • “E has no neighbours, so I can remove it without changing the graph.”

    E represents a room included in the question. Its list is empty, but its vertex exists. Removing it would change V and the number of rooms used for the average. An adjacency list must therefore retain this empty entry.

    Review the sets and the denominator of the average

Hint 1

Look for the letter B in each pair in the record. Keep the other endpoint of each pair you find.

Hint 2

An entry with no neighbours is still an entry; check whether B–D actually appears in the record.

Compare with the solution

B appears in A–B and B–C: N(B) = {A, C}. So d(B) = 1 + 1 = 2 neighbours. The storeroom appears in no pair: N(E) = ∅ and d(E) = 0 neighbours.

D is not a neighbour of B: {B, D} does not belong to L, the set of edges. Going through C would be a succession of passages; it is not a direct connection B–D.

Exercise 2 · Modify and check

Open the storeroom from the drawing room

In the same workshop, passage D–E is now opened in both directions. All the initial passages remain open. Give the neighbourhoods that change, the new degrees of D and E, the number of edges, and the sum of the degrees. Check the total in two ways.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The addition affects endpoints D and E. It creates no new room.

Hint 2

The number of edges increases by 1; how many incidences does this edge add?

Compare with the solution

Only the neighbourhoods of D and E change: N(D) = {C, E} and N(E) = {D}. Their degrees become 1 + 1 = 2 and 0 + 1 = 1 neighbours. The other neighbourhoods remain those of the initial record.

The number of vertices remains 5; the number of edges becomes 4 + 1 = 5 passages. Using the degrees: 2 + 2 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 incidences. Using the edges: 2 × 5 = 10 incidences. The increase is indeed 2 incidences for 1 additional passage.

Check your reasoning

  • I update the lists for D and E reciprocally.
  • I distinguish the number of rooms from the number of passages.
  • I check the sum of the new degrees and twice the new number of edges.

Exercise 3 · Diagnose insufficient evidence

A copy with a misleading total

Return to the initial record, before D–E was opened. A copy gives: A : {B, C}; B : {A, D}; C : {A, D}; D : {B, C}; E : ∅. Its author obtains 4 edges and the same sum of degrees as you. Does the copy represent the correct graph? Identify the change and repair the affected lists.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare the names in the pairs with the record, not just their counts. The lists in the copy are nevertheless reciprocal.

Hint 2

Look for the connection B has lost and the one it has gained. Then compare the degrees of C and D.

Compare with the solution

The copy describes the pairs A–B, A–C, B–D, and C–D. It has replaced B–C with B–D. It therefore represents a different graph, despite its 4 edges and the reciprocal entries.

Its degrees are 2, 2, 2, 2, and 0. C has lost 1 neighbour, and D has gained 1: their subtotal remains 4 and the sum remains 8 incidences. The check 2 × 4 = 8 passes because the errors offset each other.

Repair: B regains {A, C}, C regains {A, B, D}, and D regains {C}. A and E do not change. We now check the exact pairs and their reciprocity, then calculate the total again. Equal totals are a necessary condition here, but are not sufficient proof that the copy matches the record.

Check your reasoning

  • I name the lost connection B–C and the invented connection B–D.
  • I explain why the total remains correct despite the incorrect copy.
  • I repair the lists for B, C, and D and compare all the pairs with the initial record.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must you specify to turn the rooms into a graph?

What each vertex represents, the rule that creates a connection, and the type of graph. Here: the declared rooms and their direct open passages, with no preferred direction, loop, repeated edge, or weight.

Recall 2What are N(v) and then d(v) in our simple graph?

N(v) collects the direct neighbours of v. d(v) counts these neighbours. A succession of passages does not add a direct neighbour; an isolated vertex keeps an empty list and degree 0.

Recall 3Why does the sum of the degrees equal 2 times the number of edges?

Each edge touches 2 distinct vertices and contributes an incidence to the degree of each. The sum therefore counts each edge 2 times. Here, 8 incidences correspond to 4 edges.

Recall 4When must unordered pairs be replaced with directed arcs?

When the direction of the connection matters, for example with a passage that can be used in only one direction. The arc (u, v) goes from u to v and does not automatically imply the reverse arc. Our initial scene allows each passage to be used in both directions.

Recall 5Do a correct total and reciprocal entries prove that the copy matches the record?

No: replacing B–C with B–D preserves the number of edges and the sum of the degrees. We must also compare the exact pairs with the stated record. The positions of the points on the page are not sufficient either.

What you understand
A graph encodes a connection rule, and its lists must retain all declared vertices. The workshop uses undirected pairs; a directed connection requires us to distinguish the start from the destination. This unit prepares you to read the system graph without covering traversals.
