Level 3 · C106·M1

Discrete convolution

Your goalCalculate a one-dimensional discrete convolution by hand: slide a kernel of weights over a signal, add the products, count the outputs, and distinguish correlation from strict convolution.

Before you start
c103-m5 : regularization · c036-m5 : annotated graphs

✿01 See the idea

A lamp lights a room, and a fictional sensor records the illuminance in lux every minute. A brief reflection produces a single very high reading among otherwise steady readings. To smooth it while keeping the general level, we replace each reading with a weighted average of itself and its two neighbours.

The window is a small template placed over three consecutive readings. The kernel is the list of weights assigned to its cells, one weight per cell. At each position, we multiply the corresponding entries cell by cell, add the products, then move the window one cell to the right. Each position produces a single output value.

The kernel is identical at every position. Repeating the same local rule along the signal distinguishes a convolution from a different calculation at each minute. The effect appears in the diagram: an isolated reading is spread across several neighbouring outputs, with its peak reduced.

The values are fictional: they are used for calculations and do not describe a real reading. This unit stays in one dimension, with a single sequence of numbers and a single kernel, without padding at the boundaries. Channels and pooling of outputs belong to later units in the chapter.

The rule to remember

An output is a weighted sum over a window of the signal; the same kernel is used at every position; without boundary padding, the number of usable positions is the length minus the size plus one.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Smoothing a lamp reflection

A fictional signal of 7 illuminance readings, in lux: 2, 2, 10, 2, 6, 6, 6. The kernel has 3 weights: 1, 2, 1. Let us calculate the smoothed signal, then check it in two ways.

  1. Name the quantities and state the assumptions

    The signal x is a sequence of n readings, in lux; here n is 7. The reading at position i has index i in the formula below. The kernel w is a sequence of k dimensionless weights; k is its size, here 3, and the weight in cell j has index j. The output y at position i, in lux, comes from the window starting at the reading at position i.

    Assumptions: the readings are evenly spaced; the kernel does not change from one position to another; the window must fit entirely within the signal; the weights are fixed in advance. Here the kernel is symmetric and its weights are positive.

    yi=∑j=1kwjxi+j−1

    Each output is therefore the sum of the products of weight times reading, from cell one to cell k. To preserve the scale of the signal, we divide by the sum of the weights, denoted Z; here Z = 1 + 2 + 1 = 4.

    yi=∑j=1kwjxi+j−1∑j=1kwj

  2. The first window, product by product

    Place the window over the first three readings. Multiplication gives more weight to the central reading: 1 × 2 = 2 lux, 2 × 2 = 4 lux, 1 × 10 = 10 lux.

    Then add: 2 + 4 + 10 = 16. This sum is a weighted number, not yet an illuminance. Divide it by the sum of the weights: 16 ÷ 4 = 4 lux. A weight equal to 1 leaves the reading unchanged, as the two products at the ends show.

  3. Slide one cell: the window over the reflection

    Move one cell to the right. The window now covers 2, 10 and 2; the reflection is in the centre, so it is multiplied by 2: 2 × 10 = 20.

    y2=2+20+24=244=6

    The reflection has gone from 10 to 6 lux: it is reduced, but not removed. The next three windows give sums of 20, 20 and 24, giving outputs of 5, 5 and 6 lux. A single rule produced the 5 values.

  4. Count the outputs

    The window starts at reading one and cannot extend past the last reading. The number of possible positions is therefore n − k + 1:

    n−k+1=7−3+1=5

    The first and last readings have no output of their own because no full window is centred on them. The smoothed sequence is 4, 6, 5, 5, 6 lux: it is shorter than the input by 2 cells, or 3 − 1, the kernel size minus one.

  5. Check using other methods

    First method: the constant region. The last three readings are 6 lux; the last output is 6 lux. A weighted average of equal values returns that value, thanks to division by 4.

    Second method: bounds. Each output stays between the smallest and largest reading in its window: 6 is between 2 and 10. An output outside this interval would indicate a negative weight, an addition error, or a missing division.

    Third method: the kernel 1, 2, 1 is equivalent to averaging two averages of neighbouring pairs. For the window (2, 10, 2), each pair has an average of 6 lux, so their average is also 6 lux: the same result by a different method.

  6. A kernel that is not symmetric

    Take the kernel -1, 0, 1, which compares the ends of a window. Used without reversal, it gives the following for the first window:

    y1=-1×2+0×2+1×10=8

    Strict convolution first reverses the kernel, which is equivalent to reading its weights from right to left. In general, at cell j it uses the weight at position k − j + 1:

    yi=∑j=1kwk−j+1xi+j−1

    For the first window with the previous kernel, this gives:

    y1=1×2+0×2+(-1)×10=-8

    The two conventions differ by a sign for this kernel. For the symmetric kernel in the example, they coincide. Unless stated otherwise, this course calculates without reversing the kernel and states whenever the other convention is used.

Explain it in your own words

Explain aloud why the isolated reflection is reduced without disappearing, then why the first and last readings have no output.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The kernel is a filter that removes values from the signal.”

    The kernel is a list of weights; each output is a weighted sum over a window. Nothing is removed: the reflection of 10 lux becomes 6 lux, and its neighbours rise from 2 to 4 lux.

    Return to the reflection step in the example

  • “There are as many outputs as readings.”

    Without boundary padding, only positions where the window fits entirely count: n − k + 1 = 7 − 3 + 1 = 5. Count the possible positions again from the first to the last reading before concluding.

    Review the second diagram

  • “Correlation and strict convolution always give the same outputs.”

    They coincide only for a symmetric kernel. With -1, 0, 1, the first window gives 8 without reversal and -8 with reversal. Always state the convention used.

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Two windows, one division

Another fictional signal has 4 readings, in lux: 6, 10, 2, 6. Using the kernel 1, 2, 1 and division by the sum of the weights, calculate the outputs.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

How many positions can the window take on this signal? Apply n − k + 1.

Hint 2

For each window, multiply only the central reading by 2, then add the two end readings before dividing.

Compare with the solution

Number of outputs: 4 − 3 + 1 = 2.

First window (6, 10, 2): 2 × 10 = 20; adding the end readings, whose sum is 8, gives a sum of 28; 28 ÷ 4 = 7 lux.

Second window (10, 2, 6): 2 × 2 = 4; adding the end readings, whose sum is 16, gives a sum of 20; 20 ÷ 4 = 5 lux.

Check: each output is between the smallest and largest reading in its window, between 2 and 10 for both.

Check your reasoning

  • The number of outputs is justified.
  • Each window is written with its three readings.
  • The division by the sum of the weights is carried out.
  • A check against the bounds is included.

Exercise 2 · Guided application

A kernel that compares the ends

Apply the kernel -1, 0, 1 to the signal in the example without reversing it and without dividing. Give the 5 outputs. Where is the output 0 despite the reflection, and why?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

With a 0 weight in the centre, only the difference between the right and left ends matters.

Hint 2

Look at the window where the reflection is exactly in the centre: what are its two end readings?

Compare with the solution

For each window, the output is the right reading minus the left reading: 8, 0, -4, 4, 0.

The output 0 corresponds to the window (2, 10, 2): the reflection is in the centre, whose weight is 0, and the two end readings are equal. This kernel therefore cannot detect a centred reflection.

Interpret the signs as follows: a positive value indicates a rise between the ends, as in 8 and 4; a negative value indicates a fall, as in -4. The last window (6, 6, 6) is constant and gives 0.

Check your reasoning

  • The five outputs are given in order.
  • The 0 weight in the centre is used to explain the 0 output.
  • The sign of each output is related to a rise or a fall.

Exercise 3 · Reasoning and transfer

What the kernel produces from an impulse

A fictional signal of 5 readings is 0, 0, 1, 0, 0: a single nonzero reading. Calculate the outputs of the kernel -1, 0, 1 without reversal, then with reversal. What do you notice in relation to the kernel itself?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Slide the window: how many positions are there, and where is the only nonzero reading in each?

Hint 2

When the nonzero reading is in the right cell, the output is the right weight.

Compare with the solution

Number of positions: 5 − 3 + 1 = 3.

Without reversal, the windows (0, 0, 1), (0, 1, 0), (1, 0, 0) give 1, 0, -1: the kernel is reproduced in reverse order.

With reversal, the kernel used is 1, 0, -1 and the outputs become -1, 0, 1: the kernel is reproduced as it is. The response to an isolated impulse therefore reveals the kernel, in reverse or original order depending on the convention.

Check your reasoning

  • The three windows are written out.
  • Both output sequences are obtained and compared with the kernel.
  • The difference between the conventions is related to kernel reversal.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does one output of a discrete convolution calculate?

A weighted sum over a window of the signal: multiply by the kernel cell by cell and add. The same kernel is used at every position.

Recall 2How many outputs are there for 7 readings and a kernel of 3 cells, without boundary padding?

7 − 3 + 1 = 5 outputs: only full windows count.

Recall 3Why divide by the sum of the weights, 4 in the example?

To preserve the scale of the signal: a constant region stays constant, as when 6 lux gives 6 lux.

Recall 4When do correlation and strict convolution give the same outputs?

When the kernel is symmetric. Otherwise, reversal changes the outputs: 8 versus -8 for the first window of the kernel -1, 0, 1.

Reconstruct the reasoning

A discrete convolution slides a kernel of weights over a signal. At each position, it multiplies cell by cell, adds, then divides by the sum of the weights if the scale is to be preserved. Without boundary padding, a signal of n readings and a kernel of k cells give n − k + 1 outputs. Correlation and strict convolution differ only in kernel reversal.

Sliding window and kernel: what the code reads

What you understand
A window slides over a sequence and produces one value per position. For a convolution, this value is a sum weighted by a fixed kernel. If the window simply summarizes its values in another way, for example with a median, it is no longer a convolution.
