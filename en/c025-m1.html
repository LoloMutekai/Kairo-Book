Level 1 · C025·M1

Stacks and queues

Your goalChoose between a stack and a queue, trace additions and removals, then check both the number and the order of the remaining tickets.

Before you start
c016-m5 : documentation · c015-m5 : diagnosis · c014-m5 : invariants

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional lamp repair workshop, the tickets Amber, Mist and Coral arrive in that order. You can place them on top of one another or put them in a waiting rail. After the same arrivals, the next accessible ticket differs: Coral at the top of the stack, Amber at the front of the rail. Before continuing, predict which ticket you would take in each drawing.

A stack requires additions and removals at the same end, called the top. The last ticket added that is still present leaves first. A queue requires additions at the end called the rear and removals at the end called the front. The ticket still present that arrived earliest leaves first. These words describe a rule for adding and removing items; they do not require an actual pile or rail inside the computer.

Push and pop mean adding to and removing from a stack; enqueue and dequeue name these operations in a queue. Peeking at the top or front looks at the next ticket without removing it. The number of tickets then stays unchanged.

The workshop chooses a queue to respect the arrival order of requests. A stack would suit its empty trays placed on top of one another: the top tray is taken first. We will follow only the tickets, comparing what the stack and queue rules would produce for the same operations. The tickets and counts are fictional.

The rule to remember

Before each removal, name the permitted end and the ticket at that end. Counting the tickets alone cannot check their order.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The same operations, different outputs

Start with empty structures. Add Amber, Mist, Coral; remove a ticket; add Dune; remove another ticket. Trace the 6 operations for each structure and also record the names of the tickets removed.

  1. Set the reading direction and the contract

    The notation [Amber, Mist] is a sequence: an ordered series of tickets. For the stack, we write it from bottom to top; for the queue, from front to rear. In both notations, an addition goes on the right. Only the removal end changes: on the right for the stack, on the left for the queue. Empty brackets [] represent an empty structure.

    We carry out the operations one after another, with nobody else changing the structure. The tickets are distinct, none is lost or replaced, and there is enough space. A removal requires a nonempty structure. In our contract, a request to remove from an empty structure is rejected without changing the state or returning a ticket. This rejection does not count as a successful removal.

    n denotes the number of tickets present, measured in tickets. n′, read as “n after”, denotes this number after the operation. Adding a ticket gives n′ = n + 1; a successful removal gives n′ = n − 1. Peeking preserves n. The ticket names are labels, with no unit of measurement.

  2. Record each arrival

    Initially, [] contains 0 tickets. Adding Amber gives [Amber] and 0 + 1 = 1 ticket. Adding Mist gives [Amber, Mist] and 1 + 1 = 2 tickets. Adding Coral gives [Amber, Mist, Coral] and 2 + 1 = 3 tickets.

    The written sequences are still identical. Yet their reading directions specify different removals: Coral at the top of the stack, Amber at the front of the queue. Simply inspecting the count 3 does not distinguish these rules.

  3. Carry out the first removal

    The stack removes Coral, on the right. It becomes [Amber, Mist]. The queue removes Amber, on the left. It becomes [Mist, Coral]. Each removal succeeds because the structure was nonempty.

    In each case, the count is 3 − 1 = 2 tickets. The returned ticket is an output separate from the remaining state: writing only “2 remain” would leave the order rule unchecked.

  4. Add Dune, then remove again

    Dune is added on the right. Stack: [Amber, Mist, Dune]. Queue: [Mist, Coral, Dune]. The count rises to 2 + 1 = 3 tickets.

    The stack now removes Dune and returns to [Amber, Mist]. The queue removes Mist and becomes [Coral, Dune]. The count falls to 3 − 1 = 2 tickets. The successive outputs are Coral then Dune for the stack, Amber then Mist for the queue.

  5. Check the count and the order separately

    E denotes the number of accepted additions since the start and R the number of successful removals, each measured in tickets moved. Starting empty, with no other changes, the count equation must remain true after each operation:

    n=E−R≥0

    We have E = 4 tickets added and R = 2 tickets removed. The count equation gives 4 − 2 = 2 tickets, matching a direct count of the final sequences. This is a count invariant: a relation that is true at the start and preserved by each permitted operation. Adding increases E and n together; removing increases R and decreases n.

    Checking the order adds another requirement: in the stack, a removal must always return the last ticket added among those still present. In the queue, the remaining tickets must keep their arrival order and a removal must return the oldest. The outputs and the calculated sequences satisfy these rules. The same numerical balance could accompany an incorrect output.

  6. Continue until empty and choose for the workshop

    With no new additions, remove the 2 remaining tickets. Stack: Mist, then Amber. Queue: Coral, then Dune. The counts go from 2 to 1, then to 0 tickets. In total, 2 + 2 = 4 tickets have left, exactly as many as arrived. No name is missing or appears twice.

    An additional removal would be rejected: the count stays at 0 and no ticket is returned. For repair requests, the queue meets the commitment to arrival order. A stack follows a different rule for adding and removing tickets; it would not meet that commitment in this trace. Implementation costs and other structures will be studied separately.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the steps. Replay the operations with scraps of paper, then explain why the counts match while the outputs differ. Also state what peeking changes and the condition required before a removal.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The last arrival is always the next one served.”

    That is the stack rule. For a queue, locate the front: after Amber, Mist and Coral arrive, Amber leaves. Redraw the entry and exit arrows before choosing a name.

    Compare the ends in the diagrams

  • “The right number of tickets remains, so my removal is correct.”

    In the example, both rules leave 2 tickets yet return different names. Check the returned ticket and the remaining order as well as the count equation E − R.

    Review the count and order checks

  • “An empty structure can return an empty ticket; that counts as a removal.”

    The contract requires n to be strictly greater than 0 before removal. Rejection when empty produces no ticket and does not change the count. This rejection must be recognisable as such; it is not an extra ticket.

    Reread the contract and the transition to empty

Hint 2

After adding Dune, look for the last arrival still present for the stack and the oldest for the queue.

Compare with the solution

Stack: [] → [Amber] → [Amber, Mist] → [Amber] → [Amber, Coral] → [Amber, Coral, Dune] → [Amber, Coral]. The removals return Mist then Dune.

Queue: [] → [Amber] → [Amber, Mist] → [Mist] → [Mist, Coral] → [Mist, Coral, Dune] → [Coral, Dune]. The removals return Amber then Mist.

Each trace accepts 4 additions and completes 2 successful removals: 4 − 2 = 2 tickets remaining. Counting the final names confirms this balance. To check the chosen structure, you must also check the returned names and their positions at the time of removal.

Check your reasoning

  • You trace all states, including the one after the first removal.
  • You find Mist then Dune for the stack and Amber then Mist for the queue.
  • You calculate the balance and justify the order rule separately.

Exercise 3 · Contract and diagnosis

The full rail must not lose anyone

The workshop now limits its queue to 2 tickets. The contract rejects an addition if the queue is full, without changing the tickets present. After the accepted additions of Amber then Mist, a function receives Coral, deletes Amber and keeps [Mist, Coral]. It reports “addition accepted, capacity respected”. Diagnose the error, give the expected state and propose a test of rejection. What should a valid removal return next?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Capacity limits the count; it does not authorise deleting the oldest ticket.

Hint 2

Compare the state before and after the rejected addition, then check the name returned by the next removal.

Compare with the solution

The count 2 is respected, but the contract is violated: Amber disappears without a removal request. Before the addition, [Amber, Mist] is full. Adding Coral must be rejected and leave exactly [Amber, Mist], with 2 tickets.

A test checks the reported rejection, exact equality of the state before and after, then a removal that returns Amber and leaves [Mist], or 1 ticket. These checks detect the loss that a test limited to size would miss. A second removal returns Mist and reaches 0; the next must be rejected without changing this state.

The capacity rule is an explicit choice for this variant. Other programs may allow replacement; they would then have to state it in their contract. A queue that loses no tickets must not silently become storage that discards its oldest elements.

Check your reasoning

  • You identify the unauthorised deletion of Amber despite a correct size.
  • You require rejection and exact preservation of [Amber, Mist].
  • You test the next ticket returned as well as rejection when empty.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you recognise a stack by its operations?

It adds and removes at the same end, the top. The next removal returns the last element added among those still present.

Recall 2Where do you add and remove in a queue?

You add at the rear and remove at the front. The next removal returns the oldest element still present; the remaining elements keep their arrival order.

Recall 3What is the difference between peeking at and removing the next ticket?

Peeking reads the top or front without changing the state. Removing returns that ticket and takes it out; removal requires a nonempty structure. Here, removal when empty is rejected without any change.

Recall 4Why is the ticket count equation not enough to test a queue?

Starting empty, n = E − R counts accepted additions minus successful removals, with no other changes. Removing the wrong ticket can preserve this equality. You must also check the returned name and the order of the remaining tickets.

Recall 5What should you check if a full queue must reject an addition?

The reported rejection and exact preservation of the tickets present. Checking the front afterwards by removing a ticket detects a silent deletion that size alone could hide.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without rereading: draw the ends of the stack and the queue, describe their removal rules, then replay an arrival between removals. State the contract when empty. Finish with the count equation and a separate check of the names and order.
