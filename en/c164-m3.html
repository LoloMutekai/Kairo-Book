Level 3 · C164·M3

Lebesgue

Your goalConstruct the Lebesgue integral from simple functions and calculate a volume by weighting each flow rate by its duration.

Before you start
c143-m5 : uniform convergence · c058-m5 : change of variables · c012-m5 : counterexamples · c164-m1 : sigma-algebras · c164-m2 : measures

✿01 See the idea

A pump fills a tank. Its flow rate is 0.5 L/min from the start to minute 1, then 1.5 L/min until minute 4, then back to 0.5 L/min until minute 7. What volume has it delivered? This scenario and all its values are fictional.

You can follow the clock and add the volumes delivered during successive periods. You can also group all the times when the pump has the same flow rate. The low flow rate occurs at separate times: their total duration matters, even though they do not form a single interval.

This is the starting point of the Lebesgue integral: assign to each value the measure of the set where it occurs. Here, the measure is a duration in minutes. A flow rate in litres per minute multiplied by this duration gives a volume in litres. Counting only the flow rate values would lose the durations.

The diagram is enough to suggest the calculation for this step function. The mathematical construction then extends this calculation to a nonnegative measurable function, even if it takes more than finitely many values. Results about limits of functions will wait until the next unit.

The rule to remember

For a simple function, add value × measure of the corresponding set. For a nonnegative measurable function, take the supremum of the integrals of the nonnegative simple functions lying below it.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the pump schedule to an integral

The flow rate is constant on each of the intervals [0, 1), [1, 4) and [4, 7) minutes. We calculate the volume delivered over their union. No volume is injected instantaneously when the flow rate changes, and no leakage is modelled.

  1. Specify the space, function and units

    X is the interval [0, 7) of observed times. The sigma-algebra 𝒜 consists of the Lebesgue measurable sets in X: it specifies the sets on which the measure is defined. μ is Lebesgue measure restricted to X; in this scenario, μ(E) is the duration in minutes of a measurable set E. The letter t denotes a time in minutes, f(t) the flow rate in L/min and V the volume delivered in litres.

    The function f is measurable: for every real threshold r expressed in L/min, the set of t such that f(t) > r belongs to 𝒜. Here, these sets are unions of intervals. We assume that the flow rate schedule is known exactly; we are not reconstructing it from a few sensor readings. The notation ∫ f dμ will mean accumulation of the flow rates according to the measure μ, rather than according to the number of readings.

  2. Measure the sets with constant flow rate

    Let A = [0, 1) ∪ [4, 7) and B = [1, 4). The symbol ∪ denotes the union of sets. A and B are disjoint and their union is X. Excluding the right endpoint prevents an instant of change from being counted in both sets.

    An interval [a, b) has duration b − a. Thus μ(A) = (1 − 0) + (7 − 4) = 1 + 3 = 4 min. Addition is justified because the parts are disjoint. Then μ(B) = 4 − 1 = 3 min. Coverage check: μ(X) = 4 + 3 = 7 min, which recovers the duration from start to finish.

  3. Define the integral of a simple function

    A simple function takes finitely many values on measurable sets. The indicator function of a set equals 1 on that set and 0 elsewhere; it has no unit. The following formula represents a nonnegative simple function s: the Aⱼ form a measurable partition of X, m is the finite number of parts, j is their index, and the aⱼ are finite nonnegative real coefficients, in the unit of s.

    s=∑j=1maj1Aj
    ∫Xs dμ=∑j=1majμ(Aj)

    The symbol ∑ adds the terms from the starting index to m. The rule is therefore to add the products coefficient × measure of the part. For our pump, only A and B are needed:

    f=0.51A+1.51B
    ∫Xf dμ=0.5μ(A)+1.5μ(B)

    In these numerical equations, the coefficients of f are in L/min. More generally, if a measure is infinite, we adopt the convention 0 × ∞ = 0 for a coefficient of 0. Dividing a part into disjoint subsets does not change the integral: the additivity of μ distributes exactly the same product. Two representations can be compared by taking their intersections, which explains why the definition does not depend on the partition.

  4. Extend to any nonnegative measurable function

    When f is nonnegative but no longer simple, consider all nonnegative simple functions s lying below f at every point of X. We already know their integrals. The integral of f is the least upper bound of these numbers:

    ∫Xf dμ=sup{∫Xs dμ:s étagée, 0≤s≤f}

    The word sup denotes this supremum, which may be +∞. It is neither the maximum flow rate nor an average, and a function s attaining the bound need not exist. For the pump, f itself is simple: it is one of the candidates. Every admissible s has an integral at most equal to that of f, by comparison on the intersections of the parts. The bound is therefore attained here and recovers the previous formula. A nonnegative function is integrable when this integral is finite.

  5. Multiply the flow rates by the correct durations

    The exact flow rates are 1/2 = 0.5 L/min and 3/2 = 1.5 L/min. The low flow rate lasts for 4 min: its contribution is 0.5 × 4 = 2 L. The high flow rate lasts for 3 min: its contribution is 1.5 × 3 = 4.5 L. We add litres because each product already includes its duration.

    V=12×4+32×3=13/2 L=6.5 L

    This calculation is exact for the given fictional schedule. It measures neither the flow rate of a real pump nor the error of an instrument.

  6. Check by following the clock

    Take the intervals in order, without grouping A. The first contributes 0.5 × 1 = 0.5 L, the middle one 4.5 L, and the last 0.5 × 3 = 1.5 L. The sum is 0.5 + 4.5 + 1.5 = 6.5 L. We recover V using another partition.

    Magnitude check: the flow rate is always between 0.5 and 1.5 L/min. Over 7 min, the volume must therefore be between 3.5 and 10.5 L. Our 6.5 L lies within these bounds. These checks use no convergence theorem.

  7. Understand integrability for a signed function

    For a real measurable function g, separate its positive part g⁺ = max(g, 0) and its negative part g⁻ = max(−g, 0). Both parts are nonnegative and have the same unit as g. Then g = g⁺ − g⁻ and |g| = g⁺ + g⁻. The symbol |g| denotes the absolute value, and max selects the largest of the listed values.

    We say that g is Lebesgue integrable if ∫ |g| dμ is finite. The integrals of both nonnegative parts are then finite, and we define ∫ g dμ = ∫ g⁺ dμ − ∫ g⁻ dμ. If both were infinite, the subtraction ∞ − ∞ would be undefined. Our flow rate f is nonnegative, bounded and observed over a finite duration: its integrability is guaranteed. This does not justify any interchange of a limit and an integral; that is precisely the question for the next unit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I average the displayed flow rates and multiply by the whole duration.”

    The two flow rates do not last for the same duration. The correct average weights each by its share of time. The unweighted average gives 7 L here instead of 6.5 L.

    Review averaging by duration

  • “A is split into several parts, so its measure cannot be used.”

    A measure applies to measurable sets, including disjoint unions of intervals. Add the durations of the parts of A, then multiply by their common flow rate. The chronological check gives the same integral.

    Review the measure of A and the partition check

  • “A very large flow rate at one instant must add volume.”

    An isolated instant has duration measure 0. A finite value at that instant alone does not change the integral. A constant segment, however short, has positive duration: compare these two changes in the last exercise. The reasoning depends on the chosen measure.

    Review the isolated instant and the constant segment

Exercise 1 · Application and checking

The log separates the two periods of low flow

The log for the same pump keeps the three chronological intervals. Find their durations and volumes, then explain why grouping the two periods of low flow gives exactly the same total.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For each interval, subtract the start time from the end time, then multiply by the flow rate during that interval.

Hint 2

Compare low flow rate × (initial duration + final duration) with the sum of the two volumes delivered at low flow.

Compare with the solution

The durations are 1, 3 and 3 min. The volumes are respectively 0.5, 4.5 and 1.5 L. They total 6.5 L.

Since the periods of low flow are disjoint, their durations add: 1 + 3 = 4 min. Distributivity gives 0.5 + 1.5 = 0.5 × 4 = 2 L. The additivity of the measure allows us to change the representation without changing the integral.

Check your reasoning

  • Match each flow rate to the duration of the correct interval.
  • Recover the total volume in litres.
  • Justify grouping by disjointness and additivity, rather than merely saying that the numbers agree.

Exercise 2 · Change the measure and interpret

From volume to average flow rate

For every measurable set E in the same period, define ν(E) = μ(E)/μ(X). ν is a normalized measure, with no unit. Calculate ν(A), ν(B), then the average flow rate ∫ f dν. Compare it with the unweighted average of the two flow rate values and explain the volume discrepancy that this would produce.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The weights are durations divided by the total duration; check that their sum is 1.

Hint 2

The time average also equals the total volume divided by the total duration.

Compare with the solution

ν(A) = 4/7 = 4/7 and ν(B) = 3/7 = 3/7. Their sum is 1. The time units cancel: these weights are not minutes.

The average flow rate is 1/2 × 4/7 + 3/2 × 3/7 = 2/7 + 9/14 = 13/14 L/min, or approximately 0.929 L/min. Independent check: 6.5 L / 7 min = 13/14 L/min. Division by duration is used to calculate the average; it was not part of the volume calculation.

The unweighted average gives (0.5 + 1.5)/2 = 1 L/min. It gives equal weight to the two flow rates despite their different durations. It would predict 7 L, which is 0.5 L too much. This is not a performance measurement: it is the exact error of this rule in our fictional example.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain weights with no unit whose sum is 1.
  • Keep the exact fractions until the final approximation.
  • Distinguish volume, in litres, from average flow rate, in litres per minute.
  • Link the incorrect result to the wrong choice of weights.

Exercise 3 · Reason about a change

An isolated instant or a constant segment?

We change the fictional schedule by setting the flow rate to 10 L/min only at t = 2 min. Everything else stays the same. Does the volume change? Compare this with another change: the flow rate is 10 L/min throughout the interval [2, 2.5) min. Calculate the new volume in this case. These are ordinary flow rate functions, with no instantaneous injection of liquid.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The Lebesgue measure of a singleton is 0. A large finite value multiplied by duration 0 adds no volume.

Hint 2

The constant segment replaces part of the high flow period. Calculate the extra flow rate, then multiply it by the duration being replaced.

Compare with the solution

A singleton is a set containing a single point. Here μ({2}) = 0 min. The contribution of this point, both before and after the change, is 0: even 10 × 0 = 0 L. The volume remains 6.5 L. The functions are equal almost everywhere: this means that they differ only on a set of measure 0. They have the same integral with respect to μ.

On the constant segment, the duration is 2.5 − 2 = 0.5 min. It lies entirely within B: the previous flow rate there was 1.5 L/min. The extra flow rate is 10 − 1.5 = 8.5 L/min. Hence 17/2 × 1/2 = 17/4 L, or 4.25 L added, giving 6.5 + 4.25 = 10.75 L in total.

Adding the entire volume of the new constant segment to the initial total would count the flow already present twice. Finally, a measure assigning mass to the isolated instant could give a different result: “almost everywhere” always refers to an explicitly chosen measure.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify invariance for the isolated point by its measure of 0, while acknowledging that the value of f changes at that instant.
  • Identify the flow rate being replaced in B and calculate only the extra contribution.
  • Give a volume in litres and specify the measure used.
  • Do not equate a finite value at a point with an instantaneous injection of volume.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you calculate the integral of a nonnegative simple function?

On a measurable partition, add each value multiplied by the measure of the corresponding part. Combining or dividing parts with the same value does not change the result, thanks to the additivity of the measure.

Recall 2Which supremum defines the integral of a nonnegative measurable function?

The supremum of the integrals of all nonnegative simple functions s such that 0 ≤ s ≤ f. It may equal +∞. It is not the maximum of the values of f.

Recall 3What must you check before subtracting the positive and negative parts of a real function?

For Lebesgue integrability, the integral of the absolute value must be finite. The integrals of both parts are then finite, so their difference is defined. The expression ∞ − ∞ does not define an integral.

Recall 4Why does changing a finite flow rate at a single instant leave our volume unchanged?

That instant has duration measure 0. The two flow rates are equal almost everywhere for Lebesgue measure and therefore have the same integral. The conclusion depends on the measure and assumes an ordinary flow rate here, with no instantaneous injection.

Recall 5What is the difference between the volume and the average flow rate of our pump?

The volume is the integral of the flow rate with respect to the duration measure μ, in litres. The average flow rate uses the normalized measure ν(E) = μ(E)/μ(X), with no unit; it equals the volume divided by the duration and is expressed in L/min.

Choose the measure behind an average

What you understand
The integral depends as much on the values as on the measure that weights them. Our pump gives a volume with the duration measure and an average flow rate with its normalization. Averaging data rows chooses yet another weighting.
