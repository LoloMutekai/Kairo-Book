EM

✿01 See the idea

A drawer holds two biased coins, A and B. Each batch of tosses used just one of the two coins, but the batch label has been lost. We have two batches of 2 tosses: the first has 1 head, the second 2 heads. All values on this page are fictional; they are used for calculations, not to describe a real coin.

The problem has two halves. If we knew which coin produced each batch, estimating each coin’s probability of heads would be a matter of counting. If we knew these probabilities, we could judge which coin was more likely to have produced each batch. We know neither. The EM algorithm, short for expectation followed by maximization, handles these two halves in turn.

In k-means, each point is assigned to a single group. Here, an ambiguous batch can be split: its responsibility, written r, is the share of the batch assigned to group A, a number between 0 and 1. Group B receives the remainder, 1 − r. This share is calculated using the current parameters; it is not an observation.

The E-step calculates these shares for all batches, with the parameters fixed. The M-step re-estimates each coin using a frequency of heads weighted by these shares, with the shares fixed. We then repeat. Between iterations, we check that the likelihood, meaning the probability of the observed sequences under the model, does not decrease.

What this procedure does not promise: an increasing likelihood guarantees neither the best possible parameter setting nor that the model describes the world. To keep the fractions exact, each group here follows a coin-toss distribution. In a Gaussian mixture, the Gaussian density replaces the probability of the sequence, and the weighted average, together with a weighted variance, replaces the weighted frequency; the alternation between E and M stays the same.

The rule to remember

E-step: r = weight of A ÷ (weight of A + weight of B), batch by batch. M-step: new probability = weighted heads ÷ weighted tosses. Check: the likelihood index does not decrease.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A complete iteration on two batches with hidden coin identities

Fictional starting point: A gives heads with probability a = 3/5, B with probability b = 1/5. Each batch comes from A or B with the same weight, one over 2, known and not re-estimated. Data: batch 1 has 1 head in 2 tosses, batch 2 has 2 heads in 2 tosses. Carry out one EM iteration, then check it.

  1. Define the quantities and assumptions

    The probabilities a and b are dimensionless numbers between 0 and 1. The integer n = 2 is the number of tosses in a batch, N = 2 the number of batches, and h the number of heads in a batch. Assumptions: each batch comes from a single coin; its tosses are mutually independent once the coin is known; the choice of coin is independent from one batch to the next; A and B each have weight one half.

    For batch i, the raw weight of A is the probability that a coin with probability a produces this exact sequence. The number of ways to arrange the heads in the batch is the same for A and B; it cancels in the shares and does not change the likelihood comparison, so we do not write it.

    uA,i=ahi×(1−a)n−hi

    The raw weight of B has the same form with b. The responsibility r of batch i is the probability of A given the batch, under the current parameters. The equal weights of one half cancel in the ratio:

    ri=uA,iuA,i+uB,i

  2. E-step for batch 1

    Batch 1 contains 1 head and 1 tail. The raw weight of A is a × (1 − a), because the probability of tails is the complement of the probability of heads: 3/5 × 2/5 = 6/25. The weight of B is 1/5 × 4/5 = 4/25. Their sum is 2/5. The share of A is the weight of A divided by this sum:

    r1=6/252/5=3/5

    The value 3/5, or 0.6 in decimal notation, says that this batch with one head favors A slightly, without a definite assignment: B keeps the complement.

  3. E-step for batch 2

    Batch 2 contains 2 heads and no tails. The raw weight of A is a × a = 9/25, the weight of B is b × b = 1/25, and their sum is 2/5.

    r2=9/252/5=9/10

    Two heads are much more plausible for the coin that gives heads with probability 3/5 than for the coin that does so with 1/5: the share of A rises to 0.9. This share depends on the starting parameters; it does not tell us which coin was actually used.

  4. M-step for A: a weighted count

    If the shares were certainties, A would have received exactly the batches with share 1, and we would count its heads and tosses. With shares, we weight each batch. Batches assigned to A, in shares: 3/5 + 9/10 = 3/2. Expected heads from A: 3/5 × 1 + 9/10 × 2 = 3/5 + 9/5 = 12/5. Weighted tosses for A: 2 × 3/2 = 3.

    a′=∑irihin∑iri
    a′=12/53=4/5

    This weighted frequency is the value of a that maximizes the following weighted log-likelihood, found by setting its derivative to 0:

    QA(a)=∑iri[hilna+(n−hi)ln(1−a)]

    We do not detail the derivation; calculs.py only checks that the derivative is indeed 0 at a′.

  5. M-step for B: the remainder

    B receives the complementary shares. Batches for B, in shares: 2 − 3/2 = 1/2. Total observed heads: 1 + 2 = 3; the heads for B are those that A did not receive: 3 − 12/5 = 3/5. Weighted tosses for B: 2 × 1/2 = 1.

    b′=3/51=3/5

    Check: the heads for A and B, 12/5 + 3/5, give 3 again; no head is lost or created. This equality holds by construction here; calculs.py therefore also recalculates hb directly, by summing (1 − r) × h over the two batches, and obtains the same number. We move from a = 3/5 and b = 1/5 to a′ = 4/5 and b′ = 3/5.

  6. Check using the likelihood index

    To compare two parameter settings, we use the index Λ, the product over batches of the sum of the raw weights of A and B. The likelihood of the two sequences is this index multiplied by a constant factor, the weight of one half for each batch, which depends on neither a nor b: comparing the indices is sufficient.

    Λ=∏i(uA,i+uB,i)

    Before the iteration:

    Λ=2/5×2/5=4/25

    After the iteration, with 1 − a′ = 1/5 and 1 − b′ = 2/5. Batch 1: 4/5 × 1/5 + 3/5 × 2/5 = 4/25 + 6/25 = 2/5. Batch 2: 4/5 × 4/5 + 3/5 × 3/5 = 16/25 + 9/25 = 1.

    Λ′=2/5×1=2/5

    Since 0.16 < 0.40, the index has increased, as EM theory guarantees: under these assumptions, it never decreases. A decrease would indicate a calculation error or a violated assumption.

  7. Interpret the result with care

    One iteration has moved a from 3/5 to 4/5 and b from 1/5 to 3/5. We need to repeat E then M until the parameters and the index barely change; the next iteration is the subject of the first exercise.

    Why the index does not decrease: the E-step constructs a lower bound on the log-likelihood that touches it at the current parameters, and the M-step increases this lower bound, so the actual likelihood is at least as high. We do not prove this lower bound here.

    Limitations: two batches do not justify two groups; an increasing index proves neither a global optimum nor that the model is true; another starting point may lead elsewhere. Choosing the number of groups will be covered in the next unit.

Explain it in your own words

Explain why the batch with one head receives a less decisive share than the batch with two heads. Then say what would change if both coins had the same probability of heads.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “EM always ends at the best possible parameter setting.”

    Each iteration increases or preserves the likelihood, but the final point depends on the starting point. A symmetric start even stays stuck: check this in the exercise on symmetry.

    Repeat the exercise on symmetry

  • “To re-estimate A, average the heads in the batches where the share of A exceeds one half.”

    That would be a single assignment, as in k-means. EM keeps all batches, weighted by their shares: weighted heads divided by weighted tosses, here 12/5 ÷ 3 = 4/5.

    Revisit the M-step

  • “If the likelihood index increases, the model is true.”

    The index measures the fit under the chosen model: here from 0.16 to 0.40 on just two batches. It tells us neither that two groups exist nor that the toss distribution is correct.

    Review the card on the check

Compare with the solution

Batch 1: 4/5 × 1/5 = 4/25 for A; 3/5 × 2/5 = 6/25 for B; sum 2/5; share of A: 4/25 ÷ 2/5 = 2/5. Batch 2: 16/25 for A, 9/25 for B, sum 1; share of A: 16/25 ÷ 1 = 16/25.

The two groups move closer together: with just two batches, the data do little to distinguish the coins. This is not a calculation error.

Check your reasoning

  • Raw weights calculated with a′ and b′, rather than the starting values.
  • Share of A equal to the weight of A divided by the sum of both weights.
  • Comparison with the shares in the example, without concluding that the model is true.

Exercise 3 · Analysis of a limiting case

A symmetric starting point

Start again with the same batches and weights of one half, but with a = b = c, where c is any common value strictly between 0 and 1. Show that all shares equal one half, calculate a′ and b′ after the M-step, then say what this implies for EM.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For the same batch, compare the raw weights of A and B when a = b.

Hint 2

With shares of one half, A and B each receive half the heads and half the tosses.

Hint 3

The result does not depend on c.

Compare with the solution

When a = b, each batch has the same raw weight for A and B, so the share is one half for each batch, whatever c is.

A then receives half the heads and half the tosses: the halves cancel, and a′ is the overall frequency of heads, 3 ÷ 4 = 3/4. B receives the same split: b′ = 3/4.

Since a′ = b′, the raw weights remain equal at the next iteration, the shares remain one half, and the same calculation repeats: EM stays stuck and does not separate the groups. The lesson is that a starting point must break symmetry; it says nothing about the likelihood value at this point.

Check your reasoning

  • Equality of the raw weights of A and B when a = b.
  • Shares equal to one half for all batches.
  • Overall frequency calculated, independent of c.
  • Limited conclusion: the starting point must be asymmetric to separate the groups.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the responsibility r of a batch represent?

The share of the batch assigned to group A under the current parameters: the raw weight of A divided by the sum of the raw weights of A and B. It lies between 0 and 1, and B receives the complement.

Recall 2What do the E-step and M-step do?

E calculates each group’s share for each batch with the parameters fixed. M re-estimates each group using counts weighted by these shares, with the shares fixed.

Recall 3What numerical check follows an EM iteration, and what does it prove?

The likelihood index must not decrease; here it moves from 0.16 to 0.40. It proves neither a global optimum nor that the model is true.

Recall 4Why is a symmetric starting point a trap?

If a = b, all shares equal one half and a′ = b′; the same calculation repeats, so EM does not separate the groups.

Recall 5How does a responsibility differ from a k-means assignment?

k-means assigns each point to a single group; EM keeps a split between the groups, with a share between 0 and 1.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the procedure: name the hidden groups and set a starting point; E-step, calculate each group’s share for each batch; M-step, re-estimate each group using counts weighted by these shares; recalculate the likelihood index and check that it does not decrease; repeat until stable; keep the starting point, local optima and model limitations in mind.
