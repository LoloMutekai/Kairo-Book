Level 2 · C044·M3

Softmax, temperature and sampling

Your goalTurn scores into probabilities, predict the effect of temperature and identify the token selected by a given draw.

Before you start
c029-m5 : Bayes · c030-m5 : Poisson · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c018-m5 : contract tests and test doubles · c021-m5 : origin, authentication and authorization · c044-m1 : tokens and text segmentation · c044-m2 : next-token distribution

✿01 See the idea

The text stops after “In the garden, the”. In our small fictional model, the only possible continuations are “cat”, “dog” and “wind”. Each is represented by a token, including its leading space. This closed vocabulary is used for the calculation; a real model has other tokens and a different way of segmenting text.

Imagine a strip divided among these continuations. The portion for “cat” is the widest, but a pointer placed at random can land in “dog”. The distribution gives the chances of each continuation; the draw selects the one that is actually added to the text.

Softmax builds this strip from scores: it turns each score into a positive weight, then divides the total among the candidates. Temperature controls the differences between the weights. Lowering it favors the highest score more strongly; raising it brings the portions closer in size, without reversing the order of the scores.

All the values below are fictional. We examine only the next token, with the same scores and all candidates retained. Temperature is a setting with no unit and has nothing to do with degrees. A likely continuation is no proof of truth.

The rule to remember

Divide the scores by the temperature, take their exponentials, divide each weight by the total, then locate the draw in the cumulative intervals.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Choosing what follows “In the garden, the”

The given scores are z_chat = ln(9), z_chien = ln(4) and z_vent = 0 = ln(1). We will build the distribution at T = 1, then use u = 7/10 = 0.70. These data are chosen to give exact fractions; they do not come from measurements of any model.

  1. Define the objects and assumptions

    V is the set of 3 allowed tokens, in the order cat, dog, wind. i and j each denote a token in V. zᵢ is its finite real score, sometimes called a logit; this score is not yet a probability. T is the temperature, strictly greater than 0. The text and scores stay fixed throughout this comparison.

    exp is the natural exponential function; ln is its inverse: exp(ln a) = a for positive a. In particular, exp(0) = 1. The scores, T, weights, probabilities and draw all have no unit. Their values are read neither as degrees nor as confidence in the truth of the sentence.

    u denotes a realized value from a uniform draw on [0, 1): intervals of equal length have equal probability. We fix its value so we can check the selection by hand. This page does not perform any random sampling.

  2. Go from score to weight, then to probability

    wᵢ is the positive weight exp(zᵢ/T). Z is the sum of these weights over all allowed tokens. The symbol Σ means to add a term for each j in V. pᵢ(T) is then the probability of selecting i at this temperature.

    wi=exp(ziT);Z=Σj∈Vwj;pi(T)=wiZ

    The exponential function makes the weights strictly positive, even if a score is negative. Dividing by their sum makes their total equal to 1. This is softmax with temperature. Dividing the scores directly by their sum would not provide these guarantees.

    Shifting all scores by the same amount does not change the probabilities: if we subtract the same number m, each weight is multiplied by exp(−m/T). This common factor cancels with the one in the denominator. Choosing the largest score as m avoids computing excessively large exponentials; it preserves the same distribution.

  3. Calculate the weights for our text

    At T = 1, dividing by T leaves each score unchanged. The exponentials undo the logarithms chosen in the data: weight of cat = exp(ln(9)) = 9; weight of dog = 4; weight of wind = exp(0) = 1.

    Add all retained candidates, since each will occupy a portion of the strip: Z = 9 + 4 + 1 = 13 + 1 = 14. The weights express a relative allocation, rather than probabilities that have already been normalized.

  4. Divide by the total and check

    Each token’s portion is its weight divided by 14. We obtain p_chat = 9/14 ≈ 0.6429, p_chien = 2/7 ≈ 0.2857 and p_vent = 1/14 ≈ 0.0714. All these probabilities have no unit; the decimals are only for readability.

    914+414+114=1414=1

    Independent check: the ratio of the probabilities of cat and dog is 9/4, equal to the ratio of the weights 9/4. Normalization has preserved their ratio and order. Keep the fractions, rather than their rounded values, when deciding near a boundary.

  5. Build the intervals and read the draw

    Accumulate the portions in the stated order. Cat occupies [0, 9/14), dog [9/14, 13/14), wind [13/14, 1). The left endpoint is included and the right endpoint excluded: a boundary therefore belongs to the next token. This convention leaves no gaps or overlapping assignments.

    Our u = 7/10 satisfies 9/14 < 7/10 < 13/14. Check without rounding: 9 × 10 < 7 × 14 < 13 × 10. Since the denominators are positive, these multiplications preserve the direction of the inequalities. The token added is therefore dog.

    Cat was the most likely, but was not selected. Always taking the highest score is another rule, called maximum selection. Sampling from the distribution preserves a chance for the other candidates. A single draw does not measure long-term frequencies.

  6. Lower the temperature while keeping scores fixed

    Take T = 1/2 = 0.5. Dividing a score by this fraction is equivalent to multiplying it by 2. The identity exp(2 ln a) = a squared therefore gives the new weights 81, 16 and 1. The new total is 97 + 1 = 98; we must normalize again.

    The portions become 81/98 ≈ 0.8265, 8/49 ≈ 0.1633 and 1/98 ≈ 0.0102, still with no unit. Their exact sum is 1. The cat/dog ratio is now 81/16, the square of 9/4: the former is more strongly favored.

    The same u = 0.70 now belongs to [0, 81/98): cat would be selected. Keeping u fixed isolates the effect of T in this comparison; this does not describe two outputs that were actually observed. The scores have not changed and the page has not retrained anything.

  7. State the limits of what temperature guarantees

    For positive T, dividing by T and taking the exponential are increasing operations: they preserve the order of the scores. As T increases, the ratio of the probabilities of a higher score and a lower score approaches 1; the portions therefore become closer in size.

    With fixed scores and no candidates removed, as T grows without limit the distribution approaches equal portions. As T approaches 0 through positive values, it concentrates on the highest score; if several tokens tie for the maximum, they share the limiting probability. The formula contains a division by T: never substitute 0 for T in it. A software setting with this name may specify a separate rule, which must be checked in its implementation.

    Temperature controls this allocation, rather than the truth of the continuations. Our closed vocabulary and sampling without any other filter are assumptions for the calculation. A generation policy containing other rules must be studied with those rules before this exact distribution can be attributed to it.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The weights 9, 4 and 1 are already the probabilities.”

    Their sum is 14, rather than 1. Divide each weight by the total. Then check the sum of the probabilities and the cat/dog ratio: these checks detect inconsistent normalization.

    Repeat the division and its checks

  • “A higher temperature makes cat even more certain.”

    With fixed scores, a higher temperature brings the portions closer in size. The last exercise lets you recover the weights using roots. In every case, a generation probability does not certify the content.

    Test the higher temperature

  • “Cat has the best score: the draw must select cat.”

    The highest score identifies the largest portion. The result of the draw depends on the interval containing u: at T = 1 and u = 7/10, this is dog. Always choosing the maximum would remove this draw.

    Compare the strips and the position of the line

The weights are 3, 2, 1, because their squares give the initial weights. The total is 5 + 1 = 6. The probabilities are 1/2, 1/3 and 1/6, with no unit. Their exact sum is 1.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What chain turns a score into a probability, and why is a total needed?

For strictly positive T, the weight is exp(score/T). Divide each weight by the sum of all retained weights: the probabilities are positive and their sum is 1. A score or weight alone is not a probability.

Recall 2With fixed scores, what changes and what stays the same when T is lowered?

The allocation concentrates on the best scores, without changing their order or the given scores. Here, going from T = 1 to 1/2 replaces the weights with their squares. The total must be recalculated. T has no unit and does not measure truth.

Recall 3How can you identify the selected token from the probabilities and a uniform u?

Accumulate the probabilities in a fixed order to divide [0, 1). Select the interval containing u, with the left endpoint included and the right endpoint excluded. Its length is the token’s probability: the most likely token is therefore not always selected.

Recall 4Why can you not replace T with 0 in the formula, even if software accepts this setting?

The formula divides by T and requires positive T. Its limit as T approaches 0 concentrates on the highest scores, but a 0 software setting requires a separate rule whose implementation must be read.

Recall 5What does a high probability for the next token fail to prove?

It does not prove that the continuation is true. It describes the generator’s selection under a given text, scores and policy. The truth of the content and the actual effect of a setting require other evidence.
