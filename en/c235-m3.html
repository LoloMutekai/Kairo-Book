Level 4 · C235·M3

Wiener chaos

Your goalDecompose a quadratic response to Gaussian noise into orthogonal chaos components, calculate its projections and justify truncation in mean square.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A fictional sensor turns a small Gaussian perturbation into a voltage. Its response includes a fixed offset, a part proportional to the perturbation and a curvature term. If the perturbation changes sign, the proportional part changes sign; the squared part stays the same. This curvature therefore also shifts the mean.

To separate the effects, subtract the mean of the squared part from that part. What remains fluctuates around 0. The voltage then splits into a mean, a linear fluctuation and a centered quadratic fluctuation. In the Gaussian model, the two fluctuations are orthogonal: the mean of their product is 0. Their contributions to the variance add up.

Here the word chaos refers to a family of components classified by order. It describes neither disorder over time nor a sequence of independent draws. All the components depend on the same perturbation; orthogonality concerns the mean of their product.

The whole page uses this same sensor. Its response law and values are fictional. We calculate expectations under an assumed distribution, without presenting them as measured voltages or as a validated physical model.

The rule to remember

Center the polynomials under the Gaussian distribution, project using their squared norms, then add the energies of the orthogonal components.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Decompose the sensor’s curvature

Let Z be a dimensionless centered Gaussian variable with variance 1. The response is Y = a + bZ + cZ squared, with a = 5 V, b = 2 V and c = 1/2 V, or 0.50 V. We seek the chaos components, their variance and the error of an affine approximation.

  1. Set out the distribution and the space

    E denotes expectation under the model’s Gaussian distribution. We use E[Z] = 0, E[Z squared] = 1 and E[Z to the power 4] = 3; the odd moments are 0 by symmetry. The last moment follows from Gaussian integration by parts: for a positive integer r, E[Z to the power r + 1] = r E[Z to the power r − 1]. These identities concern a standard normal distribution, rather than arbitrary noise with 0 mean.

    The space L squared consists of square-integrable variables, meaning those for which E[U squared] is finite. Its dot product is E[UV] for two real variables U and V; its squared norm is E[U squared]. For voltages, this product and this squared norm are in V². Orthogonality means E[UV] = 0; if U and V are centered, this also means 0 covariance.

    To connect the scene to the noise in C235·M1, let H denote a real separable Hilbert space of dimensionless directions. An isonormal Gaussian noise W is a linear map from H to centered Gaussian variables such that E[W(h)W(g)] = ⟨h,g⟩H. The letters h and g are directions, and ⟨·,·⟩H is their dot product. Choose h with norm 1 and set Z = W(h). The sensor depends only on this direction.

  2. Replace the raw square with a centered polynomial

    We use the probabilists’ convention for the Hermite polynomials Hen, without normalization to unit norm. The index n is their order, a nonnegative integer. Their definition by a generating series is as follows; t and x are dimensionless real numbers, n! is the factorial and 0! = 1.

    exp(tx−t22)=∑n=0∞Hen(x)tnn!

    In other words, the coefficient of t to the power n in this exponential is Hen(x)/n!. Comparing coefficients gives He of order 0 equal to 1, He of order 1 equal to x and He of order 2 equal to x squared − 1. To continue the construction, we can use He of order n + 1 equal to x Hen(x) − n He of order n − 1, for n at least 1.

    He0(Z)=1;He1(Z)=Z;He2(Z)=Z2−1

    The raw square therefore contains a constant and a centered polynomial. Indeed, E[He of order 2(Z)] = 1 − 1 = 0. Its product with Z also has 0 expectation, because it contains only odd powers. Its squared norm is 3 − 2 × 1 + 1 = 2, or 2!. The squared norm of Z is 1. The polynomials are orthogonal, but the one of order 2 does not have squared norm 1.

    The general factorial also follows from the generating series. For two dimensionless real numbers s and t, the expectation of the product exp(sZ − s squared/2) exp(tZ − t squared/2) is exp(st), by the exponential moment formula for the standard normal distribution. Comparing the coefficients of s to the power m and t to the power n gives E[Hem(Z) Hen(Z)] = 0 if m and n differ, and n! if they are equal. Here m and n are nonnegative integers. This proves the orthogonality and norm used in the projections.

  3. Define chaos and specify what converges

    In the space generated by Z alone, the Hen(Z) form a complete orthogonal basis of L squared for its distribution. For every square-integrable response U that is a function of Z, there is therefore a unique series U = sum of an Hen(Z), for n starting at 0. Equality and convergence hold in mean square: the expectation of the squared difference between U and the partial sums tends to 0. They do not promise uniform convergence over all values of Z.

    For the full noise W, the chaos of order n, denoted Cn, is the closure of the linear combinations of Hen(W(g)) as g ranges over directions of norm 1. The closure adds their limits in mean square. C of order 0 is the space of constants; chaos spaces of distinct orders are orthogonal. With several directions, a chaos space generally does not reduce to a single polynomial in a single variable.

    The decomposition theorem states that every square-integrable variable U measurable with respect to σ(W) is the unique orthogonal sum of its projections JnU onto the Cn. The notation σ(W) denotes the information generated by all the variables W(g). An independent random source added to the model does not automatically admit an expansion in these chaos spaces.

    The connection with C235·M2 uses the symmetric tensor power n of H: here H⊙n denotes the subspace of tensors invariant under permutation, equipped with the inherited tensor dot product. The map In sends a symmetric tensor f to its chaos component. Our convention is In(h⊗n) = Hen(W(h)) if h has norm 1, and I of order 0 acts as the identity on scalars. For two tensors f and g of the same order n, it satisfies:

    E[In(f)In(g)]=n!⟨f,g⟩

    The symbol h⊗n is the tensor product of n copies of h; the dot product in the formula is that of H⊙n. Each JnU can be written as In(fn) with a unique deterministic symmetric kernel fn. The full decomposition is written U = sum of In(fn). For a voltage U and dimensionless directions, the kernels carry volts and n! times their squared norm carries V². We will use only the orders present in the sensor’s polynomial.

  4. Calculate the coefficients by projection

    In our single direction, the coefficient an is obtained by taking the dot product with Hen(Z), then dividing by its squared norm, n!. The products with the other orders vanish. This coefficient carries volts; the Hermite polynomial is dimensionless.

    an=E[YHen(Z)]n!

    Order 0: E[Y] = 5 + 2 × 0 + 1/2 × 1 = 11/2 V, or 5.50 V. The curvature contributes to this mean, even though Z is centered.

    Order 1: E[YZ] = a E[Z] + b E[Z squared] + c E[Z cubed] = 2 V. Dividing by 1! gives a of order 1 = 2 V.

    Order 2: E[Y(Z squared − 1)] = c(E[Z to the power 4] − E[Z squared]) = 1/2 × (3 − 1) = 1 V. The terms in a and b vanish by centering and symmetry. Dividing by 2! = 2 gives a of order 2 = 1/2 V. The dot product 1 V is not itself the coefficient. The higher orders are 0, since Y already belongs to the sum of these orthogonal spaces.

  5. Reconstruct the voltage and its kernels

    Y=5.5+2Z+12(Z2−1) V

    The three components are J of order 0 Y = 11/2 V, J of order 1 Y = 2 Z V and J of order 2 Y = 1/2 (Z squared − 1) V. The corresponding kernels are f of order 0 = 11/2 V, f of order 1 = 2 h V and f of order 2 = 1/2 (h⊗h) V. The tensor h⊗h is already symmetric and its norm is 1.

    Algebraic check: expanding the final parentheses subtracts 1/2 V from the constant 11/2 V. This recovers 5 V, followed by the terms bZ and cZ squared. In particular, I of order 2(h⊗h) is Z squared − 1, whereas W(h) squared is Z squared: the centering adjustment is essential.

  6. Add the variances and check by another method

    The constant component adds no variance. Order 1 contributes b squared × 1! = 4 V². Order 2 contributes c squared × 2! = 1/4 × 2 = 1/2 V². The centered components are orthogonal, so:

    Var(Y)=4+0.50=4.50 V²

    Another method is to expand Y squared directly in ordinary powers of Z. The odd terms vanish under expectation. What remains is a squared + b squared + 2ac + 3c squared = 139/4 V², or 34.75 V². Subtracting the squared mean, 121/4 V², gives 9/2 V² again. This check uses Gaussian moments rather than only adding the energies of the chaos components.

    In the general decomposition U = sum of In(fn), the same Pythagorean identity gives E[U squared] = sum of n! times the squared norm of fn. The variance excludes the term of order 0. Convergence is understood through these sums with finite total energy; we distinguish variance from the uncentered moment.

  7. Truncate while accounting for the remaining error

    Keeping only the orders up to 1 gives the affine approximation Ŷ = 11/2 + 2 Z, in volts. The difference R = Y − Ŷ is exactly the component of order 2, with mean 0 and energy 0.50 V². Thus E[(Y − Ŷ) squared] = 1/2 V². This is not a bound on the error of each reading.

    Since R is orthogonal to every constant and to Z, Ŷ minimizes this mean error among functions α + βZ, where α and β are deterministic coefficients in volts. The transfer exercise asks you to prove this minimum rather than assume it. In a general series, truncation at order N leaves the energy given by the sum of n! times the squared norm of fn for orders n strictly greater than N; N is a nonnegative integer.

    The mean is preserved, but the variance of the approximation becomes 4 V². The variance difference 0.50 V² is exactly the energy removed in this model. No measure of a real sensor’s accuracy follows from this.

Without rereading, explain why the square of Z is not a pure chaos component, where the factorial in the projection comes from and why truncation preserves the mean but can remove variance. Then repeat the check using ordinary powers.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The square of the noise belongs entirely to the second chaos.”

    Its mean is 1. Write Z squared = 1 + (Z squared − 1): the constant belongs to order 0, and the remainder to order 2. Expand the reconstruction to check the shift in the sensor’s mean.

    Revisit centering and the kernels

  • “The coefficient is always the dot product with the polynomial.”

    This rule would assume a basis of unit norm. In our convention, the squared norm of Heₙ(Z) is n!. Therefore divide the dot product by n!; the case of order 2 makes the missing factor concrete.

    Repair the projection in the second exercise

  • “Orthogonal chaos components are independent noises.”

    Z and Z squared − 1 have a product with 0 mean, but the latter is a function of the former. In particular, knowing Z determines its centered square. Independence does not follow from 0 covariance for these variables, which are not jointly Gaussian.

    Review the branches from the same Z

Exercise 1 · Application · distinguish value from mean

A particular value of the perturbation

Evaluate the sensor function at z = 2, which is dimensionless. Calculate its voltage using the initial formula and then using the chaos components. Also calculate the affine approximation Ŷ, its error and the square of that error. Why can this last number exceed 0.50 V²?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate z squared and He of order 2(z).

Hint 2

A particular error value is not an expectation over the entire distribution.

Compare with the solution

z squared = 4 and He of order 2(z) = 4 − 1 = 3. From the initial response, the linear part is 2 × 2 = 4 V, and the quadratic part is 1/2 × 4 = 2 V; hence Y(z) = 5 + 4 + 2 = 11 V.

Using the chaos components, Ŷ(z) = 5.5 + 4 = 9.5 V and the remaining component is 1/2 × 3 = 1.5 V. The sum 9.5 + 1.5 = 11 V recovers the initial voltage.

The error Y(z) − Ŷ(z) is 1.5 V and its square is 2.25 V². The mean squared error remains 0.50 V²: a mean is not a maximum. This evaluation of a polynomial is neither an observed draw nor an empirical estimate of the mean error.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover 11 V using both expressions.
  • Center the square before calculating the chaos component.
  • Distinguish 2.25 V² at this value from 0.50 V² on average.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis · repair a convention

The forgotten denominator

Someone directly uses E[Y He of order 2(Z)] = 1 V as the coefficient of the second chaos, keeping the other coefficients correct. Find the correct coefficient and compare the variance of the reconstructed response with that of Y. The Hermite polynomials keep our probabilists’ convention.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Ask what the squared norm of He of order 2(Z) is.

Hint 2

A chaos component’s contribution to the variance is the squared coefficient multiplied by the squared norm of the polynomial.

Compare with the solution

The correct coefficient is 1/2 = 1/2 V. The incorrect coefficient is 1 V. It multiplies a polynomial with squared norm 2, rather than squared norm 1.

With the incorrect coefficient, the energy of the second chaos is 1 × 2 = 2 V² and the total variance is 4 + 2 = 6 V². The true variance is 4.50 V². Centering preserves the same mean, but does not repair the incorrect amplitude.

Dividing by the factorial restores both the projection and the reconstruction of the initial polynomial. We could also work with a normalized Hermite basis; we would then have to change the polynomials and their coefficients together, never just remove a denominator.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify division by 2 using a norm, rather than merely reciting a factor.
  • Obtain 6 V² for the incorrect reconstruction and 4.50 V² for Y.
  • Explain why an unchanged mean is not enough to check a decomposition.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · proof on paper

Why is this affine approximation optimal?

Still using the same Y, take an approximation α + βZ, with α and β deterministic real numbers in volts. Express its mean squared error as a sum of squares and find the unique minimum. Then compare it with the approximation a + bZ, which simply removes the term cZ squared from the initial formula.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write Y = 11/2 + 2 Z + R, with E[R] = E[RZ] = 0.

Hint 2

Expand the square of (11/2 − α) + (2 − β)Z + R. The cross products have 0 expectation.

Compare with the solution

Orthogonality gives E[(Y − α − βZ) squared] = (11/2 V − α) squared + (2 V − β) squared + 1/2 V². The three terms have the same unit V², because E[Z squared] = 1. The deterministic squares are nonnegative.

The unique minimum is therefore attained at α = 5.50 V and β = 2 V. It is 0.50 V². Uniqueness follows because both squares must vanish. This proof establishes optimality among affine functions of Z under the stated distribution, without claiming observed accuracy.

Removing the raw quadratic term gives a + bZ. Its error is cZ squared, with mean c = 1/2 V. Its mean squared error is c squared E[Z to the power 4] = 1/4 × 3 = 3/4 V², or 0.75 V². The extra error 0.25 V² is the square of the mean bias. Keeping the chaos components up to order 1 already includes the curvature’s contribution to the mean.

Check your reasoning

  • Write why each cross product vanishes.
  • Find the minimum 0.50 V² and justify its uniqueness.
  • Recover 0.75 V² when the raw square is removed and explain the bias.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why is Z squared not a pure chaos component of order 2?

Under the standard normal distribution, its mean is 1. It splits into the constant 1 and He of order 2(Z) = Z squared − 1, which is centered and orthogonal to Z.

Recall 2How do we calculate the coefficient aₙ with our Hermite convention?

Divide E[Y Heₙ(Z)] by E[Heₙ(Z) squared] = n!. The polynomials are orthogonal, without all having unit norm. The sensor’s coefficients carry volts.

Recall 3What happens to h⊗h and the quadratic coefficient in the sensor’s decomposition?

With Z = W(h) and h of norm 1, I of order 2(h⊗h) = Z squared − 1. The kernel is 1/2 (h⊗h) V and its component has energy 2! × 1/4 = 1/2 V².

Recall 4What assumptions and what sense of convergence apply to the full decomposition?

The variable must be square-integrable and measurable with respect to the information from the Gaussian noise under consideration. The series of projections onto the chaos spaces converges in mean square, with no guarantee of uniform convergence over all values of the noise.

Recall 5What error does the affine truncation of Y leave, and what does this number fail to prove?

It leaves the second chaos, with energy 0.50 V². This is a mean squared error under the model, neither a bound on each error nor a measure of real accuracy. Orthogonality of the components does not mean independence.

What you understand
A variance can be split among orthogonal components when the model and centering justify it. For our fictional Gaussian sensor, the Hermite decomposition establishes this orthogonality. It does not follow merely from a program adding variances.
