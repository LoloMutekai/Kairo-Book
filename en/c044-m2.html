Level 2 · C044·M2

Next-token distribution

Your goalRead a next-token distribution for a fixed prefix, check its sum and distinguish the probability of a continuation from the truth of the text.

Before you start
c029-m5 : Bayes · c030-m5 : Poisson · c010-m5 : Logarithmic scales · c018-m5 : Contract tests and test doubles · c021-m5 : Origin, authentication and authorization · c044-m1 : Tokens and text tokenization

✿01 See the idea

You see a cup and start a sentence: “The tea is”. Before writing the continuation, a language model assigns probabilities to the tokens that could come next. A distribution is this complete list of possibilities together with their probabilities. It exists before a token is selected.

To see the idea without a huge vocabulary, let us invent a miniature model whose only possible tokens at the next position are ␠hot, ␠cold and ␠lukewarm. The ␠ symbol makes the leading space included in each token visible; the symbol will not be written in the sentence. This tokenization and these values are fictional and have no connection to the vocabulary of any particular model.

The bar for ␠hot is the longest: this token receives the highest individual probability. Yet the other possibilities together have just as much probability. “Most probable” therefore does not mean “certain”. The rule that decides which token is written will be studied separately.

The distribution concerns the continuation of the text, rather than the cup itself. Even if the text becomes “The tea is hot”, these probabilities do not give the actual temperature of the tea. Finding that out would require an appropriate observation.

The rule to remember

For the same prefix and the same model, read all the possibilities, check their sum, then specify the event being discussed. A written token does not reveal the whole distribution.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Read the whole distribution after “The tea is”

Keep the prefix “The tea is” and the miniature model fixed. Its fictional distribution assigns 5/10 to ␠hot, 3/10 to ␠cold and 2/10 to ␠lukewarm. Let us check this distribution, then calculate the probability that the next token is ␠hot or ␠lukewarm under this distribution.

  1. Define the object being predicted

    c denotes the prefix, meaning the sequence of tokens already available before the next position. V denotes the fixed vocabulary of the miniature model: the set {␠hot, ␠cold, ␠lukewarm}. v denotes any token in this set. These are textual objects with no physical unit.

    T is the random variable “next token” under the model’s distribution. p(v | c) is the probability that T equals v, given c; the vertical bar is read as “given”. The model and the tokenization are held fixed. All probabilities are dimensionless: they are neither degrees nor numbers of tokens.

    p(v|c)=P(T=v|c)

    P denotes the probability of an event under this distribution. The rule that will produce the written token has not yet been specified: it can change how these probabilities are used. No training calculation or automatic selection is needed to read the given distribution.

  2. Put the values in a readable form

    The fractions are the data supplied in the exercise. Divide each numerator by the common denominator to obtain a decimal representation of the same probability. These numerators are not frequencies measured in a corpus.

    TokenProbabilité sans unité␠chaud510=0.50␠froid310=0.30␠tiède210=0.20

    The reduced fractions are 1/2, 3/10 and 1/5, respectively. The decimals displayed here are exact; keeping the fractions lets you check the operations without rounding.

  3. Check that the list forms a distribution

    Each probability must be between 0 and 1, including both bounds. The sum over all possibilities must equal 1. The symbol ∑ means “add up”; v ∈ V means “for each token v in vocabulary V”.

    0≤p(v|c)≤1;∑v∈Vp(v|c)=1

    Here, 0.50 + 0.30 = 0.80, then 0.80 + 0.20 = 1.00. An independent check using fractions: the sum of the numerators 5 + 3 + 2 equals the denominator 10. The sum is therefore indeed 1.

    This check requires an exhaustive list. In a real model, all tokens in the vocabulary must be counted, including tokens that mark the end of a sequence when they are part of that vocabulary. A list containing only the highest-ranked candidates can have a sum below 1 without the full distribution being incorrect.

  4. Combine the requested possibilities

    Call A the event “T is ␠hot or ␠lukewarm”. At this position, T cannot be both tokens at once. The events are disjoint: their probabilities add up. A concerns the text, without any judgment about what would be a pleasant temperature.

    P(A|c)=510+210=0.70

    The exact result is 7/10, or 0.70, with no unit. We add because these are alternatives at the same position; no assumption of independence between successive positions is involved.

  5. Check using the complement

    Our vocabulary contains no other tokens. Not belonging to A therefore means being ␠cold. Its probability is 0.30. Subtracting this possibility from the total gives 1 − 0.30 = 0.70 again.

    Another check: 0.70 + 0.30 = 1.00. If this equality failed, we would need to look for a possibility that was omitted, counted more than once or associated with another prefix. The completeness of the vocabulary is what allows reasoning by complement.

  6. State exactly what the result allows us to conclude

    The token ␠hot is individually the most probable, because 0.50 exceeds 0.30 and 0.20. It is not certain under the distribution: its probability is below 1. Group A reaches 0.70, but this group is not itself a token to be written.

    If ␠cold is selected, the result is “The tea is cold”. Continuing after this text will require a distribution conditioned on this new prefix. The previous table does not provide the probabilities at that next position. Nor does it let us deduce the actual temperature of the tea.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the table. Reconstruct the fractions and justify the addition that gives the probability of A, then the check using its complement. Why would the appearance of ␠cold allow you neither to recover the whole table nor to measure the tea’s temperature?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The most probable token will definitely be written.”

    Here the highest probability is 0.50, below 1. The distribution still includes other possibilities; the output also depends on the selection rule. Looking only at the ranking does not establish a certain selection.

    Reread the interpretation of the result

  • “0.50 means that the tea has this probability of actually being hot.”

    The variable T denotes a text token. The distribution is neither a reading of the cup’s temperature nor a probability that the whole sentence is true. Naming the object being predicted prevents us from changing the question along the way.

    Return to the cup scene

  • “If I display only the best tokens, their probabilities must already sum to 1.”

    The sum is 1 over the complete vocabulary. Omitting ␠lukewarm leaves 0.80. Rescaling the remaining values to sum to 1 would describe a restricted distribution, which must be identified as such.

Exercise 1 · Reading followed by calculation

The next token is not ␠hot

Keep the distribution and prefix from the example. Give the probability that T is not ␠hot. Find it again using another operation, then explain why this result does not promise regular alternation between tokens.

Hint 1

The total is 1. Subtract the probability of ␠hot.

Hint 2

The only other possibilities are ␠cold and ␠lukewarm. You can add their probabilities.

Compare with the solution

By complement: 1 − 0.50 = 0.50. By addition: 0.30 + 0.20 = 0.50. The methods agree because the vocabulary is complete and the alternatives are disjoint.

A probability does not impose an alternating order of outcomes. Moreover, no procedure for repeated selection has been specified here. We have calculated a property of the distribution at a fixed prefix, without predicting a particular sequence.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain 1/2, with no unit, by complement and by addition.
  • You justify that the excluded and retained tokens together cover V.
  • You do not turn the probability into an output schedule.

Exercise 3 · Audit and conditioning

The table no longer shows ␠lukewarm

A display keeps only ␠hot: 0.50 and ␠cold: 0.30, for the same prefix. What probability mass is missing? To obtain a sum equal to 1, someone divides each displayed value by their sum. Calculate the new values and explain what has changed. Could either table be reused after “The tea is cold”?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the visible values, then subtract their sum from the total of the full distribution.

Hint 2

Dividing the values by their sum describes the proportions among only the retained tokens. To discuss the next position, you must also check the prefix.

Compare with the solution

The visible sum is 0.50 + 0.30 = 0.80. The missing mass is 1 − 0.80 = 0.20, or 1/5. In this miniature vocabulary, it belongs to ␠lukewarm.

0.500.80=55+3=0.625;0.300.80=0.375

The new values give 0.625 + 0.375 = 1.000. Call B the event “T is one of ␠hot and ␠cold”. We are now describing the distribution conditioned on B, still for prefix c: the proportion of ␠hot among the possibilities in B is 5/8. This quantity is not its initial probability 1/2 over all of V.

After “The tea is cold”, the prefix has changed. Neither the initial table nor its restriction provides the distribution at the new position. A correct sum is not enough: we must also state the vocabulary or selected event and the prefix to which the distribution applies.

Check your reasoning

  • You recover the missing mass 1/5 without treating it as an error in the full distribution.
  • You obtain 5/8 and 3/8 and check their sum 1.
  • You name the additional conditioning on B.
  • You reject applying the same probabilities to another prefix without new information.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does p(v | c) describe, and what must be kept fixed to read this quantity?

The probability the model assigns to the next token v given prefix c. The model, tokenization and vocabulary are fixed; a different sequence already written requires a new distribution.

Recall 2What checks are needed before interpreting the list as a complete distribution?

Each probability is between 0 and 1, and the sum over the entire vocabulary is 1. A partial display can sum to less; you must know which possibilities are missing.

Recall 3Why add the probabilities of ␠hot and ␠lukewarm, and how can you check the result?

These tokens are disjoint alternatives at the same position. Their sum is 7/10; the complement gives 1 − 3/10 = 7/10, since only ␠cold remains outside the group in our miniature vocabulary.

Recall 4Why does the highest probability prove neither which token is ultimately written nor the temperature of the tea?

The individual maximum 1/2 remains below 1 and the selection rule is not specified. The distribution concerns a textual continuation; the actual temperature requires a separate observation.

Recall 5What changes if we divide the probabilities of the displayed tokens by their sum?

We obtain the conditional proportions among only the displayed tokens, with the prefix unchanged and provided their sum is positive. We do not recover their initial probabilities over the complete vocabulary.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the table, state the prefix and possibilities again, then reconstruct the checks for a complete distribution. Explain how to calculate the probability of a group by addition and by complement. Finish by distinguishing the model’s distribution, the written token and the actual temperature; specify what changes when the prefix or retained set changes.
