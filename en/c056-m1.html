Level 1 · C056·M1

Hypotheses

Your goalState a hypothesis about an unknown mean, choose what would contradict it, and separate this question from the result of taking a sample.

Before you start
c055-m5 : confidence interval

✿01 See the idea

A filling machine should produce bottles with a mean volume of 500 mL. You take a sample of 4 bottles: 498 mL, 500 mL, 503 mL and 506 mL. The volumes differ. Does this mean that the machine misses its target on average? Before answering, we need to specify what the question concerns.

The sample consists of the bottles actually measured. The target population consists of the bottles produced at this setting during the period being studied. Its mean is unknown; we denote it by μ, the Greek letter pronounced “mu”. The mean calculated from the sample is known once the measurements have been made. These means therefore play different roles.

A statistical hypothesis is a statement about the population, written here using μ. The null hypothesis sets the reference: the mean equals 500 mL. The alternative hypothesis describes what we want to distinguish from this reference: the mean differs, either below or above it. “Null” does not mean that the bottles are empty: it is the difference from the reference that would be 0.

The situation and all its measurements are fictional. We are studying the mean volume, without certifying the contents of each bottle. Even if the population mean equals the reference, some bottles may contain less and others more.

The rule to remember

Write the question about the population mean before looking at the results. The sample provides information for examining this question; its mean alone does not settle it.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Write the question, then calculate what was observed

The question chosen before the measurements is: “Does the mean of this production differ from 500 mL, in either direction?” Let us use the fictional sample of 4 bottles to calculate an observed difference, then specify what it allows us to say.

  1. Define the quantities and conditions

    μ denotes the unknown mean of the production at the setting being studied. It is a parameter: a number that describes the target population. μ with the reference subscript denotes the known reference, 500 mL. The letters x with subscripts a, b, c and d denote the measured volumes. The letter n counts the bottles and has no physical unit: n = 4. The letter x with a bar above it, read as “x bar”, will denote the mean of these measurements. All volumes and all means are in millilitres, abbreviated mL.

    We assume that production is stable over the period: the bottles come from the same setting and the same distribution, meaning the same distribution of possible volumes, with a mean that exists. Sampling is random, without favouring the fullest bottles. The measurements are assumed to be independent: in the model, knowing one volume gives no information about the volume of another bottle. The measuring device adds no systematic offset. These conditions connect what we measure to the stated population; they are not results of the calculation.

    The numbers are treated as exact for the arithmetic exercise. No bell-shaped distribution is needed to write the hypotheses or calculate this mean. Choosing a testing procedure would then require its own conditions.

  2. State the hypotheses about μ

    H with subscript 0 is the name of the null hypothesis; H with subscript 1 is the name of the alternative. These are labels, not probabilities. The sign ≠ is read as “not equal to”.

    H0:μ=μréf;H1:μ≠μréf

    Here, the reference is 500 mL. The alternative allows a mean below or above this value: this is what “two-sided” means. For any possible mean, the equality statement and the inequality statement cannot both be true, and one of them applies.

    We do not write the hypothesis about x bar: its value can be calculated directly from the observed bottles. The unknown quantity the question concerns is μ.

  3. Calculate the sample mean

    First, add the volumes to obtain the total amount measured. To make the addition easy to check, group the first measurements: 498 + 500 = 998 mL; then the next ones: 503 + 506 = 1009 mL. Their sum is 2007 mL.

    x¯=xa+xb+xc+xdn

    Dividing by the number of bottles shares this total volume equally among them: it gives the mean per bottle.

    x¯=20074 mL=501.75 mL

    Immediate check: the mean lies between the smallest and largest volumes in the sample. It summarizes these bottles without removing their differences.

  4. Measure the observed difference and check it another way

    Let d denote the observed difference between the sample mean and the reference. The subtraction is in this order so that a positive result indicates an observed mean above the target. d is in mL.

    d=x¯−μréf=501.75−500=1.75 mL

    Another route: subtract the same reference from each volume before taking the mean. The differences are -2 mL, 0 mL, 3 mL and 6 mL. The pairwise sums are -2 mL and 9 mL; the total is 7 mL.

    74 mL=1.75 mL=d

    We obtain the same result: subtracting the reference from the mean is equivalent to taking the mean of the differences from that reference. The value 1.75 mL is a difference observed in the sample, not a known difference for the population.

  5. Set limits on the conclusion

    The sample has a mean 1.75 mL above the reference. We can report this description and show its calculation. We cannot infer from it that μ exceeds the reference: different samples have different means, even when the null hypothesis is true.

    A statistical decision requires a rule that accounts for these fluctuations and the conditions under which the rule can be used. Simply observing a nonzero difference does not provide this rule. Conversely, an observed difference of 0 mL would not demonstrate equality of the means at the population level.

    The hypotheses are the statements we examine. Stable production and the sampling conditions are the assumptions that make the examination interpretable. Correct addition does not verify these assumptions.

  6. Choose the direction of the question in advance

    If the stated question were only “is the production filled to a lower mean volume than the reference?”, the alternative would consider only lower means. It is called one-sided. The null hypothesis would then cover everything else, including equality. The sign ≥ means “greater than or equal to”.

    H0:μ≥μréf;H1:μ<μréf

    This other formulation answers a different question about the same filling machine. It is chosen according to the problem, before inspecting the results. Choosing the side on which the observed mean falls after seeing the results changes the investigation: we must then describe it as exploration and plan a separate check using new observations.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why the equality statement concerns μ, then recover the observed difference by each route. Finish with a sentence describing the sample and another saying what remains unknown.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The observed mean differs from the target: the null hypothesis is false.”

    A sample can differ from the target even when the population mean equals it. Repair the sentence: “In this sample, the difference is 1.75 mL; deciding about μ still requires a rule suited to the fluctuations.”

    Review the conclusion from the calculation

  • “The hypothesis concerns the mean of the bottles I measured.”

    This mean can already be calculated: it equals 501.75 mL. The hypothesis concerns μ, the unknown mean of the target production. Repair the statement by first naming this population and the reference, then writing the statement about μ.

    Distinguish the sample from the population

  • “I choose the direction of the alternative after seeing which side the mean falls on.”

    This choice uses the result it was supposed to examine. Write the direction based on the need before taking the measurements. A new question suggested by these measurements can be explored, but that does not make it a question stated in advance.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same situation

Look only for underfilling

Before taking a new sample, the team wants only to look for a mean volume below 500 mL. Write hypotheses that cover all possibilities. A colleague then proposes looking only for overfilling, because the first sample gave 501.75 mL. What needs to be made clear about this change?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The alternative must express “below”. What remains if this statement is false?

Hint 2

Distinguish a question chosen before the measurements from a question suggested by reading them.

Compare with the solution

The alternative is μ < 500 mL. The null hypothesis covers μ ≥ 500 mL, including equality. Means above the reference are therefore covered, without being cases of underfilling.

The first sample had a positive difference of 1.75 mL. This sign describes that sample; it is not enough to conclude that the production is overfilled on average or to change the stated question retrospectively.

An investigation of overfilling suggested by this result is exploration. The team can propose it for a future check, but must make clear when the choice was made and use new observations for that check.

Check your reasoning

  • You place lower means in the alternative and equality in the null hypothesis.
  • You also cover higher means with the null hypothesis for this one-sided question.
  • You distinguish the observed sign, the stated question and a new exploration.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the difference between μ and x bar in the filling machine situation?

μ is the unknown mean of the production at the same setting during the target period. x bar is the mean calculated from the bottles in the sample. The statistical statement concerns μ; x bar provides information about it.

Recall 2Recall the hypotheses for looking for any difference between the mean and 500 mL. What does “null” mean?

The null hypothesis states μ = 500 mL; the alternative states μ ≠ 500 mL, either below or above. Here, “null” refers to a 0 difference from the reference, without requiring 0 volume or identical bottles.

Recall 3What can we report from an observed difference of 1.75 mL? And what would a 0 difference fail to establish?

We can describe the sample: its mean exceeds the reference by 1.75 mL. This difference is not enough to decide about μ. Conversely, an observed mean equal to the reference does not prove the null hypothesis: samples fluctuate.

Recall 4How does stable production differ from the null hypothesis about its mean volume?

Stability is one of the assumed conditions for connecting the sample to a single population. The null hypothesis is the equality statement we want to examine. A correctly calculated mean does not, on its own, verify the sampling and measurement conditions.

Recall 5When looking only for underfilling on average, where should equality go? When should the direction be chosen?

The alternative is μ < 500 mL; the null hypothesis is μ ≥ 500 mL, including equality. The direction follows from the need before inspecting the results. A direction chosen after reading them belongs to exploration and must be checked separately.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the approach: name the population and its unknown parameter; set the reference and the direction of the question; write the hypotheses; calculate the sample mean and its difference from the reference; check the calculation using the individual differences; distinguish what is observed, what is assumed and what remains to be decided.
