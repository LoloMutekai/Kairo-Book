Level 2 · C053·M1

Surprise and information

Your goalCalculate the surprise of an announcement in bits, then explain which probability model it depends on.

Before you start
c029-m5 : Bayes · c030-m5 : Poisson · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c025-m5 : O, Omega and Theta

✿01 See the idea

At a parcel dispatch counter, you are waiting to hear the destination of the next parcel. In this fictional scene, a container holds 8 tickets that feel identical: 4 say Lyon, 2 Lille, 1 Dijon and 1 Pau. The tickets are mixed, then a ticket is drawn at random. A frequent destination is less surprising than a rare destination.

Imagine that the ticket says Lille. Before the draw, you assigned it a probability of 1/4. After the announcement, you know the destination. The self-information of this event measures how unexpected the announcement was according to the probability assigned before seeing it. It measures neither the urgency of the parcel nor the importance of its contents.

To quantify this surprise, we choose a scale on which dividing the probability by 2 adds 1 bit each time. Lyon gives 1 bit, Lille 2 bits, Dijon 3 bits. Here, the bit is a unit of information: these numbers do not tell you the file size of a parcel label.

The tickets, the draw and their probabilities are invented for the calculation. Each ticket names a single city, and all tickets have the same chance of being drawn. For successive draws in the exercise, the ticket is replaced and the tickets are mixed again; we assume the draws are independent. No actual delivery frequencies are measured.

The rule to remember

Specify the event and its probability before the announcement. The smaller this probability, the more bits of surprise the announcement provides; this number remains relative to the chosen model.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The next parcel is going to Lille

The draw announces Lille. Start again from the model with 8 tickets to calculate the information in this announcement, then check the result without a logarithmic calculator.

  1. Name the event and use the right point in time

    X denotes the random destination of the next parcel; x denotes a possible city. A is the event “X is Lille”. P is the probability rule for the container, and p = P(A) is its probability, which has no unit. The number n of tickets for this event is 2 tickets; the total number N is 8 tickets.

    We use the model from before the draw. Once Lille has been observed, the announcement does not retrospectively become unsurprising just because its destination is now known.

  2. Go from counting to a probability

    The equal probability of the tickets justifies the quotient n/N. The list of cities is complete and the categories are disjoint: their total count is 6 + 2 = 8 tickets. If the tickets had different chances, this quotient would not be sufficient.

    p=nN=28=14=0.25

  3. Define the measure in bits

    I(A) denotes the self-information, or surprise, of event A in model P. It is expressed in bits when the logarithm has base 2. The symbol log denotes the logarithm: the logarithm of y to base 2 is the exponent to which 2 must be raised to obtain y. Its argument y is a number with no unit.

    I(A)=−log2(p)=log2(1p) bits

    The formula applies for 0 < p ≤ 1. The logarithm of p is then negative or 0; the minus sign makes the surprise positive or 0. The base determines the unit: using a natural logarithm without conversion would not give bits.

  4. Read the logarithm as an exponent

    The reciprocal of p is 1 ÷ 1/4 = 4, with no unit. Find the exponent that gives 4 from base 2. The product 2 × 2 equals 4, so the exponent is 2.

    22=4⇒I(A)=log2(4)=2 bits

    This result is exact. The reciprocal of each chosen probability is an integer power of the base. In other models, the surprise can be a noninteger number of bits.

  5. Check by recovering the probability

    122=14=28

    The inverse operation does recover the share of Lille. Another check: the probabilities of Lyon, Lille, Dijon and Pau sum to 1. The surprise of Lyon is 1 bit; Dijon, which is less likely than Lille, gives 3 bits. The resulting order agrees with intuition.

    A certain event, such as “a city listed on the tickets will be drawn”, has probability 1 and surprise 0 bit. For p = 0, the logarithm is undefined: the formula has no finite value. If an announcement considered impossible occurs, the model or the observation must be reconsidered. A surprise of 0 bit corresponds to certainty, not impossibility.

  6. Understand why amounts of information can add up

    A still denotes Lille on the first draw, and B denotes Lille on the next draw. A ∩ B means “events A and B both occur”. With replacement, mixing and assumed independence, the joint probability is the product P(A)P(B). The logarithm turns this product into a sum.

    I(A∩B)=−log2(P(A)P(B))=I(A)+I(B)

    The exercise will ask you to calculate the values for this pair. Without independence, you must start from the actual joint probability; multiplying the separate probabilities is no longer justified. Here we measure the surprise of an announcement or a specific pair. The average surprise across all announcements will be the subject of the next unit.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, explain why announcing Lille provides 2 bits in this model. First recover its probability, then the exponent; explain why knowing the destination afterwards does not change this calculation.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A small probability gives a small number of bits.”

    Probability and its surprise move in opposite directions. Dividing p by the base increases the logarithm of its reciprocal. Revisit Lille and Dijon, then check that the surprise remains positive.

    Reread the sign and the inverse check

  • “Lille is now certain, so the announcement was worth 0 bit.”

    Use the probability assigned before the announcement. The certainty obtained after the observation is not the forecast used to measure the surprise.

    Return to the model before the draw

  • “More bits mean a more important parcel.”

    Bits describe expected rarity in a model, not the value of the message. The last exercise changes the probability of Dijon without changing the content of the announcement or the importance of the parcel.

    Try the change of model

Exercise 2 · Combination under an assumption

Lille, then Lille again

The ticket is replaced and the tickets are mixed after each draw; the draws are assumed independent. Calculate the probability of the ordered pair Lille then Lille and its surprise. Explain why adding the probabilities would be incorrect.

Hint 1

Both announcements must occur: use the product of the probabilities under the given assumption.

Hint 2

Calculate the surprise either from the reciprocal of this product or from the sum of the individual surprises.

Compare with the solution

The product gives 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16, with no unit. Adding the probabilities would correspond to different reasoning and does not represent both draws occurring here.

The reciprocal is 16. The product 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 equals 16: the pair provides 4 bits. The second method gives 2 + 2 = 4 bits; the results agree.

Replacement preserves the proportions, and independence allows multiplication. Without this assumption, the probability of the second announcement could depend on the first; the sum of the separately calculated surprises would no longer be guaranteed.

Exercise 3 · Analysis of a conclusion

The same announcement under a different forecast

Before the next draw, the container is replaced. The new draw model assigns Dijon probability 1/2; the other destinations share the remainder. Dijon is announced. A colleague keeps 3 bits because the name of the city has not changed. Correct the calculation and explain what can be concluded from the difference.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The information depends on the probability assigned before this announcement, so it depends on the new model.

Hint 2

Compare the surprises, then distinguish lower expected rarity from lower importance of the parcel.

Compare with the solution

With the new probability 1/2, the reciprocal is 2. The surprise is therefore 1 bit. The old model gave 3 bits; the difference is 2 bits.

The colleague has kept the wrong probability reference. The announcement was less unexpected under the new forecast. This difference demonstrates no decrease in the importance of the parcel, no operational gain and no superiority of the new model for actual deliveries.

If the forecast had been changed only after seeing Dijon, the new score could not be presented as the surprise under the original forecast. The model must be dated before the observation.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the new probability and state the unit, bit.
  • Calculate the difference, specifying the model for each score.
  • Distinguish surprise, importance and validation of a model in actual use.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you define the surprise of an event in bits?

Using its probability p before the announcement, I = −log to base 2 of p, for 0 < p ≤ 1. The base 2 determines the unit, bit.

Recall 2What happens to the surprise when the probability is divided by 2?

It increases by 1 bit. Lille and Dijon give 2 and 3 bits under the original model.

Recall 3Why do certainty and impossibility not give the same score?

For p = 1, the surprise is 0 bit. For p = 0, the logarithm is undefined and there is no finite value; observing an impossible announcement requires reconsidering the model or the observation.

Recall 4When can you add separately calculated surprises?

When independent events occur jointly: their probabilities multiply, and the logarithm turns the product into a sum. Without independence, you need to know the joint probability.

Recall 5Does the name of a destination determine its surprise or its importance?

The surprise depends on the probability assigned to the event before observing it. It does not measure the importance of the parcel. Changing the forecast changes the score, not the content of the announcement.
