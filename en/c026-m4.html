Fairness and starvation

Your goalRecognize a wait that can continue indefinitely and justify a waiting time bound for a protected request.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You are waiting at a shared printer in a workshop. Your document N has been accepted and is ready to print. A job R is already running. Whenever the printer becomes free, an urgent document U goes ahead of yours. The printer works continuously, but your sheet never comes out if urgent jobs keep arriving in time.

This indefinite postponement of a ready request is called starvation. Observing a long wait is not enough to prove it: you need to understand why the rule can always choose someone else. Strict priority for urgent jobs allows this postponement, even if every print job finishes.

Fairness here means an explicit rule for taking turns that protects ordinary requests against repeatedly being overtaken. It requires neither an equal share of time nor the same wait for everyone. We will allow at most 2 urgent jobs before serving the oldest ready ordinary request.

The whole scene and its durations are fictional. We follow 1 printer; N is already at the head of the ordinary queue at time 0 min. R has 1 min of work left and each new document takes 2 min. U represents a different urgent job in each block of the diagram. Before reading on, predict in which block N will be able to start.

The rule to remember

To guarantee a turn, limit how often a request that stays ready can be overtaken; to guarantee a time limit, also bound the duration of the jobs that can precede it.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Giving the ordinary document a turn

At time 0 min, N has been accepted, is ready and is first among the ordinary documents. R finishes in 1 min. An urgent job is available at every choice; each new print job lasts 2 min. Let us compare strict priority with a limit of K = 2 overtaking jobs, then justify what is guaranteed.

  1. Define what the waiting time measures

    a is the time when N becomes ready and first among ordinary requests; here a = 0 min. s is the time when it starts printing. W is the wait between those times, in minutes. r is the time left for the job already running, here 1 min. We do not start counting r again at every arrival.

    W=s−a

    The printer handles a document at a time, without interrupting a document once it has started. N stays ready and accepted. Switching documents is instantaneous here; in another model, its cost would need to be included in the durations. There are no breakdowns or pauses. Arrival order is preserved within each category.

  2. Construct the risk of starvation

    Strict priority chooses an urgent job whenever one is available as the printer becomes free. After R, the first urgent job occupies the interval from 1 to 3 min, the next from 3 to 5 min, then another from 5 to 7 min.

    If the stream stops after these 3 urgent jobs, N starts at 7 min: 1 + 3 × 2 = 7 min of waiting; it finishes at 9 min. This is a finite wait. But if an urgent job is always ready whenever the printer becomes free, the same choice repeats without ever selecting N. Each urgent job finishes; N suffers starvation.

    The finite trace illustrates the mechanism. The possibility of extending it indefinitely is the counterexample to a guarantee of a turn. It does not claim to describe a stream that has actually been observed.

  3. Define protection that keeps its count

    K is the maximum number of urgent jobs allowed to overtake the ordinary request at the head of the queue; it is an integer with no unit. b counts the urgent jobs already chosen ahead of that request since it became the head. Initially b = 0. As long as b < K and an urgent job is ready, we can serve it and then increase b by 1.

    As soon as b = K, we choose the ordinary request at the head of the queue for the next slot, even if an urgent job is waiting. If no urgent job is available, we serve that request immediately. A new urgent job never resets b to 0. After the ordinary head request has been served, the next request becomes the new head with its own counter at 0; when no ordinary request is waiting, urgent jobs proceed freely.

    This rule gives urgent jobs limited priority and preserves the order of ordinary requests. If an ordinary request has a finite number of predecessors, they too will move forward; however, the bound calculated here starts only when it becomes the head. It does not bound the time since admission while other ordinary requests are still ahead of it.

  4. Work through the protection of N

    R finishes at 1 min; b is 0. The first urgent job is allowed: it finishes at 1 + 2 = 3 min, and b becomes 1. The next is still allowed: it finishes at 3 + 2 = 5 min, and b reaches K = 2.

    At 5 min, a new urgent job may be present, but the limit requires N to be chosen. Its start time is therefore s = 5 min; W = 5 − 0 = 5 min. Its own printing adds 2 min: it finishes at 7 min. Including this printing in W would confuse waiting before service with the finish time.

  5. Move from the trace to a bound

    d denotes the maximum duration of an urgent job, in minutes. For this scene d = 2 min. Before N, at most r minutes of the current job remain, followed by K urgent jobs, each lasting at most d. Adding these periods of printer use gives a bound B, in minutes. The product Kd measures time, since K counts jobs.

    W≤B=r+Kd
    B=1+2×2=5 min

    The bound is reached here because all the allowed urgent jobs are present and last exactly d. If some are absent or finish earlier, N starts earlier. The number of urgent arrivals does not appear in B: new arrivals create no additional right to overtake N.

  6. Check the guarantee and its limits

    Check another way: the start time of N is 1 + 2 + 2 = 5 min. There are exactly 2 U blocks before N, and no print jobs overlap. The timeline and the bound give the same result.

    If no overtaking were allowed, K = 0 would give W ≤ r: N would go after R. With no current job, r = 0 would remove only the first term. These cases explain why K × d is insufficient when a print job has already started.

    This protection does not promise that all urgent jobs will meet their deadlines, or that all requests will have the same wait. If a print job starts but never finishes, nobody behind it makes progress: this blockage comes from service. If every job finishes but no maximum duration is known, N is still guaranteed a turn after a finite number of overtaking jobs, without a uniform numerical bound in minutes.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why print jobs that finish can still leave N starving, then reconstruct r + Kd by naming what occupies the printer before N. Say when this guarantee begins.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The printer is making progress, so nobody can be forgotten.”

    Tracking job completions proves overall activity. Track N too: strict priority can always choose a new urgent job. A finite wait does not prove starvation, but the possibility of this repetition prevents a guarantee of its turn.

    Compare the work completed with what happens to N

  • “I reset the counter to 0 as soon as an urgent job arrives.”

    b belongs to the ordinary request at the head of the queue. Resetting it at every urgent arrival allows unlimited overtaking: the quota no longer has any effect. Keep b until that request is served; the counter exercise shows the flaw.

    Examine the counter that forgets

  • “Limiting how often a request can be overtaken always gives the same maximum wait.”

    The number of overtaking jobs does not measure their duration. The bound requires a known r and a maximum duration d. Here it concerns N already ready and at the head of the queue. A very long print job or ordinary predecessors change what remains to wait for.

    Review the assumptions for W ≤ r + Kd

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Protect N sooner

Use the same printer, with 1 min remaining for R and urgent jobs lasting 2 min always ready. The quota becomes K = 1. N is at the head of the ordinary queue at time 0 min and its printing takes 2 min. Find its waiting time and finish time. Draw the blocks that justify them.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Keep the end of R first; the quota does not interrupt it.

Hint 2

Place only the allowed urgent jobs ahead of N. Its own block starts after the wait.

Compare with the solution

The current job still takes 1 min. The quota then allows 1 urgent job, taking 1 × 2 = 2 min. N starts at 1 + 2 = 3 min; its wait since 0 is 3 min.

Its printing finishes at 3 + 2 = 5 min. The sequence R, U, N gives these same times. The quota counts the U jobs that overtake N, not N itself.

Check your reasoning

  • Include the remaining 1 min of R.
  • Obtain 3 min of waiting and a finish at 5 min.
  • Relate the times to the diagram and distinguish waiting from printing.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis on paper

The counter that forgets

Return to the quota K = 2. At 3 min, the first urgent job has finished and b is 1. A program resets b to 0 when a new urgent job arrives, just before choosing the next job. This arrival scenario repeats before every choice. Does N keep the promised protection? Work through the choices and repair the rule.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write the value of b just after the arrival, then after the urgent job is chosen.

Hint 2

Does resetting to 0 prevent b from reaching K? Which request should the counter belong to?

Compare with the solution

Just before every choice, the new arrival sets b to 0. The program then chooses an urgent job and increases b to 1, still less than K = 2. At the next choice, it forgets this overtaking again. The same sequence can continue indefinitely: the protection of N is lost.

Repair: attach b to the ordinary request at the head of the queue and keep it when an urgent job arrives. At 3 min, b stays 1; after the next urgent job, it reaches 2 and requires N to be chosen at 5 min. Resetting happens only when the ordinary head request has been served and another becomes the head.

Check your reasoning

  • Show the repetition of 0 then 1, which never reaches 2.
  • Distinguish keeping the count from merely having a field named quota.
  • Repair the reset timing and recover the turn of N.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same scene

Print jobs with varying durations

N is still at the head of the ordinary queue at time 0 min. R has 1 min left; K remains 2, but each urgent job can now last up to 4 min. N still takes 2 min. Give a bound on its waiting time and a bound on its finish time, then explain what remains guaranteed if all print jobs finish but no maximum duration is known.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For a guarantee valid in every allowed case, use the maximum duration of each overtaking job.

Hint 2

Separate “a finite number of jobs ahead of N” from “the same limit in minutes for all their durations”.

Compare with the solution

The urgent workload ahead of N is at most 2 × 4 = 8 min. So W ≤ 1 + 8 = 9 min. Since a = 0, N starts no later than 9 min and finishes no later than 9 + 2 = 11 min.

Check: R followed by 2 urgent jobs of 4 min each, then N, reaches exactly these bounds. With shorter urgent jobs, the times are earlier; always writing an equality would be wrong.

With no known maximum but with every job finishing, at most 2 urgent jobs remain ahead of N after R: N will therefore get its turn. However, durations can be chosen as large as we wish; no uniform bound in minutes follows from the quota alone. If a job does not finish, even the eventual turn of N is no longer guaranteed by this rule.

Check your reasoning

  • Find W ≤ 9 min and a finish no later than 11 min.
  • Explain why these are attainable bounds rather than mandatory times.
  • Keep the distinction between a guaranteed turn, a bounded wait and a job that never finishes.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1When can we speak of starvation in the printer scene?

When N remains accepted and ready but is never selected while other jobs go ahead. A rule that permits this sequence creates a risk of starvation; a finite waiting trace is not enough to establish this endless postponement.

Recall 2What fairness does our rule promise, and does it require equal waits?

It limits to K the urgent jobs that overtake the ordinary request at the head of the queue. It protects its turn if jobs finish; it requires neither equal waits nor all urgent deadlines to be met.

Recall 3Who does b belong to and when can it be reset to 0?

b counts the urgent jobs that have gone ahead of the ordinary head request since it became the head. New urgent jobs do not erase it. The next head starts with b = 0 after the previous one has been served.

Recall 4Reconstruct W ≤ r + Kd and state its assumptions.

N is ready and at the head of the ordinary queue; r is the time left for the current job, K the maximum number of overtaking jobs and d the maximum duration of each. The printer works without breakdowns or pauses, without interruption, with document switches included in the durations or instantaneous. Printer use before N totals at most r + Kd minutes.

Recall 5Do a limit on simultaneous jobs and a refusal counter prove bounded waiting?

No. They describe capacity and refused attempts. A rule is still needed that actually reserves a turn for the request, preserves its progress and justifies a bound under explicit assumptions about durations.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct this reasoning: strict priority can leave N waiting indefinitely; a limit on overtaking protects its turn; keeping the count makes that limit effective; a maximum duration and the remainder of the current job give W ≤ r + Kd. Finish by distinguishing this wait from the printing of N. Reading and an assisted answer never constitute an attained level.
