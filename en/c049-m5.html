Level 2 · C049·M5

Power series solutions

Your goalBuild an oscillator’s power series solution from its governing law and initial conditions, then choose a truncation with an error bound.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A small cart is attached to a horizontal spring. You pull it 2 cm away from its equilibrium position, then release it from rest. At the very start, its position does not follow a straight line over time: the spring pulls it back, and its position curve bends. How can you calculate its position without first looking for a formula in a list?

We can build the position curve by adding polynomial terms. The constant term sets the initial position; the linear term sets the initial velocity; the higher powers adjust the curvature. The spring’s law determines the successive coefficients. We do not fit them to measured points.

A power series is a sum of powers continued indefinitely. A truncation keeps only the terms up to a chosen degree: it is a polynomial we can calculate. The series can be an exact solution, whereas its truncation is generally only an approximate solution. We must justify passing to the infinite sum and control what has been left out.

The entire scene and its parameters are fictional. We assume a linear spring, a cart with no friction, and no push after release. We study this ideal model rather than the accuracy of a real device. The exercises use the same cart and the same initial conditions.

The rule to remember

Set the initial conditions, expand in powers, differentiate and align matching powers, and impose the law on each coefficient; then justify the series and bound the truncation error.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Where will the cart be after 1 s?

The mass m is 1 kg; the spring stiffness k is 1 N/m. The initial displacement A is 2 cm and the initial velocity is 0 cm/s. Let us build the series about the release time, then approximate the position after 1 s, with an error of at most 0.01 cm under this model.

  1. State the law and remove units from the series calculation

    t is elapsed time in seconds; x(t) is the cart’s signed displacement in centimetres, positive to the right. The 2 primes on x indicate acceleration in centimetres per second squared. The spring stiffness k is the restoring force per unit extension; N/m means newtons per metre. The ideal law is x″ + (k/m)x = 0. The quotient k/m is 1 per second squared. The angular frequency ω is its positive square root: ω = 1 per second, also expressible in radians per second.

    Set u = ωt, a dimensionless time variable, and y(u) = x(t)/A, a dimensionless displacement. A is positive and constant. From now on, primes on y mean derivatives with respect to u. By the chain rule, x″(t) equals A times ω squared times y″(u). Dividing the law by A times ω squared gives:

    y″(u)+y(u)=0

    The initial conditions become y(0) = 1 and y′(0) = 0: displacement has been divided by A, and velocity by Aω. The physical position will be recovered using x(t) = A y(ωt). All the coefficients we build will therefore be dimensionless.

  2. Differentiate the series, then shift the indices

    Let us seek y in the following form. The symbol Σ means “add the terms”; n runs through the integers starting at 0, and an is the coefficient of the power uⁿ. The symbol ∞ indicates that the sum has no last term.

    y(u)=∑n=0∞anun

    The second derivative of anuⁿ is n(n − 1)an times u to the power n − 2, for n ≥ 2. The constant and linear terms disappear. To add y″ and y, we must express the sums using the same power uⁿ. The coefficient of uⁿ in y″ therefore comes from the original term of degree n + 2:

    y″(u)=∑n=0∞(n+2)(n+1)an+2un

    We first work with the coefficients as we would in a polynomial expansion. Before drawing a conclusion, we will check the convergence needed to differentiate the infinite sum.

  3. Impose the law at each power

    In y″ + y, we add an to the product of (n + 2)(n + 1) and the coefficient with index n + 2. This is the total coefficient of uⁿ. For the sum to be identically 0 around the origin, every coefficient must be 0. Making the value 0 at a single instant would not be enough.

    an+2=−an(n+2)(n+1), pour n entier ≥ 0

    This relation is a recurrence: it produces a coefficient from an earlier one. Its denominator is nonzero for all these indices. It advances by 2 places, so it needs 2 starting values: a0 = y(0) and a1 = y′(0).

  4. Calculate the coefficients, keeping fractions

    The initial conditions impose a0 = 1 and a1 = 0. For n = 0, the denominator is 2 × 1 = 2: a2 = −1/2 = -1/2. The negative sign represents curvature towards equilibrium.

    For n = 1, a3 = −0/(3 × 2) = 0. Then, with n = 2, the denominator is 4 × 3 = 12; the negative of a2 is 1/2, so a4 = (1/2)/12 = 1/24.

    The next link gives a5 = −0/(5 × 4) = 0, then a6 = −(1/24)/(6 × 5) = -1/720. The entire odd chain remains 0: multiplying or dividing its 0 coefficient does not create any additional initial motion.

    y(u)=1+(-1/2)u2+1/24u4+(-1/720)u6+⋯

    An independent check comes from differentiating y″ = −y: the successive derivatives at the start alternate between 1, 0, −1, 0, then repeat. The coefficient of degree n is the derivative of order n at the start divided by n!, rather than the derivative itself.

  5. Check that the sum really defines a solution

    The pattern of even coefficients is written below, with j running through integers starting at 0. The factorial r!, for a positive integer r, is the product of the integers from 1 to r; by convention 0! = 1. The odd coefficients are 0.

    a2j=(−1)j(2j)!

    For fixed nonzero u, the ratio of the absolute values of 2 successive even terms is u squared divided by (2j + 2)(2j + 1). This ratio tends to 0 as j increases: the series converges absolutely. At u = 0, only the constant term remains. On every bounded interval of u, the same check uses a common bound; it also applies to the derivative series. We can therefore differentiate term by term and check y″ + y = 0.

    The sum also satisfies the 2 initial conditions. It is therefore the required solution of this linear equation, for which these conditions determine a unique solution. We recognize the cosine: y(u) = cos(u). The recurrence has built it here without starting from a given cosine formula. For a different equation, a recurrence alone would not be enough: we would need to justify convergence again.

  6. Truncate, then evaluate with the correct units

    Let Pn denote the polynomial that keeps the terms of y up to degree n. At t = 1 s, u = ωt = 1 × 1 = 1, with no unit. Keeping terms up to degree 4 gives:

    P4(1)=1+(-1/2)+1/24=1/2+1/24=13/24

    This value is dimensionless: it represents the fraction of the initial displacement still remaining. We must multiply by A again to recover the position: 2 × (13/24) = 13/12 cm, or approximately 1.083333 cm to the right of equilibrium. The result is smaller than the initial displacement, which is consistent with the start of the restoring motion.

  7. Bound what the truncation has left out

    The bars |…| denote absolute value, the size of a quantity without its sign. On |u| ≤ 1, the nonzero terms alternate in sign, and their absolute values decrease and tend to 0. The remainder of such an alternating sum is at most the absolute value of the first omitted term: successive corrections offset each other and bracket the limit. Here, the omitted term of degree 6 has absolute value 1/720 at u = 1.

    |x(t)−AP4(ωt)|≤A|ωt|66!

    At our chosen time, the physical bound is 2 × (1/720) = 1/360 cm, approximately 0.002778 cm. It is below the tolerance of 0.01 cm: the truncation meets this requirement under the model’s assumptions.

    The first omitted term is negative. The approximation of degree 4 is therefore above the solution. Adding this term gives P6(1) = 13/24 + (-1/720) = 389/720. After multiplying by A, we obtain the exact bounds 389/360 cm ≤ x(1 s) ≤ 13/12 cm, or approximately 1.080556 cm to 1.083333 cm. The decimals are rounded readings; the fractions carry the guarantee.

  8. Check the law while distinguishing residual from error

    Substituting P4 into the reduced law gives P4″ + P4 = (1/24) times u to the power 4. The lower degree terms cancel: 2a2 + a0 = 0 and 12a4 + a2 = 0. The remaining term shows that this finite polynomial is not the exact solution.

    The residual is this discrepancy in the equation; here it is dimensionless because the law has been reduced. It does not directly give the position error in centimetres. The previous error bound controls that difference. We have thus checked the initial conditions, the coefficients in the law, and the truncation separately: none of these checks measures the friction omitted by the model.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, explain why the second derivative brings in the coefficient 2 places further on, why 0 initial velocity makes only the odd chain vanish, and why obtaining a nonzero residual does not contradict the stated error bound.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I differentiate the powers but keep the same indices.”

    After 2 differentiations, the term of degree n + 2 becomes a term of degree n, multiplied by (n + 2)(n + 1). Rewrite y″ and y in terms of the same power before comparing their coefficients. The denominator in the recurrence comes from this differentiation.

    Review the index shift and the recurrence

  • “The initial velocity is 0, so all the coefficients are 0.”

    Velocity only sets a1 to 0. Position imposes a0 = 1 after normalization. The recurrence connects indices 2 places apart: the odd coefficients vanish, while the even ones describe the cart’s return.

    Review the two chains arising from the initial conditions

  • “My polynomial has the correct initial values: it is the exact solution.”

    The initial values do not verify the law at every instant. Substitute the polynomial into y″ + y, then distinguish this residual from the difference between the polynomial and the infinite sum. A useful approximation can have a nonzero residual and a bounded error.

    Review the error bound, then the residual check

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · justify a recurrence

Extend the two chains

For the same cart, start again from a3 = 0 and a4 = 1/24. Find a5 and a6, writing out the denominators. Explain why the second result is negative and why you cannot choose these coefficients freely.

Hint 1

Each new coefficient comes from the coefficient 2 places before it.

Hint 2

To calculate the coefficient with index r, take the negative of the previous one, then divide by r(r − 1).

Compare with the solution

a5 = −a3/(5 × 4) = −0/20 = 0. This link belongs to the odd chain arising from 0 velocity.

a6 = −a4/(6 × 5) = −(1/24)/30 = -1/720. The previous coefficient is positive; taking its negative produces the negative sign. The coefficients are dimensionless because y and u are dimensionless.

The law imposes the relation 30a6 + a4 = 0. Changing a6 freely would break this relation, even if the initial position and velocity remained correct.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain 0 and -1/720, with the products 20 and 30 in the denominators before simplifying.
  • You justify the sign and the relation imposed by the law, rather than simply copying the pattern.

Exercise 2 · Application · calculation and unit check

Look at the same motion earlier

Calculate the position using the polynomial of degree 4 at t = 0.5 s. Give an exact fraction in centimetres, then a decimal reading. Also calculate the error bound from the first omitted term, and compare it with the example’s bound.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with u = ωt. Here, the value 0.5 s becomes u = 1/2, with no unit.

Hint 2

Calculate u squared, then u to the power 4. The bound uses the power 6, and the physical result must still be multiplied by A.

Compare with the solution

u = 1 × (1/2) = 1/2. The powers are u squared = 1/4, u to the power 4 = 1/16, and u to the power 6 = 1/64.

The quadratic term is (-1/2) × (1/4) = -1/8. The term of degree 4 is (1/24) × (1/16) = 1/384. The sum gives P4(1/2) = 1 + (-1/8) + 1/384 = 7/8 + 1/384 = 337/384, with no unit.

Approximate position: 2 × (337/384) = 337/192 cm, or approximately 1.755208 cm. The change from the initial position is smaller than at 1 s, which is consistent with this early stage of the motion.

Since |u| ≤ 1, the alternating series bound applies. It is (1/360) × (1/64) = 1/23040 cm, or approximately 0.000043 cm. This is the example’s exact bound of 1/360 cm divided by 1/(1/64). Closer to the expansion point, the omitted terms are smaller here; this comparison does not measure the accuracy of the real spring.

Check your reasoning

  • You substitute the time into the dimensionless time variable before calculating the powers.
  • You obtain 337/384 with no unit, then 337/192 cm.
  • You justify the exact bound of 1/23040 cm using the omitted power and the alternating signs.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · decide with a guarantee

Require a tighter tolerance

At t = 1 s, the required error is now at most 0.0001 cm. Is the bound used for degree 4 enough to certify this? Examine degree 6: calculate a8, then the new physical bound. Finally, give the bounds between the truncations of degrees 6 and 8.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

An upper bound that is too large does not by itself prove that the actual error exceeds the tolerance: it cannot guarantee that the tolerance is met.

Hint 2

After degree 6, the first nonzero omitted term is the one of degree 8. Its coefficient is the negative of a6 divided by 8 × 7.

Compare with the solution

The bound of 1/360 cm exceeds 0.0001 cm: this check does not certify degree 4 for the new requirement. By itself, it is not enough to establish the exact size of the error.

The recurrence gives a8 = −(-1/720)/(8 × 7) = (1/720)/56 = 1/40320. The term is positive. At u = 1, the new bound is A × a8 = 2 × (1/40320) = 1/20160 cm, or approximately 0.000050 cm.

The exact comparison 1/20160 < 1/10000 is enough to certify degree 6 under this model, because the alternating signs and decreasing magnitudes still hold at this time. The approximate position remains 389/360 cm; the bound is not added as a compulsory correction.

The truncation of degree 6 is below the sum and the one of degree 8 is above it. The new upper bound is 389/360 + 1/20160 = 4357/4032 cm. Therefore 389/360 cm ≤ x(1 s) ≤ 4357/4032 cm. The bounds are exact fractions; numbers rounded too early could improperly narrow this interval.

Check your reasoning

  • You distinguish “not certified by this bound” from “error proved too large”.
  • You calculate 1/40320 with no unit and 1/20160 cm, then compare this bound with 1/10000 cm.
  • You justify which truncation is above or below the sum using the sign of the first omitted term.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What distinguishes a power series solution from its truncation?

The series adds powers indefinitely. Its convergence and differentiation must be justified for it to be a solution. The truncation keeps only a finite polynomial; we then control the difference from the sum using an error bound.

Recall 2Which coefficient of y produces the coefficient of uⁿ in y″, and with what factor?

It is the coefficient with index n + 2, multiplied by (n + 2)(n + 1). The second derivative lowers the degree by 2. Aligning the powers before comparing gives the recurrence.

Recall 3Why are the odd coefficients 0 for our cart?

0 initial velocity imposes a1 = 0, then the recurrence passes this 0 value on to all the odd indices. The initial position imposes a0 = 1: the even chain does not vanish. This result depends on the chosen initial conditions.

Recall 4What conditions allow a bound using the first omitted term?

The nonzero terms alternate in sign, decrease in absolute value, and tend to 0. On |u| ≤ 1, our series satisfies these conditions. The dimensionless bound on y must be multiplied by A to give an error on x in centimetres.

Recall 5Is the residual P″ + P the position error?

No. It measures the polynomial’s discrepancy in the reduced equation; the position error compares A P(ωt) with x(t). A nonzero residual shows that the polynomial is not exact, but does not directly give this error in centimetres.
