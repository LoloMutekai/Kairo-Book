Language

Your goalBreak down the understanding of a sentence into words, relations and context, then justify what remains ambiguous.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

At the park, you read: “The guide watches the visitor with the glasses.” All the words are familiar. Yet draw the scene: is the guide looking through the glasses, or watching a visitor carrying them? Recognizing each word has not yet settled how they relate.

The lexicon is the set of known words and the information associated with their meaning and use. Syntax concerns their organization: which groups go together, and how they relate. Semantics concerns the meaning of words and constructions. Here, “with the glasses” can specify the means of watching or describe the visitor.

Pragmatics concerns interpretation in a communication situation: who is speaking, about what, with what shared information and for what purpose. If we see the guide using the glasses, this context helps us choose a reading. If we only know that a tour is taking place, the ambiguity remains. A plausible expectation is not an observed fact.

Language allows us to construct and understand organized messages. Speech is a vocal form of language; the written sentence in our scene also uses language. Here we study the understanding of meaning, without measuring voice quality, hearing or a brain capacity. Difficulty with this isolated sentence does not allow any diagnosis.

The scene and the counts are fictional. We compare only 2 readings, named A and B, in the literal sense specified below. The diagrams separate questions to help us reason; they describe neither timed stages of brain activity nor brain regions.

The rule to remember

Work out who does what, specify which group the phrase attaches to, then check each reading against the available context. Keep the ambiguity when this context does not settle it.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From words to a justified reading

The sentence is still “The guide watches the visitor with the glasses.” At that same moment, the guide is looking through the only pair of glasses; the visitor is not carrying any. These facts are given as reliable for the exercise. We introduce no other person, figurative meaning or change of moment.

  1. Fix the words and their referents

    A referent is what we are talking about in the situation. “The guide” and “the visitor” refer here to the people in the scene; “the glasses” refers to the optical instrument, rather than drinking vessels. This choice of lexical meaning is an explicit assumption shared by everything that follows.

    Recognizing the name of each object is not enough: we still need to relate these objects to the action. We do not infer from familiar vocabulary that the whole message is understood.

  2. Reconstruct who does what

    In this active sentence, “the guide” is the subject of the verb “watches” and refers to the person watching. “The visitor” is its direct object and refers to the person being watched. The phrase “with the glasses” adds a relation that still needs to be specified.

    Syntax is not just about neighboring words: a group placed after “the visitor” can still attach to the action. This attachment is precisely what remains ambiguous here. The meaning of each word and the organization of the groups both contribute to the interpretation.

  3. Write out the readings before choosing

    A can be rephrased as: “Using the glasses, the guide watches the visitor.” The phrase indicates the instrument used for watching. B can be rephrased as: “The guide watches the visitor who is carrying the glasses.” The phrase describes the visitor.

    For B, “carrying” means in our model that the visitor actually has the instrument on their person at that moment. Each letter names a distinct reading; they are not grades. We have proposed 1 reading A and 1 reading B, giving N0 = 1 + 1 = 2 readings. The symbol N0 means the number before checking against the context.

  4. Turn the context into explicit tests

    Let c_A and c_B be the compatibility indicators: each equals 1 if the context does not contradict the corresponding reading, and 0 if it does. They have no unit; they are counting marks, not probabilities. An incomplete context can leave several indicators equal to 1.

    The context confirms that the guide uses the instrument: A remains compatible, so c_A = 1. It specifies that the visitor is not carrying it: B is contradicted, so c_B = 0. Each mark thus has a reason stated in words; we do not choose it to obtain a desired total.

    cA=1;cB=0

  5. Count and check by another route

    N denotes the number of readings kept, measured in readings. Addition is appropriate because the letters denote distinct items in the list and each item is counted at most 1 time. The calculation is N = 1 + 0 = 1 compatible reading.

    N=cA+cB=1+0=1

    R denotes the number of readings ruled out, in the same unit. By subtraction: R = N0 − N = 2 − 1 = 1. Let us check by returning directly to the sentences: the list kept contains A; the list ruled out contains B. The 1 kept and 1 ruled out give the original 2 proposed, with no omission or duplicate.

  6. State exactly what the result establishes

    Under the stated assumptions and among the proposed readings, the context selects A. The initial sentence, presented alone, remains ambiguous. Our result therefore depends on added facts and the list examined; it does not turn “with” into a mandatory marker of an instrument.

    The procedure describes an analysis of understanding that can be checked: identify the words, organize their relations and check possible meanings against a situation. It does not prove that the brain performs this addition, or that every speaker necessarily meant A. If the facts are uncertain, ask for clarification rather than hide doubt behind the count.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the solution. Rephrase A and B, show how their attachments differ and cite the fact that rules out B. Then explain why the same calculation would no longer be justified if we only knew that a tour was taking place at the park.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I know the words, so the meaning is settled.”

    Keep the words from the scene and write the rephrasings A and B. The vocabulary is identical, but “with the glasses” does not attach to the same group. Correct this by specifying who watches whom and what role the instrument plays.

    Return to the referents and attachments

  • “The closest group must take the phrase.”

    The proximity of “visitor” makes B possible, without imposing B. A attaches the phrase to the action of watching. Compare the rephrasings, then look for a fact in the context; a rule about neighboring words does not replace this check.

    Review readings A and B in the diagram

  • “1 reading remains, so I am certain I have understood.”

    This total depends on a restricted list and facts treated as reliable. It assesses neither your level nor a person’s actual intention. If the context is vague or contradicts the proposed readings, keep that finding and ask for a rephrasing.

    Test an insufficient context, then a contradictory one

Hint 2

Compare with the solution

The visitor is watching; the guide is being watched. In this construction, changing the positions of the noun groups has exchanged their roles. The same list of nouns therefore does not describe the same scene on its own.

Instrumental reading: the visitor uses the glasses to watch the guide. Descriptive reading: the visitor watches the guide who is carrying the glasses. No context is provided for this variant: the attachment remains undecided. Do not automatically transfer the choice A from the example to a sentence whose roles have changed.

Check your reasoning

  • You identify the visitor as the observer and the guide as the person being watched.
  • Your rephrasings distinguish the instrument of the action from the equipment carried by the person being watched.
  • You explain the ambiguity without inventing a fact.

Compatible means here “not contradicted by the facts provided”, rather than “proved true”.

Hint 1

Check the condition for A and the condition for B separately, keeping the same moment.

Hint 2

A sentence can have a correct grammatical structure while describing the given situation poorly.

Compare with the solution

A assumes that the guide uses the glasses; the context contradicts this, so c_A = 0. B assumes that the visitor is carrying them; the context also contradicts this, so c_B = 0. The total is 0 + 0 = 0 compatible reading among those proposed.

This signals a disagreement between our list of readings and the context taken to be true. This disagreement does not invalidate the grammatical construction of the sentence. In a real exchange, we would need to check the intended moment, the referent and the accuracy of the facts rather than diagnose the person.

We can ask: “Describe the scene again: who is watching whom, and where are the glasses at the moment you are talking about?” If the facts in the question are maintained, the sentence can be corrected to “The guide watches the visitor with the naked eye; the glasses are put away.” This rephrasing explicitly acknowledges the correction; it does not rescue the initial sentence by silently changing its meaning.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain 0 and explain separately why A and B are contradicted.
  • You distinguish a difficulty in description from a grammatical error or a diagnosis.
  • You propose a clarification followed by a consistent rephrasing, without secretly changing the facts.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why is knowing every word in our sentence not enough to understand it?

We also need to establish the relations between groups: who watches whom and what “with the glasses” attaches to. The lexicon provides information about words; it does not settle these relations on its own. Language can be used in writing, as well as in speech.

Recall 2Reconstruct readings A and B of the initial sentence without looking at the drawing again.

A: the guide uses the glasses to watch the visitor; the phrase specifies the means of the action. B: the guide watches the visitor who is carrying the glasses; the phrase describes the visitor. The words remain identical; their attachment differs.

Recall 3What difference do you make between context that settles the reading and a mere expectation?

A reliable fact about the scene can contradict a reading. An expectation about what usually happens can guide a preference without establishing that fact. “A tour at the park” leaves readings A and B open; the guide’s use of the instrument and its absence from the visitor’s person select A in our model.

Recall 4What does N = c_A + c_B calculate, and what does it not calculate?

Each indicator equals 1 if the reading is not contradicted by the facts, 0 otherwise. The sum counts the compatible readings in the stated list. It provides neither a probability of an intention, a comprehension grade, nor a model of brain computation.

Recall 5What should you do if the context leaves everything open, or contradicts all the proposed readings?

Keep the uncertainty or disagreement, check the assumptions and ask for clarification about the roles, objects and moment. A total does not resolve missing information. An isolated sentence does not allow us to conclude that someone lacks language ability.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the scene, then connect these questions: which words and which referents? Which groups and which roles? Which possible meanings? Which facts allow a reading to be kept or ruled out? Finish by explaining the limit of the count. An answer after a hint is an assisted answer; reading a page or recognizing a correction does not demonstrate any acquired level.
