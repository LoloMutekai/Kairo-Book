Level 2 · C043·M2

Retrieve, then assemble

Your goalTurn a list of retrieved excerpts into a useful context within a size limit, then check what was actually kept.

Before you start
c042-m5 : recall, ranking and evaluation · c021-m5 : origin, authentication and authorization · c009-m5 : reading notes · c018-m5 : contract tests and test doubles · c022-m5 : untrusted inputs and injection · c043-m1 : limited context and budget

✿01 See the idea

You are preparing a visit to a bookbinding room. Your question is specific: “Where do I collect the key, and where do I return it after reception closes?” A search retrieves several passages from the room handbook. Retrieving them is not enough: the right passages still need to go into the file that the person answering will read.

This file is the context: the information actually presented to prepare the answer. Retrieval produces a list of candidates; assembly builds what will actually be read. A sentence can rank very highly and repeat information already kept. Another can supply the missing detail while appearing further down the list.

The scene, handbook and budget are fictional. We limit the section reserved for excerpts to 160 characters. The instruction and question have their own space. Here, a character means a Unicode code point, counted by Python; it is neither a byte, a word nor a language model token. A token is a unit into which a particular model splits text. No fixed conversion rate turns this character count into a token budget.

The search has already ranked 4 excerpts: A, then B, then C, then D. We accept this order as a given in the exercise. All these passages are assumed to be authorized, current and applicable to this room. We reread their content to make our selection; we do not recalculate the ranking here.

The rule to remember

For each candidate in order: check which part of the need it covers, discard unnecessary repetition, then keep the whole block if it fits. Next, check the assembled text and the parts of the need that are still missing.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Build the file about the key

The question asks for the collection location and the return location after closing. The supplied ranking is A, B, C, D. The excerpt budget is M = 160 characters. We keep whole blocks in their selection order, adding no separator between them.

  1. Read the candidates before choosing

    Here are the exact fictional blocks. Each block starts at the opening square bracket and ends with the full stop followed by 1 LF newline character. The quotation marks and their display spacing are outside the block. The final newline is counted even though it is not visible in the paragraph.

    A: “[A] Collect the key for the binding room from reception.” Cost: 57 characters, including the final newline.

    B: “[B] To borrow the binding room key, please talk to the staff at reception.” Cost: 75 characters, including the final newline.

    C: “[C] After the reception desk closes, please return the key to the blue box near the exit.” Cost: 90 characters, including the final newline.

    D: “[D] The bookbinding paper is stored in the grey cabinet.” Cost: 57 characters, including the final newline.

    The cost counts the label, spaces, letters including any accented letters, apostrophes, punctuation and final newline. Together, the 4 blocks therefore take up 279 characters. We will not count only the sentences while forgetting their labels.

  2. Define the assembly contract

    M denotes the capacity of the excerpt section, in characters. The letter c with subscript A, B, C or D denotes the cost of the corresponding block, in the same unit. L denotes the total length kept; r denotes the remaining space. The subscripts identify a block; they do not mean multiplication. The sign ≤ means “less than or equal to”.

    L≤M;r=M−L

    Adding the costs is exact here because each block already includes its newline and no other character is inserted. A different assembly would require recounting the text actually produced. A real token budget would require applying the model’s text segmentation to the final input.

    Our local rule goes through the ranking, keeps an excerpt if it supplies requested information that is still missing and fits in full, then continues. It gives priority to the first useful passage encountered; it does not guarantee the best choice for every possible handbook. Relevance decisions are explained here rather than inferred from length.

  3. Keep the collection location and discard its repetition

    A explains collection at reception. It fits within the budget, so we keep it. We subtract its cost to find the space still available, without changing the passage.

    r=160−57=103 caractères

    B also discusses collection at reception. Its wording differs, but it adds nothing to what this question needs. We discard it as redundant; the remaining space stays at 103 characters. This does not mean B is false or useless for every other request.

  4. Add the return information and check the budget

    C supplies the other requested information: after reception closes, the key goes in the blue box near the exit. Its cost of 90 characters is less than the remaining 103 characters, so we keep it in full. The condition “after closing” must stay with the location.

    L=cA+cC=57+90=147 caractères
    r=160−147=13 caractères

    The capacity check gives 147 ≤ 160 characters. D concerns paper storage: it gives neither of the requested locations. We discard it because of its content, regardless of its length.

  5. Write what will actually be presented

    The prepared input contains an instruction, an excerpt section and the question. Suggested instruction: “Answer the question using the supplied excerpts; do not add any location that they do not mention.” The excerpt text is the following concatenation, in this order; each paragraph below represents a block with its final newline.

    [A] Collect the key for the binding room from reception.

    [C] After the reception desk closes, please return the key to the blue box near the exit.

    Question kept: “Where do I collect the key, and where do I return it after reception closes?” The instruction and question are outside the 160-character section studied here. They will also need to be counted when checking a complete input.

    Assembly means supplying these texts. The answer has not yet been produced. The labels in square brackets let us track the blocks in this exercise; their presence alone does not certify their origin. Attributing the information will be the subject of the next unit.

  6. Check the text and the need in two ways

    Length check: directly counting the characters in A followed by C gives 147 characters, matching the sum 57 + 90. Adding the remaining space recovers the capacity: 147 + 13 = 160 characters. The local script repeats this count on the exact strings.

    Content check: circle the collection location in A, then the condition and return location in C. The 2 blocks cover the parts of the question under the stated assumptions. A positive amount of remaining space is not enough to establish this.

    Finally, distinguish the retrieved list A, B, C, D, the assembled text A then C, and any answer written afterwards. A passage being present in the input proves neither that a model will use it correctly nor that the handbook describes the actual situation.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Describe each block’s route to the final input, then explain why discarding B makes room for different information. Repeat the capacity check and the content check separately.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The engine retrieved the passage, so the person answering must have received it.”

    Retrieval and assembly are distinct steps. B is in the search results but absent from the final context. To find out what was received, reread the assembled input after selection and application of the size limits.

    Reread the text actually presented

  • “I take the first passages until the space is full.”

    A then B take up 132 characters and explain only collection. They leave 28 characters, which is not enough for C. Reread the need before filling the space: A then C fit and supply complementary information.

    Diagnose selection based only on rank

  • “If it goes over the limit, I cut off the end of the last passage.”

    Cutting can remove the location, the condition or both. Our contract keeps whole blocks. If C does not fit, report its omission and the uncovered need; do not turn a sentence fragment into a complete instruction.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis · repair an assembly

The ranking was followed blindly

Someone keeps A and B because they come first. They then want to add C without removing anything. Calculate the length of A followed by B, the remaining space, then the amount over budget if C is added. Repair the selection without changing the budget of 160 characters or rewriting the blocks.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Do not confuse the space remaining after A with the space remaining after A then B.

Hint 2

Look for the block whose information is already covered by another.

Compare with the solution

A followed by B takes up 57 + 75 = 132 characters; 160 − 132 = 28 characters remain. C requires 90: it does not fit in that remaining space.

With C, the length would be 132 + 90 = 222 characters; the amount over budget would be 222 − 160 = 62 characters. Remove B, whose fact is already in A, then insert C in full. This restores 147 characters kept and 13 characters free.

A then B fitted, but did not cover return. A then C use more space and cover the rest of the need. Neither length alone nor the number of passages is enough to judge the selection.

Check your reasoning

  • You calculate 132 characters, then 28 characters remaining, before testing C.
  • You find that blocks A, B and C together exceed the budget by 62 characters.
  • You remove B for a reason based on content and preserve the condition in C.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · state a limitation

The section becomes too small

The excerpt budget decreases by 30 characters: it is now 130 characters. The question, blocks, order and rule stay the same. What selection do you obtain? Calculate the extra space needed to add C after A, then give the minimum budget that would allow A and C to be kept in full. Explain what information is missing from the current input.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

After A, subtract its cost from the new budget. B remains redundant and D remains off topic.

Hint 2

Compare the cost of C with the remaining space; the minimum budget for these whole blocks is their combined length.

Compare with the solution

After A, 130 − 57 = 73 characters remain. B is discarded as redundant. C costs 90 characters; 90 − 73 = 17 extra characters are needed to add it. D is off topic. The rule therefore keeps only A.

Collection at reception is explained. Return after closing does not appear in this input: keeping the words “key” and “reception” is not enough to cover that part. This omission must be reported when preparing the context.

For the given blocks A and C, without rewriting them, the minimum budget is 147 characters, or 130 + 17. We can request this capacity or search for another complete, more focused passage. This minimum concerns these blocks and this section; it proves no universal minimum for answering the question.

Check your reasoning

  • You find 73 characters available after A and 17 extra characters needed for C.
  • You keep only A under the given rule, without truncating C.
  • You report return after closing as an uncovered need and limit your minimum to these whole blocks.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What output does retrieval produce, and what output does assembly produce?

Retrieval produces a list of ranked candidates. Assembly produces the text actually presented, after selection and a capacity check. A retrieved candidate may be absent from this input.

Recall 2Why discard B and keep C in the key scenario?

After A, B repeats collection at reception. C adds the return location and its condition of being after closing. Selection depends on the need covered, as well as rank.

Recall 3What must a block’s cost count, and why is the sum exact here?

The label, spaces, text, punctuation and final newline. Each block already includes its separator and nothing is inserted between them: the length of A followed by C is 57 + 90 = 147 characters. A token budget would require a different counter applied to the final input.

Recall 4Is a context that fits within its budget necessarily sufficient?

No. A then B fit but do not explain return after closing. Capacity, the presence of the requested information and its conditions must be checked separately. Even a complete context does not prove that a later answer will be correct.
