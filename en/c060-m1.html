Level 2 · C060·M1

Transformations

Your goalFind the distribution of a squared deviation, preserving probabilities and counting every inverse branch.

Before you start
c035-m5 : covariance and conditioning in finite models · c058-m5 : change of variables · c046-m5 : approximation error

✿01 See the idea

A print head aims at a line. Its lateral deviation X is negative to the left and positive to the right. The report keeps only Y, the square of this deviation: an error of -1 mm and an error of 1 mm give the same score of 1 mm². The score measures the size of the deviation, penalizing larger deviations more heavily; it removes the sign.

We adopt a fictional model: before each print considered, X has a uniform distribution between -2 mm and 2 mm. This means that the probability of an interval is its length divided by the total width, 4 mm. This is neither a machine reading nor a distribution fitted to measurements.

Transforming a variable means applying the same rule to each of its possible outcomes. Probabilities follow the outcomes: if several deviations lead to the same region of scores, their contributions combine. The rule is deterministic; no additional noise is added.

Before looking at the formulas, look at the second diagram: the central region of deviations maps to the beginning of the score axis. Squaring distorts lengths. A uniform distribution on the deviation axis therefore does not automatically give a uniform distribution on the score axis.

The rule to remember

To find the probability of a region after transformation, find all the deviations that lead to it. To obtain a density, also account for the change of scale on each branch.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The distribution of the squared printing deviation

Starting with the uniform deviation X on [-2; 2] mm, determine the distribution of the score Y = X squared, then calculate the probability that this score lies between 1 and 4 mm². The calculations use the fictional model stated above.

  1. Name the quantities and the initial distribution

    X is the random variable for deviation, in millimetres; x is a possible value of X. Y is the score variable, in square millimetres; y is a possible value of Y. The function g describes the transformation: g(x) = x squared. Here, the support means the interval of possible values: for X it is [-2; 2] mm, and for Y it is [0; 4] mm².

    The density of X, written f with subscript X, is 1/4 per millimetre on this interval, and 0 elsewhere. A density is not a probability: its integral over an interval gives the probability of that interval. The width is 2 − (-2) = 4 mm; multiplying this width by 1/4 per millimetre does give 1, with no unit.

    fX(x)=14 par millimètre, sur le support de X

    In the formulas, x and y are the numerical coordinates expressed in these fixed units. P denotes a probability, with no unit. The model describes one print; no assumption of independence between prints is needed here.

  2. Transform events before densities

    Let B be an interval of scores. The event Y in B occurs exactly when g(X) belongs to B. To calculate its probability, we therefore integrate the density of X over all deviations that lead to B. The symbol ∈ means “belongs to”; dx indicates that we integrate over deviations x.

    P(Y∈B)=P(g(X)∈B)=∫g(x)∈BfX(x)dx

    For 0 ≤ y ≤ 4, the inequality X squared ≤ y is equivalent to −√y ≤ X ≤ √y. The square root √y is nonnegative and is expressed in millimetres. The interval has length 2√y; dividing it by the total width gives the cumulative distribution function, written F with subscript Y: F_Y(y) = P(Y ≤ y).

    FY(y)=2y4=y2, sur le support de Y

    Below 0 mm², F_Y equals 0; from 4 mm² onwards, it equals 1. At the reference value y = 1 mm², the interval is [-1; 1] mm: its length 2 mm gives 2/4 = 1/2, or 0.50 with no unit.

  3. Count every inverse branch and its scale factor

    In the interior 0 < y < 4, the solutions of g(x) = y are x = −√y and x = √y. The squaring function is monotone on each side of the origin, but not on the whole support: a single inverse is insufficient.

    On a monotone branch with a continuous, nonzero derivative, the change of variables gives a contribution equal to the initial density divided by the absolute value of the derivative of g. With several branches, we add the contributions. In the formula, ∑ runs over all solutions x in the support; g′ is the derivative of g, and the bars | | denote its absolute value. Here this formula applies to interior values, where the inverse branches are differentiable.

    fY(y)=∑x:g(x)=yfX(x)|g′(x)|

    Here g′(x) = 2x; its unit is the millimetre, since g gives square millimetres from millimetres. On each branch, its absolute value is 2√y. The reciprocal of this factor is in inverse millimetres: multiplied by the density of X, it gives a density per square millimetre.

    fY(y)=142y+142y=14y

    This density is 0 outside the support. At y = 1 mm², |g′| is 2 mm, the reciprocal factor is 1/2 per millimetre, and each branch contributes 1/8 per square millimetre. Their sum is 1/4 per square millimetre. Differentiating F_Y(y) = √y/2 gives exactly the same density: this provides another check.

  4. Calculate the requested probability using deviations

    The score belongs to [1; 4] mm² when X belongs to [-2; -1] mm or [1; 2] mm. These intervals do not overlap: their probabilities add. Each length is 2 − 1 = 1 mm, so each contribution is 1/4.

    P(1≤Y≤4)=14+14=1/2

    Result: 0.50 with no unit. We divided lengths in millimetres by a width in millimetres; the score itself remains expressed in square millimetres.

  5. Check using the integral and at the boundaries

    An antiderivative of the interior density is √y/2. Between 1 and 4 mm², it again gives 2/2 − 1/2 = 1/2. The central complement has probability 1/2; 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. The whole distribution must also have total mass equal to 1.

    ∫0414ydy=[2y4]04=1

    The density becomes arbitrarily large near 0, but this integral remains finite; at the lower boundary, we interpret it as a limit. The change of variables formula cannot be used at x = 0, where g′ vanishes. Nevertheless, there is no point mass at Y = 0: this would require X = 0, an event with probability 0 under this continuous distribution. We can choose the density value exactly at the boundary arbitrarily without changing the distribution.

    A transformation can create a point mass if it maps an entire interval of deviations to the same score. Squaring does not do this. Our conclusion that there is no point mass depends on this transformation and this initial distribution.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Uniform before squaring, so uniform afterwards.”

    Probability follows the original deviations, rather than the length along the new axis. The score interval [0; 1] mm² covers a quarter of the support in length, but receives probability 1/2. Transform the events, then account for the local scale.

    Compare the segments on the two axes

  • “The inverse is √y, so one branch is enough.”

    On the support containing both signs, −√y also maps to y. The contribution from the negative branch is positive because of the absolute value of the derivative. One branch alone carries only mass 1/2 instead of 1.

    Repair the incomplete density

  • “A very high density at the lower end means a very large probability at a point.”

    Probability comes from an area. Here P(Y = 0) = 0, even though the density grows without bound nearby. The cumulative distribution function and the integral at the boundary let us check this.

Exercise 1 · Application · one interval

A score closer to the origin

The print head keeps the same model. Calculate P(Y ≤ 1/4 mm²). First give the corresponding deviations, then an exact fraction and its decimal form. Can this probability be obtained by evaluating only the density at the threshold?

Hint 1

The square root of 1/4 mm² is 1/2 mm: check its square.

Hint 2

Find both endpoints of the deviation interval, then divide its length by 4 mm.

Compare with the solution

The score is at or below the threshold exactly when -1/2 mm ≤ X ≤ 1/2 mm. The square of 1/2 mm is 1/4 mm². The length is 1 mm, so the probability is 1/4 = 1/4, or 0.25 with no unit.

At the threshold, |g′| is 1 mm and the reciprocal factor is 1 per millimetre. Each branch contributes 1/4 per square millimetre; the total density is 1/2 per square millimetre, or 0.50 per square millimetre. This number describes a local density, which differs from the area 1/4. The cumulative distribution function confirms the area: 1/2/2 = 1/4.

Check your reasoning

  • Find the signed endpoints and the interval length.
  • Obtain 1/4 with no unit from a ratio of lengths.
  • Distinguish a density value from the cumulative probability.

f~(y)=142y

Hint 1

The antiderivative of this contribution is √y/4. Here the symbol ~ denotes the proposal to be examined.

Hint 2

Compare the resulting mass with 1, then find the other solution of x squared = y.

Compare with the solution

The integral of the proposed density is 2/4 = 1/2. It does not total 1; positivity is therefore insufficient. The initial density described deviations on both sides of the line, but the proposal has transformed only the positive side.

The branch x = −√y contributes the same positive amount. We must add it: the sum gives the density 1/(4√y), and its integral is 1. Here, doubling the proposal repairs the omission because the contributions are equal; for an asymmetric initial distribution, we would need to recalculate each contribution.

Check your reasoning

  • Show the calculation of mass 1/2.
  • Identify the missing negative branch precisely.
  • Recover mass 1 by adding the contributions, without assuming they are equal in every other model.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · respond to an objection

Is the score enough to recover the deviations?

In the same setting, another fictional adjustment produces a deviation X⁺ uniform on [0; 2] mm, always to the right. The + sign labels this other variable. Its score is Y⁺ = X⁺ squared. Someone claims: “If Y⁺ has the same distribution as Y, then the deviations have the same distribution.” Check the distribution of the scores and respond to the objection.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The new width is 2 mm: find the density of X⁺ before using its single inverse branch.

Hint 2

Compare P(X > 0) and P(X⁺ > 0), then compare the cumulative distribution functions of the scores.

Compare with the solution

The density of X⁺ is 1/2 per millimetre. For 0 < y < 4, only one branch is possible: x = √y. Its contribution is (1/2)/(2√y) = 1/(4√y), exactly the density of Y. At the reference value 1 mm², it is 1/4 per square millimetre, as before.

We can check the whole distribution without differentiating: P(Y⁺ ≤ y) = P(X⁺ ≤ √y) = √y/2 on the support, with the same values outside it, 0 and 1. The scores do have the same distribution.

Yet P(X > 0) = 1/2, whereas P(X⁺ > 0) = 1. The distributions of the deviations differ. Squaring has lost the sign information; knowing its distribution is insufficient to reconstruct the original signed distribution. This counterexample uses no conditioning.

Check your reasoning

  • Recalculate the initial density on the new support.
  • Recover the same score distribution using the single branch or the cumulative distribution function.
  • Give an event involving deviations whose probabilities differ, then explain the loss of sign.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you calculate the probability of an interval B for Y = g(X)?

Find all x whose image g(x) belongs to B, then calculate the probability of this set using the distribution of X. The transformation preserves the probability of the event identified in this way.

Recall 2Why does squaring require two contributions in our model with both signs?

For a positive interior score y, −√y and √y are both in the support of X. Their positive contributions add. Restricting the support to the positive side would change the number of branches and the initial density.

Recall 3What factor accompanies a differentiable inverse branch?

The absolute value of the derivative of the inverse, equivalent to the reciprocal of |g′(x)| when g′ does not vanish. It accounts for the change in length; transforming only the density values is insufficient.

Recall 4Why does a density that grows without bound near the origin not give a point mass here?

Probability is an integral over an interval, and the probability of a point X = 0 is 0. Squaring maps only this point to Y = 0; its density remains integrable. We also check that the total mass is 1.

Recall 5Is the distribution of a square enough to recover the distribution of the signed variable?

Not in general. In the exercise, deviations distributed on both sides and deviations that are always positive give the same score distribution. A transformation that combines distinct values can lose information.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the steps: define the distribution and support of X; find the deviations that lead to an interval of Y; count the branches; account for their scale using the derivative; check the units and total mass. Finish by explaining what information squaring has erased.
