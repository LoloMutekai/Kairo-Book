Level 4 · C237·M2

Fourth moment

Your goalCalculate the fourth moment of normalized noise energy and justify what it allows us to conclude about its Gaussian approximation.

Before you start
c236-m5 : Hörmander · c228-m5 : incompleteness · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c237-m1 : Stein’s method

✿01 See the idea

A stationary sensor still produces a fluctuating voltage. To track the energy of this noise, we add the squares of its deviations from the mean. Even if each deviation follows a Gaussian distribution, its square is not Gaussian: it is positive and amplifies large amplitudes.

In our fictional scene, a block contains 8 independent readings. We subtract the mean energy, then divide by the standard deviation of the sum. The resulting variable is centered and has variance 1. These adjustments put different block sizes on the same scale, without guaranteeing the shape of a normal distribution.

The fourth moment looks at the mean of the deviations raised to the power 4. It gives large amplitudes a great deal of weight. For a centered Gaussian variable with variance 1, its value is 3. For our normalized energy, it is 4.50: the excess over the Gaussian reference is 1.50.

Why look at this single number? Within a Wiener chaos of fixed order, a specific Gaussian structure links this excess to the approximation error. The theorem concerns this structure and a sequence of normalized variables. An isolated fourth moment calculated for an arbitrary distribution does not certify that it is Gaussian. The numbers below are results of the fictional model, never readings from the real sensor.

The rule to remember

Center and standardize first; check the Gaussian chaos of fixed order; compare the fourth moment with 3; turn the excess into a conclusion whose assumptions are stated.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The energy of a block of 8 readings

The sensor is stationary. Its voltages Vᵢ are assumed to be Gaussian and independent, with known mean μ and known strictly positive standard deviation σ, both in volts. We want to approximate the distribution of the standardized energy of a whole block, without inventing observed voltages. The parameters are not estimated from the block being studied.

  1. Define the variable and its unit

    The index i runs through the readings in the block, from 1 to n. Set Zᵢ = (Vᵢ − μ)/σ: these variables are independent, have distribution N(0, 1), and are dimensionless. The sum of the squared voltage deviations is expressed in volts squared: this is our energy indicator, without conversion to joules. We work with the squares of Zᵢ, so all the quantities that follow are dimensionless.

    Yi=Zi2−1,Sn=∑i=1nYi,Fn=Sn2n

    Yᵢ is an individual centered energy; Sn is their sum. E denotes expectation under the model. We will check that Var(Sn) = 2n, which will justify the denominator of Fn. For n = 8, its square root is 4.

  2. Obtain the moments of the individual energy

    For a standard Gaussian variable Z, integration by parts gives E[Zg(Z)] = E[g′(Z)] for a polynomial g. Taking g to be an odd power gives the recurrence below; r is a positive integer and the moment of order 0 is 1.

    E[Z2r]=(2r−1)E[Z2r−2]

    The moments of orders 2, 4, 6, 8 are therefore 1, 3, 15, 105. The moment of order 6 comes from 5 × 3; that of order 8, from 7 × 15. Subtracting 1 centers Y, since E[Z squared] = 1. Its variance is 3 − 2 × 1 + 1 = 2.

    E[Y4]=105−4×15+6×3−4×1+1

    We expanded the power 4 of Z squared minus 1, then took the expectation term by term. This gives 105 − 60 + 18 − 4 + 1: 45, then 63, then 59, and finally 60. The moment 105 concerns the normalized voltage; 60 concerns its centered energy.

  3. Add the contributions that survive

    The Yᵢ are centered and independent. The variance of Sn is therefore n × 2 = 16. In the expansion of its power 4, any product containing an index that appears only once has 0 expectation. The remaining terms involve a single index or a pair of indices, each appearing 2 times.

    E[Sn4]=nE[Y4]+3n(n−1)E[Y2]2

    Why the coefficient 3? An unordered pair allows 6 placements of the indices among the 4 factors. The number of pairs is n(n − 1)/2, giving 6/2 = 3. For our block, n − 1 = 7 and n(n − 1) = 56. The individual contribution is 8 × 60 = 480. The contribution from pairs is 168 × 4 = 672. Their sum is 1152.

  4. Normalize and perform an independent check

    Dividing Sn by 4 divides its variance by 16, but its fourth moment by 4 to the power 4, which is 256. We obtain E[Fn] = 0 and Var(Fn) = 1.

    mn=E[Fn4]=1152256=9/2=4.50

    mn denotes the normalized fourth moment. Its excess κn = mn − 3 is 3/2, or 1.50. For a centered variable X, the fourth cumulant denotes E[X to the power 4] − 3 Var(X) squared. Cumulants add for independent variables: this provides another way to check.

    For Y, it is 60 − 12 = 48. For Sn, 8 × 48 = 384. After standardization, 384/256 = 3/2, matching κn. Keeping n symbolic, the same operations give mn = 3 + 12/n and κn = 12/n.

  5. Read the fourth moment theorem precisely

    Recall the framework from C235·M3: a Wiener chaos of order q is the closed subspace generated by products of Hermite polynomials of total degree q, built from the same independent Gaussian family. It does not contain all arbitrary polynomials of degree at most q. The Hermite polynomial of order 2 is H(Z) = Z squared minus 1. Thus Fn belongs to the chaos of order 2, whatever the block size n.

    The theorem states the following: fix an integer q at least equal to 2. For a sequence Fk belonging to this same chaos, with mean 0 and variance 1, convergence in distribution to a Gaussian variable G with distribution N(0, 1) is equivalent to convergence of its fourth moment to 3. k is the sequence index; q stays fixed while k varies.

    Fk→loiG⇔E[Fk4]→3

    This is an asymptotic equivalence under a structural assumption. The necessity uses the control of higher moments specific to a chaos of fixed order: arbitrary convergence in distribution would not be enough to ensure convergence of moments. We recall the general theorem here; the following steps directly prove its conclusion and a bound for our family of energies, without developing the contractions covered in the next unit.

  6. Link the energy to Stein’s identity

    To measure an error between distributions, take the Wasserstein distance d_W(Fn, G): the supremum of the differences |E[h(Fn)] − E[h(G)]| over functions h such that |h(x) − h(y)| ≤ |x − y|. It is dimensionless here. It is a distance between distributions, not a probability that the sensor works correctly.

    Stein’s equation from the previous unit is f′(x) − xf(x) = h(x) − E[h(G)]. For these functions h, its solution f satisfies a uniform bound |f′| ≤ 1. Approximation by smooth functions allows us to apply the identities below; Gaussian moments ensure the required integrability.

    The derivative of Fn with respect to Zᵢ is 2Zᵢ/√(2n). Apply integration by parts to Zᵢf(Fn): E[(Zᵢ squared − 1)f(Fn)] = E[Zᵢ ∂ᵢf(Fn)]. Summing and dividing by √(2n) gives the following identity.

    E[Fnf(Fn)]=E[τnf′(Fn)],τn=1n∑i=1nZi2

    τn is the mean of the normalized squares. In these Gaussian coordinates, the Malliavin derivative D is the gradient; its squared norm is the sum of the squares of the partial derivatives. We recover τn = ∥DFn∥ squared /2. The link with Stein therefore comes from a calculated identity, not from similar-looking curves.

  7. Turn the excess into a justified bound

    E[τn] = 1 and Var(τn) = 2/n: each square has variance 2, and independence eliminates the covariances. Stein’s equation and |f′| ≤ 1 give an error of at most E[|1 − τn|]. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality bounds this expectation by the square root of Var(τn).

    dW(Fn,G)≤2n=mn−36

    For n = 8, Var(τn) = 1/4 = 0.25, giving d_W ≤ 1/2 = 0.50. Check: the square of this bound is 1/4, equal to 1/6 × 3/2. We know an upper bound; we have not calculated the exact distance.

    As n tends to infinity, κn = 12/n tends to 0 and the bound tends to 0: normal convergence is proved for this family. The readings must remain independent and Gaussian, with μ and σ known. Estimating these parameters from the same block would change the variable being studied. On a Poisson space, a derivative adds a point rather than following a Gaussian variation; transferring this proof and this bound requires additional work.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I compare the fourth moment of the sum directly with 3.”

    The reference 3 concerns a centered variable with variance 1. The sum Sₙ has variance 16; its fourth moment must be divided by 256. Dividing only by the variance would leave the normalization incomplete.

    Review the normalization and the check using cumulants

  • “A fourth moment close to 3 always proves a normal distribution.”

    State the structure before the conclusion: the same Wiener chaos, fixed order, centering and normalized variance. The theorem concerns a sequence; the finite calculation requires a bound. A mixture of chaoses, a varying order or a jump model requires another justification.

    Reread the assumptions of the theorem

  • “The kurtosis displayed by a program is the proof we need.”

    Our calculation concerns a theoretical expectation under a known model. A descriptive number calculated from readings is random, depends on its variance convention and may even be a fallback value. Reread its calculation and conditions before assigning it a probabilistic conclusion.

Compare with the solution

E[Y] = 1 − 1 = 0. The variance is E[Y squared] = 3 − 2 × 1 + 1 = 2. All these quantities are dimensionless, since Z is a voltage divided by its standard deviation.

E[Y to the power 4] = 105 − 4 × 15 + 6 × 3 − 4 × 1 + 1 = 60. The number 105 is only the term coming from Z to the power 8; subtracting the mean energy introduces all the other terms.

Exercise 2 · Complete application

The same sensor, a longer block

Now take n = 32 readings under the same assumptions. Recover the variance of Sₙ, the normalization divisor, the fourth moment of Fₙ, its excess and the Wasserstein bound. Keep fractions until the end.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Hint 1

The variance of the sum is 2n. Check the divisor by squaring it.

Hint 2

Reuse mₙ = 3 + 12/n and the bound √(2/n), then check the fourth moment by expanding the sum.

Compare with the solution

Var(Sn) = 32 × 2 = 64. The divisor is 8, since 8 squared is 64. The fourth moment of Sn is 32 × 60 + 3 × 32 × (32 − 1) × 4 = 13824.

The divisor for the fourth moment is 8 to the power 4, which is 4096. Thus mn = 13824/4096 = 27/8 = 3.375; κn = 3/8 = 0.375. We recover 3 + 12/32.

Var(τn) = 2/32 = 1/16. The bound is therefore 1/4 = 0.25, whose square is 1/16. This bounds a distance between distributions; it is neither the exact distance nor a rate of correct readings.

Exercise 3 · Decision and critique of a conclusion

Choose a size, then check the claim

For the same energy indicator, what minimum integer block size guarantees, through our bound, d_W ≤ 1/4? A technician answers: “I repeated the same block of readings 8 times and my software displays an excess close to 0; the guarantee is achieved.” Examine both parts of the statement.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Both sides are positive: you can square the inequality √(2/n) ≤ 1/4.

Hint 2

Counting more stored values does not create new independent readings. An estimate of a moment does not replace the model’s expectation.

Compare with the solution

The inequality is equivalent to 2/n ≤ 1/16, so n ≥ 2/(1/16) = 32. This value is an integer, and equality in the bound is attained for n = 32. It is the minimum guaranteed by this bound; a smaller actual distance could meet the target with fewer readings.

Repeating the same block violates independence among the added readings: the covariances no longer vanish in the variance of τn. The nominal number of stored readings is therefore not enough. The displayed excess is also descriptive, calculated from observed values; our proof uses a theoretical moment and a verified chaos structure.

To repair the conclusion, we need new readings consistent with the model, known μ and σ, and the corresponding theoretical calculation. The displayed value can help raise a question about the model; it does not restore the missing assumptions.

Check your reasoning

  • I give 32 as the minimum size certified by the bound, without claiming it is necessary for the actual distance.
  • I explain precisely the role of independence that has been lost.
  • I distinguish a theoretical fourth moment, a descriptive statistic and observation of actual operation.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1To compare a centered variable X with the Gaussian reference 3, what normalization must be applied to its fourth moment?

Divide E[X to the power 4] by Var(X) squared, with strictly positive variance. This amounts to calculating the fourth moment of the variable X/√Var(X).

Recall 2Which assumptions make the fourth moment decisive in the theorem recalled here?

A sequence in the same Wiener chaos of fixed order q, with q at least equal to 2, centered and with variance 1. Convergence of the fourth moment to 3 is then equivalent to convergence in distribution to the standard Gaussian distribution.

Recall 3Why do pairs of indices appear in the fourth moment of the sum of centered energies?

Any isolated index makes the expectation 0 through independence and centering. The remaining terms involve a single index or pairs where each index appears 2 times; their total coefficient is 3n(n − 1).

Recall 4In our family of sensor energies, how does the excess κₙ control the distance to the Gaussian distribution?

κₙ = 12/n and Var(τₙ) = 2/n = κₙ/6. Stein’s identity and Cauchy–Schwarz give d_W ≤ √(κₙ/6). This is an upper bound on a distance between distributions, dimensionless here.

Recall 5Is an empirical kurtosis close to the reference enough to apply this guarantee?

No. We must check the variable being studied, the normalization convention and the model assumptions. A theoretical expectation and a statistic from a finite block are different; an extension to a Poisson space requires an appropriate proof.

Reconstruct the reasoning
