Level 3 · C169·M3

Supermartingale

Your goalVerify that an adapted sequence is a supermartingale by comparing, block by block, the forecast of the next value with the current value, then distinguish this property from a simple decrease in the mean.

Before you start
c168-m5 : Radon-Nikodym · c129-m5 : backward induction · c130-m5 : financing conditions · c169-m1 : filtration · c169-m2 : martingale

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a hypothetical urn with 19 equally likely slots: 9 slots give a gain of one token and 10 slots give a loss of one token. You start with 10 tokens and play independent rounds, restoring the urn to its original state after each draw. The tokens, the urn and all their numbers are hypothetical: they are used for calculations and do not describe a real game.

After one round, your wealth can rise to 11 tokens or fall to 9. Yet before the draw, the best forecast of your next wealth is 9.947 tokens, which is less than 10: on average, one round reduces your wealth by 0.053 of a token. This is the idea of a supermartingale: it does not promise that every path decreases; it states that the forecast for tomorrow, made using what is known today, does not exceed today’s value.

What is known at time n is the information available that day, denoted by Fn. The successive information sets F0, F1, F2… form the filtration from C169·M1: each contains the previous one. Wealth Xn is adapted because it is known as soon as Fn is known. The forecast for tomorrow is the conditional expectation E[Xn+1 | Fn]: the average of Xn+1 over the draws compatible with today’s information, weighted by their probabilities. Here, the set of draws is finite: this is just a calculation with fractions.

E[Xn+1|Fn]≤Xn

The martingale from C169·M2 is the equality case: the forecast equals today’s value. A supermartingale only requires the inequality ≤: today’s value is above what is expected, hence the prefix super. A martingale is therefore a special case of a supermartingale. Reversing the inequality gives a submartingale.

One precaution applies throughout this page: the inequality must hold for every possible information set, block by block, rather than only for the average over all paths.

The rule to remember

Supermartingale: at each time and for every possible information set, the forecast of the next value does not exceed the current value; equality everywhere gives a martingale.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Two rounds with the hypothetical urn

A hypothetical urn has 19 equally likely slots: 9 give a gain of one token and 10 give a loss of one token. Two independent draws are played, starting from X₀ = 10 tokens. Let us verify step by step that the wealth sequence X₀, X₁, X₂ is a supermartingale for the filtration generated by the draws.

  1. Set out the information, the sequence and the inequality

    The possible outcomes of two rounds are the sequences GG, GP, PG and PP, with G denoting a gain and P denoting a loss.

    The information F0 is empty: no draw is known, and a single block contains all sequences. The information F1 reveals the first draw: there are two blocks, the sequences starting with G and those starting with P. The information F2 reveals both draws. Each information set is finer than the previous one: this is the filtration.

    Xn is wealth, in tokens, after n draws; X0 = 10. It is adapted because a block of Fn fixes its value. There are finitely many sequences, so each Xn is bounded and therefore integrable.

    On a block of Fn, E[Xn+1 | Fn] is the average of Xn+1 over the sequences in that block, weighted by their probabilities. The sequence is a supermartingale if the following inequality holds on every block, for n = 0 and then n = 1. The game assumes independent draws, constant probabilities and a stake of one token; these assumptions come from the game, rather than the definition.

    E[Xn+1|Fn]≤Xn

  2. Give the probabilities and wealth values after one round

    Each draw lands in one of the 19 slots. The probability of G is 9/19, or approximately 0.474; the probability of P is 10/19, or approximately 0.526. Their sum is 1, which checks the slot count: 9 + 10 = 19.

    Pr(G)=919,Pr(P)=1019

    After the first draw, wealth is 10 + 1 = 11 tokens for G, or 10 − 1 = 9 tokens for P.

  3. Verify the condition at time 0

    At time 0, F0 has only one block: the forecast of X1 is the average over the whole urn. Block G contributes 9/19 × 11 = 99/19 and block P contributes 10/19 × 9 = 90/19.

    E[X1|F0]=919×11+1019×9=99/19+90/19=189/19

    This total is approximately 9.947 tokens, less than X0 = 10 tokens: the condition holds at time 0. The gap has a simple explanation. A draw returns +1 with probability 9/19 and −1 with probability 10/19; the difference 10/19 − 9/19 = 1/19, or approximately 0.053 of a token, is lost on average. This gives 10 − 1/19 = 189/19, as expected.

  4. Verify the condition at time one, block by block

    At time one, F1 has two blocks, which must be treated separately. On block G, current wealth is 11 tokens. The next draw is independent of the previous draws: it adds −1/19 of a token on average. The forecast is therefore 11 − 1/19 = 208/19, or approximately 10.947, which does not exceed 11.

    E[X2|F1] sur le bloc G=11−1/19=208/19

    On block P, current wealth is 9 tokens and the same reasoning gives 9 − 1/19 = 170/19, or approximately 8.947, which does not exceed 9.

    E[X2|F1] sur le bloc P=9−1/19=170/19

    The three blocks encountered, one at time 0 and two at time one, satisfy the inequality: the sequence is a supermartingale over these two times. Independence of the draws gives the same average loss on each block; the definition does not require this.

  5. Check by listing the four sequences

    Check by another method: list the sequences. GG has probability 81/361 and X2 = 12; GP and PG each have probability 90/361, giving 180/361 for both together, with X2 = 10; PP has probability 100/361 and X2 = 8. These three probabilities 81/361, 180/361 and 100/361 sum to 1.

    E[X2|F0]=81/361×12+180/361×10+100/361×8=972/361+1800/361+800/361=188/19

    The block method gives the same number: 9/19 × 208/19 + 10/19 × 170/19 = 1872/361 + 1700/361 = 188/19, or approximately 9.895 tokens. This also equals 10 − 2/19, since 2/19 is twice 1/19. All three methods agree: the calculation is consistent.

  6. Conclude and state the scope

    On each block, the forecast does not exceed current wealth: the wealth sequence is a supermartingale. It is not a martingale because the inequality is strict. Consequently, the overall means decrease: 10, then 9.947, then 9.895 tokens.

    This decrease in the means follows from the property; it does not define it. A path can rise: the sequence GG reaches 12 tokens. The calculation concerns a hypothetical urn and two rounds. It says nothing about a real game or about stopping the game at a chosen time, the subject of C169·M4 and C169·M5.

Explain it in your own words

Why check the inequality on block G and on block P instead of only comparing the mean of X₁ with the mean of X₂? Then write down where independence of the draws was used.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A supermartingale is a decreasing sequence.”

    The constraint applies to the conditional forecast rather than to each path. In the example, a first winning draw takes wealth from 10 to 11 tokens, and the sequence remains a supermartingale.

    Review the calculation block by block

  • “If the expectation decreases from one time to the next, the sequence is a supermartingale.”

    The inequality must hold on every information block. In the counterexample in the last exercise, the expectation falls from 9.947 to 9.917 tokens, yet one block has a forecast of 210/19 that is greater than 11.

    Work through the counterexample again

  • “A fair game is not a supermartingale because it loses nothing on average.”

    The inequality is non-strict: equality is allowed. Every martingale is a supermartingale; the converse is false. The fair coin in the exercise gives a forecast equal to current wealth.

    Reread the definition and its equality case

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

A fair coin

In this hypothetical game, replace the urn with a fair coin: a gain or loss of one token, each with probability 1/2, starting from 10 tokens. Calculate the forecast of X₁ at time 0. Is the sequence a supermartingale, a martingale, or both?

E[X1|F0]=1/2×11+1/2×9

Hint 1

Calculate the two products, then add them and compare the total with the starting wealth.

Compare with the solution

Block G contributes 1/2 × 11 = 11/2 and block P contributes 1/2 × 9 = 9/2. Their sum is 10 tokens, exactly 10.

The forecast equals current wealth: the non-strict inequality is satisfied with equality. The sequence is a martingale and therefore also a supermartingale. It is not strictly decreasing in mean.

Check your reasoning

  • Forecast calculated as a weighted average.
  • Equality recognized as a case allowed by the non-strict inequality.
  • The conclusion “both” is justified.

Exercise 2 · Application with explanation

A doubled stake for the second round

Return to the urn with 19 slots. The second round uses a stake of 2 tokens, announced before the draw: a gain or loss of 2 tokens. Calculate the forecast of X₂ on each block of F₁, then the forecast of X₂ at time 0. Does the supermartingale inequality still hold?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The average loss in one round is multiplied by the stake.

Hint 2

Treat block G, then block P, then take the weighted average.

Compare with the solution

The average loss is 2 × 1/19 = 2/19 of a token. Block G: 11 − 2/19 = 207/19, less than 11. Block P: 9 − 2/19 = 169/19, less than 9.

Weighting gives 9/19 × 207/19 + 10/19 × 169/19 = 1863/361 + 1690/361 = 187/19, or approximately 9.842 tokens. The condition holds on both blocks: the sequence remains a supermartingale. Since the stake is announced before the draw, it depends only on information already known.

Check your reasoning

  • Forecast calculated separately on each of the two blocks.
  • The stake multiplies the average loss, rather than the probabilities.
  • Conclusion based on both blocks.

Exercise 3 · Counterexample to verify

The mean decreases, the property fails

After a first winning draw, the second round uses a favorable urn: 10 gain slots and 9 loss slots, with a stake of one token. After a first losing draw, the stake is 2 tokens in the original urn. Calculate the forecast of X₂ on each block of F₁, then the forecast of X₂ at time 0. Compare it with the forecast of X₁, then conclude.

E[X2|F1] sur G=11+1/19,E[X2|F1] sur P=9−2/19
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In the favorable urn, the average gain in one round is +1/19.

Hint 2

Compare each block’s forecast with its current wealth before taking the average.

Compare with the solution

Block G: 11 + 1/19 = 210/19, greater than 11: the inequality fails on this block. Block P: 9 − 2/19 = 169/19, less than 9.

The forecast of X2 at time 0 is 9/19 × 210/19 + 10/19 × 169/19 = 1890/361 + 1690/361 = 3580/361, or approximately 9.917 tokens. The forecast of X1 is 189/19, or approximately 9.947, and the gap is 11/361, or approximately 0.030 of a token: the mean decreases.

Yet the sequence is not a supermartingale because block G violates the inequality. A decrease in the overall mean does not replace verification by blocks.

Check your reasoning

  • Each block’s forecast is compared with its current wealth.
  • The block that violates the inequality is identified.
  • A decrease in the mean is distinguished from the conditional property.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which inequality defines a supermartingale?

For an adapted and integrable sequence, the conditional expectation of the next value, given today’s information, does not exceed today’s value. This must hold at each time, on every information block.

Recall 2How are martingales and supermartingales related?

A martingale is the equality case. Every martingale is a supermartingale; the converse is false, as shown by the hypothetical urn, which reduces wealth by 0.053 of a token per round.

Recall 3Why is comparing only the overall means insufficient?

The property applies to every information block. A mean can decrease while a block has a forecast greater than its current value, as in the counterexample where the mean falls from 9.947 to 9.917 tokens.

Recall 4Can a path of a supermartingale rise?

Yes. Only the conditional forecast is constrained, rather than each realization: in the example, the sequence GG leads from 10 to 12 tokens.

Recall 5Which assumptions in the example come from the game rather than the definition?

Independence of the draws and constant probabilities: they give the same average loss on every block. The definition requires only the conditional inequality.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Set out the information Fₙ and the adapted sequence Xₙ; calculate the weighted average of the next value on each block; compare it with the block’s current value; conclude that the sequence is a supermartingale only if the inequality holds everywhere, with equality giving a martingale; then relate the overall means without substituting them for verification by blocks; state the game’s assumptions.
