Hinge loss

Your goalCalculate the hinge loss of a batch, explain why a correct classification can still be penalized, and recognize the threshold where the slope changes.

Before you start
c059-m6 : Deterministic discrete Bellman · c099-m5 : probability calibration · c040-m5 : least squares · c140-m1 : margin

✿01 See the idea

A workshop wants to route its parcels for fragile or standard handling. Its model produces a score: the positive side selects “fragile”, and the negative side selects “standard”. Predicting the correct class does not tell us whether the parcel is far from the decision boundary. A small change in the score could still move it to the other side.

Hinge loss penalizes the shortfall from a signed margin of 1. Here, loss means a mathematical cost to reduce. A parcel on the wrong side is penalized; a parcel on the correct side but too close to the boundary is still penalized; a parcel that has reached the required margin has no further cost under this loss.

Our single scenario is a fictional batch of 4 parcels, A, B, C and D. A and B are fragile; C and D are standard. The labels are assumed to be known and reliable for this exercise. The visual features, scores and all values are fictional and have no physical units. They provide no measure of the quality of a real sorting process.

The first diagram compares the parcels after accounting for their true class. B is correctly classified, but its loss remains positive. C receives the same score as B with a different label: the same side of the boundary works against it. The second diagram shows why the loss is called a hinge: the descending line meets a plateau at the required threshold.

The rule to remember

Orient the score using the true label, subtract this margin from 1, then keep the larger of this shortfall and 0. Calculate the loss for each parcel before taking the average.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Evaluate the batch before changing the model

The fictional model uses a visual feature x and the score f(x) = wx + b, with w = 1/2 and b = 0. gvgkvg zvqrtt ndpk The true labels are 1 for A and B, -1 for C and D. Let us evaluate the average loss of this fixed model without claiming to have trained it.

  1. Define what the score means

    x is the parcel’s visual feature, w is the coefficient multiplying it, and b is the score offset. f is the name of the function and f(x) is its value for this parcel. These quantities have no physical units. A positive score predicts “fragile”, and a negative score predicts “standard”; by convention, we assign a 0 score to “fragile”. No score in the batch is at this tie.

    y denotes the true label, encoded in the set { -1, 1 }. This is a class known for the calculation, rather than the model’s prediction. We keep the same encoding, parameters and score scale across the whole batch. B and C have the same feature value but different classes: this feature alone cannot distinguish them.

    f(x)=wx+b

    Since b = 0, the scores are calculated using products alone: A gives 1/2 × 3 = 1.50; B gives 1/2 × 1 = 0.50; C gives 1/2 × 1 = 0.50; D gives 1/2 × (-2) = -1.

  2. Orient each score toward the true class

    The signed margin m is the product of the label and the score. The factor y preserves the sign of the score for a fragile parcel and reverses it for a standard parcel. A strictly positive margin therefore means that the score favors the correct class; a strictly negative margin means that it favors the wrong class.

    m=yf(x)

    The margins are therefore m_A = 1 × 1.50 = 1.50; m_B = 1 × 0.50 = 0.50; m_C = (-1) × 0.50 = -0.50; m_D = (-1) × (-1) = 1. C is the only misclassified parcel.

    m is a functional margin, meaning that it is measured on the score scale. It is neither a physical distance nor a probability. Changing the size of the parameters can change m even if the signs of the predictions stay the same; the second exercise will let you check this.

  3. Turn a margin shortfall into a loss

    We require m to be at least 1. The raw shortfall is 1 − m. If it is negative, the required margin has been exceeded: keeping this shortfall as a cost would reward that excess. We therefore replace negative shortfalls with 0. The symbol ℓ denotes the individual loss, and max selects the larger of its arguments.

    ℓ(m)=max(0,1−m)

    We can reconstruct the formula without memorizing it. Let ξ, the Greek letter xi, be the dimensionless margin compensation. Require ξ ≥ 0 and m + ξ ≥ 1. This last condition requires ξ ≥ 1 − m. The smallest compensation satisfying both conditions is precisely ℓ(m). This reasoning explains the maximum and the floor at 0.

    ℓ(m)={1−msi m<10si m≥1

    The margin threshold 1 sets a scale convention. The loss is not bounded by 1: a very negative margin produces a larger cost. All losses on this page are dimensionless, on this conventional scale.

  4. Calculate A and B: two correct classifications, two costs

    For A, the shortfall is 1 − 1.50 = -0.50. It is negative: the maximum selects 0, giving ℓ_A = 0. We do not keep the negative shortfall as a reward.

    For B, the shortfall is 1 − 0.50 = 0.50. It is positive: the maximum keeps it, giving ℓ_B = 0.50. B is correctly classified, but its margin does not yet meet the requirement. Reducing its loss can therefore encourage clearer separation without correcting a wrong classification.

  5. Calculate C and D: the wrong side and equality

    For C, first preserve the sign of the margin: 1 − (-0.50) = 1.50. Subtracting a negative margin increases the shortfall. The maximum keeps this value, giving ℓ_C = 1.50, greater than 1.

    For D, the margin is exactly 1. The shortfall is 1 − 1 = 0 and ℓ_D = 0. Equality does belong to the region of 0 loss. D reaches the margin; it is not on the decision boundary, which corresponds to a 0 score.

  6. Add, average, then check

    i denotes a parcel in the batch, n is the number of parcels, and L is their average loss. Here n = 4. The letter Σ asks us to add the losses across all parcels, from index 1 to index n. We give them all the same weight.

    L=1n∑i=1nℓ(mi)

    In pairs, A and B cost 0 + 0.50 = 0.50; C and D cost 1.50 + 0 = 1.50. The total is 0.50 + 1.50 = 2. Dividing by the number of parcels gives L = 2/4 = 0.50, dimensionless.

    Individual check: all losses are nonnegative; m_A + ℓ_A exceeds 1, while m_B + ℓ_B, m_C + ℓ_C and m_D + ℓ_D reach exactly 1. Each loss is the smallest admissible compensation. Total check: B and C are the only contributors, and their losses do add up to 2.

    L evaluates this model on this fictional batch. It is neither an error probability, a measure of performance on new parcels, nor evidence that training has occurred. The last exercise shows why each shortfall must be clipped before averaging.

  7. Read the slope and the point where it changes

    Consider the loss as a function of m. The slope, the change in loss per unit of margin, is -1 as long as m is below 1: increasing the margin by a given amount reduces the loss by the same amount, provided we stay on this branch. For m above 1, the slope is 0. The branch values meet at the threshold, so the loss is continuous there.

    At m = 1, the slopes from the left and right differ: there is no single ordinary derivative at this hinge. The curve is convex: the line segment between two points on the curve stays on or above it. We can see this here because the slope increases as we move from the descending branch to the plateau.

    The slope -1 concerns m, rather than the score f(x) directly. For a standard parcel, increasing the score reduces the margin because y = -1: as long as the loss is active, this increases the cost. The penalty indicates what should be improved; by itself, it does not provide the entire training procedure for a support vector machine. Controlling the size of the parameters and choosing an algorithm also require their own conditions.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Why does B have a cost even though it is correctly classified? Why does C’s positive score work against it? Reconstruct the loss from the conditions on ξ, then explain exactly what happens at the margin threshold.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The class is correct, so the loss must be 0.”

    The correct sign is enough for classification, but the loss requires a margin of at least 1. B has margin 0.50 and loss 0.50. Start again from the two thresholds: the boundary at 0, and the required margin at 1.

    Compare B and D, then the two thresholds in the diagram

  • “I take the maximum of the score and the threshold; the label is no longer needed.”

    First multiply the score by the true label. For C, y = -1 and the score is 0.50, so the margin is -0.50 and the loss is 1.50. The maximum then acts on 0 and the shortfall 1 − m. Do not cap a loss greater than 1.

    Review the orientation of the score and the calculation for C

  • “I can average the margins before applying the loss.”

    The maximum removes negative shortfalls separately. Taking the average before applying it would let a very well separated parcel offset a penalty that the individual loss should have kept. Calculate both expressions in the last exercise.

    Compare the two orders of calculation

Exercise 2 · Comparison · calculation on the batch

Is doubling the scores enough?

Return to the entire original batch. Multiply w and b by 2, and therefore all scores by 2, without changing the labels or the margin threshold 1. Before calculating, predict the classes. Then calculate the new margins, individual losses and average. Does eliminating B’s cost prove that the batch’s cost has decreased?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A strictly positive factor preserves the sign of the score and multiplies each margin by the same factor.

Hint 2

B reaches the hinge. For C, the increased positive score favors the wrong class even more.

Hint 3

After clipping, only C contributes to the new sum.

Compare with the solution

The predicted classes stay the same: multiplying a nonzero score by 2 preserves its sign. The margins become, in the order A, B, C, D: 3, 1, -1 and 2.

A, B and D reach or exceed 1: their losses are 0. For C, the shortfall is 1 − (-1) = 2, so its loss is 2. The new losses are 0, 0, 2, 0.

The average is 2/4 = 0.50, exactly the same as the original model’s 0.50. B no longer contributes, but C costs more. In this batch, the reduction in B’s cost is fully offset by the increase in C’s cost.

The loss depends on the score scale, even when the classes stay the same. This comparison shows neither an optimal model nor a real improvement. The identical feature values of B and C with opposite labels explain why amplifying their shared score cannot satisfy both margins at once.

Check your reasoning

  • You predict unchanged classes before doing the calculations.
  • You recover the new margins, then C’s cost of 2.
  • You calculate the average instead of drawing a conclusion from parcel B alone.
  • You explain the role of the opposite labels of B and C.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis · order of operations

Repair an average calculation

Someone uses the original batch, adds the margins, divides by 4, then applies max(0, 1 − average margin). They claim to have calculated the batch’s average loss. Repeat their calculation, compare it with L, and explain the repair. Do not use the doubled scores from the previous exercise.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the margins with their signs: C contributes negatively.

Hint 2

The claimed calculation applies the maximum once. The definition of L applies it separately to each parcel.

Hint 3

For A, the negative shortfall is removed before taking the correct average; in the claimed calculation, it can still offset other shortfalls.

Compare with the solution

The sum of the margins of A and B is 2; that of C and D is 1/2. Together, they total 5/2. Their average is 5/2/4 = 5/8 = 0.625.

The loss of this average margin is max(0, 1 − 5/8) = 3/8 = 0.375. It differs from the correct average L = 1/2 = 0.500. The difference is 1/8 = 0.125, dimensionless.

A’s raw shortfall is -0.50. The individual maximum replaces it with 0. When we average the margins first, this negative shortfall remains in the total and offsets part of the shortfalls of B and C. We have changed the quantity being measured.

Repair: for each parcel, calculate y × f(x), form 1 − this margin, take the maximum with 0, and only then add and divide by the number of parcels. The average of the losses and the loss of the average may coincide on other batches; our counterexample is enough to refute their general interchangeability.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain 5/8 for the original average margin.
  • You distinguish 3/8 from 1/2 and calculate their difference.
  • You locate the error in placing the maximum after the average.
  • You explain concretely the role of A’s negative shortfall.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why multiply the score by the true label before evaluating the loss?

With labels -1 and 1, m = y × f(x) becomes positive when the score favors the correct class. For a standard parcel, a positive score therefore produces a negative margin.

Recall 2Reconstruct the formula from the conditions on the compensation ξ.

We need ξ ≥ 0 and m + ξ ≥ 1, hence also ξ ≥ 1 − m. The smallest compensation is ℓ(m) = max(0, 1 − m). It is dimensionless on the chosen score scale.

Recall 3When does a correctly classified parcel still have a positive loss?

A margin strictly between 0 and 1 gives the correct class but a positive loss: B has margin 0.50 and loss 0.50. A 0 margin also gives a loss of 1; the class then depends on the tie convention. At the margin threshold 1, the loss becomes 0.

Recall 4What are the slopes of the loss as a function of m, and what happens at the hinge?

For m below 1, the slope is -1; for m above 1, it is 0. At m = 1, the loss is continuous but has no single ordinary derivative. These slopes concern the margin, rather than directly the score for a given class.

Recall 5In what order should you evaluate a batch, and what does this result fail to demonstrate?

Calculate each signed margin, then each clipped loss; add the losses and divide by the number of parcels. On our fictional batch, L = 0.50. This average demonstrates neither training, an error probability, nor the quality of the model on new parcels.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without support: start with the score and the true label; orient the score into a margin; find the smallest nonnegative compensation needed to reach 1; derive the loss; calculate each loss before the average. Include the case of a correct classification that is still penalized, the point where the slope changes, and one limit on what the batch’s cost lets you conclude.
