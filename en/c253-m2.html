Level 4 · C253·M2

Conditional calibration

Your goalCalibrate the leverage using the average variance at the same price, check the resulting variance and explain why this calculation must be reassessed when the simulated distribution changes.

Before you start
c252-m5 : model risk · c239-m5 : calibration and identifiability · c182-m5 : trinomial tree and admissibility · c220-m5 : numerical reproducibility · c198-m5 : independent validation · c253-m1 : leverage function

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional simulator, the same unit of an asset can reach 100 € along very different paths. Some paths arrive with small fluctuations, others with large ones. To adjust the fluctuations at a price of 100 €, we must look at the paths that reach that price: paths that reach 120 € answer a different question.

Here, a particle is a possible state of the simulator, described by its price and the instantaneous variance of its relative changes. We freeze 4 equally probable particles at the same instant. The 2 priced at 100 € have variances of 0.04 and 0.16 per year; the other 2 are priced at 120 € and have variances of 0.25 and 0.35 per year. These numbers are entirely fictional.

The leverage multiplies the amplitude of the fluctuations. Its square therefore multiplies their variance. The local target requires a variance of 0.09 per year at the chosen price. The idea is to divide this target by the average variance of the paths at that price, then check the resulting product.

We work with a single time slice. This task involves neither choosing the best financial product nor calculating an option price. Before looking at the result, predict whether the leverage will need to amplify or reduce the fluctuations of the group at 100 €.

The rule to remember

First condition on the price, estimate the average variance, set the square of the leverage, then check the projected variance for this same group.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Adjust the slice at a price of 100 €

At t = 1 year, the target local model provides a volatility σloc = 0.30 per square root of a year at price s = 100 €. Particles A, B, C and D are those in the diagram, each with probability 1/4. Let us find the nonnegative leverage and check its projected variance.

  1. Name the objects and the constraint

    t is time in years; St is the random price, in euros; s is a fixed price, in the same unit. Vt is the instantaneous variance of relative changes, expressed per year. L(t,s) is the dimensionless deterministic leverage, chosen to be nonnegative. The amplitude of price fluctuations is s × L(t,s) × √Vt, in euros per square root of a year. The target local volatility σloc(t,s) has units of inverse square root of a year; its square is a variance per year.

    Q denotes the pricing distribution, called risk neutral. E_Q is the mean under this distribution, and the vertical bar means “given that”. Define m_L(t,s) as the mean of Vt given St = s under the model using L. The symbol m denotes a mean, rather than a number of particles. The constraint to satisfy is:

    mL(t,s)=EQ[Vt∣St=s]
    σloc2(t,s)=L2(t,s)mL(t,s)

    This relation compares instantaneous variances of relative changes. Once the price is fixed, L(t,s) no longer varies across the states in the group: its square can be taken outside the conditional mean. No independence between St and Vt is assumed. The continuous framework assumes a strictly positive price, a diffusion without jumps, the same drift as the local model, an admissible target and a finite, strictly positive conditional mean at the point studied. We use the local constraint; solving it numerically does not by itself demonstrate equality of all marginal distributions.

  2. Form the group before calculating the mean

    In our fictional discrete distribution, St = 100 € selects A and B. Their conditional probabilities are equal to 1/2: the equal initial probabilities are renormalized within the group. C and D are excluded. The empirical mean, denoted by m̂ with a hat, is:

    m^=0.04+0.162=0.202=0.10 par an

    We average the variances V, without taking their square roots or subtracting their mean: we are not calculating the variance of the V values. Here, m̂ is exact for the stated small discrete distribution. In a model with a continuous price, a fixed price can have probability 0: E_Q[Vt | St = s] then denotes a conditional expectation, rather than a naive selection by equality in a finite sample.

    In practice, this mean is estimated using nearby prices, for example within a window around s. A wide window mixes different states; a narrow window may contain too few states. The neighborhood rule, weights and resulting sample size must be stated. With unequal weights, we divide the sum of weighted variances by the sum of the retained weights.

  3. Express the target in the correct unit

    The given σloc is a volatility, whereas m̂ is a variance. We therefore square σloc: 0.30 × 0.30 = 0.09 per year. This calculation converts units of inverse square root of a year into inverse years. Dividing the volatility directly by m̂ would leave the leverage with a unit.

    The target of 0.09 per year is below the mean of 0.10 per year. The factor applied to the variance must be less than 1: we expect a reduction.

  4. Isolate the square of the leverage

    The mean m̂ is positive, so division is allowed. We set the square of the leverage by dividing the required variance by that of the frozen group:

    L2=σloc2m^=0.090.10=0.90;L=0.90

    The division cancels the units. The exact rational result for the square is 9/10. The leverage itself is the positive square root of this fraction; keeping it in this form avoids rounding. Do not use 0.90 as the amplitude: it is the variance factor.

  5. Check by returning to the particles

    Multiply the variances of A and B separately by 0.90. For A: 0.90 × 0.04 = 0.036 per year. For B: 0.90 × 0.16 = 0.144 per year. The projected conditional variance is then:

    0.036+0.1442=0.182=0.09 par an

    The residual, defined as projected variance minus target, is 0.09 − 0.09 = 0 per year. The direction of change matches our expectation: every variance in the group has decreased. The check recalculated a mean over the particles; it went beyond simply rereading the quotient.

  6. Show why the wrong conditioning fails

    If all the states are mixed together, their sum of variances is 0.20 + 0.60 = 0.80 per year and their mean is 0.80/4 = 0.20 per year. The incorrect square of the leverage becomes 0.09/0.20 = 0.45.

    At a price of 100 €, however, this factor gives 0.45 × 0.10 = 0.045 per year. This falls short of the target by 0.045 per year, compared with the target of 0.09. The global calculation is arithmetically correct; it addresses the wrong condition.

  7. Place this calculation within a calibration

    The subscript L in m_L reminds us that the joint distribution of St and Vt itself depends on the leverage function used to evolve the model. Even if the equation for V does not contain L, changing the paths of S can change which variance states are found at the same price. Evaluating the quotient on a frozen distribution gives a candidate update, rather than proof that this new function will reproduce the same mean after simulation.

    A procedure on a grid of times and prices estimates the means using the current leverage, proposes a new leverage, recalculates the distribution it induces and checks the residuals. Depending on the method, these operations are arranged as time advances or are iterated. A fixed point here means that the leverage and the means it produces jointly satisfy the constraint. The quotient identity guarantees neither existence, uniqueness nor convergence of this procedure.

    A 0 mean with a positive target permits no finite solution; an empty cell does not even provide a mean. A very small mean can produce a very large leverage. Widening the window, interpolating or imposing a floor changes the estimate or the constraint: this must be declared and the corresponding residual measured. Our example specifies none of these settings. The density equations belong to the next unit.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the final values. Reconstruct the group, the unit of the numerator and the quotient; then check the particles one by one. Explain why moving particles to the neighboring price can change the next leverage without changing the target.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “An average of all the variances is enough.”

    The leverage is set at price s. Repeat the selection by price before averaging: the global mean includes states outside the condition. The counterexample calculation produces 0.045 per year instead of 0.09.

    Review the selected group and the global counterexample calculation

  • “The quotient directly gives the leverage.”

    The quotient gives the square of L. Variances are multiplied by this square; the amplitude is multiplied by its positive square root. Also check that the numerator is the target volatility squared.

    Review the units and the square root that gives the leverage

  • “A 0 residual on these particles completes the calibration.”

    It confirms the algebra for the frozen distribution. A recalculated distribution can change the conditional mean; an empty cell does not justify any quotient. Reconstruct the test in which the mean changes and state what remains to be checked.

    Test the new mean and the empty cell

Exercise 1 · Application to the neighboring group

Same target, different condition

In the same frozen sample, now adjust the price of 120 €. The target local volatility there is also 0.30 per square root of a year. Calculate the conditional mean, the square of the leverage and the mean of the adjusted variances. First predict which price will require the greater reduction.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Keep only C and D. The variance target remains 0.09 per year.

Hint 2

Multiply each retained variance by the square of the leverage before recalculating the mean.

Compare with the solution

The neighboring group has higher variances, so it requires a greater reduction. Its mean is (0.25 + 0.35)/2 = 0.60/2 = 0.30 per year.

The square of the leverage is 0.09/0.30 = 0.30, less than 0.90. The positive leverage is the square root of 0.30, with no unit.

The adjusted variances are 0.075 and 0.105 per year. Their sum is 0.18, then their mean is 0.09 per year: a different leverage reaches the same target because the conditional group differs.

Check your reasoning

  • Select only C and D.
  • Square the target volatility before dividing.
  • Check the adjusted variances and explain the direction of change.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosing an update

The mean changes after recalculating the distribution

A new fictional pass of the same simulator gives, at a price of 100 €, a conditional mean of 0.20 per year. The target remains 0.09 per year. What does the old square of the leverage, 0.90, produce? Calculate the new candidate square, then say whether this new quotient proves convergence.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First check the old factor by multiplication, without repeating its division.

Hint 2

The new quotient uses the mean from the new pass. The distribution induced by the candidate leverage must then be checked.

Compare with the solution

The old factor produces 0.90 × 0.20 = 0.18 per year. The residual is 0.18 − 0.09 = 0.09 per year: the target is exceeded.

The new candidate square is 0.09/0.20 = 0.45. The frozen check gives 0.45 × 0.20 = 0.09 per year.

Since the mean doubled, the candidate square was divided by 2. This does not demonstrate convergence: this leverage can still change the distribution and therefore the mean at the next recalculation. The residuals must be checked using the distribution it induces, followed by sensitivity to discretization and conditional estimation.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the residual of the old factor in the unit of variance.
  • Recalculate the candidate square using the current mean.
  • Distinguish a check on a frozen slice from a consistency check after evolution.

Exercise 3 · Limitation and checking protocol

The price with no particle

Return to the initial particles. At a price of 110 €, the sample size is 0. A proposal is to replace the missing mean with 0, then divide the target of 0.09 by this number. Diagnose this proposal and describe a sequence of checks without inventing a leverage value.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Is a missing mean a mean variance of 0?

Hint 2

Separate what the sample allows you to calculate from the additional choice of a neighborhood rule.

Compare with the solution

The sample has no state at 110 €. Its empirical conditional mean obtained by exact selection is undefined: an empty sum divided by a sample size of 0 does not give a variance of 0. The proposed division is therefore unjustified and, with a 0 denominator, impossible.

The cell must be marked as not estimated. One possible sequence would be to state a neighborhood that retains nearby prices, its weights and its sample size; the mean would then need to be recalculated, other widths tested and the residuals checked. Interpolation from the existing groups would also be an additional assumption. Without a stated rule, no numerical leverage is determined at this price.

Even if another sample provided a true 0 mean, a positive target could not be reached with finite leverage. This mathematical obstruction is distinct from the lack of observations in our cell.

Check your reasoning

  • Do not confuse missing data with 0 variance.
  • Give no numerical quotient for the empty cell.
  • Propose a declared estimation rule with a check of its sensitivity.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why condition on the price before averaging V?

The local target concerns a given price and instant. We must average the variances of states matching this condition. The global mean can mix groups whose variances differ.

Recall 2What does the quotient of the target local variance and the conditional mean of V give?

The dimensionless square of the leverage, if the mean is finite and strictly positive. The positive leverage is its square root. The numerator is the square of the local volatility, rather than the volatility itself.

Recall 3How can the result be checked beyond simply rereading the quotient?

Multiply each variance in the group by the square of the leverage, recalculate the mean using the same weights and subtract the target. This check verifies the algebra and the group selected for the frozen distribution.

Recall 4Why write m_L rather than a mean independent of the leverage?

The leverage changes the price paths and can change the joint distribution of price and variance. The states found at the same price can therefore differ. The constraint must be checked using the distribution induced by the candidate leverage.

Recall 5Does a cell with no particle justify a mean variance of 0?

No. The empirical mean is not estimated there. A neighborhood rule or interpolation requires declared assumptions and checks. A true 0 mean with a positive target would be a different problem: no finite leverage would satisfy the constraint.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct this sequence: choose the instant and the price; form the condition and its weights; estimate the average variance; square the target; divide to obtain the square of the leverage; check the product on the particles; reassess the induced distribution and the cells where no estimate is available. Explain aloud what the frozen residual proves and what it leaves open.
