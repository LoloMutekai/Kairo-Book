Level 3 · C170·M2

Increments

Your goalCalculate a Brownian displacement, determine its distribution from its duration, and decide when several displacements are independent.

Before you start
c169-m5 : optional stopping under assumptions · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c170-m1 : construction and properties

✿01 See the idea

A suspended particle moves along a marked axis. You record its position, then ask: “How much has its position changed since my last reading?” This signed displacement is an increment. A position tells you where the particle is; an increment compares an end position with a start position.

In our fictional record, the particle moves from 10 μm to 11 μm, then to 9 μm, and finally to 13 μm. It moves forward, backward, then forward again. The symbol μm means micrometre. The times, positions and model coefficient are invented for the calculation; no experimental readings are presented.

The Brownian model without drift has no preferred direction. Before the reading, a displacement is random: its mean under the model is 0 and its spread depends on the duration. After the reading, you know one particular value. A 0 mean therefore does not force the particle to return to its starting point.

Two time windows of the same duration have the same displacement distribution, even if they start at different times. This is stationarity of increments. To assess independence, look at their overlap: successive disjoint windows give independent displacements; windows that share a portion reuse part of the same displacement.

The rule to remember

End position minus start position gives the realized increment. The duration gives its variance in the model. Here, the absence of overlap gives independence.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Read the displacements of a single particle

The model describes X(t) as homogeneous Brownian motion without drift, with X(0) = 10 μm and κ = 4 μm²/s. The times are fixed in advance. The fictional record gives X(1) = 11 μm, X(2) = 9 μm and X(6) = 13 μm. We will calculate displacements and spreads, then check the results, without estimating κ from this record.

  1. Separate the random variable from its recorded value

    X(t) is the random position at time t, expressed in μm; s and t are deterministic times in seconds, with 0 ≤ s < t. The duration h = t − s is in seconds. The symbol Δ means difference: here ΔX denotes X(t) − X(s), in μm. We will always specify the window concerned.

    κ is a displacement variance per unit time, strictly positive and constant, in μm²/s. The starting position is certain. We assume the Brownian model from the previous unit: continuous paths, 0-mean Gaussian increments that are independent on disjoint intervals. The model assumes these properties; the fictional readings do not prove them.

    h=t−s, ΔX=X(t)−X(s)

  2. Read the distribution before using the record

    The notation 𝒩(m, v) denotes the normal distribution with mean m and variance v; its second parameter is a variance. E denotes expectation, meaning the theoretical mean, and Var denotes variance. σ is the positive standard deviation, the square root of the variance, in μm. The symbol ∼ before 𝒩 means “has the distribution”: it describes the possible values and how they are distributed; it does not give the realized displacement.

    ΔX∼𝒩(0,κh)
    E[ΔX]=0, Var(ΔX)=κh, σ=κh

    The mean is in μm and the variance in μm². The product κh does have the latter unit. Stationarity means that the distribution depends on h, without depending on the start time s. It does not mean that the displacements will be equal. If κ were 1 in these units, we would recover the usual normalization with variance equal to duration.

  3. Subtract the positions and the times

    Let A be the displacement from 0 to 1 s, B the displacement from 1 to 2 s, and C the displacement from 2 to 6 s. A, B and C are random variables in μm; the following subtractions give their realized values in our record.

    Realized A: 11 − 10 = 1 μm; duration 1 − 0 = 1 s. Realized B: 9 − 11 = -2 μm; duration 2 − 1 = 1 s. Realized C: 13 − 9 = 4 μm; duration 6 − 2 = 4 s.

    The negative sign of B indicates backward movement along the axis. None of these calculations divides by duration: we are calculating displacements, not velocities. A and B have the same duration and therefore the same distribution; their realized values differ.

  4. Associate each window with its spread

    For A and B, the variance is κh = 4 × 1 = 4 μm². Their standard deviation is 2 μm, because 2 × 2 = 4 μm². For C, the variance is 4 × 4 = 16 μm², and the standard deviation is 4 μm, whose square is 16 μm².

    The duration of C is 4 times that of A. The variance is therefore multiplied by 4, but the standard deviation only by 2. This square root is essential: a squared measure of spread grows with duration, while a measure of spread expressed as a distance grows with its square root.

    The value 4 μm and the standard deviation 4 μm coincide here because of how the record was chosen. A realization need not equal its standard deviation and is not bounded by it. The normal distribution also allows displacements with larger absolute values.

  5. Compare realizations over different durations

    Let Z denote the standardized displacement: the displacement divided by its standard deviation. Z is dimensionless and follows 𝒩(0, 1), because the mean displacement is 0 and its variance is divided by σ squared. This compares displacements relative to their spreads; it does not give a likelihood percentage.

    Z=ΔXκh∼𝒩(0,1)

    A gives 1/2 = 1/2 = 0.50, with no unit. B gives -2/2 = -1. C gives 4/4 = 1. The displacement of C is greater in distance than that of A, but its duration also gives it a greater spread.

  6. Justify independence without confusing it with stationarity

    The windows (0, 1], (1, 2] and (2, 6] are disjoint. The notation (s, t] excludes the start and includes the end. The joining point is not a shared duration. The increments A, B and C are therefore mutually independent under the model: knowing some of them does not change the distribution of the others.

    More precisely, Fs denotes the information generated by all positions X(u), where u ranges over past times 0 ≤ u ≤ s. For deterministic times, X(t) − X(s) is independent of this past information. Its conditional mean E[ΔX | Fs] remains 0 μm: knowing the starting position does not give the future displacement. The symbol | means “given the information on the right”.

    The future position X(t) contains X(s) plus this increment; we are not declaring that position independent of the past. If the available information already contained a future observation, independence from that information would no longer follow. No time chosen in response to the path is studied here.

  7. Check the sum using endpoints and durations

    Over the whole window, the displacement is A + B + C. The intermediate positions cancel in this sum of differences. In the identity below, u denotes a fixed intermediate time, with s < u < t. In the record: 1 + (-2) = -1 μm, then -1 + 4 = 3 μm. The direct calculation 13 − 10 = 3 μm gives the same result. Starting again from 10 μm, we recover 13 μm.

    Under the model, independence allows us to add the variances: 4 + 4 + 16 = 24 μm². Another approach: the total duration is 6 − 0 = 6 s, so κh = 4 × 6 = 24 μm². The total displacement therefore follows 𝒩(0, 24), with positions in μm.

    The square of the realized total displacement is 9 μm²; the theoretical variance is 24 μm². The former is one particular result; the latter is the mean of the squared deviations from the mean displacement across all realizations of the model. This single record does not provide a reliable estimate of its distribution.

    X(t)−X(s)=[X(u)−X(s)]+[X(t)−X(u)]

Explain it in your own words

In the last identity, u is a fixed intermediate time, with s < u < t. Hide the calculations: explain why adding the displacements is an algebraic identity, whereas adding their variances requires an assumption. Then reconstruct the relationship between duration, variance and standard deviation.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The mean is 0: the particle must stay at its starting position.”

    The mean concerns the distribution before the reading. In our record, the total displacement is 3 μm despite a 0 expectation. Positive and negative realizations are compatible with this same 0-mean distribution.

    Separate the distribution from the realized record

  • “A duration 4 times as long gives a standard deviation 4 times as large.”

    It is the variance that follows this factor. Its square root goes from 2 to 4 μm, a factor of 2. Check by squaring the standard deviations and comparing the units.

    Recover the spread for each window

  • “If two increments differ, they are independent.”

    Different values do not prove independence. Independence concerns the joint distribution of the variables. Inspect the windows: U and V in the diagram share B, whose variance is positive. Their covariance will therefore be positive in the model.

    Decompose overlapping windows

Exercise 1 · Application · sign and distribution

Combine the first readings

Over the window from 0 to 2 s, calculate the realized displacement of the particle and its duration. Then give the distribution of this displacement under the model. Does the realized sign contradict a 0 mean?

Hint 1

Subtract the endpoint positions, then the endpoint times; you can ignore the intermediate position.

Hint 2

In 𝒩(m, v), replace m with the model mean and v with κ multiplied by the duration.

Compare with the solution

The duration is 2 − 0 = 2 s. The realized displacement is 9 − 10 = -1 μm. We also recover 1 + (-2) = -1 μm.

The variable X(2) − X(0) follows 𝒩(0, 8), with variance 4 × 2 = 8 μm². The negative realization does not contradict the 0 mean: the distribution allows displacements of either sign. The variance is not the square of this single value.

Check your reasoning

  • Duration 2 s and variance 8 μm².

Exercise 2 · Transfer · change the duration

Shorten the window without inventing a reading

A new reading of this particle is planned after a duration h = 1/4 s = 0.25 s. The coefficient κ remains 4 μm²/s. Calculate the variance and standard deviation of the displacement. Can its future value be deduced by scaling the realized displacement A in the same ratio as the durations?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate κh, then find the positive square root; squaring the result must give the variance again.

Hint 2

The same distribution does not require the same realization; the lines between the points in the first diagram give no additional readings.

Compare with the solution

Var(ΔX) = 4 × 1/4 = 1 μm². The standard deviation is 1 μm, because 1 × 1 = 1 μm². Reducing the duration by a factor of 4 therefore divides the standard deviation by 2, starting from 2 μm.

The distribution of the new displacement is 𝒩(0, 1). Its realization remains unknown. Scaling an observed displacement proportionally would construct an arbitrary straight path; it does not predict a Brownian increment. Even for a window within an already partially observed path, the intermediate position would not be determined in this way.

Check your reasoning

  • Variance 1 μm² and standard deviation 1 μm, with distinct units.
  • Check the result by squaring the standard deviation.
  • No future value invented from interpolation.

Exercise 3 · Reasoning · covariance

A portion of displacement counted twice

Return to the windows in the diagram: U = X(2) − X(0) and V = X(6) − X(1). Decompose them using A, B and C. Calculate their covariance, then the variance of U + V. Compare this with the result of incorrectly assuming independence.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

U = A + B and V = B + C. A, B and C have 0 means and are independent; only B appears in both sums.

Hint 2

Cov(U, V) = E[(U − E[U])(V − E[V])]. Expand the product; products of distinct increments have 0 expectation.

Hint 3

Var(U + V) = Var(U) + Var(V) + 2 × Cov(U, V). You can also write U + V = A + 2B + C.

Compare with the solution

U and V are random variables in μm. Cov denotes covariance, the mean of the product of their deviations from their means, in μm². Here their means are 0. With U = A + B and V = B + C, E[UV] = E[AB] + E[AC] + E[B × B] + E[BC]. The cross terms vanish because A, B and C have 0 means and are independent.

This leaves Cov(U, V) = Var(B) = κ × (2 − 1) = 4 μm². This positive covariance proves that U and V are not independent. Here it measures the shared duration multiplied by κ. Stationarity does not remove this overlap.

The durations are 2 s and 5 s, so Var(U) = 8 μm² and Var(V) = 20 μm². Var(U + V) = 8 + 20 + 2 × 4 = 28 + 8 = 36 μm². Assuming independence would give only 28 μm².

Var(A+2B+C)=4+22×4+16=36 μm²

This other approach checks the variance: multiplying B by 2 multiplies its variance by 4. In the record, U is -1 μm, V is 2 μm and their sum is 1 μm; these particular values are not used to calculate the theoretical covariance. 0 covariance would not, in general, be enough to prove independence; here a strictly positive covariance is enough to disprove it.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify B as the only shared portion and keep the same directions of movement.
  • Justify the vanishing cross terms using independence and 0 means.
  • Obtain 4 μm² for the covariance and 36 μm² for the variance of the sum.
  • Check using A + 2B + C, without confusing covariance with a product of realized values.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you define an increment, and what does its distribution give over a duration h?

ΔX = X(t) − X(s), h = t − s > 0. For Brownian motion without drift and with coefficient κ, ΔX follows 𝒩(0, κh): 0 mean, variance κh, displacement in μm in our scene.

Recall 2The duration is multiplied by 4: what happens to the variance and standard deviation?

With κ constant, the variance is multiplied by 4 and the standard deviation by 2, because σ = √(κh). A realized displacement does not necessarily follow these factors.

Recall 3Does the same duration mean the same value and independence?

Stationarity gives the same displacement distribution. It does not require equal values. Here independence comes from disjoint intervals and does not follow from stationarity.

Recall 4What is independent of the past at a deterministic time s?

The increment X(t) − X(s) is independent of Fₛ, the information generated by the positions up to s. The future position X(t) still contains X(s); we do not declare it independent of the past.

Recall 5Why do the windows U = A + B and V = B + C remain dependent?

They reuse the same increment B. With independent, 0-mean A, B and C, Cov(U, V) = Var(B), which is 4 μm² here. If this variance is positive, U and V are not independent.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without looking: start from two positions and their times; define the signed displacement and duration; state the distribution, its units and assumptions; distinguish variance from standard deviation; separate stationarity from independence; check a sum of displacements using its endpoints and a sum of variances using the shared portions.
