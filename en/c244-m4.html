Level 4 · C244·M4

Admissible interpolation

Your goalConstruct an interpolation between calibrated slices, justify the properties it preserves, and distinguish a global proof from a grid check.

Before you start
c243-m5 : SSVI · c051-m5 : error diagnostics · c220-m5 : numerical reproducibility · c198-m5 : independent validation · c244-m1 : initialization · c244-m2 : constraints · c244-m3 : quasi-explicit calibration

✿01 See the idea

You are preparing a call option price surface for a fictitious index. Prices are available for maturities of 3 and 9 months; a request arrives for 6 months. You need to fill this interval without creating a price inconsistent with its neighbors. A slice is the set of prices at a single maturity, across all strike prices.

At a fixed strike price, you can place the new price between those at the known dates. But these calculations must also remain consistent across strike prices: the price of a call option cannot increase as its strike price increases, and its price curve must be convex. Convex means that the curve stays below the chord joining any two of its points. The wings are the regions where strike prices are far from the forward price. A construction that respects each date separately can lose its convexity between dates.

The construction studied here mixes the prices of the endpoint slices with the same weights across the entire curve. This lets us add their convexity inequalities. We will also require the longer maturity slice to be at least as high as the shorter maturity slice everywhere, to preserve the ordering of prices over time.

The calculation will use a small test set of entirely fictitious prices. These numbers are not presented as the result of an SVI calibration. They allow exact checks of the mechanism that could be applied to SVI slices that have actually been validated. A check on a finite grid and a proof about complete functions will be explicitly distinguished.

The rule to remember

Choose comparable coordinates, validate the endpoint slices, then use a weight that is common across strike prices and increases with maturity. Check any fit performed after this mixture again.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Fill in the 6-month maturity and audit the result

The forward price of the index is F = 100 €, constant in this exercise. Interest rates and dividends are 0. For K = 80, 100, 120 €, the prices at 3 months are respectively 20, 8, 2 €; those at 9 months are 24, 14, 8 €. Let us construct the prices at 6 months. These fictitious data form a discrete test set, with no claim of SVI calibration.

  1. Define the space in which to interpolate

    K is the strike price: at maturity, the European call option pays max(S − K, 0), where S is the value of the index at that date. S, K, F and the option price C are in euros per index unit. The underlying is assumed to be nonnegative, with no dividends; rates are 0 and the contracts follow the same conventions. Here, static arbitrage opportunities mean combinations of options whose prices contradict the inequalities imposed by their payoffs.

    Let T denote the maturity date, t its remaining time, and tA and tB the times to the shorter and longer maturities. For the interpolation weight, these durations are all measured in months. We normalize by F: x = K/F and c(T,x) = C(T,K)/F, both dimensionless. Since F is constant here, comparing the same x amounts to comparing the same K. With a varying forward price, this shortcut would require a new analysis of the carry and discounting conventions.

    x=KF;c(T,x)=C(T,K)F

    An SVI slice describes total implied variance w(k,T), with dimensionless k = ln(x). If τ is time in years and σ is implied volatility in inverse square roots of years, then w is σ squared multiplied by τ, and is dimensionless. These coordinates are not prices: the conversion from w to c uses the model’s pricing formula. Here, we mix the functions c after this conversion, without interpolating the SVI parameters or σ.

  2. State the assumptions for preservation

    Let cA(x) and cB(x) denote the complete functions at the endpoint dates, for all nonnegative x. To apply the proof, they must be non-increasing and convex, have slopes between −1 and 0, satisfy max(1 − x, 0) ≤ c ≤ 1, equal 1 at x = 0, and tend to 0 as x tends to infinity. The boundary conditions ensure the correct normalization of prices. We also need cA(x) ≤ cB(x) everywhere: this is calendar ordering under the assumptions of our scenario.

    These properties are assumptions to check on the SVI slices that will actually serve as endpoints. Our grid does not prove them for all x. At its points, the prices at the longer maturity exceed those at the shorter maturity by 4, 6, 6 €; both rows of prices decrease and their successive slopes increase. This permits the local calculation that follows, without certifying functions that are not provided.

  3. Calculate a single weight for the entire slice

    α is the weight of the longer maturity slice; 1 − α is that of the shorter maturity slice. The target maturity is 3 months from the shorter maturity over a total interval of 6 months: the quotient is dimensionless. We impose 0 ≤ α ≤ 1, and α does not depend on x.

    α(t)=t−tAtB−tA=6−39−3=1/2

    The complementary weight is 1/2. Time check: α equals 0 at tA and 1 at tB; the rule therefore reproduces both endpoint dates. We stay between them: extrapolation would make one of the weights negative and invalidate the proof.

  4. Mix the prices, then normalize

    c(T,x)=(1−α)cA(x)+αcB(x)

    Since F is shared, we can perform exactly the same mixture on C in euros. For each K, starting from the price at the shorter maturity and adding α times the gap to the price at the longer maturity gives the same formula, expanded differently.

    At K = 80 €: 20 + 1/2 × (24 − 20) = 20 + 2 = 22 €. At K = 100 €: 8 + 1/2 × 6 = 11 €. At K = 120 €: 2 + 1/2 × 6 = 5 €.

    Dividing each coordinate and each price by F = 100 € gives x = (0.80 ; 1.00 ; 1.20) and c = (0.22 ; 0.11 ; 0.05), both dimensionless. Multiplying the normalized prices by F recovers the prices in euros: this is an independent check of the normalization.

  5. Prove the shape and ordering over time

    For a fixed date T, α is constant in x. Multiplying the monotonicity and convexity inequalities for cA by 1 − α, those for cB by α, and then adding them preserves their direction: the weights are nonnegative. Their sum is 1; the mixture therefore also preserves the bounds, the value at x = 0, and the limit at infinity. If the functions are twice differentiable, we recover this proof using their second derivatives:

    ∂2c∂x2=(1−α)cA″(x)+αcB″(x)≥0

    The displayed second derivative and the double primes denote curvature with respect to x, at a fixed date. The proof using inequalities remains valid for a curve with corners, without an ordinary second derivative.

    For calendar ordering, choose times u and v within the interval, with u ≤ v, in the same unit as t. We also write c(t,x) for the normalized price at duration t. The affine weight increases, so α(u) ≤ α(v). Subtracting the two mixtures, at fixed x, gives:

    c(v,x)−c(u,x)=[α(v)−α(u)][cB(x)−cA(x)]≥0

    Both factors are nonnegative, which establishes calendar ordering over the entire interval if the assumptions on the complete functions hold. Another progression of weights, still increasing and shared across all x, would also work. The affine construction can have a change in its time slope at the calibrated dates: admissibility does not promise a perfectly smooth surface.

  6. Check numerically what the grid establishes

    The prices 22, 11, 5 € stay between their prices at the shorter and longer maturities. They are below F and above the intrinsic payoff max(F − K, 0). Now let us check the slopes: the strike prices are spaced by h = 20 €, where h denotes this constant gap.

    The left slope is (11 − 22)/20 = -11/20 = -0.55 euros per euro. The right slope is (5 − 11)/20 = -3/10 = -0.30 euros per euro. They are between −1 and 0, and the second exceeds the first by 1/4. These segments therefore satisfy monotonicity and convexity.

    Another check: the price B of a butterfly portfolio, consisting of one option bought at each wing and 2 options sold at the center, is B = 22 − 2 × 11 + 5 = 27 − 22 = 5 €. It must be nonnegative, because its payoff at maturity is nonnegative for equally spaced strikes. Here, B = h × the slope jump also gives 20 × 1/4 = 5 €.

    These checks establish properties of the points and segments checked. They prove neither the convexity of a smoothed curve passing between them nor the boundary conditions beyond the extreme strikes. Additional interpolation in K or an extension of the wings must therefore preserve its own guarantees.

  7. Connect the result to SVI without losing the guarantee

    With actual admissible and ordered SVI endpoint functions, this mixture of their price functions provides the guarantee just established. It does not generally remain within the SVI parametric family. If an interpolated price lies strictly between its intrinsic bound and its upper bound, its total implied variance can be recovered by inverting the pricing formula, which is increasing in that variance. At the bounds, 0 variance or a limit must be handled separately.

    This inversion at each point expresses the same prices and their inequalities in another form; it is not a new SVI fit. If the tool requires SVI parameters at each date, fit the curve again and then audit calendar ordering, convexity and wings again. Direct interpolation of SVI parameters or volatility does not benefit from the proof using linearity in prices given here. Diagnosing discrepancies from market quotes belongs to C244·M5.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the formulas. Explain why using the same weight for all strike prices preserves convexity, then why its increase over time requires the endpoint slices to be ordered. Finish by stating precisely what our fictitious grid does not certify.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Each price lies between its bounds: the new slice must be admissible.”

    A different weight at each strike price preserves these bounds without preserving convexity. In the last exercise, the wings come from the shorter maturity and the center from the longer maturity: the butterfly price becomes negative. Use a common weight to benefit from the proof, then check the starting slices too.

    Construct and then repair the counterexample

  • “Averaging SVI parameters is equivalent to averaging prices.”

    The conversion from parameters to variance, then to price, is not a linear transformation. The proof adds the inequalities of the price functions. Review the coordinates of the mixture; a new parametric fit requires its own audit.

    Reread the interpolation space and the connection to SVI

  • “The grid checks are green: all maturities and all wings are covered.”

    The checked points do not establish the assumptions on the complete functions. Smoothing can change the curvature between points; the wings and extrapolation are outside the check performed. State the domain checked before drawing a conclusion.

    Review the limits of the discrete check

Exercise 1 · Application · exact calculation

A date closer to the shorter maturity slice

Using the same test set, request a price at t = 4.5 months for K = 100 €. Calculate the weight of the longer maturity slice, the price in euros, then the normalized price. First predict which endpoint the result will be closer to.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Measure the distance from the shorter maturity date, then divide by the total interval of 6 months.

Hint 2

At this strike price, the gap between the prices at the longer and shorter maturities is 6 €. Apply the weight only to this gap.

Compare with the solution

The target date is closer to the shorter maturity. Its distance from that maturity is 4.5 − 3 = 1.5 months, giving α = (3/2)/6 = 1/4 = 0.25. The shorter maturity weight is its complement to 1.

The price change is 1/4 × 6 = 3/2 €. We obtain C = 8 + 3/2 = 19/2 € = 9.50 €. Normalization gives c = (19/2)/100 = 19/200 = 0.095, dimensionless.

Check: this price lies between 8 and 14 €, closer to the first. With a longer maturity weight of 1/4, the result is also the weighted average that assigns the complementary weight to the shorter maturity price; we recover 9.50 €. For the complete slice, this same weight must be retained at every K.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain 1/4 by dividing two durations expressed in the same unit.
  • You give 9.50 € and the dimensionless value 0.095, with a check that the price lies within its bounds.
  • You distinguish the variation of the weight with the date from its constancy across strike prices.

Exercise 2 · Justification · two consistent checks

Change the scale without changing the conclusion

Return to the 6-month maturity. Starting from x = (0.80 ; 1.00 ; 1.20) and c = (0.22 ; 0.11 ; 0.05), recalculate the slopes of the segments. Then recover the butterfly price B in euros from their difference. Explain why the conclusion remains the same after normalization.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Each slope divides a difference in normalized prices by a difference in normalized strike prices.

Hint 2

The same F divides the numerator and denominator. For B in euros, use h multiplied by the slope jump.

Compare with the solution

Left slope: (0.11 − 0.22)/(1.00 − 0.80) = -11/20. Right slope: (0.05 − 0.11)/(1.20 − 1.00) = -3/10. Since both axes were divided by the same F, these slopes are exactly those calculated in euros per euro.

The jump is -3/10 − (-11/20) = 1/4. Returning to strikes in euros, B = 20 × 1/4 = 5 €. The direct calculation 22 − 2 × 11 + 5 gives the same result.

The slopes remain in the interval from −1 to 0 and are increasing: the same segments are decreasing and convex. This still does not certify another curve between the points. The change of unit preserves the conclusion; it does not extend its scope.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain the same exact slopes in both coordinate systems.
  • You recover 5 € from the slope jump and from the combination of prices.
  • You limit your conclusion to the points and segments checked.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis · counterexample and repair

Local weights that create a negative butterfly price

At the 6-month maturity, a program chooses a longer maturity weight of 0 for K = 80 €, of 1 for K = 100 €, then of 0 for K = 120 €. It reports that each price stays within its bounds. Calculate the resulting prices and their butterfly price. Repair the result with a common weight, then state the check needed if the program refits an SVI curve to the repaired prices.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A longer maturity weight of 0 takes the shorter maturity price; a longer maturity weight equal to 1 takes the longer maturity price.

Hint 2

The center is sold 2 times in B. A negative result is enough to refute convexity at these strikes.

Hint 3

To repair the maturity, reuse the time weight 1/2 across all columns. The guarantee then applies to the prices constructed.

Compare with the solution

The program produces 20, 14, 2 €. Each price does lie within its interval between maturities, but B = 20 − 2 × 14 + 2 = 22 − 28 = -6 €. The center exceeds the average of the wings; convexity is violated. Staying within the bounds in each column was not enough.

With the common weight 1/2, we recover 22, 11, 5 €, and B = 5 €. The calculation eliminates the defect shown on this grid. The global proof would additionally require the assumptions on the endpoint functions, across all strikes and in the wings.

Refitting an SVI curve may change the prices: their shape constraints and their ordering relative to neighboring dates must therefore be checked again, including outside this grid. A small fitting error does not replace this audit. Simply inverting each price to express it as implied variance, without changing it, is a different operation.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain -6 € even though each price lies within its bounds.
  • You identify the dependence of the weight on K as the loss of an assumption needed for the proof.
  • You repair the result with the common time weight and distinguish a local repair, a global proof and a new fit.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In what space is the mixture studied here defined?

In normalized prices c = C/F, at fixed x = K/F, in the scenario with a constant forward price and 0 rates. We do not directly mix SVI parameters or volatilities.

Recall 2What assumptions preserve the convexity of a slice?

Both endpoint functions are convex over the entire domain. Their weights are nonnegative, sum to 1, and are shared across all strike prices. We can then weight and add the convexity inequalities.

Recall 3Why is a weight that increases with the date insufficient on its own?

We also need cB(x) ≥ cA(x) everywhere. The difference between two dates is the product of the increase in weight and the gap between the endpoint slices. Both factors must be nonnegative under the stated assumptions.

Recall 4What does the positive butterfly price in the fictitious test set demonstrate?

The convexity of the points checked and the segments joining them, with the verified slopes. It does not demonstrate the shape of a smoothed curve between these points, the wings that are not shown, or the admissibility of a refitted SVI curve.

Recall 5Why distinguish inversion to implied variance from a new SVI fit?

An exact inversion expresses the same price in another form, when its position within the bounds permits inversion. A new SVI fit can move prices and lose the guarantees: it must undergo another audit.
