Level 2 · C059·M2

Convexity

Your goalDistinguish a check on a segment from a proof of convexity, then justify a unique global minimum of the cost being studied.

Before you start
c057-m5 : Hessian · c048-m5 : positivity · c054-m5 : Newton’s method · c059-m1 : gradient and Hessian

✿01 See the idea

In a model ventilation system, you can adjust the control inputs x and y of two fans independently. Setting A switches both inputs off; B takes them to the chosen upper limits. Before calculating, imagine the setting halfway between them: its inputs are the averages of those at A and B. Must its cost be the average of their costs?

Convexity gives a bound: mixing the inputs costs no more than mixing their costs. Draw a chord between the cost heights at the endpoints. For a convex function, the actual cost stays below or on this chord. This property must hold on every segment in the domain, in every direction.

It must also be possible to make the mixture. A domain is convex when the entire segment between two allowed settings remains allowed. Our rectangle of inputs meets this condition; an allowed region with a hole could invalidate this reasoning.

The entire scene and all its numbers are fictional. The inputs are unitless reference values, and the cost is a score in points constructed for the exercise. We are measuring neither electricity consumption nor the performance of an installation. The cost diagram follows only the segment A–B; it does not represent the entire surface.

The rule to remember

On a convex domain, a convex function stays below or on all its chords. A diagram or a carefully chosen midpoint illustrates this property; a proof must cover all segments and all weights.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Certifying the shape of the ventilation cost

The allowed settings form D = [0 ; 4] × [0 ; 2]. The cost penalizes the deviation of x from 2 and of y from 1, with weight 2 for the latter. Let us calculate a mixture, then establish a property throughout D.

  1. Specify the model and its units

    A point z = (x ; y) is a setting; x and y are its unitless coordinates. D is the allowed rectangle, the product of the stated intervals. f(z), or f(x ; y), is the cost in points. The targets 2 and 1 are unitless; the weight 2 is unitless. The implicit conversion is 1 cost point per unit of squared deviation in the first term, and this weight then multiplies the second term.

    f(x;y)=(x−2)2+2(y−1)2

    The equality gives the numerical value of the score in points. We assume this model is exact for the exercise, with no interaction between the inputs. It is a polynomial, so its second derivatives exist and are continuous throughout the plane. This allows us to check the Hessian.

  2. Define what all chords must satisfy

    Let u and v be any two settings in D; their coordinates have subscripts x or y. The unitless weight λ lies between 0 and 1; 1 − λ is the weight of u. The mixture zλ = (1 − λ)u + λv is calculated coordinate by coordinate. Each coordinate stays in its interval, so D is convex.

    f((1−λ)u+λv)≤(1−λ)f(u)+λf(v)

    f is convex on D if this inequality holds for all u, v and λ chosen in this way. The right side is the height of the chord at the same weight. Both sides are in points. Convexity is strict if the inequality is strict whenever u and v differ and 0 < λ < 1. At the endpoints, equality is expected.

  3. Calculate the endpoints, then mix the inputs

    Choose A = (0 ; 0) and B = (4 ; 2). At A, the deviations are 0 − 2 = -2 and 0 − 1 = -1. Squaring gives 4 and 1. The contribution from y is 2 × 1 = 2 points; f(A) = 4 + 2 = 6 points.

    At B, the deviations are 4 − 2 = 2 and 2 − 1 = 1. Their squares are 4 and 1; the second contribution is 2 points. Thus f(B) = 4 + 2 = 6 points.

    Take λ = 1/2. The weights of A and B are 1/2 and 1/2, and their sum is 1. The mixture M has horizontal coordinate 1/2 × 0 + 1/2 × 4 = 2, and vertical coordinate 1/2 × 0 + 1/2 × 2 = 1. We have first mixed the settings, not the costs.

  4. Compare the cost of the mixture and the mixture of the costs

    At M, the deviations from the targets are 2 − 2 = 0 and 1 − 1 = 0. Their squares are 0 and 0. The weighted contribution from y is 0 points: f(M) = 0 + 0 = 0 points.

    Calculate the chord separately: 1/2 × 6 + 1/2 × 6 = 3 + 3 = 6 points. The gap, chord minus actual cost, is 6 − 0 = 6 points, which is positive. This mixture therefore satisfies the convexity inequality strictly.

    Check: the deviations at B are the negatives of those at A; their squares and costs are identical. At the midpoint, the deviations vanish. These checks recover the results without repeating the entire calculation chain. But one chord still proves nothing about the others.

  5. Check the Hessian in every direction

    The gradient ∇f is the vector of first derivatives; the Hessian H is the matrix of second derivatives. Its rows and columns follow the order x then y. Here the mixed derivatives are 0, and H does not depend on the point where it is evaluated.

    H=(2004)

    Let d be any unitless direction; its components have subscripts x and y. The symbol T means transpose: dᵀ is the row obtained from the column vector d. The quantity dᵀHd is the curvature in this direction; under our scoring convention, it is in points.

    dTHd=2dx2+4dy2≥0

    A symmetric matrix is positive semidefinite if this quantity is nonnegative in every direction; it is positive definite if the quantity is positive in every nonzero direction. Here the coefficients 2 and 4 are positive, so H is positive definite.

    For a function with continuous second derivatives on a convex open set, having a positive semidefinite Hessian at every point is equivalent to convexity. A positive definite Hessian at every point is sufficient for strict convexity. Our polynomial satisfies this throughout the plane; its restriction to the rectangle D remains strictly convex. Checking the Hessian only at the preferred setting would not suffice if it varied elsewhere.

  6. Recover the proof using squares

    We can check the conclusion without differentiating. Let E be the gap, in points, between the chord and the cost at the mixture. Expanding the squares cancels the terms containing the targets; only the differences between the inputs at the endpoints remain.

    E=(1−λ)f(u)+λf(v)−f(zλ)
    E=λ(1−λ)[(vx−ux)2+2(vy−uy)2]≥0

    Why is the sign guaranteed? λ and 1 − λ are nonnegative. Each square is nonnegative, and the weight 2 is positive. If the settings differ, at least one square is positive; if the mixture is in the interior of the segment, its factor λ(1 − λ) is positive too. This recovers strict convexity for all segments, not just the one in the diagram.

    Checks: with λ = 0 or λ = 1, the gap vanishes. If u = v, it also vanishes. With our A, B and λ = 1/2, the identity gives 6 points again. This algebraic approach and the Hessian establish the same property through different calculations.

  7. Move from the shape to the minimum, while keeping the scope clear

    Let z★ be the candidate setting (2 ; 1), which belongs to D and lies in its interior. Its gradient is 0: the derivatives of f are 2(x − 2) and 4(y − 1). We indeed have ∇f(z★) = (0 ; 0).

    A 0 gradient alone does not certify a minimum for an arbitrary function. Here, at every setting, f is a sum of squares with positive coefficients: f ≥ 0 points. At z★, f is 0 points. The bound is attained, so the minimum is global: no other setting in D does better. Equality requires every deviation to be 0; the minimizer is unique.

    More generally, for a convex function on a convex domain, every local minimum is global: if a better setting existed, the segment joining it to the local minimum would provide arbitrarily close points with better values, contradicting the local minimum. Strict convexity guarantees at most one minimizer, but does not by itself guarantee that one exists; here we have exhibited it.

    This result certifies our fictional cost on our rectangle. It does not validate a physical model. On the boundary of a domain, a minimum can have a nonzero gradient, so do not turn our check at an interior point into a compulsory rule for all constrained problems. Lagrange methods and KKT conditions belong to later units.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The midpoint is below the chord, so the entire function is convex.”

    This calculation concerns only A, B and the weight 1/2. Return to the word “all” in the definition, then use the gap identity or the Hessian valid everywhere to cover the other choices.

    Reread the definition and the proof using squares

  • “A positive Hessian at the point found is enough for a global minimum.”

    A check at one point tells us nothing about curvatures elsewhere. State the domain, smoothness and positivity at every point, in every direction. Here the extension to the entire domain works because H is constant.

    Review the Hessian check

  • “Convex always means just one minimum.”

    Convexity allows a flat valley. Removing the cost associated with y leaves several minimizing settings, as the last exercise shows. Strict convexity guarantees at most one minimizer, and its existence must still be established.

    Examine the direction without a penalty

Exercise 1 · Numerical application

A mixture away from the midpoint

Keep the same cost, A and B, then give B the weight λ = 1/4. Calculate the coordinates of the mixture C, its cost, the height of the chord and the gap between them. Is this check enough to prove convexity on D?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The weight of A is 1 − 1/4 = 3/4. First mix each input.

Hint 2

The costs at A and B are equal; any weighted average of these costs keeps this value.

Compare with the solution

C has coordinates 1/4 × 4 = 1 and 1/4 × 2 = 1/2. The contributions from A are 0. The deviations from the targets are -1 and -1/2; their squares are 1 and 1/4. The weighted y term is 2 × 1/4 = 1/2 point. Thus f(C) = 1 + 1/2 = 3/2 points, or 1.50 points.

The chord is at 3/4 × 6 + 1/4 × 6 = 6 points, because the weights sum to 1. Its gap from the actual cost is 6 − 3/2 = 9/2 points, or 4.50 points. This is another check on a specific segment and at a specific weight; it does not replace the universal proof.

Check your reasoning

  • Find (1 ; 1/2) by mixing the inputs, then 3/2 points by applying the cost.
  • Calculate the chord and the gap separately, with their unit.
  • State explicitly that this check does not cover all segments.

Exercise 2 · Justification

A guarantee across the domain

Someone calculates H at the target setting and announces: “It is positive definite, so I have the unique global minimum.” Repair this reasoning for our cost. Explain why the statement would be insufficient for another function whose H varied with the setting.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Recover the entries of H and ask whether they depend on x or y.

Hint 2

For the minimum, look for a lower bound on the sum of squares, then an allowed setting that attains it.

Compare with the solution

In our model, H is the diagonal matrix with coefficients 2 and 4, throughout the plane. For every nonzero direction d, dᵀHd is the sum of 2 times the square of its x component and 4 times the square of its y component; it is positive. The smoothness of the polynomial and the convexity of the domain therefore allow us to conclude strict convexity on D.

The cost is always at least 0 points and attains 0 points at the allowed setting (2 ; 1). This establishes that the minimum exists and is global. Strict convexity, or the requirement that each square vanish, gives uniqueness. For another function, positivity of H at one point tells us nothing about the entire domain; a strict local minimum can coexist with lower values elsewhere.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify that the Hessian of this model is constant and positive definite in every nonzero direction.
  • Distinguish the proof of the shape, the existence of the minimizer and its uniqueness.
  • State the local scope of a Hessian calculated at a single point for a general function.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same model

Remove the penalty on the second input

The model system stops penalizing y: replace only its coefficient 2 with 0, keeping D and the targets. Call this new cost in points g. Is it convex? Strictly convex? Give all its minimizing settings and explain what happens to uniqueness.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

g no longer depends on y. Compare two settings with x = 2 but different y values.

Hint 2

The Hessian loses its curvature in the y direction; 0 curvature remains compatible with convexity.

Compare with the solution

g is the square of x − 2, independently of y. Its diagonal Hessian has coefficients 2 and 0. The curvature in a direction d is 2 times the square of its x component, which is always nonnegative. Thus g is convex on D.

The settings (2 ; 0) and (2 ; 2) differ but each has cost 0 points. All their mixtures keep this cost, so the strict inequality fails. All minimizers are exactly the settings x = 2, with 0 ≤ y ≤ 2. The squares prove the bound and the equality condition; no other x can attain 0 points. There are several global minima, and none is better than the others.

Check your reasoning

  • Connect the 0 coefficient to a flat direction and retain the conclusion of convexity.
  • Disprove strict convexity using two distinct settings and their segment.
  • Describe the entire set of minimizers instead of giving just one.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Reconstruct the convexity inequality and explain the scope of the word “all”.

For all u and v in the convex domain and every weight λ between 0 and 1, f((1 − λ)u + λv) ≤ (1 − λ)f(u) + λf(v). The cost at the mixture does not exceed the chord. A diagram and a few checked weights do not cover this universal requirement.

Recall 2Why check the domain before discussing the cost at the mixture?

The mixture must remain allowed. A convex domain contains every segment between its points. In our rectangle, each weighted average stays within the input intervals. A function and its domain are two distinct objects to examine.

Recall 3What assumptions and scope make the Hessian check valid?

For a function with continuous second derivatives on a convex open set, convexity is equivalent to a positive semidefinite Hessian at every point: dᵀHd ≥ 0 in every direction d. Here the polynomial is defined throughout the plane and H is constant; we can then restrict it to the rectangle. A check at one point is not enough in general.

Recall 4Why is our target a unique global minimum, rather than just a point with 0 gradient?

The cost is a sum of squares with positive coefficients, so it cannot fall below 0 points. The allowed target attains this bound and is the only setting that makes all deviations vanish. The 0 gradient is consistent with this interior minimum; by itself, it would not be a general proof.

Recall 5What is lost if the penalty on y disappears? What exactly does strict convexity guarantee?

The valley becomes flat along y: all settings x = 2, with allowed y, minimize the cost. Convexity remains, while strict convexity disappears. Strict convexity guarantees at most one minimizer; we must still establish that a minimizer exists.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the reasoning: a domain closed under mixing; a cost below or on all chords; a universal check using the Hessian or the gap identity for squares; an attained bound and uniqueness. Then explain where the proof changes when the penalty on y disappears, and why an isolated numerical check cannot replace any of these steps.

What you understand
Convexity is a property of a function on its domain, with assumptions that must be justified. It can turn a local conclusion into a global guarantee; a few cost calculations and a small displacement are not enough to certify it.
