Level 1 · C034·M4

Substitution

Your goalChoose a variable that simplifies an integral, transform the differential and the limits together, then check the accumulated quantity.

Before you start
c033-m5 : linear approximation · c034-m1 : Riemann sums · c034-m2 : integral · c034-m3 : fundamental theorem of calculus

✿01 See the idea

A pump fills a tank. Its flow rate increases over time: multiplying the initial flow rate by the entire duration would underestimate the amount added. We need to accumulate the small volumes added during each small time interval.

The pump setting depends on the elapsed time divided by a reference duration. We can therefore track this setting instead of the clock. This is substitution: describing the same accumulation using another variable, chosen to simplify the formula.

Changing the scale on the clock does not change the water added. But a small increase in the new setting no longer represents the same duration. Along with the formula, we must transform the widths of the small intervals and the start and end markers.

This pump, the tank, and all the values are fictional. We assume that the flow rate is known and continuous, with no leaks, outflow, or overflow during the period studied. We calculate the volume added; the initial volume in the tank does not enter the calculation.

The rule to remember

A substitution transforms a whole set of elements: the formula, the differential that measures the small intervals, and the limits that mark the interval covered.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The volume added throughout the filling period

The pump has a flow rate q(t) equal to A times the square of (1 + t/T), with A = 3 L/min and T = 2 min. Time t starts at 0 min and ends at T. Let us calculate the volume added V over this interval, without rounding during the calculation.

  1. Write down the required quantity and its units

    t is the elapsed time, in minutes; T is the reference duration, fixed and strictly positive; A is the initial flow rate, in liters per minute. The function q gives the instantaneous flow rate. The quotient t/T is dimensionless, so the expression in parentheses and its square are dimensionless. V denotes the volume added, in liters.

    V=∫0Tq(t) dt=∫0TA(1+tT)2 dt

    The symbol ∫ expresses accumulation between the stated limits. Here, the differential dt carries the unit minute: q(t) dt is a small contribution in liters. The fundamental theorem of calculus allows us to calculate this integral using an antiderivative, since q is continuous.

  2. Choose the expression that makes the formula cumbersome

    Set u = 1 + t/T: the expression in parentheses becomes simply u, and the flow rate becomes A times the square of u. The new variable u is a dimensionless coordinate. Since T is constant, the derivative of u with respect to t is 1/T = 1/2 per minute.

    u=1+tT;dudt=1T;dt=T du

    The notation dt = T d u summarizes the change of scale for the small intervals. Here the relationship is affine: Δt = T Δu is even exact for any change. In a more general substitution, the relationship between the differentials comes from the derivative; we do not treat dt and d u as two numbers to replace at random.

  3. Transform the limits before integrating

    At the start, t = 0 min gives u = 1 + 0 = 1. At the end, t = T gives t/T = 1, so u = 2. These limits are values of u and have no units.

    The substitution is increasing, since T is positive: the order along the interval is preserved. Even if the same numerical value appeared on both axes, its role would be different. The limits of an integral with respect to u must always refer to u.

  4. Transform the entire integral

    Replace the expression in parentheses by u, dt by T d u, and the limits by their corresponding values. A and T are constant: their product can be taken outside the integral. It equals 3 L/min × 2 min = 6 L.

    V=AT∫12u2 du

    The remaining integral is dimensionless; the factor AT gives it the unit liter. No t remains in the integrand. Omitting T would leave a unit of flow rate instead of volume.

  5. Find the antiderivative, then calculate the difference

    An antiderivative H of the square function is obtained by dividing the cube of u by 3. Differentiating the cube gives 3 times the square of u; division by 3 compensates for this factor. Here H is dimensionless. The bracket notation means “value at the upper limit minus value at the lower limit”.

    ∫12u2 du=[u33]12=8−13=73

    The powers in detail: 2 × 2 = 4, then 4 × 2 = 8. At the lower limit, the cube is 1. Their difference is 7, and the integral is exactly 7/3. The difference of antiderivative values would cancel any constant added to H.

  6. State the volume and check its order of magnitude

    V=6 L×73=14 L

    The flow rate starts at 3 L/min and reaches 12 L/min. It increases over the interval: over 2 min, the volume must therefore lie between 6 L and 24 L. Our result of 14 L lies within these bounds. This check can reveal an error, but cannot establish the exact value on its own.

  7. Justify the substitution using the chain rule

    Let us check the change of variable another way. B(t) = AT H(1 + t/T) is an antiderivative with respect to time, expressed in liters. The chain rule requires us to multiply H′ by the derivative of its argument. The prime symbol ′ means “derivative with respect to the function’s argument”.

    B′(t)=AT×(1+tT)2×1T=q(t)

    The factor T is indeed needed to compensate for 1/T. B′ is a flow rate, in liters per minute. Since B′ = q, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives B(T) − B(0), which is the same volume as our integral in u.

    This check shows the general rule. Suppose g is a continuously differentiable, dimensionless function of time, f is continuous and dimensionless on an interval containing the values of g, and H is an antiderivative of f on that interval. The derivative of H(g(t)) is f(g(t)) g′(t). For times a and b in the interval studied, the following formula is a consequence of the fundamental theorem of calculus. The unit of g′ is the reciprocal of a minute; both integrals are dimensionless.

    ∫abf(g(t))g′(t) dt=∫g(a)g(b)f(u) du

    For our tank, g(t) = 1 + t/T and f is the square function. We have rewritten q(t) = AT f(g(t)) g′(t). We need to recognize and compensate for the inner derivative g′; substitution therefore involves more than renaming a letter.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I replace the expression in parentheses by u and keep dt.”

    Start again from the derivative d u/dt = 1/T, then write dt = T d u. Replace the formula and the differential together. Without T, you would find 7 with the unit L/min: that would be a flow rate, rather than the required volume.

    Review the choice of u and the transformation of the integral

  • “The limits remain the start and end times.”

    The limits specify values of the variable of integration. When integrating with respect to u, calculate u at each endpoint: 1 and 2. Another possible method is to return fully to the antiderivative in t, then use the times. Mixing an antiderivative in u with limits in t no longer describes the original interval.

    Compare the two ways of labeling the same interval

  • “Once I have cubed the upper limit, I am finished.”

    A definite integral requires a difference of antiderivative values. Here, the start t = 0 corresponds to u = 1, whose cube is not 0. Subtract the contribution 1 before dividing by 3, then multiply by AT.

    Review the antiderivative and the subtraction of endpoint values

Compare with the solution

The derivative is 1/T, so dt = T d u. The times 0 min and 2 min become 1 and 2, with no units. The coefficient is AT = 6 L.

V=6 L×∫12u2 du

The changes fit together: an expression in u, a differential d u, and limits in u. The unit liter appears even before the antiderivative is calculated.

Exercise 2 · Exact calculation over a shorter interval

Stop at the intermediate minute

The pump follows exactly the same rule and T remains 2 min. Calculate the volume added between 0 min and 1 min. Keep exact fractions, then give a decimal value in liters. The observation period changes; the setting T does not.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The new upper limit is 1 + 1/2. The coefficient AT remains the same as in the example.

Hint 2

Calculate the cube of this limit, subtract the cube of the lower limit, then divide by 3 to use the antiderivative.

Compare with the solution

The lower limit remains 1. The upper limit is 1 + 1/2 = 3/2, or 1.5. The coefficient remains 6 L, since A and T still describe the same pump.

The square of the upper limit is 9/4, then its cube is 27/8. Subtract 1: the difference is 19/8. After division by 3, the integral in u is 19/24.

The volume is therefore 6 × (19/24) = 19/4 L, or 4.75 L. It is positive and less than the total volume 14 L; the flow rate increases, so the first half of the time contributes less than half the total volume.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep T unchanged and transform only the stopping limit.
  • Show the cubed value as a fraction and the subtraction at the lower limit.
  • Give 19/4 L, with the decimal value 4.75 L, and explain the physical check.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and cross-checking

Begin after the initial start

Calculate the volume added between 1 min and 2 min directly by substitution, without first subtracting the previous result from the total. Then check by combining the two parts. Explain why the pump adds more water during this part than during the previous one, even though their durations are equal.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The limits are now the corresponding values for 1 min and 2 min. The starting value in u is no longer 1.

Hint 2

In the difference of cubes, the upper limit remains 2 and the lower limit becomes 3/2.

Compare with the solution

Over this interval, u goes from 3/2 to 2. The difference of cubes is 8 − (27/8) = 37/8; division by 3 gives 37/24.

The coefficient AT remains 6 L. The volume added is 6 × (37/24) = 37/4 L, or 9.25 L.

Check by adding the contributions over the intervals: 4.75 L + 9.25 L = 14 L, the total in the example. The contributions at the intermediate limit cancel in this sum of differences of antiderivative values.

The flow rate at each instant in the second part is higher than at the corresponding instant in the first. For equal durations, this part therefore adds more water. This reasoning complements the arithmetic check without replacing the exact calculation.

Check your reasoning

  • Also transform a lower limit that differs from the initial start.
  • Obtain 37/4 L directly before checking by subtraction or addition.
  • Recover the total and justify the unequal contributions using the increasing flow rate.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which expression did we choose as the new variable, and why?

u = 1 + t/T makes the flow rate equal to A times the square of u. This expression simplifies the function to integrate; u is dimensionless because t and T are durations.

Recall 2Where does the factor T in front of the integral in u come from?

From d u/dt = 1/T, so dt = T d u. The chain rule requires this change of scale; A multiplied by T is a volume, in liters.

Recall 3How do you choose the limits of an integral after substitution?

Evaluate the new variable at each endpoint of the original interval. Here, t = 0 min gives u = 1 and t = T gives u = 2. If you return to an antiderivative in t, use the time limits instead.

Recall 4How can you check a substitution without repeating the same operations?

Construct an antiderivative in the original variable and differentiate it: B(t) = AT H(1 + t/T) must satisfy B′(t) = q(t). Also check the units and the bounds on the volume given by the minimum and maximum flow rates.

Recall 5In f(g(t)), which factor should you look for before setting u = g(t)?

Look for the inner derivative g′(t), since d u = g′(t) dt. If you need to compensate with a factor, justify it. Simply renaming the expression in parentheses does not guarantee a correct integral.

Interpret a change of scale without treating it as integration

What you understand
A new coordinate requires us to preserve its relationship with the original quantity. In the tank integral, the factor T comes from this relationship and preserves the volume. A simple unit conversion illustrates the need to account for scale, but is not enough to constitute substitution in an integral.
