Level 4 · C230·M1

Test functions

Your goalConstruct a local test function and justify its compact support and its smoothness at every order separately.

Before you start
c165-m5 : duality · c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c167-m5 : initial and boundary value problem

✿01 See the idea

To study the temperature near the centre of a rod, you choose a weighting: central positions count, while distant positions no longer count. You also want to prevent an abrupt cutoff in this weighting from introducing an artificial boundary in the middle of the rod.

Imagine a small bump that joins the 0 line with all its derivatives equal to 0. It vanishes completely before the ends of the rod. A test function provides exactly this combination: strict localization and smoothness at every order.

The scene and dimensions are fictional. The rod occupies the open interval Ω = (0 m ; 6 m). Our bump is centred at c = 3 m and its support radius is a = 1 m. The letter φ, read as “phi”, denotes the dimensionless weighting; it does not represent a temperature.

Being very small is not enough to be 0. Likewise, joining the 0 line without a visible corner does not guarantee smoothness at every order. The diagrams help show the localization; proving smoothness will require examining the formula.

The rule to remember

Check both conditions: continuous derivatives of every order and compact support contained entirely in the open set being studied.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Construct and check the central weighting

On our fictional rod, let us construct a weighting with central height 1, with c = 3 m and a = 1 m. We will check its regularity class, its localization and its value at the point x = 7/2 m, that is, 3.5 m.

  1. Specify what a test function means

    In this unit, Ω is an open interval of the real line ℝ. The notation C∞(Ω), with ∞ as a superscript in the formula, denotes the set of real functions whose derivatives of every order exist and are continuous on Ω. This superscript means “every order”, rather than an infinite value of the function.

    The support supp φ is the closure in ℝ of the set of points of Ω where φ is nonzero. Taking the closure adds its limit points. On the real line, a compact set is a closed and bounded set. We also require it to be contained in Ω.

    Cc∞(Ω)={φ∈C∞(Ω): supp φ compact contenu dans Ω }

    The space on the left of the formula is also denoted by 𝒟(Ω). It is the space of test functions; the subscript c stands for “compact”. A test function need not be positive, and its integral need not equal 1. Our positive choice simply serves to illustrate a weighting.

  2. Separate the physical position from the profile

    Set t = (x − c)/a. The positions x and c and the length a are in metres; a is strictly positive. The quotient t is dimensionless. Define the profile β, read as “beta”, then φ(x) = β(t). exp denotes the real exponential function; its argument must be dimensionless.

    β(t)={exp(1−11−t2)si |t|<10si |t|≥1

    The definition is piecewise: at a joining point, use the 0 branch. It would be incorrect to substitute a value of t with absolute value 1 into the fraction, since its denominator would be 0.

    At the centre, t = 0, so the exponent is 1 − 1 = 0 and φ(c) = exp(0) = 1. The added shift in the exponent sets the central height; it does not normalize the area.

  3. Calculate the support and margins

    The exponential is strictly positive where |t| < 1. This is equivalent to c − a < x < c + a. The points where the function is nonzero therefore form the open interval (2 m ; 4 m). Their closure is [2 m ; 4 m].

    Left endpoint: 3 − 1 = 2 m. Right endpoint: 3 + 1 = 4 m. Left margin: 2 − 0 = 2 m. Right margin: 6 − 4 = 2 m.

    This support is closed, bounded and contained in Ω with a strictly positive margin. The function is identically 0 in neighbourhoods of the ends of the rod. The points 2 m and 4 m nevertheless belong to the support: points with nonzero values approach them.

  4. Justify the joins at every order

    Inside |t| < 1, the operations in the formula are smooth. Outside, the function is constantly 0. The joins remain to be checked. Let s denote the difference between 1 and the square of t: as a joining point is approached from inside, s tends to 0 through positive values.

    Every derivative of a fixed order of the inner branch is a finite sum of terms of the form P(t) s^(−m) exp(1 − 1/s). Here P is a polynomial, so it remains bounded near the joining point, and m is a nonnegative integer. This form is preserved under differentiation: the product rule and the chain rule only add powers of the denominator and polynomial factors.

    Pour tout entier m≥0: lims→0+s−mexp(−1s)=0

    For every fixed m, s^(−m) exp(−1/s) tends to 0. To check this, set y = 1/s. A positive term in the exponential series gives exp(y) ≥ y^(m + 1)/(m + 1)!, so y^m exp(−y) ≤ (m + 1)!/y, which tends to 0. The exclamation mark means factorial, the product of the integers from 1 to m + 1.

    All the inner derivatives therefore tend to 0, just as the derivatives of the outer branch do. Joining each derivative in succession shows that β is smooth at every order on ℝ. The affine transformation x ↦ (x − c)/a preserves this property; the derivative of order k of φ introduces the factor a^(−k). For φ, this derivative therefore has unit m^(−k), where k is a nonnegative integer.

    With regularity and support checked separately, φ does belong to 𝒟(Ω). A plot and a few calculated derivatives could not replace this argument, which applies at every order.

  5. Calculate the value halfway along the radius

    The chosen point is 1/2 m to the right of the centre: x = 3 + 1/2 = 7/2 m. Subtracting c centres the position; dividing by a expresses this distance as a number of radii.

    Thus t = (7/2 − 3)/1 = 1/2. Its square is 1/4, so the positive denominator is 1 − 1/4 = 3/4. Its reciprocal is 4/3; the exponent is 1 − 4/3 = -1/3.

    φ(3.5 m)=exp(−13)

    This is an exact dimensionless value, strictly between 0 and 1, because its exponent is negative. There is no need to produce a decimal approximation to complete the calculation.

    Another way to check: write the exponent over a common denominator.

    1−11−t2=−t21−t2

    Using the square 1/4, we obtain -1/3 again. Replacing t with -1/2 leaves its square unchanged: the weighting is symmetric about c.

  6. Check vanishing with an integral

    The prime in φ′ denotes the derivative with respect to x. The fundamental theorem of calculus relates its integral to the difference between the values of φ at the ends. Here we use the smooth extension that is 0 near these ends, even though Ω does not contain the boundary points.

    ∫0 m6 mφ′(x) dx=0−0=0

    The rise and fall cancel exactly. φ′ is in inverse metres and dx in metres: the result is dimensionless. This check is consistent with vanishing at the boundaries, but it is not enough to prove smoothness at every order. It concerns the integral of φ′; the area under φ has not been calculated.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The support contains only the points where the function is nonzero.”

    You must take their closure. At 2 m, φ equals 0, but every neighbourhood contains points where φ is positive. This point therefore belongs to the support [2 m ; 4 m].

    Review the calculation of the support and margins

  • “It vanishes at the ends, so its support is compact in Ω.”

    A positive margin inside Ω is required. With radius 3 m, the support reaches the boundaries at 0 m and 6 m, which are not in the open set. 0 values at these points do not remove the nearby points where the function is nonzero.

    Compare the two radii in the localization exercise

  • “The drawing has no corner: the function must be smooth at every order.”

    A join can have a continuous derivative and fail at the next order. The truncated polynomial in the last exercise has exactly this defect. An argument covering all orders of differentiation is required.

    Examine the derivatives of the polynomial profile

Compare with the solution

At the point x = 2 m, t = (2 − 3)/1 = -1. Its absolute value is 1: this is the 0 branch, so φ(2 m) = 0.

This point belongs to the support because it can be approached by positions to its right, strictly between 2 m and 4 m, where φ is positive. A 0 value at a joining point is compatible with belonging to the support.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and decision

Widen the weighting on the same rod

Keep c = 3 m and Ω = (0 m ; 6 m). Compare the radii a = 2 m and a = 3 m using the same profile β. Calculate each support, then decide whether the function belongs to the space 𝒟(Ω) of test functions.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The support of the transformed profile is [c − a ; c + a].

Hint 2

Do not confuse being smooth at every order on Ω with having compact support contained in Ω.

Compare with the solution

With a = 2 m: c − a = 1 m and c + a = 5 m. The support [1 m ; 5 m] leaves a margin of 1 m on each side. The affine transformation preserves smoothness at every order: both conditions are satisfied.

With a = 3 m: the endpoints are 0 m and 6 m, and the margin is 0 m. The function remains smooth and positive at every interior point of Ω, but its closed support in ℝ is [0 m ; 6 m], which is not contained in Ω. It is therefore not a test function on this open set.

Saying “the support is bounded” is not enough. Even taking the relative closure in Ω would give the whole of Ω here, which is not compact. The choice of closure cannot remove the requirement of compactness.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate both intervals and their margins in metres.
  • Accept the first radius and reject the second on Ω.
  • Attribute the rejection to localization, without inventing a lack of regularity.

Is a polynomial bump enough?

For the same rod and the original radius, the polynomial profile below is proposed as a replacement for β. The support is unchanged. Check p and p′ at the joining point t = 1, then compare the limits of p″ from each side. Does this fix produce a test function? Here the primes refer to t.

p(t)={(1−t2)2si |t|<10si |t|≥1

Hint 1

Inside, expand the square: a constant term, a term in t to the power 2 and a term in t to the power 4. Then differentiate.

Hint 2

You must compare the inner derivatives with the derivatives of the 0 outer branch.

Compare with the solution

Inside, the expansion and its derivatives give:

p(t)=1−2t2+t4
p′(t)=−4t+4t3

At t = 1, p and the inner limit of p′ equal 0, as they do outside. The join therefore has a continuous derivative; we say that it has regularity class C of order 1.

p″(t)=−4+12t2

As t tends to 1 from the left, the inner second derivative tends to -4 + 12 = 8. On the right, the second derivative of the 0 branch equals 0. The join therefore has no continuous second derivative and is not smooth at every order.

On the rod, the change of variable multiplies second derivatives by a^(−2), without removing this difference; their units become inverse square metres. The compact support remains correct, but the regularity condition fails. Returning to the exponential profile fixes this defect because all its derivatives vanish at the joins.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the derivatives of the inner branch and those of the 0 branch.
  • Find the limits 8 and 0 at order 2.
  • Reject smoothness at every order despite correct support and a continuous derivative.
  • Explain why the change of scale does not fix the defect.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What conditions define the space 𝒟(Ω) of test functions?

Continuous derivatives of every order on Ω and compact support contained in Ω. On our interval, the support must leave a positive margin at the ends. Positivity and an integral equal to one are not part of the definition.

Recall 2Why can a point where φ equals 0 belong to its support?

The support is the closure of the set where φ is nonzero. The joining points of our bump are limits of points with positive values; they therefore belong to the closed support.

Recall 3What argument ensures that the exponential profile joins smoothly at every order?

Each derivative contains an exponential exp(−1/s) multiplied by inverse powers of s and bounded factors. As s tends to 0 through positive values, the exponential makes these terms tend to 0 at every fixed order. The derivatives match those of the 0 branch.

Recall 4Why does the radius 3 m fail on our open rod?

The support reaches 0 m and 6 m. It is compact in ℝ, but is not contained in Ω. The function remains smooth at every order; it is the localization requirement that fails.

Recall 5What disqualifies the truncated polynomial profile in the exercise?

It has the correct support and a continuous derivative, but its inner second derivatives tend to 8 at the joins, compared with 0 outside. It fails as early as order 2; it is therefore not smooth at every order.

What you understand
A test function is localized within an open set and smooth at every order. On our rod, being bounded, being small far from the centre or vanishing only at the boundary cannot replace either of these conditions.
