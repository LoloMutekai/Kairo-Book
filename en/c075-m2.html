Level 3 · C075·M2

Synthetic data and external data

Your goalChoose what fabricated data or data from elsewhere can test, then compare errors while keeping their provenance visible.

Before you start
c067-m5 : pipeline · c074-m5 : identification assumptions · c073-m5 : interaction and repeated measures · c063-m5 : synthetic dataset and external validity · c062-m5 : prior audit and sensitivity · c072-m5 : extracted text, metadata and verification · c075-m1 : baseline and stated assumption

✿01 See the idea

A repair workshop wants to predict the time needed for each job ticket. Its baseline rule always predicts 30 min. A candidate rule distinguishes adjustment from replacement: it predicts 20 min and 40 min respectively. Before changing the rule, we need to know which tickets it is being judged on.

You can fabricate tickets and set their expected durations: these are synthetic data. They let you test a specific case, check a calculation or create a difficult situation. If you set the durations to exactly what the candidate predicts, its 0 error mainly tells you that it reproduces your data generation rule.

You can also receive tickets from a neighbouring workshop: their provenance makes them external. That word does not tell you how they were obtained. The neighbour might send observed tickets or tickets it has fabricated itself. Synthetic describes how the data are constructed; external describes their origin relative to our workshop. Both properties can apply at once.

The entire scene and all its durations are fictional. We imagine that the neighbour recorded repair work; no real tickets were collected here. The example teaches how to limit a conclusion, without claiming real performance.

The rule to remember

Attach each score to its source and the question that source allows you to ask. Reproducible data generation checks its assumptions; transfer to other situations requires relevant observations.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A duration rule tested against its data

We prepare S with 4 fabricated tickets, then E with 4 tickets from a fictional neighbour. Each batch contains 2 adjustments and 2 replacements. Rules B and C are fixed before examining E. We will calculate their mean absolute errors separately.

  1. Define the target, the rules and the provenance

    x denotes the ticket, of which we use only the category here. y is its reference duration for the work, in minutes. In S, y is set by construction. In E, y is assumed to have been timed under the same fictional protocol: from the start to the end of active work, excluding waiting time. B(x) and C(x) are the predicted durations, in minutes.

    B(x) is 30 min regardless of category. C(x) is 20 min for an adjustment and 40 min for a replacement. The construction of S assigns these same durations to y: it therefore tests a world that already favours C.

    Before a real trial, a provenance record would specify who produces each batch, the period and the selection of tickets, the timing definition, the units and the version of the rules. For S, we would also retain the generation procedure and its parameters; for E, the collection and annotation procedure. Here, these details define a fictional problem, without documenting any real collection.

    We assume that durations are available and categories are defined in the same way across the batches. Each ticket has equal weight. E was used neither to choose nor to tune B or C. These conditions make our descriptive comparisons clear; they do not make the neighbour representative of our customers.

  2. Choose an error that does not cancel out

    For a rule f, representing B or C, the absolute error of a ticket is ℓ = |f(x) − y|. The bars take the positive magnitude of the difference: a prediction that is too short and one that is too long count equally here. A signed error could cancel out another and hide differences.

    ℓ=|f(x)−y|

    Let D denote a batch, n its strictly positive number of tickets, and i the index of a ticket, from the first to the last. R_D(f) is the mean absolute error on this batch. xᵢ and yᵢ belong to the same ticket; ℓᵢ is its absolute error. The ellipsis represents all the intervening errors. ℓ and R_D(f) are in minutes; n is a count of tickets.

    RD(f)=ℓ1+⋯+ℓnn

    We can write this mean without abbreviation: add all the errors in the batch, then divide by its number of tickets. A smaller score is preferable under this criterion. No ratio between these scores is a probability that the rule is correct.

  3. Calculate errors on the fabricated batch S

    The Greek letters distinguish tickets, not durations. Tickets Sα and Sβ are adjustments; Sγ and Sδ are replacements. All numerical entries in the table are in minutes.

    Tickety (min)B (min)C (min)Sα · réglage203020Sβ · réglage203020Sγ · remplacement403040Sδ · remplacement403040

    For B, each adjustment gives |30 − 20| = 10 min; each replacement gives |30 − 40| = 10 min. The sign changes, but the magnitude of the error remains positive.

    An adjustment–replacement pair totals 10 + 10 = 20 min. The 2 pairs give 2 × 20 = 40 min of summed errors. Mean: 40 / 4 = 10 min.

    For C, each prediction equals the fabricated duration: each error is 0 min. Total: 0 × 4 = 0 min; mean: 0 min. This result checks agreement with the generator, not how faithfully it represents a real workshop.

  4. Calculate each error on data from source E

    Tickets Eα and Eβ are adjustments; Eγ and Eδ are replacements. The predictions remain those of the rules fixed at the start. All the following durations, including those attributed to the neighbour, are invented for the calculation.

    Tickety (min)B (min)C (min)Eα · réglage253020Eβ · réglage353020Eγ · remplacement303040Eδ · remplacement303040

    For B, in ticket order: |30 − 25| = 5 min; |30 − 35| = 5 min; |30 − 30| = 0 min; |30 − 30| = 0 min.

    For C, in the same order: |20 − 25| = 5 min; |20 − 35| = 15 min; |40 − 30| = 10 min; |40 − 30| = 10 min.

    The calculation uses a reference duration for each ticket. If the neighbour measured total elapsed time including waiting, the same numbers would no longer describe the same target; the protocol would need clarification before interpreting a difference in scores.

  5. Add, divide and check

    For B, adjustments total 5 + 5 = 10 min and replacements 0 + 0 = 0 min. The total is 10 min. Dividing by 4 tickets gives 5/2 min, or 2.50 min on average.

    For C, adjustments total 5 + 15 = 20 min and replacements 10 + 10 = 20 min. The total is 40 min; the mean is 40 / 4 = 10 min.

    RE(B)=104=2.50 min
    RE(C)=404=10 min

    Check using another breakdown: for B, the mean for adjustments is 5 min and the mean for replacements is 0 min; since the groups have the same number of tickets, the midpoint of their means is 2.50 min. For C, each group totals 20 min for 2 tickets, so each mean is 10 min: the overall mean is too.

    Recovering the totals from the means provides another check: 2.50 × 4 = 10 min and 10 × 4 = 40 min. A mean error does not become an observed duration of repair work.

  6. State the conclusion at the appropriate scale

    On S, C has the smallest error; on E, B has the smallest error. The construction of S follows the rule used by C, whereas E contains different durations. We have a counterexample to the argument “perfect on my fabricated data, therefore preferable elsewhere”.

    E remains a small batch from another workshop. Its result describes these tickets under our metric; it demonstrates neither the general superiority of B nor the cause of the difference. The selection of repairs, the tools and the timing could change the comparison. We have calculated no sampling uncertainty.

    To prepare a useful comparison for our workshop, we would need to define the target repairs and plan a relevant observed batch kept separate from decisions about the rules. Synthetic data retain their role in targeted checks. Scores remain separate by provenance; multiplying or merging them does not, by itself, provide this new evidence.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading the table, explain why 0 error on S can coexist with a larger error on E. Then give a piece of provenance information whose absence would prevent a comparison of the durations.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If another workshop sends it to me, it is no longer synthetic.”

    Ask how the neighbour produced the durations. A dataset fabricated there is external to us and remains synthetic. Record both properties separately.

    Review construction and provenance

  • “With a reproducible generator and many tickets, transfer is proven.”

    Reproducibility lets you repeat the data generation. It adds neither situations missing from the generator nor new observations of the target. Revisit the 0 score of C on S and its ranking on E.

    Recalculate on the separate batches

  • “I can mix everything together and publish the most reassuring mean.”

    A pooled mean depends on the quantities you choose to add. Keep scores separate by source and justify any weighting through a stated question. The exercise on copies shows what pooling hides.

Exercise 1 · Assess evidence

The neighbour sends a generator

The neighbour has timed nothing: it fabricated tickets using the same duration rule as C, then exported them. Are these data synthetic, external, or both? What would they allow you to check, and which conclusion would remain unsupported?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Separate “where the file was produced” from “how the reference duration was obtained”.

Hint 2

A duration chosen to equal the candidate prediction does not test the accuracy of that prediction on repair work.

Compare with the solution

The dataset is external to our workshop and synthetic by construction. Sending it changes the provenance, not how the durations were obtained. The same rule still gives C 0 error if applied correctly.

The dataset can check that categories are read correctly and errors are calculated correctly in these chosen cases. It does not demonstrate that the predicted durations suit observed repairs. Ask for the generation procedure, its version and its parameters, then a separate relevant collection to examine this transfer.

Check your reasoning

  • Describe provenance and construction separately.
  • Name a check that the data support and a conclusion that goes beyond the evidence.
  • Explain why a different sender does not turn the durations into observations.

Exercise 2 · Independent calculation

An additional external ticket

A new replacement from the same fictional neighbour has a reference duration of 50 min. The rules are unchanged. Calculate its errors for B and C, then the new means for the enlarged batch E. Compare the rules on this batch, without drawing conclusions for all workshops.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with the absolute difference for this single ticket, then add it to the corresponding total.

Hint 2

The number of tickets also increases: do not divide the new total by the old number of tickets.

Compare with the solution

B predicts 30 min: the error is |30 − 50| = 20 min. C predicts 40 min: its error is 10 min. C has a smaller error on this new ticket.

The total for B becomes 10 + 20 = 30 min; the total for C becomes 40 + 10 = 50 min. The number of tickets increases to 4 + 1 = 5 tickets.

The new means are 30 / 5 = 6 min for B and 50 / 5 = 10 min for C. B remains preferable for the mean of this batch, despite its larger error on the added ticket.

Check: 6 × 5 = 30 min and 10 × 5 = 50 min. This is still neither a real measurement nor a guarantee about the target customers.

Check your reasoning

  • Take the positive magnitude of the differences.
  • Update both totals and the denominator.
  • Distinguish the verdict on a ticket from the verdict on the batch mean.
  • Limit the conclusion to the fictional batch used in the calculation.

Exercise 3 · Critique a result

Copies change the displayed verdict

Return to the original E, without the added ticket. A report replaces S with 4 identical copies of S, then combines these copies and E into a single batch. Calculate the number of tickets and the pooled means for B and C. Has the external ranking improved for C? Rewrite the conclusion of the report.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The copies multiply the number of tickets and the total error of S; they do not change E.

Hint 2

Add the totals before dividing by the pooled number of tickets. Do not take the simple mean of the means of S and E.

Hint 3

Ask whether new repair work has been observed or only the weights in the report have changed.

Compare with the solution

The copies contain 4 × 4 = 16 recorded tickets. With E: 16 + 4 = 20 rows. These are duplicated rows, not that many independent observations.

For B, the total for the copies is 4 × 40 = 160 min, then the pool totals 160 + 10 = 170 min. Its mean is 170 / 20 = 17/2 min, or 8.50 min.

For C, the total for the copies is 0 min. With E, it reaches 40 min; the mean is 40 / 20 = 2 min. The pool therefore places C ahead of B for this mean.

Yet on the unchanged E, the means remain 2.50 min for B and 10 min for C. No new external trial has improved the candidate: we have increased the weight of a world fabricated in its favour.

A defensible conclusion: “On the construction S, C reproduces the imposed durations exactly. On tickets E, B has the smallest mean error. The pooled mean here depends on the added copies; it does not demonstrate better transfer by C.”

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate using the numbers of tickets actually pooled.
  • Do not count copies as new situations.
  • Keep the verdict on the external batch intact.
  • Replace the promise of transfer with a conclusion tied to each source.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Are synthetic and external opposing categories?

No. Synthetic describes how data are fabricated; external describes an origin outside the stated scope. A neighbour can send a synthetic dataset. Both properties must be recorded.

Recall 2What does 0 error prove when durations are fabricated using the candidate rule?

The candidate agrees with this construction on the calculated cases. This does not prove that the construction represents observed repair work or that the rule transfers to the target.

Recall 3How can you reconstruct the mean error of a rule on a batch?

For each ticket, take the magnitude of the difference between the predicted duration and the reference duration, in minutes. Add these errors, then divide by the positive number of tickets. Attach the mean to the batch and its provenance.

Recall 4Why is an external origin insufficient to validate our use?

The categories, the duration measured, the selection of tickets and the working conditions may differ from our target. These correspondences and any missing information must be made explicit; the score primarily describes the available batch.

Recall 5Why can adding synthetic copies improve the displayed mean without new evidence?

The copies increase the weight of favourable cases in the calculation. They add no observed repair work or independent origin. The result for the external batch remains unchanged.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Close the page and reconstruct the reasoning: define the target duration and the rules; describe construction and provenance separately; state the role of each batch; recover the absolute errors and their means by source; explain the reversed ranking; show why copies do not prove transfer. What you understand
A fabricated batch is used to check stated cases and assumptions. A batch from another origin examines transfer if its construction and its connection to the target are known. This distinction comes before any interpretation of a score.
