Level 2 · C162·M4

Measures of attention

Your goalBuild a report of omissions, false alarms and response times, then state precisely what it measures and what it does not demonstrate.

Before you start
c161-m5 : limits of application · c087-m5 : measures and limits · c162-m1 : formal practices · c162-m2 : adaptation · c162-m3 : programmes

✿01 See the idea

Shapes appear one after another on a screen. Press a key when you see a star; let the other shapes pass. You might miss a star, press for a circle or take a long time to find the right key. Simply saying “I was concentrating” does not let you reconstruct these events.

The star is the target: the shape that requires a keypress. A detection is a keypress for a target; an omission is a target with no keypress. A false alarm is a keypress for another shape; a correct rejection is letting that other shape pass. Making no keypress can therefore be correct or incorrect depending on what was displayed.

Here we construct an entirely fictional teaching task and record, chosen so that the counts can be checked by hand. This is not a validated test or a diagnostic tool. Keeping the instruction in mind as the shapes appear calls on attention; the report describes performance on this task, without providing a quantity of pure attention.

A feeling of being present and a recorded response answer different questions. The first describes what the person reports about their experience; the second describes their behaviour under an instruction. We can compare them without automatically treating either as proof of the other.

The rule to remember

Cross-classify the shape and the action, relate each error to its opportunities, then calculate response times for detections alone. Keep these measures together and state their limits.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the shape record to a verifiable report

In fictional block A, there are 4 targets and 8 other shapes. We will construct the categories, their proportions and the mean response time, keeping the record that allows them to be checked.

  1. Set the rule before counting

    A trial starts when a shape appears. The response window lasts Δ = 1000 ms; ms means millisecond. The first keypress strictly after onset and no more than Δ after it is assigned to that trial. With no keypress in this window, the trial is classified as no press. The next shape appears after the window closes: the trials do not overlap.

    The instruction and the window are known in advance. We assume the shapes are visible and the recording is complete, with no equipment failure or anticipatory keypress; there are no late keypresses in this record. A trial lost by the device would be missing data, to be reported separately: it would not be converted into an omission by the person.

  2. Read the events before their summaries

    Targets, in order of appearance: keypress at 300 ms; keypress at 400 ms; no press; keypress at 500 ms.

    Other shapes, in order of appearance: no press; keypress at 200 ms; no press; no press; keypress at 250 ms; no press; no press; no press.

    C denotes the number of targets; A, the number of other shapes; D, detections; O, omissions; F, false alarms; R, correct rejections. These are counts of trials. Here C = 4, A = 8, D = 3 and F = 2. Each trial goes into exactly one cell of the first diagram.

  3. Complete the cells and check the totals

    Targets are either detected or omitted: O = C − D = 4 − 3 = 1 trial. Other shapes produce either a false alarm or a correct rejection: R = A − F = 8 − 2 = 6 trials.

    Independent check: count the trials with no keypress again in each list. You find 1 with no press for a target and 6 for other shapes. Then combine all the cells: 3 + 1 + 2 + 6 = 12 trials, matching C + A = 4 + 8 = 12.

  4. Choose the denominator that answers the question

    A proportion is a part divided by the number of corresponding opportunities. The letter p with subscript O denotes the proportion of omissions among targets; with subscript F, it denotes the proportion of false alarms among other shapes. These proportions have no unit. C and A must be strictly positive. Multiplying a proportion by 100 expresses it as a percentage.

    pO=OC=14=0.25

    The proportion of omissions is therefore 25 % of targets. We divide by the targets because only targets could be omitted.

    pF=FA=28=0.25

    False alarms account for 25 % of other shapes. These percentages are equal in our task, but refer to different opportunities: their numerators are not identical. Neither is a percentage of attention that the person possesses.

  5. Calculate response time for detections alone

    Response time t is the time of the keypress minus the time of onset, measured on the same clock, in ms. The subscripts a, b and c distinguish the response times of detections in order: 300 ms, 400 ms and 500 ms. T is their sum, in ms; m is their mean, in ms. A mean shares the sum equally among the observations included.

    T=ta+tb+tc=300+400+500=1200 ms
    m=TD=12003=400 ms

    The omission has no observed keypress response time. Assigning it 0 ms would invent an instantaneous response and give 1200 ÷ 4 = 300 ms. False alarms are also excluded because m describes responses to targets. If D = 0, this mean is undefined, rather than equal to 0 ms.

    Check: 400 × 3 = 1200 ms. The mean is between 300 and 500 ms. The range, defined as the longest response time minus the shortest, is 500 − 300 = 200 ms: the mean alone does not convey this spread.

  6. Write a conclusion with the right scope

    Block A report: 1 omission out of 4 targets, or 25 %; 2 false alarms out of 8 other shapes, or 25 %; mean response time for the 3 detections: 400 ms, with a range of 200 ms. Keep the counts alongside the percentages so that the small denominators remain visible.

    The record does not indicate why an error occurred. A misunderstood instruction, a shape that is hard to distinguish, a slow motor response or a technical incident must be open to examination before attributing everything to attention. An omission is an observed category; “distraction” would be an explanation requiring evidence.

    After a mindfulness practice, another block might yield different numbers. To compare them, we would need to keep the same rule, comparable difficulty and comparable equipment, and document in particular the order of the blocks and the fatigue context. A before–after comparison alone does not separate the effect of the practice from that of familiarisation or order. Studying effectiveness would still require an appropriate comparison and repeated trials; we are not conducting that study here.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results and redraw the cells with their opportunities. Explain why making no keypress can be correct, why an omission does not have a response time of 0 and why the report is not enough to demonstrate the effectiveness of a practice.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I divide all errors by all trials.”

    That would give a different measure. To answer “what proportion of targets was omitted?”, divide O by C. For false alarms among other shapes, divide F by A. Name the opportunities first, then write the fraction.

    Review the choice of denominators

  • “A target with no response counts as 0 ms in the mean.”

    Without a keypress, no keypress response time is observed. Keep that trial among the omissions and calculate the mean response time for detections alone. If no target was detected, report an undefined mean.

    Review the selection of response times

  • “A shorter mean proves that meditation improved my attention.”

    Check omissions and false alarms together: responding more often can change the report in opposite directions. Then separate the difference between blocks from its explanation; without an appropriate comparison, you cannot attribute it to the practice.

Exercise 1 · Identify and justify

No keypress: the shape matters

In block A, a star and a circle receive no keypress. Classify these events. Then reconstruct O and R from C, A, D and F, followed by the proportions of omissions and false alarms. Justify each denominator.

Hint 1

The instruction requires a keypress for the star and no keypress for the circle.

Hint 2

Complete targets and other shapes separately; the total number of trials is not the denominator for the proportions requested.

Compare with the solution

The star with no keypress is an omission; the circle with no keypress is a correct rejection. The absence of an action is not enough to define an error.

O = 4 − 3 = 1 trial; R = 8 − 2 = 6 trials. Omissions are relative to targets: 1/4 = 0.25, or 25 %. False alarms are relative to other shapes: 2/8 = 0.25, or 25 %.

Check your reasoning

  • You distinguish the two instances of no keypress by the shape displayed.
  • You find 1 omission and 6 correct rejections.
  • You name the opportunities for each proportion and give the counts alongside the percentages.

Exercise 2 · Calculate, then discuss

Faster, but what does the report show?

Imagine block B of the same task, with 4 targets and 8 other shapes, the same window and the same instruction. It contains 4 detections, each at 300 ms, and 4 false alarms. Calculate omissions, correct rejections and error proportions. Compare with block A. Can we claim an overall improvement, then attribute it to a practice performed between the blocks?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Complete the cells first, without changing the numbers of opportunities.

Hint 2

Write down the changes in omissions, false alarms and mean response time separately.

Compare with the solution

O in B is 4 − 4 = 0 trials; R is 8 − 4 = 4 trials. The proportion of omissions is 0/4 = 0, or 0 %. The proportion of false alarms is 4/8 = 1/2, or 50 %.

The mean response time is 300 ms because all detection response times equal that value. It is 400 − 300 = 100 ms shorter than in A. Omissions decrease, but the proportion of false alarms rises from 25 % to 50 %.

The report changes in several directions: each must be stated, without inventing an overall score. It is consistent with a greater willingness to respond, without establishing its cause. Even if a practice took place between the blocks, order, familiarisation and context remain competing explanations to examine.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain 0 omissions and 4 correct rejections in B.
  • You compare false alarms while keeping their denominator.
  • You present the opposing changes and distinguish description from causal attribution.

Exercise 3 · Audit a measure

An exact number that answers a different question

For block A, a report adds all keypress response times, including those for other shapes: 300, 400, 500, 200 and 250 ms. It reports 330 ms as the “mean response time for detections”. Check its arithmetic, fix the label or the calculation, then write a result sentence that keeps omissions visible.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Separate the set of all keypresses from the set of keypresses for targets.

Hint 2

Correct division does not guarantee that the events included match the label.

Compare with the solution

False alarm response times total 200 + 250 = 450 ms. Adding the 1200 ms for detections gives 1650 ms for 5 keypresses. The division 1650/5 = 330 ms is arithmetically correct.

It describes the mean response time for all keypresses, rather than for detections. To keep the stated label, include only 300, 400 and 500 ms: 1200/3 = 400 ms.

Acceptable sentence: “In this fictional block, the mean response time for the 3 detections is 400 ms; 1 target out of 4 is omitted.” Add the 2 false alarms out of 8 other shapes if presenting the complete report. A response time calculated only from responses does not make the missed targets disappear.

Check your reasoning

  • You check the total of 1650 ms and the division by 5.
  • You identify the selection of events as the reason for the incorrect label.
  • You restore 400 ms and keep the number of omitted targets in the conclusion.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why is making no keypress not enough to identify an error?

For a target, it is an omission; for another shape, it is a correct rejection. You must cross-classify the shape presented and the action, within the specified window.

Recall 2Which denominators should be used for omissions and false alarms?

Targets for omissions; other shapes for false alarms. Each proportion relates a category to the opportunities on which it could occur. Keep the raw counts too.

Recall 3How do you calculate the mean response time for detections and handle omissions?

Add the response times only for keypresses on targets, then divide by their number. Omissions remain in their count without being assigned a fictional response time. With no detection, this mean is undefined.

Recall 4Is a shorter mean enough to establish better overall performance?

You must also examine omissions and false alarms, as well as individual response times. Their changes may conflict; a mean for responses does not represent trials with no response.

Recall 5What is missing if we want to attribute a difference between blocks to a practice?

A comparison that separates the practice from other changes, with documented conditions and appropriate repetitions. A feeling, a task score and proof of effectiveness are not interchangeable.
