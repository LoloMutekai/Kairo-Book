Level 2 · C059·M1

Gradient and Hessian

Your goalCalculate the slopes and curvatures of a cost with several settings, then check what they predict for a given displacement.

Before you start
c057-m5 : Hessian · c048-m5 : positivity · c054-m5 : Newton's method

✿01 See the idea

On a fictional alignment bench, the knobs x and y act together on a marker. Their sum must reach 3, while x must reach 1. These settings are normalized numbers with no units: the same scale division has been chosen for both knobs. We add the squares of the deviations from the targets to form a cost f, also with no units. The smaller it is, the more closely the targets in this model are met.

Initially, x = 2 and y = 2. Decreasing x reduces the deviations from the targets; decreasing y affects only the sum. These different effects explain why a single slope is no longer enough. The gradient collects the slopes obtained by moving each knob while keeping the other fixed.

A slope can itself change when a knob turns. The Hessian describes these changes in slope, including the effect of y on the slope along x. It helps assess the difference between a prediction that extends the current slopes and the cost actually obtained after a displacement.

The targets, positions and cost function are entirely fictional. We assume that the controls are unconstrained in the neighborhood under study and that the polynomial function is exact. Hardware limits, constraints and automatic selection of displacements are outside the scope of this unit.

The rule to remember

The gradient gives the first-order change; the Hessian adds curvature and coupling. Always check the prediction by returning to the original cost function.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Decreasing a knob setting: from slope to checked cost

At the setting p = (2 ; 2), let us calculate the gradient and Hessian, then predict the effect of the displacement h = (-0.50 ; 0). The settings, their deviations and the cost have no units. We use the same fictional bench throughout the calculation.

  1. Write the cost and define the symbols

    x and y are the coordinates of the setting; p denotes the starting point. r is the deviation of their sum from its target; s is the deviation of x from its own target. f is the sum of the squares of these deviations. Squaring prevents a positive deviation from canceling a negative deviation in this cost.

    r=x+y−3;s=x−1
    f(x,y)=r2+s2

    This function has continuous second derivatives throughout the plane: the differentiation and local expansion used below are valid. For another function, this smoothness would need to be checked near p.

  2. Calculate the initial situation

    The sum of the settings is 2 + 2 = 4. Thus r = 4 − 3 = 1 and s = 2 − 1 = 1. The cost is 1 × 1 + 1 × 1 = 1 + 1 = 2, with no units.

    This calculation provides the reference for comparing any prediction. A favorable displacement must lead to a cost below 2.

  3. Form the gradient while keeping the other setting fixed

    The symbol ∂ denotes a partial derivative. f_x means ∂f/∂x: the slope when x changes and y stays fixed; f_y denotes the other slope. The gradient g = ∇f(p) is the column of these slopes, evaluated at the same point. A column preserves the coordinate order, x followed by y. In prose, an underscore indicates a subscript: g_x = f_x(p) and g_y = f_y(p). The displayed formulas place these subscripts below the letters.

    When x varies, r and s each vary at the same rate as x. Differentiating the squares therefore gives 2r + 2s. When y varies, only r changes: the slope is 2r. At the point p:

    g=[fx(p)fy(p)]=[2r+2s2r]=[42]

    Numerically, f_x(p) = 2 + 2 = 4 and f_y(p) = 2. These slopes have no units per unit of normalized setting. With the Euclidean dot product and these fixed scales, g gives the direction of steepest increase among directions of unit length; −g gives the direction of steepest local decrease. A direction does not determine the length of the displacement.

  4. Differentiate the slopes to build the Hessian

    H is the Hessian evaluated at p. Its entry H_xx is the derivative of f_x with respect to x; H_xy is the derivative of f_x with respect to y. The next row contains the derivatives of f_y with respect to x and y, in that order. It therefore describes how the gradient changes when the point moves.

    In f_x = 2(x + y − 3) + 2(x − 1), differentiating with respect to x gives 2 + 2 = 4; with respect to y, it gives 2. In f_y = 2(x + y − 3), each derivative gives 2.

    H=[HxxHxyHyxHyy]=[4222]

    Here H is constant: it is the same at every point. Its units are those of the cost divided by those of the settings concerned; here everything has no units. The mixed entries are equal because the second derivatives are continuous. The entry H_xy = 2 shows that changing y also changes the slope along x: keeping only the diagonal entries would lose this coupling.

  5. Connect slopes, curvature and displacement

    h is a column of displacements (h_x ; h_y). p + h is the new setting. The superscript ᵀ transposes a column into a row. The product gᵀh equals g_xh_x + g_yh_y; it gives the first-order change. The norm of h denotes its Euclidean length in the chosen coordinates.

    f(p+h)≈f(p)+gᵀh+12hᵀHh

    For a function with continuous second derivatives, the omitted term becomes negligible compared with the square of the length of h as h tends to the 0 displacement. Here f is a polynomial of degree 2: no higher-degree term remains, and this formula is an exact equality for every h.

    hᵀHh=Hxxhx2+2Hxyhxhy+Hyyhy2

    The coefficient 2 of the mixed term comes from the entries H_xy and H_yx, both present in the matrix product. If gᵀh is negative, sufficiently small displacements in this direction decrease f; this does not guarantee a decrease for an arbitrary length.

  6. Make the linear prediction

    Choose h_x = -0.50 and h_y = 0. We reach x = 2 − 0.50 = 1.50, with y = 2 unchanged. The product gᵀh equals 4 × (-0.50) + 2 × 0 = -2.

    The prediction based on slopes alone gives 2 + (-2) = 0. It extends the initial slopes over the entire displacement. It does not prove that the deviations from the targets have become 0.

  7. Add curvature and check directly

    Since h_y is 0, only H_xx and the square of h_x contribute. This square equals 0.25. The product hᵀHh equals 4 × 0.25 = 1; half of it equals 0.50. The predicted cost therefore becomes 0 + 0.50 = 0.50, with no units.

    Now return to the original cost function, without using the derivatives. At the new point, r = 1.50 + 2 − 3 = 0.50 and s = 1.50 − 1 = 0.50. Each square equals 0.25: their sum is 0.50, with no units.

    The direct and quadratic methods agree. The cost decreases from 2 to 0.50, but remains above 0. The linear approximation missed exactly the curvature contribution 0.50.

  8. Also check the slope using neighboring costs

    A centered finite difference compares the costs on either side of p while moving only x. With step ε = 0.50, where ε is a positive distance in the normalized setting, the point on the right has x = 2.50. Its deviations both equal 1.50, so its cost is 4.50. The cost at the point on the left is the value already checked, 0.50.

    f(x+ε,y)−f(x−ε,y)2ε=4.50−0.501=4

    The numerator is 4 and the total distance between the points is 1: we indeed recover f_x(p) = 4. This equality is exact for our quadratic polynomial in exact arithmetic. For another function, the quotient is generally only an approximation; with rounded numbers, choosing too small a step can also amplify subtraction errors.

Explain it in your own words

Explain why a positive slope along x supports our negative displacement, then why it is not enough to predict the final cost. Recover the role of the mixed term if the knobs move together.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A gradient component is the cost after turning the knob.”

    A component is a slope at the starting point. Multiply it by the corresponding displacement, add the contributions and start from the initial cost. For a finite displacement, then check the curvature and the directly calculated value.

    Review the linear prediction and its check

  • “The curvatures along x and y are enough: the mixed entries serve no purpose.”

    H_xy measures the change in the slope along x when y varies. If the settings move together, the product hᵀHh also contains their cross term. The oblique displacement exercise shows exactly what is lost by using only the diagonal entries.

    Calculate the oblique displacement

  • “A 0 gradient alone proves that I have found a minimum.”

    In an unconstrained neighborhood where the function is differentiable, an interior minimum must have a 0 gradient, but this condition is not sufficient in general. We must study what happens nearby, for example using the Hessian and, if it is inconclusive, other terms. On our bench, making r and s 0 simultaneously gives a 0 cost; this check comes from the sum of squares, not just from the word stationary.

    Return to the cost and the meaning of the derivatives

Exercise 1 · Application · a single coordinate

Turn only the other knob

Start again from p = (2 ; 2) and take h = (0 ; 0.50). Calculate the cost directly, then recover it using the gradient and Hessian. State the contribution of each order.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Only y changes: r increases by 0.50, but s remains equal to 1.

Hint 2

The linear term is g_yh_y. The quadratic term is half of H_yy multiplied by the square of h_y.

Compare with the solution

At the point (2 ; 2.50), r equals 1.50 and s equals 1. The direct cost is 2.25 + 1 = 3.25, with no units.

The linear term is 2 × 0.50 = 1. The quadratic contribution is 2 × 0.25 ÷ 2 = 0.25. Including the initial cost, we recover 2 + 1 + 0.25 = 3.25.

The cost rises: we turned y in the direction of its positive slope. The linear prediction alone misses 0.25 here.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep s fixed and recalculate r.
  • Distinguish the initial cost, linear change and quadratic contribution.
  • Obtain 3.25 with no units by both the direct and quadratic methods.

Exercise 2 · Reasoning · coupling

Move the knobs in opposite directions

Start from p again and choose h = (0.50 ; -0.50). Does the sum x + y change? Calculate the cost directly and the quadratic term. Explain why removing the mixed entries of H would give an incorrect prediction.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The displacements cancel in x + y; only s changes.

Hint 2

In the quadratic term, keep 2H_xyh_xh_y. Its sign is negative here.

Compare with the solution

The sum of the settings remains 4. Thus r remains 1, while s becomes 1.50. The direct cost equals 1 + 2.25 = 3.25, with no units.

The linear change equals 4 × 0.50 + 2 × (-0.50) = 1. The full quadratic contribution is:

12[4×0.25+2×2×0.50×(-0.50)+2×0.25]=0.25

The cost is therefore 2 + 1 + 0.25 = 3.25. In the sum inside the brackets, the mixed term exactly cancels the contribution 4 × 0.25 from x. Omitting it would leave a quadratic contribution too high by 0.50. Here the coupling reflects the fact that the sum of the settings stays fixed.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify the deviation that stays fixed before calculating.
  • Keep the mixed term with its sign and coefficient.
  • Recover 3.25 and explain the error caused by using only the diagonal entries.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · examine a claim

Does local descent allow any length?

Someone directly chooses h = −g = (-4 ; -2) from p and claims: “The cost will decrease, since this is the opposite of the gradient.” The coordinates remain unconstrained in our fictional model. Check the cost at the endpoint and respond to this claim.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the displacement to the starting coordinates before recalculating r and s.

Hint 2

The direction indicates what happens for a sufficiently small displacement. Compare the direct cost with the initial cost, without extending the slope alone.

Compare with the solution

We reach x = 2 + (-4) = -2 and y = 2 + (-2) = 0. The deviations are r = -2 + 0 − 3 = -5 and s = -2 − 1 = -3.

The cost is 25 + 9 = 34, with no units, greater than the initial cost 2. The direction −g is indeed a direction of local descent when g is nonzero; the chosen displacement is too long to guarantee a decrease. The curvature term matters even if the product gᵀh is negative.

The remedy is to distinguish direction from length, then check the cost at the endpoint. The displacement in our example, h = (-0.50 ; 0), did produce a verified decrease. No real setting is being proposed here.

Check your reasoning

  • First calculate the endpoint coordinates, then the deviations.
  • Compare 34 with 2 in the same unit.
  • Explain why a local property does not guarantee a decrease for every length.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does each gradient component represent, and what must be kept fixed?

A slope of the cost along a coordinate, with the other coordinates kept fixed. All components are evaluated at the same point and ordered in the same way as the settings.

Recall 2What does a mixed entry of the Hessian describe on our bench?

The change in the slope along a knob setting when the other knob turns. The equal mixed entries contribute together to the cross term of hᵀHh; removing them can distort the predicted cost.

Recall 3How can we reconstruct the cost after a displacement using g and H?

Add the product gᵀh and half of hᵀHh to the initial cost. This is a local approximation under sufficient smoothness, and an equality for our polynomial of degree 2. Then check by calculating the cost directly.

Recall 4Why does −g not guarantee a decrease for every displacement in this direction?

The gradient describes the first-order behavior at the start. With nonzero g, a sufficiently small length decreases the cost in the direction −g; a large length can allow curvature to dominate.

Recall 5What can we conclude from a 0 gradient in an unconstrained neighborhood?

The point is stationary: all partial slopes are 0. This is necessary for a differentiable interior minimum, but not sufficient in general; the changes around the point still need to be examined.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking, start from the deviations from the targets; reconstruct the cost, the slopes with the other coordinate fixed and the changes in slope. Then write the initial cost, linear term and quadratic term for a specific displacement. Finish with a direct calculation and the limits: smoothness, coordinate scales, displacement length and the fact that a 0 gradient alone does not prove a minimum.
