Level 2 · C049·M4

Matrix systems

Your goalWrite the exchanges between two tanks in matrix form, separate their modes and check a solution in the original equations.

Before you start
c047-m5 : stability of an equilibrium · c048-m5 : positivity · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c046-m5 : approximation error · c049-m1 : higher order · c049-m2 : oscillations · c049-m3 : Laplace

✿01 See the idea

Two tanks, A and B, exchange the same liquid through two pipes fitted with pumps. Initially, A contains 6 L and B contains 2 L. Each pump continually adjusts its flow rate to the volume in its source tank: the more liquid that tank contains, the more it sends to the other. The tanks have sufficient capacity; there is no external inflow or leakage. The scene and all its values are fictional.

At first, A sends out more than it receives: its volume decreases. B receives the difference. The flow rates change with the volumes; keeping the initial flow rates indefinitely would give a different outcome. Before calculating, predict what should remain constant and what should diminish.

In the sum of the volumes, liquid only moves from one tank to the other: the sum stays constant. Their difference measures the imbalance that makes the net transfer favour one direction. Tracking the sum and the difference therefore simplifies the problem. We will find these two behaviours in the eigendirections of the matrix.

A matrix system combines several differential equations in a single notation. The matrix describes how each volume contributes to the rate of change in each tank. Here it lets us change coordinates to turn coupled equations into equations that we solve separately.

The rule to remember

Fix the order of the components, write the balance equations, then look for eigendirections: solve each mode before returning to the tank volumes.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Solve the exchanges between A and B

Each outgoing flow rate equals k times the volume in its source tank, with k = 1/4 per minute, or 0.25 per minute. At t = 0 min, the volumes are 6 L and 2 L. Let us find how they evolve and the time when their difference has been divided by 2.

  1. Define the state and the assumptions

    Time t is measured in minutes and is nonnegative. The functions a(t) and b(t) give the volumes in litres. A prime denotes the derivative with respect to t: a′ and b′ are therefore net flow rates in L/min. The column vector X(t) lists a(t) before b(t), and this order stays fixed.

    Both pumps have the same constant, positive coefficient k. The flow rate from A to B is k a; the flow rate from B to A is k b. The settings respond immediately to the volumes. No other phenomenon is modelled: we seek the exact solution of this ideal rule, rather than a measurement of real pumps.

    X(t)=[a(t)b(t)]

  2. From balance equations to the matrix

    For each tank, rate of change = inflow − outflow. This gives a′ = −k a + k b and b′ = k a − k b. The name of tank A and the matrix symbol A are distinct: the matrix records the coefficients of the balance equations, in the order chosen for X.

    X′=AX,A=[−kkk−k]=[-1/41/41/4-1/4]

    X has size 2 × 1, and A has size 2 × 2. All entries of A have units of inverse minutes: multiplying them by a volume gives a flow rate. A row calculates a derivative; a column identifies the volume used. For example, the top right entry indicates the effect of b on a′.

    Initially, the outgoing flow rate from A is 1/4 × 6 = 3/2 L/min; its incoming flow rate is 1/4 × 2 = 1/2 L/min. So a′(0) = 1/2 − 3/2 = -1 L/min. In B, the same balance gives 1 L/min. Their sum is 0, providing an initial conservation check.

  3. Find the directions that evolve independently

    An eigenvector is a nonzero vector that A transforms into a multiple of itself; this multiple may be 0. The factor is its eigenvalue. Here u represents an equal change in both volumes, and v represents opposite changes. Their components are dimensionless. The eigenvalues λs and λh have units of inverse minutes.

    u=[11],Au=0u,λs=0
    v=[1-1],Av=λhv,λh=−2k=-1/2

    Check the products: for u, each row gives −k + k = 0. For v, the first row gives −k − k = -1/2 per minute, and the second gives the opposite. The decay rate of the imbalance is therefore 1/2 per minute; it is not k alone.

    The matrix P places u and then v in its columns. Its determinant is 1 × (-1) − 1 × 1 = -2, which is nonzero. These directions therefore form a basis: every state has a unique decomposition X = m u + h v. The coordinates m and h are in litres.

    P=[111-1],P-1AP=[000λh]

    P to the power -1 denotes the inverse matrix. This equality is the diagonalization: in coordinates m, h, the off-diagonal entries vanish. It uses a constant matrix A and a fixed eigenbasis. We restrict ourselves here to this diagonalizable case.

  4. Decompose the initial volumes

    The equality X = m u + h v reads a = m + h and b = m − h. Adding these lines isolates m; subtracting them isolates h. Thus m is half the sum, and h is half the difference. Let s = a + b be the total and d = a − b the imbalance, in litres.

    m=a+b2,h=a−b2

    Initially, s(0) = 6 + 2 = 8 L and d(0) = 6 − 2 = 4 L. We therefore find m(0) = 8/2 = 4 L and h(0) = 4/2 = 2 L.

    Check by reconstructing the volumes: 4 + 2 = 6 L and 4 − 2 = 2 L. The coefficient of the opposing mode is 2 L, rather than the full difference of 4 L.

  5. Solve the modes, then return to the tanks

    Since P is constant, differentiating X = m u + h v gives X′ = m′u + h′v. The eigenvector relations and uniqueness of the decomposition require m′ = 0 and h′ = λh h. We recover this without a matrix: adding the balance equations gives s′ = 0, and subtracting them gives d′ = −2k d.

    The function exp is the natural exponential. The equation h′ = λh h has solution h(t) = h(0) exp(λh t), while m(t) = m(0). The product λh t is dimensionless, as the argument of an exponential must be.

    X(t)=m(0)u+h(0)exp(λht)v

    The time constant τ is 1/(2k) = 2 min, with λhτ = -1. The negative sign makes mode h decay. Substituting the initial coefficients gives the following volumes; the volume coefficients are in litres and τ is in minutes.

    a(t)=4+2exp(−tτ)
    b(t)=4−2exp(−tτ)

    The total remains 8 L. For nonnegative t, the exponential factor lies between 0 and 1, including only the endpoint 1: the volumes remain nonnegative and approach their common value without passing it.

  6. Calculate when the imbalance has halved

    Let t_d denote the time when d has been divided by 2. Since d(t) = 4 exp(−t/τ), we need exp(−t_d/τ) = 1/2. The natural logarithm, denoted ln, is the inverse of the exponential. The exact time is therefore t_d = τ ln(2), or 2 ln(2) min. The number inside ln is dimensionless; we keep this duration in exact form.

    At this time, h(t_d) = 2 × 1/2 = 1 L. Returning to the tanks gives a(t_d) = 4 + 1 = 5 L and b(t_d) = 4 − 1 = 3 L.

    Check the requested result: a(t_d) − b(t_d) = 5 − 3 = 2 L, which is indeed half of 4 L. Conservation check: 5 + 3 = 8 L. It is not the individual volumes that have been divided by 2.

  7. Check the equation as well as the total

    The initial condition has already been reconstructed. For every t, differentiating the vector solution gives X′ = λh h(0) exp(λht) v. On the other side, AX = m(0) Au + h(0) exp(λht) Av gives exactly the same vector because Au is 0 and Av = λhv. The solution therefore satisfies both equations at every instant.

    We can check the signs and units at t_d. The flow rate from A to B is 1/4 × 5 = 5/4 L/min, or 1.25 L/min. The flow rate from B to A is 1/4 × 3 = 3/4 L/min, or 0.75 L/min. Their net balance in A is -1/2 L/min, or -0.50 L/min; in B, it is 1/2 L/min, or 0.50 L/min.

    By another route, the derivative of the mode gives λh h(t_d) = (-1/2) × 1 = -1/2 L/min for A, and the opposite for B. The balances and differentiation agree: this check is more demanding than checking only the sum.

  8. Interpret equilibrium and stability correctly

    As t increases without bound, h tends to 0 L; m remains 4 L. For our initial total, the limit is therefore (4 L; 4 L). However, all states where a = b are equilibria: the flow rates are equal there, whatever their common volume.

    The 0 eigenvalue preserves the common mode. Adding the same amount to both tanks initially would change their final value: the system does not return to a single equilibrium after every perturbation. In this particular system, the equilibrium is stable, but it is not asymptotically attractive throughout the plane. With a fixed total, the imbalance decays and the limit is determined.

    We have dealt with a constant matrix with a basis of real eigenvectors. This calculation does not provide a universal method for a matrix that varies with time or has no eigenbasis. The next unit will address series solutions.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the formulas, reconstruct the balance equations, then explain why the sum stays fixed and why the difference decays with coefficient 2k. Then recover the volumes from coordinates m and h. Why is checking the total insufficient to validate a solution?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The entry −k gives the volume to remove each minute.”

    An entry of A is a rate per minute. It multiplies the current volume. In A, the initial outflow is 3/2 L/min, and then it changes. The first row calculates −k a + k b; it does not subtract a fixed volume.

    Reread the balance equations and the units of the matrix

  • “I use the full difference directly as the coefficient of the opposing vector.”

    The vector v adds h to A and subtracts h from B: it creates a difference of 2h. We therefore need h = (a − b)/2. Reconstruct 6 L and 2 L from 4 L and 2 L to check this division.

    Revisit the decomposition of the initial state

  • “One mode decays, so everything returns to the same equilibrium.”

    The exchange mode decays, but the common mode has a 0 eigenvalue. It preserves the total chosen initially. An equilibrium attracts states with the same total; it does not attract states with a different total. All modes must be examined.

    Reread the scope of the stability result

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · read a row

An outgoing flow rate and a net rate of change

At the start of the same experiment, calculate the flow rate from A to B, then a′(0). Someone claims that A loses 0.25 L every minute because the diagonal contains −k. Correct this interpretation by giving the units of k, k a and a′.

The pump reads the volume in its source tank: multiply k by that volume.

Hint 2

Compare with the solution

The outgoing flow rate is k a(0) = 1/4 × 6 = 3/2 L/min. The incoming flow rate is k b(0) = 1/4 × 2 = 1/2 L/min. The net rate of change is therefore 1/2 − 3/2 = -1 L/min.

k has units of inverse minutes; k a and a′ have units of L/min. Even the net flow rate found is instantaneous: it does not imply a constant loss of 1 L during every minute. The volumes and flow rates evolve during that interval.

Exercise 2 · Application · keep the fractions

Wait another halving time for the imbalance

The pumps keep the same setting, without intervention. At time 2t_d, calculate a, b and their difference. Start with the exponential factor; do not apply a factor of one half to the volumes themselves.

Hint 1

exp(λₕ × 2t_d) is the product of two factors exp(λₕt_d).

Hint 2

The coefficient m does not change. Multiply only the initial coefficient h by the new factor.

Compare with the solution

The factor is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. The opposing coefficient becomes h(2t_d) = 2 × 1/4 = 1/2 L. The common coefficient remains 4 L.

So a(2t_d) = 4 + 1/2 = 9/2 L, or 4.5 L. Similarly, b(2t_d) = 4 − 1/2 = 7/2 L, or 3.5 L.

Checks: the total remains 8 L; the difference is 1 L. The initial difference of 4 L has been multiplied by 1/4, while the volumes have moved closer to 4 L.

Check your reasoning

  • I multiply the exponential factors rather than adding them.
  • I keep m constant and obtain 9/2 L and 7/2 L.
  • I check the total of 8 L and the difference of 1 L separately.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · show why a check is insufficient

A proposal preserves the total and starts with the right slope

Someone extends the initial derivatives as constants. They propose a_c(t) = a(0) + a′(0)t and b_c(t) = b(0) + b′(0)t, from the start up to τ = 2 min. The symbols a_c and b_c denote their candidate volumes. These have the right starting values, the right initial slope and the right total. Are these checks sufficient? Calculate the candidates at τ, then compare their derivatives with A applied to this state.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Multiply each initial net flow rate by τ to calculate the candidate changes.

Hint 2

If the two candidate volumes are equal, what balance do the exchanges give? Compare it with the proposed constant slopes.

Compare with the solution

The candidate changes are -1 × 2 = -2 L and 1 × 2 = 2 L. At τ, the candidates are therefore a_c(τ) = 6 + (-2) = 4 L and b_c(τ) = 2 + 2 = 4 L. Their sum remains 8 L.

In this state, the difference is 0 L: the matrix requires a′ = 0 L/min and b′ = 0 L/min, because the flow rates balance. Yet the candidate functions still have slopes of -1 L/min and 1 L/min. They therefore fail to satisfy X′ = AX at this instant.

An initial condition and an invariant do not replace the differential equation. The linear extension uses the initial slope as a local approximation; it is not our exact solution. In the true solution, at τ the imbalance is 4 exp(-1) L, which is strictly positive: equal volumes are reached only in the limit.

Check your reasoning

  • I reconstruct the candidate volumes at τ with consistent units.
  • I compare the vector of candidate derivatives with the matrix product and show their disagreement.
  • I explain why the correct total and initial slope fail to reveal this error.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In X′ = AX, what do a row and a column of A represent?

Once the order of the volumes in X is fixed, a row calculates the derivative of the corresponding volume. A column indicates which volume contributes to these derivatives. Here each coefficient has units of inverse minutes.

Recall 2How can you recover the coefficients of u and v from the tank volumes?

With u = (1, 1) and v = (1, -1), the coordinates are m = (a + b)/2 and h = (a − b)/2. Check the reconstruction a = m + h and b = m − h. The vectors are dimensionless; the coefficients are in litres.

Recall 3Why does the common mode stay constant while the difference diminishes?

Au = 0u gives m′ = 0. Av = (−2k)v gives h′ = −2k h, so h(t) = h(0) exp(−2kt). The sum of the balance equations is 0, and their difference recovers the same decay.

Recall 4What must you add to conservation of the total to check the proposed solution?

Recover the initial conditions, differentiate the proposed function and compare its derivative with AX at every instant. Conservation of the total is not sufficient: the linear proposal in the exercise preserves the total but fails this comparison.

Recall 5Why does convergence of the two volumes not imply a single attractive equilibrium?

The difference decays, but the common mode preserves the initial total. Every state a = b is an equilibrium. Our experiment converges to (4 L; 4 L) because its total is 8 L; a different total would give a different limit.
