Level 2 · C064·M1

Latent state and observation

Your goalDistinguish the hidden temperature from its sensor reading, write their relationship, and show why a single reading is not enough to recover the true value.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A sensor in a room displays 22 °C. This number is available; the exact temperature of the room is not. The sensor can read too high or too low. Before calculating an update, we need to separate what we are looking for from what we receive.

Here, the state is the temperature that our model aims to track. It is a latent state because the sensor reading does not give it directly. The observation is the number actually read. Measurement noise is the difference between this reading and what a perfect sensor would give at the same instant.

The scene and all its values are fictional. To make the reasoning checkable, the author knows a reference temperature of 20 °C and a difference of +2 °C. The person who receives only the reading does not have this hidden information. The first diagram reveals how the example was constructed; it does not give that person a second sensor.

The second diagram gives another explanation for the same reading. As long as both explanations satisfy the sensor assumptions, the displayed number cannot distinguish between them. An estimate will be a proposed value for the state, together with its limitations; simply renaming it will not make it the true value.

The rule to remember

Name the state being sought, the available observation, and the hidden noise separately; write their relationship before treating a reading as the true value.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What the sensor reading actually contains

At a fixed instant, our fictional reference temperature is 20 °C. The sensor model adds either +2 °C or -2 °C, each possibility having probability 1/2, or 0.50 with no unit. The received reading is 22 °C. Let us reconstruct how it was produced, the properties of the noise, and the limit on reconstructing the state.

  1. Set the observation model

    x denotes the actual temperature at the measurement point; z, the sensor reading; v, its measurement noise. x and z are in °C; v is a temperature difference, also expressed in °C. We assume a direct measurement, at the same place and the same instant, with no delay or change of scale. The coefficient of x is therefore 1, with no unit.

    z=x+v

    For every temperature considered, we assume the same two possible values of v and the same probabilities. This is a discrete distribution chosen for the exercise. We do not assume that it has a bell shape, and we do not claim to describe all the errors of a real sensor.

  2. Produce the observation, then recover the difference

    In the outcome described, the noise is +2 °C. Adding this noise to the temperature explains the displayed number. Then subtracting the reference from the reading checks the sign of the difference.

    z=20+2=22 °C

    Check: z − x = 22 − 20 = 2 °C. Positive noise means that the reading is too high. This check uses x, which the author knows; with only the sensor, we could not calculate the actual noise in this way.

  3. Understand what it means for noise to have 0 mean

    E denotes expectation, meaning the weighted average using the probabilities in the model. Here, each possibility has equal weight: we can add the two noise values, then divide by 2. This average concerns the possible outcomes at a fixed temperature, not a list of measurements already taken.

    E[v]=2+(-2)2=02=0 °C

    The noise has 0 mean: its average is 0 °C. Yet every reading in this model has a nonzero error. “0 mean” means no average offset under these assumptions; it does not mean that every reading is free from error.

  4. Calculate the noise variance

    R denotes the noise variance, in °C²: the average squared difference between v and its mean E[v]. Since E[v] is 0 °C, here it is the average of the squares of v. Squaring prevents the signs from cancelling as they did when the mean was 0.

    Positive noise squared: 2 × 2 = 4 °C². Negative noise squared: (-2) × (-2) = 4 °C². The sum of these squares is 8 °C²; we divide it by 2 because the outcomes are equally likely.

    R=E[v2]=4+42=4 °C²

    R describes a spread predicted by our noise distribution. It is not the signed noise v of the received reading. The square of each noise value equals R here because of the particular choice of two opposite noise values with the same magnitude; this is not a general rule.

  5. Try to work back from the reading to the state

    Isolating x in z = x + v gives x = z − v. If the noise in the reading were known, we would recover 22 − 2 = 20 °C. But the sensor does not provide v separately.

    Take the other allowed noise value, -2 °C. The same reading can then come from a state of 22 − (-2) = 24 °C. Checking in the forward direction: 24 + (-2) = 22 °C. We therefore have two allowed state–noise pairs that give exactly the same display.

    This does not make these two states equally likely after the reading: no prior probability for x has been given. Nor does it prove that the model could not be identified with more information. The conclusion concerns this single observation and these assumptions.

  6. Check in the other direction and name the estimate

    At a fixed temperature x = 20 °C, the other possible reading is 20 + (-2) = 18 °C. The mean of the possible readings is (18 + 22)/2 = 40/2 = 20 °C. We recover the reference: the reading is correct on average under the model, even though neither of these two outcomes equals it.

    Another check of R: the readings 18 °C and 22 °C differ from their mean by -2 °C and +2 °C. Squaring these differences gives 4 °C² again. A noise variance is therefore not a number of degrees to subtract from the display.

    x̂, read as “x hat”, denotes an estimate of x, in °C, constructed from the available information. x remains the actual state; z remains the observation. Provisionally choosing x̂ = z would not demonstrate x̂ = x. This unit establishes these roles; propagating the estimate will come in the next unit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The sensor displays 22 °C, so the state is 22 °C.”

    Write z = x + v. The pairs x = 20 °C with v = +2 °C and x = 24 °C with v = -2 °C give the same reading. Keep z as the observation and ask what information would allow you to choose x.

    Compare the two explanations in the diagram

  • “Noise with 0 mean never changes the reading.”

    Calculate the mean of +2 °C and -2 °C, then look at each outcome separately. The cancellation occurs in the mean of the model; neither noise value is 0.

    Review the 0 mean property and its check

  • “To correct the sensor, I subtract R from the reading.”

    R = 4 °C² is an average of squares. To isolate x, you would need to subtract the signed noise v, in °C, which remains hidden. Write the units before any subtraction and distinguish variance from a temperature difference.

    Review the definitions of v and R

Hint 1

Add each noise value separately to the same temperature; do not let the room change.

Hint 2

For the variance, subtract the mean from each reading, then average the squares.

Compare with the solution

Low reading: 20 + (-2) = 18 °C. High reading: 20 + 2 = 22 °C. Each outcome has probability 1/2, or 0.50 with no unit.

Their mean is (18 + 22)/2 = 40/2 = 20 °C. The differences from this mean are -2 °C and +2 °C. Their variance is (4 + 4)/2 = 4 °C².

The mean describes the distribution of possible outcomes at fixed x. The only readings in this distribution are 18 °C and 22 °C; neither equals the mean. We have neither simulated nor taken a series of measurements.

  • Distinguish the theoretical mean from a sensor outcome.

Exercise 3 · Reasoning

Is a known noise distribution enough?

You are given only z = 22 °C and the two equally likely noise values in the model. Someone says: “The noise has 0 mean and its variance is known; we can therefore recover x exactly and assign the same probability to the possible states.” Recover all the compatible states, check each one, and discuss both claims.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Rewrite x = z − v for each allowed noise value.

Hint 2

The probabilities of the noise values at a fixed state do not constitute a prior probability distribution over the states.

Compare with the solution

With v = +2 °C, x = 22 − 2 = 20 °C. With v = -2 °C, x = 22 − (-2) = 24 °C. These are all the states compatible with the only two allowed noise values.

Checks: 22 − 20 = 2 °C and 22 − 24 = -2 °C; both noise values belong to the distribution. The 0 mean and R = 4 °C² therefore do not resolve the ambiguity of this single reading.

No probability distribution for x before the reading is provided. We can list the compatible states, but cannot automatically assign them equal probabilities after the observation. We would need to specify this prior information, or obtain other information, to go further.

Check your reasoning

  • Find the two states and check each noise value in the direction z − x.
  • Limit the conclusion to this observation and this discrete distribution.
  • Distinguish compatibility from probability after observation.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In the sensor scene, what do x, z, and v denote?

x is the hidden actual temperature; z is the available reading; v is the hidden measurement error, with z = x + v. x and z are in °C; v is a temperature difference in °C.

Recall 2Why does noise with 0 mean not guarantee an exact reading?

The mean concerns the possible outcomes. Here, +2 °C and -2 °C cancel on average, but each produces a reading that differs from the state.

Recall 3What does R describe, and why can it not be subtracted from z?

R is the noise variance, in °C². It describes a spread in the model, not the signed noise of this reading. Isolating x would require v, in °C, which remains hidden.

Recall 4Construct two states compatible with the reading 22 °C, then state what you cannot deduce from them.

20 °C with +2 °C of noise and 24 °C with -2 °C both give 22 °C. The reading alone does not choose the state; without a prior probability for x, it does not automatically make these states equally likely either.

Recall 5What distinction do you keep between x, x̂, and z?

x is the actual state; x̂ is an estimate of that state based on the available information; z is the received observation. Choosing x̂ = z does not prove that this estimate equals x.
