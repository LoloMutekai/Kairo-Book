Level 4 · C257·M3

Integrated variation

Your goalDefine the integrated variance of a continuous log price, calculate it over a window, and explain what an observed sum of squares actually tells us about it.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You follow the same fictional logarithmic index for 4 s. The model assigns a low level of fluctuation until 1 s, then a higher level until 4 s. You want to quantify the fluctuation accumulated over this window, even if the index ends close to where it started.

The final displacement can hide movements back and forth. A variance intensity describes the dispersion produced per unit of time. Integrated variance accumulates this intensity over the seconds: a long period contributes more than a short period with the same intensity. The first diagram shows this accumulation as an area.

Observations, however, provide only a few points. We can add the squared differences between successive points: this is the realized quadratic variation on this grid. It targets integrated variance in a continuous model without noise as the intervals become small. On a finite grid, the target and the observed result remain distinct.

Here, “integrated variation” refers specifically to the integrated variance of the continuous component. It is neither a sum of signed displacements nor a sum of their absolute values. All values are fictional. Observations are given in index units u; no real market quotations are used.

The rule to remember

Integrate squared volatility to define the target; add squared increments to construct an estimate; state the conditions that connect the two.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A window, a target, and observations

Over the window [0, 4] s, the fictional model has 0 drift. Its volatility σ is 1 u/√s before 1 s, then 2 u/√s. The observations of X are 0, 1, 3, 2 u at times 0, 1, 3, 4 s. The profile of σ is supplied so that the target is known; in an actual estimation problem, it would generally be unknown.

  1. Define the process and its units

    Pt is a strictly positive price; Pᵣ is a fixed reference price in the same unit. We work with the index Xt = 100 ln(Pt/Pᵣ). The logarithm is applied to a dimensionless ratio. We use u for the conventional unit of this index: it is neither a euro nor a relative return on its own.

    dXt=btdt+σtdWt

    This notation is shorthand for a continuous Ito model. t is time in seconds; bt is the drift in u/s; nonnegative σt is the volatility in u/√s. W is a Brownian motion: its increments over disjoint intervals are independent, centered Gaussian variables with variance equal to the duration. W is therefore expressed in √s.

    On a fixed window ending at T, we assume that b and σ are progressively measurable, so they are consistent with the information available without anticipating the future, and that the integrals of |b| and of squared σ are finite almost surely. There are no jumps or measurement noise here. The quadratic variation of a semimartingale, covered in the prerequisites, will be recalled when we connect it to the observations.

  2. Name the target quantity

    IVT=∫0Tσs2ds

    IV denotes the integrated variance of the continuous component, s is the time variable in the integral, and ds is an elementary duration. Squared σ is an intensity in u²/s; after integration, IV is in u². It is nonnegative and additive over contiguous windows. If σ is random, IV is also a random quantity specific to the path.

    For strictly positive T, IV/T is the average intensity over the window, in u²/s. The square root of IV is in u; the square root of IV/T is in u/√s. These square roots are further distinct quantities. We do not integrate σ and then square the result: the order of operations is part of the definition. IV is generally not the unconditional variance of the total displacement.

  3. Calculate the target piece by piece

    The first duration is 1 − 0 = 1 s, and the second is 4 − 1 = 3 s. Their intensities are respectively 1 × 1 = 1 u²/s and 2 × 2 = 4 u²/s. Multiplying each intensity by its duration gives the variance accumulated in that part.

    IV=1×1+4×3=1+12=13 u²

    Check using a different partition: the observation intervals last 1, 2, 1 s. They accumulate 1, 8, 4 u²; their sum recovers 13 u². Partitioning the integral does not change the target.

    The average intensity is 13/4 = 13/4 u²/s, or 3.25 u²/s. It lies between 1 and 4 u²/s, which provides a check on the result. This average rate is not the integrated quantity 13 u².

  4. Move from points to increments

    A grid π contains the times tⱼ, strictly increasing from 0 s to T, with j ranging from 0 to n; n is the number of intervals, rather than the number of points. We write rⱼ for the index increment over the interval ending at tⱼ, and Δtⱼ for its duration.

    rj=Xtj−Xtj−1;Δtj=tj−tj−1

    Here n = 3. The successive differences are 1 − 0 = 1 u; 3 − 1 = 2 u; 2 − 3 = -1 u. The last increment is negative because the index falls again. We do not divide these differences by the durations: they are changes in the index, rather than rates of change.

  5. Construct the realized sum of squares

    RVπ=∑j=1nrj2

    RV is the realized quadratic variation on the grid π. Squaring retains magnitudes while removing signs. Each square is already in u²: to target IV, we must neither multiply again by Δtⱼ nor divide the sum by n.

    RVπ=12+22+(-1)2=1+4+1=6 u²

    The net displacement is 2 − 0 = 2 u; its square is 4 u². It contains cross products between increments and does not recover 6 u². Finally, 6 is not equal to the target 13: these few points do not make the estimate exact.

  6. Explain the connection without promising equality on a finite grid

    In the submodel used in the example, b is 0 and σ is deterministic. On each interval, rⱼ is a centered Gaussian variable with variance vⱼ, where vⱼ is the integral of squared σ over that interval. Here the vⱼ are 1, 8, 4 u². The expectation of a squared increment is therefore vⱼ: its duration is already accounted for.

    𝔼[RVπ]=∑j=1nvj=IVT

    𝔼 denotes an expectation over the possible paths of the model, rather than an average of the points on this single observed path. The expectation here is 13 u²; the fictional observed result remains 6 u². For a centered Gaussian variable with variance v, the moment of order 2 is v and the moment of order 4 is 3 times squared v. The variance of its square is therefore 2 times squared v.

    The increments are independent in this submodel. Var denotes a probabilistic variance; that of RV is in u to the power 4. Adding the variances of the squared increments, then bounding each squared vⱼ by the largest vⱼ multiplied by vⱼ, gives:

    Var(RVπ)=2∑j=1nvj2≤2(maxjvj)IVT

    If squared σ is bounded by a constant M in u²/s, each vⱼ is at most M times the longest duration in the grid. As this duration tends to 0 at a fixed horizon T, the bound tends to 0. Since the estimator is unbiased here, its mean squared error tends to 0: this explains why RV converges to IV in this submodel. Yet a new finite grid need not give a closer value; the exercise on grouping intervals demonstrates this.

  7. Place the result in the general continuous model

    The mesh |π| is the longest duration between successive times. For the continuous Ito process defined above, on deterministic grids whose mesh tends to 0, the quadratic variation result is:

    RVπ→ℙ[X]T=IVT

    [X] is the quadratic variation of the process, in u²: the limit in probability of the sums of squared increments. The arrow marked ℙ means convergence in probability: for any strictly positive tolerance δ in u², the probability that the absolute difference exceeds δ tends to 0. The preceding proof covers the deterministic case without drift; on its own, it does not prove this general result when σ is random.

    Why does the drift disappear from this limit? Its time integral A, in u, is a continuous function of finite variation: the sum of the absolute values of its increments remains bounded. The sum of their squares is bounded by the largest absolute increment multiplied by this total variation; it therefore tends to 0. Cross terms with the Brownian component also disappear, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. The drift can nevertheless affect an estimate on a finite grid.

  8. Check the limits: jumps and noise

    If the same index has jumps, ΔXs denotes the difference between its value just after and just before the jump at time s. The quadratic variation of the Ito process with jumps then contains an additional contribution:

    [X]T=IVT+∑0<s≤T(ΔXs)2

    If the observation is Yⱼ = X at time tⱼ plus εⱼ, then εⱼ is centered measurement noise in u, independent across times and independent of X. Its standard deviation η is in u, and its variance is squared η in u². A noise increment subtracts the noise at two times: its variance is 2 times squared η. When the expectations exist, we therefore have:

    𝔼[RVπ(Y)]=𝔼[RVπ(X)]+2nη2

    This identity concerns an expectation, rather than an exact correction of our observed path. At a fixed noise variance, increasing n increases this unwanted term. Continuity and the absence of noise are therefore crucial assumptions. We are still considering a single series; estimators that account for noise and frequency-domain calculations require the following units.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The square of the final displacement measures all the fluctuation.”

    The squared net displacement is 4 u², whereas the sum of successive squares is 6 u². Adding before squaring introduces cross products. Start again from each increment and keep the signed total, RV, and the target IV separate.

    Review the increments and the sum of squares

  • “The times are irregular: I multiply each observed square by its duration.”

    The expectation of a squared increment already contains the variance accumulated over its duration. The product intensity × duration calculates IV from the model. Multiplying an observed square by a duration again would give u²·s, instead of the target u².

    Review the integral, the units, and the expectation of RV

  • “The more often I observe, the more certain I am to obtain IV.”

    Even without noise, convergence is probabilistic and does not guarantee an improvement at each finite refinement. With the independent noise described here, the expectation adds 2 times n times squared η; with jumps, the limit includes their squares. The assumptions must be checked before applying the convergence result.

    Compare different groupings of the same observed path

The durations are 1 and 2 s. The contributions are 1 × 1 = 1 u² and 4 × 2 = 8 u². Their sum gives 9 u².

(a+b)2−(a2+b2)=2ab

a and b are neighboring increments, in u. Their product can be positive or negative: no general monotonicity is possible. The target IV remains 13 u² for all these grids. Convergence as the mesh tends to 0 is a property of the probabilistic model, rather than an ordering of all finite sums.

Can a standard deviation replace the sum?

For the 3 increments 1, 2, -1 u, calculate their mean r̄ and then V, their empirical variance with division by n. Without calculating a square root, recover RV from V and r̄. Can a value called “volatility” equal to the square root of V be reported as IV? The profile of σ is still the one from the lesson.

V=1n∑j=1n(rj−r¯)2

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The sum of the increments recovers the net displacement 2 u; divide by n to obtain r̄.

Hint 2

Expand the centered squares and use the fact that the sum of the increments equals n times their mean.

Compare with the solution

The mean is 2/3 = 2/3 u, or approximately 0.6667 u. Its square is 4/9 u². Centering the increments subtracts n times this square from the uncentered sum: 3 × (4/9) = 4/3 u².

The sum of centered squares is 6 − (4/3) = 14/3 u². After division by n, V = 14/9 u², or approximately 1.5556 u². This calculation treats the increments as a collection; with irregular time steps, it does not directly represent an intensity per second.

RVπ=nV+nr¯2

Numerically, 14/3 + 4/3 = 6 u². We recover the uncentered sum by another route, without confusing V with RV.

The square root of V is a standard deviation in u. It differs from RV through centering, normalization, and taking a square root; RV in turn differs from the target IV = 13 u² on this finite observed path. A field name is therefore insufficient to identify the quantity. Without the profile of σ supplied in the exercise, these points alone would not determine IV exactly.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain the mean 2/3 u and the variance 14/9 u² as exact fractions.
  • Reconstruct 6 u² by restoring the centering term.
  • Distinguish standard deviation, sum of squares, and integrated variance, with their units and assumptions.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What quantity do we integrate, and what is the unit of the result?

We integrate squared volatility σ over time. If σ is in u/√s, its square is in u²/s and IV is in u². IV/T is an average intensity; it is not the integrated quantity.

Recall 2Why do we not multiply each squared increment by its duration again?

In the submodel with 0 drift and deterministic volatility, the expectation of this square is already the integral of the intensity over the interval. Multiplying it by a duration again would change the quantity and its unit.

Recall 3Under what conditions does RV target IV, and what does convergence mean?

For the continuous Ito process and the integrability conditions in the lesson, with exact observations on deterministic grids whose longest duration tends to 0, RV converges in probability to IV at a fixed horizon. The probability of an error exceeding a fixed tolerance tends to 0; the error does not necessarily decrease at each finite refinement.

Recall 4What happens to the limit and the expectation if we add jumps or measurement noise?

Jumps add their squares to quadratic variation, beyond the continuous integrated variance. With centered noise independent across times and independent of the process, with variance η squared, the expectation of RV adds 2 times n times squared η. These findings do not provide an exact correction of a particular observed path.

Recall 5How do we distinguish a centered empirical variance V from the sum of squares RV?

With n increments of mean r̄ and V defined with division by n, RV equals n times V plus n times squared r̄. Taking a square root then gives yet another quantity. The name “volatility” establishes neither the formula nor its suitability for IV.
