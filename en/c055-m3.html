Level 1 · C055·M3

Introduction to the central limit theorem

Your goalDistinguish the distribution of durations from the distribution of means, then center and scale a mean to understand the normal limit.

Before you start
c035-m5 : Finite covariance and conditioning · c024-m5 : Quality of readings · c055-m1 : Sampling distribution · c055-m2 : Law of large numbers

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional workshop, a part check is either short, 2 min, or long, 6 min. Each duration has probability 1/2. The station is reset to the same state before each part; in our model, the durations are independent and always follow this same distribution. These are exercise assumptions, rather than workshop readings.

The supervisor keeps the mean duration of each batch. If you repeat the process with new batches of the same size, these means change. Their sampling distribution describes this variation between batches; it does not describe individual durations. A check remains short or long, even when the means cluster near 4 min.

The law of large numbers explains the clustering near the theoretical mean as batches grow. The central limit theorem specifies something else: under our assumptions, the distribution of the deviations of the means, enlarged at the right scale, approaches a normal distribution. This bell shape describes a limit; no individual check becomes a duration with a bell-shaped distribution.

To see the deviations without letting them shrink away, we subtract their center and then divide by a scale that decreases with batch size. The second diagram previews this change of reference scale; we will justify each quantity involved.

The rule to remember

First ask which variable is being discussed. For independent observations with the same distribution and finite positive variance, the mean, centered and divided by σ/√n, converges in distribution to the standard normal distribution.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Locating the mean of a batch of checks

The fictional model assigns 1/2 to 2 min and 1/2 to 6 min. An observed batch contains 45 short checks and 55 long checks. We will find its size, mean and standardized deviation, then distinguish the exact calculation from the normal limit.

  1. Define the objects before discussing a bell shape

    n is the number of checks in a batch, a strictly positive integer; i identifies a check, from 1 to n. Xᵢ is its random duration in minutes before observation; xᵢ is the duration actually observed. The bar in X̄n indicates the random batch mean; x̄ denotes its observed value. The symbol Σ tells us to add the durations of all the checks.

    X¯n=∑i=1nXin

    Each Xᵢ follows the same distribution, and all durations are mutually independent: knowing the durations of the other checks does not change the distribution of the next one. The population mean μ and the variance, written as σ squared, are finite; σ is strictly positive. Here they are known by construction. If the state of the station drifts, or a shared fault links the durations, these assumptions need to be reexamined.

  2. Find the center and spread of the durations

    E means expectation, the theoretical mean weighted by probabilities. Thus μ = E[Xᵢ] = 1/2 × 2 + 1/2 × 6 = 4 min. This center comes from the model of all possible checks, rather than the batch we have just read.

    Var means variance: the expected squared deviation from μ. The deviations of the durations are -2 and 2 min. Squaring them gives:

    σ2=1/2×(2−4)2+1/2×(6−4)2=4 min²

    The standard deviation σ is the positive square root of the variance: σ = 2 min, since 2 × 2 = 4 min². It measures the spread of individual durations. Check with another identity: the theoretical mean of the squared durations minus the square of μ also gives the variance:

    22+622−42=4 min²

  3. Count a distribution of means exactly

    First return to batches of 4 checks. Use C to label a short check and L to label a long check. Independence makes the 16 ordered sequences equally likely: each position can contain C or L. The mean varies with the number of L labels.

    The sequence CCCC gives 2 min and LLLL gives 6 min: each has probability 1/16. With a single L, its 4 positions give the mean 3 min and probability 1/4; with a single C, the mean is 5 min with the same probability.

    The sequences CCLL, CLCL, CLLC, LCCL, LCLC and LLCC all give 4 min: 6 sequences, hence probability 3/8. Check: 1/16 + 1/4 + 3/8 + 1/4 + 1/16 = 1. We have an exact distribution on separate values, rather than a continuous normal distribution.

  4. State what converges to a normal distribution

    Independence makes the covariances between distinct durations vanish: the variance of their sum is n times the individual variance. Dividing the sum by n divides its variance by the square of n. The center remains E[X̄n] = μ; the variance of the mean is therefore:

    Var(X¯n)=σ2n

    Let an = σ/√n, in minutes: this is the theoretical standard deviation of this mean.

    We center by subtracting μ, then standardize by dividing by an. Zn denotes the resulting variable, which has no unit. The central limit theorem, in the version for independent observations with the same distribution used here, states:

    Zn=X¯n−μσ/n ; Zn→loi𝒩(0,1)

    The arrow means “converges in distribution as n grows without bound.” 𝒩(0, 1) is the standard normal distribution, with mean 0 and variance 1. More precisely, for every real threshold t with no unit, the probability P(Zn ≤ t) tends to Φ(t), the probability of being at most t under this normal distribution. This is what “the distribution approaches” means.

    The law of large numbers brings X̄n close to μ. The central limit theorem describes the shape of its fluctuations after enlargement by √n/σ. These statements complement each other. For finite n, even a large n, no exact equality with a normal distribution follows from the theorem, and no universal size guarantees a given accuracy.

  5. Calculate the scale of the observed batch

    The batch size is 45 + 55 = 100 checks. With n = 100, the square root √n is 10, because 10 × 10 = 100. We therefore use an = 2/10 = 1/5 min, or 0.2 min.

    Check the units and the square: Var(X̄n) = 4/100 = 1/25 min², or 0.04 min²; squaring the scale 1/5 min gives this same variance. A duration keeps its standard deviation of 2 min. The spread of means between batches decreases; the spread of individual checks does not.

    These moments are exact under the model, even if the distribution of X̄n is not exactly normal. The limiting normal shape is an additional statement; calculating the variance does not prove it. The next unit will explore this scale further under the name standard error.

  6. Calculate the mean and locate it

    First add the durations: 45 × 2 = 90 min for the short checks; 55 × 6 = 330 min for the long checks. Total: 90 + 330 = 420 min. Then divide by the batch size, since each check contributes to the mean.

    The observed mean is x̄ = 420/100 = 21/5 min, or 4.2 min. Its deviation from the center is 4.2 − 4 = 0.2 min. The lowercase letter z denotes the observed value of Zn:

    z=21/5−41/5=1/51/5=1

    The mean is therefore 1 scale unit above its center. The minutes cancel in the quotient. This number locates the batch; it is neither a probability nor proof that the workshop model is true.

  7. Check using the sum and limit the conclusion

    Recalculate z without using the decimal form of the mean. The expected total is nμ = 100 × 4 = 400 min. Its observed deviation is 420 − 400 = 20 min. The standard deviation of the sum is σ√n = 2 × 10 = 20 min. Their quotient is 20/20 = 1, as before.

    The total and the mean therefore give the same standardized position. All these calculations are exact for the stated model and batch. The theorem allows a normal approximation to be considered, without providing an error bound for this size here. We derive no percentage, confidence interval or decision about a real workshop from it.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Why do we subtract μ rather than the batch mean? Why do we divide by σ/√n rather than by σ? Then explain what is exact in our result and what comes from a limiting distribution.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “With many checks, the durations become normally distributed.”

    A check still lasts 2 or 6 min. The limit concerns centered and standardized means. Use the bars in the diagram to distinguish the duration variable from the mean variable again.

    Compare durations and means

  • “The mean gets closer to the center, so the normal limit has already been proved.”

    The concentration of X̄ₙ near μ is the idea of the law of large numbers. To see the limiting shape of the deviations, we need to enlarge them using their scale σ/√n. Review the definition of Zₙ and the meaning of convergence in distribution.

    Reread the statement and its assumptions

  • “A large number of rows is enough.”

    Copying a duration does not create new independent checks. A large displayed count therefore does not justify dividing the standard deviation by √n. First find out how each duration was obtained, and examine the last exercise.

    Test the batch made of copies

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · exact distribution

A small batch consisting entirely of long checks

For a batch of 4 independent checks, give the mean and the probability that all of them are long. Can a continuous normal distribution give exactly the same distribution of the mean? Justify your answer without using a normal table.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Which sequence of C and L gives the largest possible mean?

Hint 2

Independence multiplies the probabilities at each position. Then compare a positive point probability with a continuous distribution.

Compare with the solution

The sequence LLLL gives a mean of 6 min. Its probability is (1/2) to the power 4 = 1/16. This is also the weight of the bar at the right end of the first diagram.

The probability that the mean is exactly 6 min is therefore positive. Under a continuous normal distribution, the probability of a single value is 0. The distributions cannot be identical. Approximating probabilities over ranges can become useful for large batches; it remains an approximation.

Check your reasoning

  • Give 6 min and 1/16, with a separate justification for each.
  • Use independence for the product of probabilities.
  • Distinguish an exact discrete distribution from a continuous limit.

Exercise 2 · Application · change of size

Same mean, different scale

A new batch of 400 independent checks from the same distribution contains 180 short checks and 220 long checks. These are new observations, rather than copies of the previous batch. Calculate its mean and z. You are given √400 = 20. Why does the same deviation in minutes occupy a different position after standardization?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with the total duration, then divide by 400.

Hint 2

The scale becomes 2/20 min. Compare it with that of the batch of 100 checks.

Compare with the solution

The batch is 4 times as large, with the same observed proportions of short and long checks. Its total is 180 × 2 + 220 × 6 = 1680 min. The mean is 1680/400 = 21/5 min, or 4.2 min.

The scale is an = 2/20 = 1/10 min, or 0.1 min. The deviation is still 0.2 min. Thus z = 1/5/1/10 = 2, with no unit.

The batch size is multiplied by 4 and its square root by 2: the scale is divided by 2. The same deviation in minutes therefore represents 2 scale units instead of 1. By itself, this determines neither an exact probability nor a decision.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover 4.2 min from the total of 1680 min.
  • Obtain 0.1 min and z = 2, with no unit for z.
  • Relate the factor in batch size to its square root and explain the change of scale.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer · assumptions

A single check, many copies

The station performs a single check, with random duration X. Its result is then copied into 100 rows, presented as a batch. The distribution of X remains the workshop distribution. What is the mean of the rows? Is its variance 1/25 min²? Can we apply our version of the central limit theorem to it?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the same duration X n times, then divide by n.

Hint 2

Once X is known, every other row is known. Examine independence before reusing the variance formula.

Compare with the solution

The mean is nX/n = X. It therefore remains 2 or 6 min, each with probability 1/2, regardless of the number of copies. Its variance remains 4 min², that of an individual duration, instead of 1/25 min².

The rows are perfectly dependent. The rule that made covariances between observations vanish no longer applies. The version of the theorem studied here therefore cannot be applied to these rows as though they formed an independent sample.

If we still divided by the incorrect scale 0.2 min, we would obtain -10 or 10, rather than a distribution approaching the standard normal distribution. The remedy is to count distinct checks and verify the collection protocol before choosing a model.

Check your reasoning

  • Show that the mean of the copies is exactly X.
  • Recover the variance 4 min² without using the formula reserved for independent observations.
  • Name the violated assumption and propose checking the data collection.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In the workshop, which variable approaches a standard normal distribution?

Zₙ = (X̄ₙ − μ)/(σ/√n), under the stated assumptions. The duration of a check remains 2 or 6 min; the unstandardized mean concentrates near μ.

Recall 2What assumptions does the version of the theorem studied here require?

The observations are mutually independent, with the same distribution, finite mean μ and finite strictly positive variance. σ is the standard deviation of this distribution. Increasing the number of copies of an observation does not satisfy independence.

Recall 3How can you recover the scale σ/√n and its unit?

Under independence, the variance of the sum is n times the individual variance; the variance of the mean is the individual variance divided by n. The positive square root is σ/√n, in minutes here. Subtracting μ and then dividing by this scale produces a quantity with no unit.

Recall 4What does the central limit theorem say beyond the law of large numbers?

The law of large numbers describes the concentration of X̄ₙ near μ. The central limit theorem describes the limiting normal shape of deviations after centering and rescaling; it does not make individual observations normally distributed.

Recall 5What is exact and what is a limit in our calculation?

The moments under the model, the batch mean and its z are calculated exactly. Convergence of the distribution of Zₙ to the normal distribution is asymptotic. Here it provides neither an exact normal distribution at finite size nor a numerical accuracy bound.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the sequence: independent durations with the same distribution → batch mean → center μ and individual variance divided by n → deviation divided by σ/√n → standard normal distribution in the limit.
