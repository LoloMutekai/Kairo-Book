Level 4 · C227·M1

Finite variation

Your goalDefine the total variation of a path, prove that it is finite, and separate its positive and negative accumulations.

Before you start
c226-m5 : integration with jumps · c165-m5 : duality · c171-m5 : the multidimensional case

✿01 See the idea

A cart slides along a marked rail. It starts at 0 m, moves forward to 3 m, moves back to 1 m, then finishes at 4 m. Its final displacement is 4 m. Yet the distance travelled is 8 m: the backward movement still counts towards the distance travelled.

Total variation applies this idea to a function of time. Choose times, take the absolute value of each change, then add them. Times that are too far apart can hide a movement out and back. We therefore need to consider every finite partition of the interval, meaning every possible division by a finite list of ordered times.

Saying “finite variation” means that these sums have a common finite upper bound. It does not mean that the position always increases, or that the number of changes of direction must be finite. The question concerns the total accumulation of changes.

The scene and its values are fictional. We first assume that the motion is affine on each segment between the stated positions: its velocity is constant there, with no hidden detour. This is an ideal kinematic model; we do not model acceleration at changes of direction.

The rule to remember

Add the absolute values of the changes, then take the supremum over all partitions; a single observed sum is not enough to prove finiteness.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Proving the total distance beyond a list of readings

The times 0 s, 1 s, 2 s and 3 s correspond respectively to the positions 0 m, 3 m, 1 m and 4 m. Between these times, the motion is affine. Let us calculate the total variation and prove that no partition gives a larger sum.

  1. Specify the path and its units

    A(t) is the position in metres at time t, measured in seconds. T is the duration under consideration; here T = 3 s and the start is at 0 s. A real-valued function here means that the position is described by a single signed number along the rail.

    The segment durations are 1 s, 1 s and 1 s. Their velocities are therefore 3 m/s, -2 m/s and 3 m/s. On each segment, A(t) is the starting position of the segment plus its velocity multiplied by the elapsed time.

    These constant velocities fully define the motion between the readings. This assumption will be essential in moving from a finite sum to a proof covering all partitions.

  2. Define the quantity to bound from above

    Π, the capital letter pi, denotes a partition, a strictly increasing list of times tᵢ. The index i runs from 0 to n; the first time is 0 s and the last is T. The indices i and n are integers with no units, with n strictly positive. ΔᵢA denotes the signed change between successive times, for i running from 1 to n. SΠ(A) is the sum of their absolute values, in metres.

    ΔiA=A(ti)−A(ti−1)
    SΠ(A)=∑i=1n|ΔiA|
    V[0,T](A)=supΠSΠ(A)

    sup denotes the supremum, the least upper bound of all these sums; it is +∞ when they are unbounded. A has finite variation on the interval if V is finite. Each SΠ is a lower bound for V. The partition consisting only of the start and end times also shows that |A(T) − A(0)| ≤ V.

  3. Understand what refinement adds

    Refining means adding times without removing the existing ones. If s < u < t are times, the change from s to t is the sum of the change from s to u and the change from u to t. The triangle inequality gives:

    |A(t)−A(s)|≤|A(u)−A(s)|+|A(t)−A(u)|

    A sum therefore cannot decrease under refinement. It can stay the same if the changes keep the same sign. Two lists, neither of which contains the other, cannot be compared directly by this argument: instead, take their ordered union, which refines both.

  4. Calculate using the changes of direction

    The signed changes are 3 − 0 = 3 m, then 1 − 3 = -2 m, then 4 − 1 = 3 m. The negative sign indicates backward movement rather than a negative distance.

    SΠ(A)=|3|+|-2|+|3|=8 m

    Adding gives 3 + 2 = 5 m, then 5 + 3 = 8 m. At this stage, we have only proved that V ≥ 8 m.

    Without absolute values, the intermediate terms cancel: 3 + (-2) + 3 = 4 m = A(T) − A(0). This is the net displacement. The extra distance is 8 − 4 = 4 m.

  5. Prove the bound for all partitions

    Start with an arbitrary partition. Add the times 1 s and 2 s. By the previous step, the original sum is less than or equal to the sum for this refinement.

    On each affine segment, the position is monotone: all successive changes have the same sign. The sum of their absolute values therefore reduces to the distance between the endpoints of the segment. Even with many additional points, we obtain 3 m on the first segment, 2 m on the backward segment and 3 m on the final forward segment.

    Every partition therefore gives a sum of at most 8 m. The partition at the changes of direction already attains this value. The lower and upper bounds coincide: V = 8 m, so the modelled motion does have finite variation. The proof uses monotonicity between the readings; the readings alone do not establish it.

  6. Check using the model velocities

    Let a(t) denote the velocity, in metres per second, on the interior of each segment. At the corners, we can assign it an arbitrary value for this integral: a finite number of times does not change the area. For our piecewise affine motion, the distance is the area under |a(t)|.

    ∫0T|a(t)|dt=3×1+2×1+3×1=8 m

    The symbol ∫ denotes the integral, and dt the element of duration in seconds. Multiply the absolute value of each velocity by its duration: for the backward segment, the absolute value is |-2| = 2 m/s. Each product has units (m/s) × s = m. Without absolute values, the integral of a gives the displacement 4 m.

    This check recovers 8 m from the velocities and durations. The identity with an integral of velocity is justified here by the affine segments; it must not be applied without assumptions to every function of finite variation.

  7. Separate the positive and negative accumulations

    For each time t, let V(t) denote the variation since the start and D(t) = A(t) − A(0) the displacement since that start. These quantities are in metres. Define the positive variation P(t) and negative variation N(t) by:

    P(t)=V(t)+D(t)2;N(t)=V(t)−D(t)2

    They start at 0 m and are nondecreasing. Indeed, for s < t, V(t) − V(s) is the variation on [s,t]: this follows by combining partitions on either side of s. It is at least |A(t) − A(s)|, so the increments of P and N are nonnegative. We recover D = P − N and V = P + N. This separation is the Jordan decomposition.

    At the end of the motion: P(T) = (8 + 4)/2 = 6 m and N(T) = (8 − 4)/2 = 2 m. The forward movements do accumulate 3 + 3 = 6 m; the backward movement accumulates 2 m. Checks: 6 − 2 = 4 m and 6 + 2 = 8 m.

    This decomposition is minimal among those starting at 0. If D = B − C with B and C nondecreasing, every sum of absolute changes is at most B(t) + C(t). Thus V(t) ≤ B(t) + C(t), and the formulas then give P(t) ≤ B(t) and N(t) ≤ C(t). Adding the same increasing function to both components would preserve D but lose this minimality.

  8. Keep the correct scope before stochastic calculus

    The definition using partitions also allows jumps. For a path that is right-continuous with a left limit, ΔA(t) = A(t) − A(t−) denotes the jump; A(t−) is the limit of the positions just before t. Its absolute value contributes to the variation. An integral of only the velocities between jumps would omit it. Our cart has no jumps in the model studied here.

    On an unbounded time interval, “locally of finite variation” means finite variation on each interval of finite duration, without requiring a common bound as the time horizon grows. For a random process A(t,ω), ω denotes an outcome. We require that, outside a single set of outcomes of probability 0, each path has this property for every finite horizon. This is not a statement about an expected variation.

    This unit establishes the notion path by path. It does not yet decompose a process into a martingale part and a finite variation part. The Jordan decomposition above only separates the increases and decreases of a path of finite variation.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculation. Why does a partition giving a large sum not establish the total variation on its own? Reconstruct the bound for all partitions, then explain the difference between displacement, variation and the two Jordan components.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The cart finishes a few metres from its starting point: that is its entire variation.”

    The displacement 4 m offsets the forward and backward movements. The variation adds their absolute values and is 8 m. Repeat the signed calculation, then the calculation using distances.

    Compare the two sums and their proof

  • “My list of readings is finite, so the path has finite variation.”

    A finite list gives a finite sum. The definition requires a bound for all partitions. The last exercise keeps the same readings while adding unseen movements between them.

    Test what the readings can hide

  • “Every refinement strictly increases the sum.”

    Refinement cannot decrease it; it can leave it unchanged on a monotone piece. Insert the point at 0.5 s from the first exercise and check the equality.

    Compare revealing a backward movement with subdividing a segment

Exercise 1 · Application · comparing partitions

A useful point and a neutral point

For the affine motion in the example, first calculate the sum at the times 0 s, 1 s and 3 s. Then add 2 s. Finally, insert 0.5 s between the start and the peak: what is the position at this time, and does the sum increase again?

Hint 1

The first list skips the minimum at 2 s. Use only the positions at the retained times.

Hint 2

On the first segment, the velocity is constant. Halfway through the time interval, the position is halfway between the endpoint positions.

Compare with the solution

For the coarse list: |3 − 0| + |4 − 3| = 3 + 1 = 4 m. Adding the trough separates the apparent change of 1 m into a backward movement of 2 m and a forward movement of 3 m; the sum becomes 8 m.

The midpoint is at (0 + 1)/2 = 0.5 s and its position is (0 + 3)/2 = 1.5 m. The distances 1.5 m and 1.5 m sum to 3 m. The refined total remains 8 m: the added point subdivides a forward movement without changing its length.

Exercise 2 · Reconstruction · two accumulations

Recover Jordan without reciting the fractions

At time 2 s, give the displacement, the variation accumulated so far, and the components P and N. Then recover the final components by counting the forward and backward movements. Does a positive component N mean that the final position is negative?

Hint 1

At the trough, only the first forward movement and the backward movement have taken place. P counts forward movements; N counts the distances moved backwards.

Hint 2

Check P − N and P + N separately. Both components are nonnegative accumulations.

Compare with the solution

At the trough, D = 1 − 0 = 1 m. The forward movements accumulate P = 3 m and the backward movements N = 2 m. Thus V = 3 + 2 = 5 m, while P − N = 1 m.

The last forward movement adds 3 m to P and nothing to N. At the end: P = 6 m, N = 2 m, D = 4 m and V = 8 m. N counts a positive distance moved backwards; the minus sign in D = P − N gives its effect on displacement. It denotes neither a position nor a negative distance.

Check your reasoning

  • At the trough, you distinguish 1 m of displacement from 5 m of variation.
  • You obtain nondecreasing functions P and N and explain what they accumulate.
  • You check the sum and difference of the final components.

Exercise 3 · Critique · missing assumption

The same readings, more distance travelled

Keep the positions at times 0 s, 1 s, 2 s and 3 s, but remove the affine assumption between the readings. After 2 s and before arrival, the cart makes 3 trips out and back from 1 m to 0 m and back to 1 m, before reaching 4 m. Each path is continuous and piecewise affine; no speed bound is imposed. Calculate the variation of this modified path. Can the readings alone give an upper bound valid for all compatible paths?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A detour returns to the same point: its net displacement is 0, but its distance is twice its amplitude.

Hint 2

Then replace the number of detours with an arbitrary integer k. The available duration does not limit the distance travelled if no maximum speed is given.

Compare with the solution

The amplitude is 1 m. Each trip out and back adds 2 × 1 = 2 m. The 3 detours add 6 m: the variation becomes 8 + 6 = 14 m. The displacement remains 4 m, since each detour returns to its starting point.

For k detours, where k is any positive integer, the distance is 8 m + k × 2 m. Any finite number of detours can fit into the remaining interval by increasing the speeds. The positions remain between 0 m and 4 m, but no common bound on the variation of these paths can be deduced from the readings.

Each path constructed with finite k still has finite variation. A family with no common bound does not prove that any particular path has infinite variation. The correct conclusion is that the readings alone determine neither the exact variation nor a uniform upper bound; an assumption about the motion between them is missing.

Check your reasoning

  • You find 14 m without changing the readings or the displacement.
  • You use arbitrary k to rule out a common bound, without claiming that a finite k gives infinity.
  • You identify the removed affine assumption and the absence of a speed bound.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you define total variation on an interval, and what does its finiteness mean?

For each finite ordered partition, add the absolute values of the successive changes. The total variation is the supremum of all these sums. Its finiteness requires a common bound for all partitions.

Recall 2Why does adding a time not decrease the sum, and when can it stay the same?

The triangle inequality bounds the absolute value of a change from above by the sum of the absolute values of the intermediate changes. Equality holds on a monotone piece; a refinement need not strictly increase the sum.

Recall 3What step turns the 8 m calculated for the cart into a proved total variation?

Any partition can be refined by adding the times of the changes of direction. On each monotone affine segment, the changes reduce to the distance between its endpoints. Every sum is therefore at most 8 m, a value already attained.

Recall 4How do you reconstruct the positive and negative accumulations from the displacement D and variation V?

P = (V + D)/2 and N = (V − D)/2. They are nondecreasing, start at 0, and satisfy D = P − N and V = P + N. They form the minimal decomposition among nondecreasing decompositions starting at 0.

Recall 5For a random process, what is the scope of “locally of finite variation”?

Outside a single set of outcomes of probability 0, each path has finite variation on every interval of finite duration. This definition requires neither a uniform bound as time grows nor a finite expectation of this variation.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the reasoning: distinguish displacement from accumulated distance; define the sums over partitions and then their supremum; prove the effect of refinement; give a sum that attains the bound and bound all others from above; recover P and N from their sum and difference; specify the time horizon and the assumptions between the readings.
