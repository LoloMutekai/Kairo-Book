Level 2 · C071·M2

Noisy-OR and independence

Your goalCalculate the probability that at least one route transmits a failure, then explain which independence assumption allows the product of complements.

Before you start
c070-m5 : minimum spanning tree · c029-m5 : Bayes · c030-m5 : Poisson · c063-m5 : synthetic dataset and external validity · c071-m1 : paths and multiplicative propagation

✿01 See the idea

A station receives commands through relays A and B. The 2 relays are already disrupted. Each may transmit its fault to the station, or the fault may stop before reaching it. The station fails as soon as at least one transmission reaches it. If the faults arrive together, the station does not fail any further: it is still the same event.

To calculate “at least one route reaches the station”, first look at the only situation that prevents it: no route reaches the station. This is the complement we need. Assuming the transmissions are independent, its probability is calculated by multiplying the blocking probabilities for each route.

This combination is called Noisy-OR: an “or” in which each route may fail to transmit. The “or” is inclusive: a single arrival or simultaneous arrivals are enough. Here we study neither the duration of a failure nor the severity of the damage.

The entire scenario and its probabilities are fictional. We hold the relays in their disrupted state throughout the same observation window. There is no other cause of station failure in this model. The diagram describes the possible routes; it does not demonstrate their independence.

The rule to remember

Define the transmission events, justify their independence in the fixed context, multiply the complements, then take the complement of the product.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From each route to station failure

Relays A and B are disrupted during the observation window. The probabilities of transmission to the station are p_A = 3/5 = 0.60 and p_B = 1/2 = 0.50. We assume these transmissions are independent in this context and that there is no other cause of failure. What probability should we assign to station failure?

  1. State exactly what the events represent

    A denotes the event “the fault transmitted along route A reaches the station”; B denotes the corresponding event for route B. V denotes “the station fails”. By assumption, V occurs exactly when A or B occurs: V = A ∪ B. The symbol ∪ means “inclusive or”, and ∩ means “and at the same time”.

    P(E) is the probability of an event E in the fixed context. All the probabilities below are conditional on the disrupted state of the relays; we omit this condition in the notation. p_A = P(A), p_B = P(B) and R = P(V) are dimensionless, between 0 and 1. They are neither durations nor frequencies measured at the real station.

    A bar over an event means that it does not occur. Thus Ā means “route A does not reach the station”. Let q_A = P(Ā) and q_B = P(B̄) be the blocking probabilities, also dimensionless.

  2. Place the assumption at the right step

    Here, independence means that, with the relay states fixed, knowing that one route has transmitted does not change the transmission probability of the other. For these 2 events, this is written P(A ∩ B) = p_A × p_B. Independence also holds for their complements.

    P(A¯∩B¯)=qA×qB

    This product is the only step that requires independence. The graph shows possible transmissions, rather than probabilistic dependencies between the events. A common disturbance or a shared segment can make this assumption false.

  3. Calculate the probability of no arrivals

    Each route either transmits or does not transmit. These outcomes are mutually exclusive and cover all its possibilities: their probabilities sum to 1. We therefore subtract from 1 to obtain each blocking probability.

    q_A = 1 − 3/5 = 2/5 = 0.40; q_B = 1 − 1/2 = 1/2 = 0.50. For the station to remain intact, the 2 routes must both be blocked.

    P(V¯)=25×12=15=0.20

    The multiplication concerns simultaneous blocking, under the stated assumption. The result is not yet the probability of failure.

  4. Return to at least one arrival

    Failure and no failure are complementary events. We therefore subtract the probability of no arrivals from the total probability 1.

    R=1−(1−pA)(1−pB)

    Numerically: R = 1 − 1/5 = 4/5 = 0.80. To express a percentage, multiply the probability by 100: 0.80 × 100 = 80 %. The probability of no failure is 20 %. Their sum accounts for the entire probability.

    These percentages describe the fictional model. They do not predict how many failures a small real series must necessarily contain.

  5. Check using the distinct outcomes

    The “A alone” cell has probability p_A × q_B = 3/5 × 1/2 = 3/10. “B alone” has probability q_A × p_B = 2/5 × 1/2 = 1/5. “Both” has probability p_A × p_B = 3/5 × 1/2 = 3/10. The “neither” cell has probability 1/5.

    These outcomes are mutually exclusive. Their sum provides a check: 3/10 + 1/5 + 3/10 + 1/5 = 1. The failure outcomes give 3/10 + 1/5 + 3/10 = 4/5, matching the complement calculated above. “Exactly one route” would give only 1/2: that is not the question asked.

    Another check starts from the identity P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B), which holds even without independence. Here, the intersection has probability 3/10, so 11/10 − 3/10 = 4/5. The naive sum p_A + p_B = 1.10, or 110 %, counted simultaneous arrivals 2 times; we subtract their probability 1 time.

  6. Check the bounds and limiting cases

    Event V contains A and contains B: R must be at least as large as each of their probabilities. We do have 0.80 ≥ 0.60 and 0.80 ≥ 0.50, without exceeding 1. A result of 1.10 would immediately have failed this check.

    An inactive route has a transmission probability of 0 in our model. If B is inactive, its complement is 1 and the product becomes 2/5 × 1 = 2/5; the risk returns to 3/5, the risk from A alone. At the other extreme, if B transmits with certainty, its complement is 0: the product is 0 and R = 1.

    Adding the independent route B increases the risk here by 1/5, since 4/5 − 3/5 = 1/5. This increase corresponds to “B alone”. It is smaller than p_B: faults in which A already reached the station do not add a new failure event.

  7. Read the same rule for several routes

    R=1−∏i∈I(1−pi)

    Mutual independence concerns the entire collection; checking only pairs is generally no longer sufficient. With no active route, the empty product is 1 by convention and R is 0, since we have included no external cause. Introducing an unrepresented cause would require an additional parameter and assumption.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I add the risks from each route.”

    The sum 1.10 includes the same outcome “A and B” 2 times. Under independence, its probability is 3/10; subtract it 1 time to obtain 4/5. Find the distinct outcomes in the table.

    Review the check using outcomes

  • “The product of the complements already gives failure.”

    The product 2/5 × 1/2 = 1/5 describes “no arrivals”. You still need to take its complement to obtain “at least one”. Find the intact cell in the diagram before choosing the operation.

    Identify the outcome that avoids failure

  • “Different paths must be independent.”

    Separate arrows guarantee nothing about the events. Fix the context, then ask whether an arrival along one route changes the probability for the other. The last exercise keeps the individual probabilities but changes the overlap; the risk changes too.

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · limiting case

Relay B no longer transmits

At the same station, B is made inactive. Its effective transmission probability is 0; p_A remains 3/5. Calculate the probability of no arrivals, then the probability of failure. Explain why the result must match the risk from A alone.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A route that never transmits is blocked with probability 1.

Hint 2

Multiply this complement by q_A before returning to failure.

Compare with the solution

The complement for route B is 1 − 0 = 1. No arrivals: 2/5 × 1 = 2/5. Failure: 1 − 2/5 = 3/5 = 0.60.

B adds no possible failure event. The union reduces to A, so the result must equal p_A. In particular, this check detects a formula that would multiply p_A directly by p_B to represent “at least one”.

Check your reasoning

  • You distinguish 2/5 for no arrivals from 3/5 for failure.
  • You justify the complement being 1 and the return to p_A.

Exercise 2 · Application · calculation and check

Reduce transmission by B

B is active again, but its transmission probability is now 1/4 = 0.25. Keep p_A = 3/5 and independence of the transmissions. Calculate the new risk using complements, then check it by subtracting the overlap from the sum. Compare it with the initial risk.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The new blocking probabilities are 2/5 for A and 1 − 1/4 for B.

Hint 2

For the other check, the intersection of the arrival events has probability p_A × p_B under this assumption.

Compare with the solution

The new complement for B is 1 − 1/4 = 3/4. No arrivals: 2/5 × 3/4 = 3/10. Failure: 1 − 3/10 = 7/10 = 0.70.

Check using the union: the sum is 3/5 + 1/4 = 17/20, and the intersection is 3/5 × 1/4 = 3/20. The difference 17/20 − 3/20 = 7/10 matches the result.

The risk falls from 0.80 to 0.70 and remains above 0.60. This is consistent: route B transmits less often, but can still reach the station when A does not. This change is a mathematical scenario, with no measured real benefit.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain 3/10 for no arrivals, then 7/10 for failure.
  • You obtain 7/10 again from the sum minus the intersection and justify this subtraction.
  • You compare with the initial risk and the risk from A alone, in the same context.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · challenge an assumption

The same marginal probabilities, a different overlap

Start again from the initial probabilities p_A = 3/5 and p_B = 1/2. A more precise description of the routes now requires every arrival along B to also be an arrival along A: B is a subset of A. Thus P(A ∩ B) = 1/2. Can we keep the previous Noisy-OR calculation? Calculate the risk with this information and quantify the difference from the previous result.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

If B is a subset of A, does the union A ∪ B contain more outcomes than A?

Hint 2

The union identity remains true without independence. Use the given overlap, without replacing it by p_A × p_B.

Compare with the solution

The union is A, since every outcome in B already belongs to A. The risk is therefore 3/5. The general calculation confirms this: 3/5 + 1/2 − 1/2 = 3/5 = 0.60.

Independence would have required an intersection equal to 3/10, whereas the description gives 1/2. The previous product of complements therefore no longer represents this model. It would have given 4/5, an excess of 4/5 − 3/5 = 1/5 in probability.

The individual probabilities are unchanged; their dependence has changed. This case shows that they are not enough to determine the union. If the overlap were not given or justified, we would have to leave the risk undetermined rather than assume independence by default.

Check your reasoning

  • You identify the contradiction between the given intersection and the product 3/10.
  • You obtain 3/5 both from the subset relation and from the union identity.
  • You quantify the difference 1/5 and name the missing information if the dependence is unknown.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why start with no arrivals when calculating at least one arrival?

No arrivals means that all routes are blocked. Under independence, multiply their blocking probabilities, then subtract this product from 1.

Recall 2Exactly what does independence concern in the relay scenario?

It concerns transmission events that reach the station, once the relay states are fixed. Knowing that one route has transmitted must not change the probability for the other. Paths drawn separately do not demonstrate this.

Recall 3Why can p_A + p_B exceed the probability of at least one arrival?

The sum counts simultaneous arrivals in A and in B. We must subtract P(A ∩ B) once. Only under independence can this intersection be replaced by p_A × p_B.

Recall 4Is knowing the individual probabilities enough to determine the risk at the station?

No. We also need a justified assumption about their dependence, or information about their overlap. With the initial marginal probabilities, independence gives 4/5; if B is a subset of A, the risk is 3/5.

Recall 5Why does the model assign 0 risk when no route is active?

Because failure was defined as an arrival along at least one active route, with no other cause. The empty product is 1 and its complement is 0. An external cause would require an additional explicit model.

Without rereading, reconstruct this sequence: define the arrivals and their context; state what makes the station fail; state the independence assumption; calculate no arrivals; take the complement; check using the distinct outcomes; explain what is missing if the routes depend on each other.
