Level 2 · C101·M5

Rolling-origin forecasting

Your goalEvaluate a one-step forecast by replaying the history: at each origin, fit using only the past, forecast the next value, record the error, then aggregate these errors and compare them with a naive benchmark.

Before you start
c090-m5 : stationarity · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c101-m1 : AR and MA · c101-m2 : ARIMA · c101-m3 : identification · c101-m4 : residual diagnostics

✿01 See the idea

A greenhouse is monitored by a sensor that records the temperature difference from the setpoint every day at noon. We have six days of history: 14, 18, 15, 19, 16 and 18 °C. These values are fictional; they allow calculations by hand and describe no actual greenhouse. The practical question is: if a rule forecasts each morning the temperature difference for that day, how far will it be wrong on days it has not seen?

A tempting first answer is to fit the rule to all six days, then measure the error on those same six days. A residual compares a fitted model with data used to fit it, as in c101-m4. A forecast error is a different quantity: it is the difference between a value that was actually unknown when the forecast was made and the forecast value.

To assess a forecast, we go back in time. At day 3, we hide the following days, refit the rule using the first three days, forecast day 4, then reveal the actual value and record the error. Day 3 is called the origin. We then move the origin forward by one day and repeat: origin 4, then origin 5. This repetition is rolling-origin evaluation, and each repetition is a fold.

Two choices make the scene easy to calculate. The fitted rule is a constant level: the forecast is the mean of the known days. It contains neither an AR term nor an MA term; this is deliberately the simplest case, to keep attention on the protocol rather than on estimation. The naive benchmark forecasts the last observed value; it can be interpreted as a random walk with neither an AR term nor an MA term. An isolated score has no scale: it must be interpreted against a benchmark evaluated on the same targets.

In this example, the training window expands at each origin, and the horizon is one day: we forecast only the next value. A window of fixed length that moves with the origin is a variant we do not calculate here. These choices are assumptions of the example, rather than general rules.

The rule to remember

At each origin, fit using only values already known, forecast the next value, record its error, then move the origin forward; aggregate these errors and compare them with a benchmark on the same targets.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Six days of temperature differences, three origins

The fictional differences from the setpoint, in °C, are 14, 18, 15, 19, 16 and 18. The horizon is one day. The rule is the mean of the known days; the naive benchmark is the last value. We measure three forecast errors, at origins 3, 4 and 5, then their mean squared error.

  1. Define the origin, target and error

    y(t) is the temperature difference recorded on day t, in °C. Origin t is the last known day: fitting receives only y(1) through y(t), and the target is y(t + 1). The rule forecasts the mean of the known days.

    y^t+1|t=1t∑k=1tyk

    ŷ(t + 1 | t) reads: the forecast for day t + 1 made at origin t. The error is y(t + 1) − ŷ(t + 1 | t), in °C. To prevent positive and negative errors from cancelling, we square each error, giving °C², then take the mean: this is the mean squared error, abbreviated MSE (EQM in the formula).

    Assumptions: no future value enters the training data; the level is assumed to have no marked trend over these days, an assumption that c090-m5 helps us question; the horizon is one day. With six values, the result illustrates a protocol and measures no actual performance.

  2. First fold: origin three

    The first three days give the sum 14 + 18 + 15 = 47 °C, hence a forecast of 47 / 3 ≈ 15.67 °C for day 4. This day is hidden during the calculation. We then reveal it: 19 °C.

    y^=14+18+153=473≈15.67 °C

    The error is 19 − 15.67 ≈ 3.33 °C; its square is approximately 11.11 °C². Division by 3 counts the known days, rather than the folds.

  3. Move the origin forward

    At origin 4, day 4 becomes known: the sum is 66 and the forecast is 16.50 °C. The target is 16 °C, the error -0.50 °C and its square 0.25 °C². At origin 5, the sum is 82 and the forecast 16.40 °C; the target is 18 °C, the error 1.60 °C and its square 2.56 °C².

    664=16.50 °C ; 825=16.40 °C

    A day enters the training data only after it has served as a target. This ordering rule defines the entire protocol.

  4. Aggregate the errors

    The sum of the three squared errors is 13.92 °C². Dividing by the number of folds, 3, gives an MSE of approximately 4.64 °C².

    EQM=11.11+0.25+2.563≈4.64 °C²

    This mean hides a large difference between folds: 11.11 °C² on the first, 0.25 °C² on the second. A single fold would have given a misleading picture.

  5. Compare with the naive benchmark

    The benchmark forecasts the previous day's value. Its errors on the same targets are 4, -3 and 2 °C, giving squared errors of 16, 9 and 4 °C², with a mean of 9.67 °C². The formula gives the ratio, labelled rapport.

    rapport=4.649.67≈0.48

    On these three targets, the rule's MSE is approximately half the benchmark's MSE. This describes three fictional days; it says nothing about a fourth day or an actual greenhouse.

  6. Measure the leakage

    Now fit the mean to all six days: the sum is 100 and the mean approximately 16.67 °C. For day 6, the error becomes 18 − 16.67 ≈ 1.33 °C.

    (18−16.67)2≈1.78 °C² contre 2.56 °C²

    The difference of 0.78 °C² arises because the mean already includes the target: the evaluation becomes overly favourable. This is information leakage from the future into fitting.

  7. Check the calculation

    Units: errors are in °C, their squares in °C²; never add an error to a squared error. Recurrence: the new mean equals the old mean plus the error divided by the number of days now known.

    15.67+3.334=16.50 °C

    Causality: if we changed the last day of the series, the three forecasts would stay the same; only the last error and the mean that includes future information would change. If a forecast changed, the protocol would be allowing information to leak from the future.

Explain it in your own words

Why does the forecast at origin three not use the following day's value? Then explain what changes in the figure for the last day when fitting uses all six days.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I can fit the model to the whole series, then measure the error on the same days.”

    The model has then already seen the target. Here, fitting to all six days gives 1.78 °C² for the last day, compared with 2.56 °C² when that day is actually hidden. Refit at each origin using only past values.

    Review the leakage measurement

  • “A single good forecast is enough to judge the method.”

    Squared errors vary greatly from one fold to another: 11.11 °C² compared with 0.25 °C² in the example. Move the origin forward, keep all folds and report their mean together with what it hides.

    Repeat the comparison on one fold

  • “I can shuffle the days, just as I might shuffle cards to make a test set.”

    Shuffling puts future days into the training data and destroys the order we want to test. The origin separates the past from the future: preserve chronological order and do not shuffle anything before the origin.

    Reread the idea of the origin

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

Compare on a single fold

At origin 4, the rule forecasts 16.50 °C for day 5, whose value is 16 °C. The naive benchmark forecasts the value of day 4. Calculate both errors and their squares, then say which method wins on this fold and what can be concluded.

Hint 1

The target is the same for both methods; only the forecasts differ.

Hint 2

The benchmark forecasts 19 °C.

Compare with the solution

Rule: 16 − 16.50 = -0.50 °C, squared error 0.25 °C². Benchmark: 16 − 19 = -3 °C, squared error 9 °C². The rule wins on this fold: 0.25 °C² compared with 9 °C².

The conclusion remains limited to this fold. The difference from the first fold, where the rule's squared error is 11.11 °C², shows that the size of the error changes greatly from one origin to another.

Check your reasoning

  • Both errors start from the same target.
  • Squared errors are in °C².
  • The conclusion is limited to one fold.

Find the leakage in a colleague's work

Hint 1

Which mean did the colleague use, and which values does it contain?

Hint 2

Repeat the calculation using only the first five days.

Compare with the solution

The colleague used 100 / 6 ≈ 16.67 °C, a mean that already includes the target 18 °C. The error is 1.33 °C and its square 1.78 °C².

With the first 5 days, the forecast is 16.40 °C, the error 1.60 °C and its square 2.56 °C². The overly favourable reduction is 0.78 °C². Corrected protocol: refit at each origin using the preceding days, then report only the error for the hidden day.

Check your reasoning

  • The faulty mean is identified together with the values it includes.
  • The honest evaluation uses only the preceding days.
  • The correction describes the sequence: fit, forecast, reveal.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

Fixed origin versus rolling origin

A second protocol fits only once at origin 3, giving the forecast 15.67 °C, and keeps this value for days 5 and 6. Calculate its MSE on these two targets, compare it with the rolling evaluation on the same targets, then say what this comparison does not allow us to conclude.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The targets are 16 and 18 °C; the forecast stays the same.

Hint 2

For the rolling evaluation, use the last two squared errors from the example.

Hint 3

Do both protocols forecast the same distance ahead of their origin?

Compare with the solution

Fixed origin: the errors are 1/3 and 7/3 °C, their squares 1/9 and 49/9 °C², giving an MSE of 25/9 °C², or approximately 2.78 °C².

Rolling origin: (0.25 + 2.56) / 2 ≈ 1.405 °C². Its forecasts are closer to the values on these two targets, but the fixed origin forecasts day 6 further ahead. The comparison therefore does not separate the effect of the update from that of the forecast distance, and it rests on only two targets.

Check your reasoning

  • Both MSEs use the same two targets.
  • Squared errors are averaged over the number of targets.
  • The limitation of differing horizons is stated.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the origin of a forecast?

The last known time when making the forecast: fitting uses only values up to that time.

Recall 2What do we do when we move the origin forward?

Repeat at the next origin: refit using the past, forecast the next value, record the error, then aggregate the errors.

Recall 3Why does fitting to the whole series make the evaluation overly favourable?

The model has already seen the target: its error appears smaller than that of a genuine forecast.

Recall 4Why compare with a naive benchmark on the same targets?

A score alone has no scale; the last value provides a simple reference, evaluated with the same protocol.

Recall 5What does an MSE calculated over three folds fail to prove?

It proves neither stability nor performance in actual use: three targets and a fictional model do not cover other days.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Choose the horizon and the rule; fix an origin; fit using only the past; forecast the target; record the error in its unit; move the origin forward; aggregate the squared errors; compare with a benchmark on the same targets; preserve chronological order; write down the limitations.
