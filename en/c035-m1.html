Level 1 · C035·M1

Univariate density

Your goalRead a waiting time density, calculate the probability of an interval from its area, and check that the total area equals 1.

Before you start
c030-m5 : Poisson · c034-m5 : mean value

✿01 See the idea

A device emits a signal every 0.5 s. You arrive between two signals and wait for the next one. In our fictional scene, your arrival has no preferred moment within the cycle: two intervals of equal duration have the same probability. We call this assumption a uniform distribution. Regular signals alone do not guarantee this assumption about your arrival.

Draw all possible waiting times on a line. A wide interval leaves more room for waiting times than a narrow interval. To represent this distribution, place a rectangle on the line: its height gives the density, and the area above an interval gives its probability.

Density therefore describes an amount of probability per unit of time. A height of 2 per second does not mean a probability of 2. You still need to multiply it by the width, in seconds, of the interval being considered. The complete rectangle has an area of 1, since the waiting time falls somewhere between 0 s and 0.5 s.

We model the waiting time as a continuous quantity, with no clock rounding or additional delay. All the numbers in the scene are fictional. No series of real waiting times has been used to validate this distribution.

The rule to remember

Density is a height; the probability of an interval is an area. Check non-negativity, total area, and units before interpreting the result.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Waiting between 0.1 s and 0.3 s

The cycle lasts T = 1/2 s, or 0.5 s. Under the uniform arrival assumption, what is the probability that the waiting time X lies between a = 0.1 s and b = 0.3 s?

  1. Define the quantity and the meaning of density

    X is the random waiting time, in seconds. x denotes a possible value of this duration, in the same unit; univariate means that we describe this single quantity. T is the fixed duration of the cycle. f is a function: it assigns to a value x a height f(x), expressed per second.

    For a distribution described by a density, probabilities are calculated by integrating f. P denotes probability, which has no unit; a and b are time bounds with a ≤ b. The symbol ∫ asks for the accumulated area, and dx indicates that the accumulation is with respect to the duration x. The bounds −∞ and +∞ ask us to include the whole line of possible values.

    f(x)≥0;∫−∞+∞f(x) dx=1
    P(a≤X≤b)=∫abf(x) dx

    Non-negativity prevents a negative probability area. A total area equal to 1 distributes all the available probability. A discrete distribution assigned probabilities to separate values; here, we add areas over intervals. We are not claiming that every distribution has a density.

  2. Determine the height of the rectangle

    Waiting times in the model range from 0 s to T. Outside this interval, f is 0 per second. Inside it, uniformity requires a constant height h. This region carrying all the probability is the support of our model. The values at the endpoints can be chosen to match those inside without changing any area.

    The complete area is h × T. To make it equal to 1, divide 1 by the total duration: h = 1 / (1/2 s) = 2 per second. The function can therefore be written as follows:

    f(x)={hsi 0≤x≤T0sinon

    Check: (2 per second) × 0.5 s = 1. A height greater than 1 per second is allowed: the constraint applies to the total area, rather than to the height alone.

  3. Select the relevant width

    Both requested bounds lie within the support. We can therefore keep the whole interval from a to b. Its width is the upper bound minus the lower bound: 0.3 s − 0.1 s = 0.2 s, or exactly 1/5 s.

    Subtracting the bounds measures a duration. Adding the bounds would not give the space occupied by this interval. If a bound extended beyond the support, we would keep only the portion where the density is nonzero.

  4. Convert the width into a probability

    The density is constant over the whole selected interval. Its integral is therefore the area of the rectangle: height × width. This simplification would be wrong for a varying density; we would then need to calculate the area under the curve.

    P(a≤X≤b)=h(b−a)=2×1/5=2/5

    The exact answer is 2/5, or 0.4, with no unit. The seconds in the width cancel the “per second” in the height. This result is a probability under the stated model; it is not a frequency measured on the device.

  5. Check by reading the same diagram another way

    Since all intervals of equal duration have the same weight, the probability is also the fraction of the cycle covered: 1/5 s / 1/2 s = 2/5. We recover the result from a ratio of durations, without starting from the height.

    Another check: the part before a has a width of 0.1 s and a probability of 0.2; the part after b has a width of 0.2 s and a probability of 0.4. Including our interval: 0.2 + 0.4 = 0.6, then 0.6 + 0.4 = 1. The pieces cover exactly the support, with no gaps or area counted twice. The complement of the requested interval therefore has probability 0.6.

  6. Handle an exact value and a rounded reading

    At the point b = 0.3 s, the height is still 2 per second. But the point alone has a width of 0 s: b − b = 0 s. Its area is 2 × 0 = 0, so P(X = b) = 0. Including or excluding an endpoint of the interval therefore does not change its probability.

    A probability of 0 for each exact value does not make waiting times impossible: a duration does occur, but no duration fixed in advance carries positive probability mass in this model. A display rounded to 0.3 s groups together an interval of durations; to calculate its probability, you need to know the rounding rule and its bounds. Reading f(b) is not enough.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations. Explain why the height exceeds 1 per second while the probability remains at most 1. Then recover the answer using the area and then the ratio of durations. What assumption allows you to make this second calculation?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The height in the diagram is the probability of this waiting time.”

    Write the units: f(x) is in probability per second. For an interval, integrate the density; in our rectangle, multiply the height by the width. For an exact point, the width is 0. A rounded display represents an interval whose bounds must be specified.

    Revisit the area and the exact point

  • “A density greater than 1 is not allowed.”

    Check the complete area. Here, 2 per second over 0.5 s gives 1, so the density is valid. A probability has no unit and is bounded by 1; a density height depends on the unit of the axis.

    Reread the rectangle and its units

  • “I always multiply the height by the requested width.”

    First identify where the density is nonzero and where it is constant. An interval extending beyond the support contains a portion with 0 area. For a curve with varying height, a single height cannot replace the integral.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis and repair

A proposed height

Someone proposes, for the same waiting time, a constant density of 3 per second between 0 s and 0.5 s, and 0 elsewhere. Is it valid? Repair it if necessary. Then explain why the correct height does not give P(X = 0.3 s).

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Non-negativity is not enough: calculate the area of the whole rectangle.

Hint 2

To repair it, find the height h such that h × T = 1.

Compare with the solution

The proposed function is positive, but its total area is 3 × 1/2 = 3/2, or 1.5. This area exceeds 1: it cannot distribute a total probability equal to 1.

Replace the height with 1 / 1/2 = 2 per second, which restores a total area of 1. The height exceeding 1 alone is not the problem: the repaired height also exceeds 1 per second.

At the point 0.3 s, the repaired density is 2 per second, but P(X = 0.3 s) = 0. An exact point has no width. We therefore distinguish height from area, and an exact point from a rounded value.

Check your reasoning

  • Reject the proposal because of the area 3/2, rather than the height 3 alone.
  • Recalculate 2 per second from the required total area.
  • Distinguish the density at a point from its probability of 0.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same scene

An interval extending beyond the cycle

Keep the original model. Calculate the probability of waiting between 0.4 s and 0.7 s. Explain why multiplying 2 directly by the width of this interval would give a wrong answer, then check the result without using the height.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Does the model allow a waiting time longer than 0.5 s? Mark the bounds on the line.

Hint 2

Keep only the intersection of the requested interval with the support, then compare its length with that of the cycle.

Compare with the solution

The portion from 0.5 s to 0.7 s is outside the support: its density is 0. Only the interval from 0.4 s to 0.5 s contributes. Its width is 0.5 s − 0.4 s = 0.1 s. The relevant area is 2 × 1/10 = 1/5, or 0.2.

Using the full width would give 0.7 − 0.4 = 0.3 s, then 2 × 3/10 = 3/5, or 0.6. This calculation wrongly extends the nonzero height beyond the cycle. An answer between 0 and 1 can therefore still be wrong.

Check independently of the height: the relevant portion occupies 1/10 s / 1/2 s = 1/5 of the cycle. It has the same duration as the interval in the first exercise, so the same probability under the uniform assumption. In a real situation, a missed signal or an additional delay would require us to reconsider the support of the model.

Check your reasoning

  • Limit the interval to 0.4 s–0.5 s before calculating the area.
  • Find 1/5 without assigning probability to the part outside.
  • Explain the error in 3/5 and check using the ratio of durations.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the difference between f(x) and the probability of an interval of waiting times?

f(x) is a density height, here per second. The probability of the interval is the integral of this height over the interval, with no unit; height × width is sufficient only when the height is constant over the relevant portion.

Recall 2What checks are needed when proposing a density?

The function must be nonnegative and its integral over the whole line must equal 1. In our rectangle, height × total duration equals 1. The height alone can exceed 1 per second.

Recall 3Why does P(X = 0.3 s) equal 0 despite a positive height?

An exact point has 0 width, so 0 area under this density. A waiting time still occurs; a reading rounded to this value corresponds to an interval whose bounds must be specified.

Recall 4What should you do before integrating over an interval that extends beyond the cycle?

Identify the intersection with the support. Here the density is 0 outside the interval 0 s–0.5 s; only the portion inside contributes. The ratio of the relevant duration to the total duration is a valid check because of uniformity.
