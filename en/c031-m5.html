Level 1 · C031·M5

Asymptotes

Your goalJustify a horizontal, vertical or oblique asymptote by studying the appropriate limit and the domain of the curve.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A workshop prepares batches of dough sold by weight. Each batch incurs 8 € in fixed costs: their share per kilogram is large for a small batch, then decreases as the batch grows. It can become as small as we wish without disappearing at any finite mass.

The full average cost also includes a part that increases with mass, because we choose a model in which larger batches become more expensive to work with. The average cost curve can therefore rise while approaching a rising straight line. Approaching a line does not necessarily mean settling at a particular height.

An asymptote is a straight line that describes a limiting behaviour of the curve. At large masses, we examine the gap from a line; near an excluded mass, we examine whether the height becomes unbounded. The drawing suggests an approach; a limit justifies the conclusion.

The setting, coefficients and cost law are fictional. Mass is assumed to be continuous and strictly positive. Limits as mass approaches 0 or infinity concern this mathematical model; they do not describe the actual capacity of a workshop. The drawings show only a window onto its curves.

The rule to remember

State which curve and direction you are studying. For a horizontal or oblique line, show that the vertical gap tends to 0; for a vertical line, establish an infinite limit at a boundary of the domain.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Share the costs, then find the asymptotes

For a batch of mass x > 0 kg, the total cost is C(x) = a x squared + b x + F, with a = 2 €/kg², b = 6 €/kg and F = 8 €. Let us find the asymptotes of the average cost f(x) = C(x)/x and its fixed cost share h(x) = F/x. These are two ways of examining the same batch, rather than two price schedules.

  1. Name the quantities and split the quotient

    x is a real mass in kilograms; C(x) is the total cost in euros. f(x) is the average cost per kilogram, h(x) is the contribution of the fixed costs alone and d(x) is the remaining part; these last three quantities are in €/kg. a measures the increase in d per additional kilogram, b is its value extended to 0 mass and F is a fixed cost per batch. The parameters remain constant throughout the study.

    f(x)=ax2+bx+Fx=ax+b+Fx

    Dividing each term by x separates the part that can follow a straight line from the remainder that needs explaining. We set d(x) = a x + b and h(x) = F/x, so f(x) − d(x) = h(x). Division requires x ≠ 0 kg. Our domain is x > 0 kg; we do not add any negative masses to it.

  2. Calculate a batch and check using the total cost

    At x = 4 kg, a x = 2 × 4 = 8 €/kg. Add b: d(x) = 8 + 6 = 14 €/kg. Distribute the fixed costs: h(x) = 8/4 = 2 €/kg. Thus f(x) = 14 + 2 = 16 €/kg, and the gap from the line is 2 €/kg.

    Another method: x squared is 16 kg². The quadratic cost is 2 × 16 = 32 €, and the linear cost is 6 × 4 = 24 €. The variable cost is 56 €, then the total cost is 56 + 8 = 64 €. Dividing by 4 kg gives 16 €/kg again. This check also confirms that we are comparing average costs, rather than comparing euros with €/kg.

  3. Read a few points without treating them as proof

    At 1 kg: d = 8 €/kg, h = 8 €/kg, f = 16 €/kg. At 2 kg: d = 10 €/kg, h = 4 €/kg, f = 14 €/kg. At 4 kg: d = 14 €/kg, h = 2 €/kg, f = 16 €/kg. At 8 kg: d = 22 €/kg, h = 1 €/kg, f = 23 €/kg.

    The gaps decrease while f eventually increases. These points check the drawings and suggest d as a candidate. On their own, they tell us nothing about all larger masses. We must now justify the trend using an expression valid for every x in the domain.

  4. Establish the horizontal asymptote of the fixed cost share

    In the plane where the vertical coordinate y is h(x), a horizontal asymptote y = L to the right means that h(x) tends to the finite value L as x tends to +∞. The symbol +∞ describes arbitrarily large masses; it is not a mass to substitute into a division.

    limx→+∞h(x)=limx→+∞Fx=0

    Justification: for any small tolerance ε > 0 in €/kg, choosing x > F/ε in kg ensures 0 < F/x < ε. ε denotes the desired maximum gap. The line y = 0 €/kg is therefore a horizontal asymptote of h at +∞. For every finite positive batch, F/x remains positive: here the curve never meets its line. This absence of intersection follows from the sign of F/x; it is not the definition of an asymptote.

  5. Establish the oblique asymptote of the average cost

    A line y = m x + p, with m ≠ 0, is an oblique asymptote to the right when f(x) − (m x + p) tends to 0 at +∞. m is the slope, here in €/kg²; p is the vertical intercept, here in €/kg. We compare the heights at the same horizontal coordinate. This definition concerns the difference, rather than their quotient.

    f(x)−(ax+b)=Fx→0 quand x→+∞

    Our candidate is therefore indeed an asymptote: y = a x + b, or y = 2 x + 6 when masses are expressed in kg and vertical coordinates in €/kg. Since a is positive, f(x) tends to +∞. So f has no horizontal asymptote to the right, even though its part h has one. Confusing the two curves would change the answer.

    The remainder F/x is positive: f lies above d throughout the domain. If this remainder changed sign at certain masses while tending to 0, crossings would be compatible with the asymptote. The limit criterion contains no prohibition on crossing.

  6. Study the boundary at 0 mass

    As x tends to 0 through positive values, dividing the fixed cost F by a smaller and smaller mass gives an unbounded share per kilogram. For example, h(0.25 kg) = 32 €/kg and h(0.10 kg) = 80 €/kg. Do not assign a value to h(0): the quotient is undefined there.

    limx→0+h(x)=+∞;limx→0+f(x)=+∞

    To exceed any height H > 0 in €/kg, taking 0 < x < F/H is enough to obtain h(x) > H. Since a x + b is positive, f(x) is even larger. This proves an infinite limit from the right for both curves. The small + on 0 means “from the side of positive masses”.

    The line x = 0 kg is a vertical asymptote of both curves: an infinite limit from one side of the domain is sufficient. An excluded value alone would not be enough; this divergence is required. We do not look for a limit from the left because our domain contains no negative masses.

  7. Check when the approximation is accurate enough

    Replacing f with d gives an absolute error of F/x in €/kg. To obtain a gap strictly less than 0.10 €/kg, we need x > 8/0.10 = 80 kg. At exactly 80 kg, the gap is still 0.10 €/kg: the strict inequality is not satisfied. The limit provides a tool for controlling the approximation.

    For the directions studied: h has y = 0 at +∞; f has y = a x + b at +∞; both have x = 0 from the positive side. The units of the lines remain those of their axes. This list is a conclusion about the stated fictional functions, rather than a prediction of actual prices.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations. Reconstruct the fixed cost share, then explain why its limit describes a horizontal asymptote for h and an oblique asymptote for f. Then justify the vertical asymptote without trying to divide by 0.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “An asymptote is a straight line that the curve can never cross.”

    The criterion at infinity requires the difference to tend to 0. It does not prevent this difference from becoming 0 and then changing sign at a finite mass. In our workshop, the absence of crossing follows specifically from the positive remainder F/x. Separate this particular property from the definition.

    Revisit the criterion for an oblique asymptote

  • “If a denominator becomes 0, I must have a vertical asymptote.”

    Start by specifying the domain, then study the limit of the quotient near the excluded value. The numerator may also become 0 and the quotient may remain bounded. In our case, F is strictly positive: the proof is F/x > H when 0 < x < F/H, rather than the mere presence of an impossible division.

    Revisit the proof at the boundary of 0 mass

  • “The ratio of the heights tends to 1, so my line is the right one.”

    Increasing heights can have a ratio close to 1 while maintaining a fixed gap. Calculate the difference from the candidate. The final exercise shifts our line by 1 €/kg and shows why the ratio alone is insufficient.

    Test the shifted line in the final exercise

Exercise 1 · Identification with justification

Which curve does the line belong to?

In the same workshop, assign y = 0 €/kg to h or f as an asymptote at +∞. Also give the vertical asymptote accessible from x > 0 kg. For each answer, write the limit that justifies it.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Separate h(x) = F/x and f(x) = a x + b + F/x.

Hint 2

At large masses, a x grows; near 0 mass, F/x grows without bound.

Compare with the solution

At +∞, h(x) tends to 0 €/kg: y = 0 is its horizontal asymptote. f(x) tends to +∞ because a is positive: this same line is not a horizontal asymptote of f.

At 0 from the right, both h and f tend to +∞. Their vertical asymptote is x = 0 kg. The one-sided limit is sufficient, and no average cost value is defined at 0 mass.

Check your reasoning

  • Explicitly associate each limit with h or f.
  • Distinguish y = 0 from x = 0 and give the units.
  • Specify +∞ or the positive side of 0 mass.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and checking

Which batch makes the line accurate enough?

You want to replace f(x) with d(x) = a x + b with an error strictly less than 0.10 €/kg. Find all admissible masses in the continuous domain, then check the batches of 80 kg and 81 kg. Use an exact calculation to decide.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The absolute error is F/x, since f remains above d.

Hint 2

Multiply the inequality by positive x. Check the equality case at the boundary separately.

Compare with the solution

We need F/x < 0.10 €/kg. Since x is positive, this is equivalent to x > F/(0.10 €/kg), so x > 80 kg. All real masses strictly above this bound qualify; there is no smallest admissible real mass.

At 80 kg, the error is 8/80 = 0.10 €/kg: equality does not qualify. At 81 kg, it is 8/81 €/kg, strictly less than 1/10 €/kg. To compare without rounding, 81 > 80 implies F/81 < F/80. If batches are restricted to whole numbers of kilograms, 81 kg is the smallest admissible mass; this is an additional constraint.

Check your reasoning

  • Transform the inequality while preserving its direction because x is positive.
  • Exclude 80 kg when the requirement is strictly less than the tolerance.
  • Give the units and distinguish the continuous domain from batches with whole numbers of kilograms.

Exercise 3 · Objection and transfer

A convincing ratio, an incorrect line

A colleague proposes the line e(x) = d(x) + δ, where δ = 1 €/kg, and claims: “f(x)/e(x) tends to 1 at +∞; this line is therefore also an asymptote.” At 4 kg, calculate d, e and f − e. Then decide using a limit. Finally, explain why an oblique asymptote does not thereby become a horizontal one.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write f(x) − e(x) = F/x − δ.

Hint 2

The quotient is also 1 + (F/x − δ)/e(x). Its denominator grows, even though the numerator does not tend to 0.

Compare with the solution

At 4 kg, d is 14 €/kg and e is 14 + 1 = 15 €/kg. Since f is 16 €/kg, f − e = 1 €/kg. This point alone allows no conclusion about asymptotes.

For every positive x, f(x) − e(x) = F/x − δ tends to −δ, or −1 €/kg, rather than 0. So the line e is not an asymptote of f at +∞. Yet f/e = 1 + (F/x − δ)/e does tend to 1: e tends to +∞ while the numerator of the second fraction tends to a finite constant. The quotient has not controlled the absolute gap.

For the true line d, it is f − d = F/x that tends to 0. It rises with mass; f also tends to +∞. The approach concerns their difference, rather than f settling at a finite height.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the shifted value and the gap with their units.
  • Reject e using the nonzero limit of f − e.
  • Explain why the ratio still tends to 1.
  • Distinguish an infinite height from a gap that tends to 0.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What criterion justifies a horizontal asymptote, and for which curve in the workshop?

A finite limit L of the height, in a stated direction, gives the line y = L. Here h(x) = F/x tends to 0 at +∞: y = 0 €/kg is a horizontal asymptote for h. f tends to +∞ and does not have this horizontal asymptote.

Recall 2What must tend to 0 to justify an oblique asymptote?

The difference f(x) − (m x + p), with nonzero m, in the direction studied. Here f(x) − (a x + b) = F/x tends to 0 at +∞. The quotient of the heights is insufficient.

Recall 3Why is x = 0 a vertical asymptote here, even though no value can be calculated there?

As x tends to 0 from the right, F/x and f(x) exceed every positive height: their limit is +∞. An infinite limit from one side is sufficient. An undefined division alone does not constitute proof.

Recall 4Does the definition of an asymptote prohibit a crossing at a finite horizontal coordinate?

No: it describes a limit. Here f does not cross d because their difference F/x is strictly positive for every positive mass. The position follows from the sign of the gap, rather than from the word asymptote.

Recall 5How do you move from the asymptote to a required accuracy in our workshop?

Solve F/x < ε, with a positive tolerance ε in €/kg: we need x > F/ε in kg. The gap remains nonzero at finite mass. A strict bound excludes the equality case.
