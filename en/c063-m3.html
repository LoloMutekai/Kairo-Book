Replications and standard error

Your goalChoose what counts as an independent replication, calculate the standard error of a simulated mean and explain what it allows you to report.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You simulate a service counter to estimate waiting time. A quiet day gives a short mean waiting time; a busy day gives a longer one. Looking at a single day does not tell you how much the result would change if you started again with different random arrivals.

Here, a replication is a complete simulation: start with an empty queue, let 20 customers arrive, then wait until all have been served. Keep only their mean waiting time, in minutes. Use the same arrival and service rules for every replication; only the random draws change. The fictional values A, B, C and D are 4, 4, 4 and 8 min. These are exercise data, chosen for calculation by hand, with no claim that a simulator has been run.

There are two kinds of spread to distinguish. The standard deviation between replications describes how much their mean waiting times vary. The standard error describes how much the mean of several replications would vary if you repeated the whole batch. It therefore concerns the precision of this mean under the fixed model.

Before reading the result in the diagram, predict which kind of spread should be smaller when the replications provide independent information. More replications can stabilize the estimate; they do not make the simulated days more alike or correct a poorly chosen model of the service counter.

The rule to remember

One value per independent replication; a mean across replications; their standard deviation divided by the square root of their number to estimate the standard error.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The mean waiting time at the service counter and its simulation uncertainty

The 4 complete replications give 4, 4, 4 and 8 min. Fix this number of replications before examining their results. We want to estimate the expected mean waiting time of a replication under this protocol, then the precision of the estimate.

  1. State what varies and under which assumptions

    n is the number of complete replications, with no unit, and n is at least 2. i is their index, from 1 to n. Yᵢ denotes the random result before simulation; yᵢ is its observed value, in minutes. μ is the theoretical mean of Yᵢ under the model. σ is its theoretical standard deviation, in minutes; its square is the theoretical variance, assumed finite. μ and σ are unknown.

    We assume that the Yᵢ are independent and identically distributed: the same service rule, the same arrival distributions, the same initial state and the same stopping criterion. Independence concerns the replications, not the customers sharing a queue. Separate draws following a documented protocol are necessary; different seed numbers alone do not prove independence.

    A seed and the protocol allow you to replay a trajectory. Starting each time from the same seed in the same generator reproduces that trajectory; it does not provide new independent information.

  2. Average the complete results

    The bar in ȳ denotes the observed mean of the replications. The symbol Σ means that we add the terms for all the indicated indices. Each replication here has the same number of customers and receives the same weight.

    y¯=Σi=1nyin

    Grouping the first two values gives 8 min; the next two give 12 min. Their sum is 20 min. Divide by the number of results, 4, to obtain 5 min. This total adds up means from replications: it is not the cumulative waiting time of all the customers.

    y¯=4+4+4+84=5 min

  3. Measure the spread between replications

    Subtracting ȳ removes the common level to leave only the deviations. They are -1, -1, -1 and 3 min. Their sum is 0 min: a useful check of the mean. Squaring them prevents opposite signs from cancelling; the squares are 1, 1, 1 and 9 minutes squared, with a sum of 12 minutes squared.

    s2=Σi=1n(yi−y¯)2n−1=123=4 min2

    The square of s is the estimated variance between replications. The denominator n − 1 accounts for the fact that the mean was estimated from these same results: the deviations are constrained to sum to 0. Under our assumptions, this adjustment makes the estimated variance unbiased for the theoretical variance, meaning correct on average over new batches.

    s is the nonnegative square root of this estimated variance: 2 min, since 2 × 2 = 4 minutes squared. Minutes squared are the units of variance; s is back in minutes.

  4. Understand why the mean has less spread

    Ȳ now denotes the random mean before any data collection. Var means variance. Independence allows us to add the variances of the Yᵢ; dividing a random quantity by n divides its variance by n squared. Since each replication has the same variance, the square of σ, we obtain:

    Var(Y¯)=nσ2n2=σ2n

    The theoretical standard deviation of Ȳ is therefore σ/√n, in minutes. We replace σ with s to estimate it. ES denotes this estimate of the standard error, rather than the unknown difference between ȳ and μ. The estimated variance is unbiased for the theoretical variance; this does not mean that its square root s, or ES, is exactly unbiased.

    ES=sn=24=22=1 min

  5. Check the result by another route

    The estimated variance of the mean is directly the square of s divided by n: 4/4 = 1 minutes squared. Its positive square root is 1 min: we recover ES without starting with s/√n. Squaring √4 gives 4 again, and squaring ES gives 1 minutes squared as expected.

    The mean of 5 min lies between the smallest mean waiting time, 4 min, and the largest, 8 min. ES is smaller than s because √n is greater than 1. If these checks fail, review the units, the deviations or the number of replications.

  6. Write a conclusion with the appropriate scope

    We can write: “In this fictional batch of 4 replications assumed independent, the estimated mean waiting time is 5 min and its estimated standard error is 1 min.” The spread s = 2 min concerns the means of the replications, not the waiting times of individual customers.

    This does not claim that μ is within 1 min of the result. A confidence interval would require its own construction and assumptions; we do not construct one here. With few replications, s and ES can themselves fluctuate considerably. The standard error rule does not require a normal distribution for the variance identity; using it to claim interval coverage would be an additional step.

    Finally, repeating the simulation reduces uncertainty due to the draws when the assumptions hold. It does not measure the difference between the model and a real service counter. A larger number of replications does not resolve this limitation.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I have 80 customers, so I have 80 replications.”

    Customers in the same trajectory share the queue, and their waiting times may depend on one another. The calculation presented uses 4 results from complete trajectories. Return to the definition of Yᵢ before choosing n.

    Review the replication boxes and what they summarize

  • “The standard deviation of 2 min is already the standard error.”

    s describes the spread of mean waiting times between replications. For the mean of these replications, we still need to divide by √4 = 2: ES = 1 min. Also check by dividing the variance between replications by n, then taking the square root.

    Work through the derivation and the variance check again

  • “Replaying the same seed gives 0 spread: I am certain.”

    Replaying exactly the same protocol and seed produces copies of a trajectory. The independence required for division by √n is missing. Separate the reproducibility check from collecting replications that provide new draws; 0 spread between copies does not prove any precision.

    Plan more replications in the final exercise

Exercise 1 · Warm-up

Count the independent information

Compare with the solution

The total is 4 × 20 = 80 customers. We have 4 results assumed independent. Their mean is (4 + 4 + 4 + 8)/4 = 20/4 = 5 min.

For this method, n = 4. Customer waiting times within a trajectory may be linked through the queue; counting them as that many independent replications does not satisfy the assumption of the calculation.

Exercise 2 · Paper exercise

A busier replication

In a fictional variant of the same batch, only value D changes to 12 min. A, B and C remain at 4 min. Recalculate the mean, the variance between replications, the standard deviation and the standard error. Why must you recalculate the deviations?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with the new mean. The deviations must be centered on this new value.

Hint 2

The first replications all have the same deviation; their contribution to the sum of squares can be grouped. The denominator n − 1 is unchanged.

Compare with the solution

The sum becomes 12 + 12 = 24 min, and the mean is 24/4 = 6 min. The first deviations are each 4 − 6 = -2 min. The last is 12 − 6 = 6 min.

The squares are 4, 4, 4 and 36 minutes squared. Their sum is 3 × 4 + 36 = 12 + 36 = 48 minutes squared. Thus the estimated variance is 48/3 = 16 minutes squared, then s = 4 min because 4 × 4 = 16 minutes squared.

ES = 4/√4 = 4/2 = 2 min. Check: the deviations sum to 0 min. Keeping the old deviations would have measured distances from the old mean, which is no longer the center of this batch.

Check your reasoning

  • Find 6 min by recalculating the mean before the deviations.
  • Justify 48 and then 16 minutes squared with the denominator 3.
  • Distinguish s = 4 min from ES = 2 min and check the centering.

Exercise 3 · Reasoned transfer

Reduce uncertainty without copying the data

Return to the initial batch. For planning, keep s = 2 min as a fixed approximation of the theoretical spread. What total number of replications and what standard error do you aim for when multiplying n by 4? Someone proposes copying the initial results 4 times to reach this sample size: respond to them. Distinguish the planning calculation from a future measured result.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The square root of the new number of replications is 4. The square root appears in the denominator of ES.

Hint 2

A copy uses no new random draw. Additional precision requires new, independent replications following the same protocol.

Compare with the solution

The target total sample size is 4 × 4 = 16 replications. Under the fixed spread approximation, target ES = 2/√16 = 2/4 = 0.50 min. Multiplying the sample size by 4 divides the standard error here by 2, under this assumption.

Copying the results still provides only the 4 initial independent trajectories. The denominator cannot become √16 simply because the table is longer. The batch must be extended with new independent trajectories.

0.50 min is a planned precision with the initial spread held fixed by assumption. After collecting the data, s and ES will need to be recalculated with the new data: the estimated spread may change. This figure is therefore neither a future result already measured nor a guarantee about the actual error.

Check your reasoning

  • Link 16 replications to 0.50 min through the square root, under the fixed spread assumption.
  • Refuse to count copies as new independent information.
  • Report a plan, then require a new calculation after data collection.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1At the service counter, what counts as a replication for our calculation?

A complete trajectory following the fixed protocol, summarized by its mean waiting time. The trajectories must be independent and identically distributed. The customers within a queue are not that many independent replications.

Recall 2What is the difference between s and ES, and what units do they have?

s is the estimated standard deviation between replication results; ES = s/√n estimates the standard deviation of their mean over repeated batches. Both are in minutes here; the variance is in minutes squared.

Recall 3Why divide by √n rather than by n in the standard error?

For independent replications with the same finite variance, the variance of their sum is multiplied by n. Dividing this sum by n divides its variance by n squared: the variance of the mean is therefore that of a replication divided by n. Its square root is σ/√n; we replace the unknown σ with s.

Recall 4Why does replaying the same seed provide no additional precision?

With the same protocol and generator, we reproduce the same trajectory. Copies provide no independent information; the precision formula assumes new replications, with an appropriate protocol for random draws.

Recall 5What does a small standard error not allow us to claim?

It does not give the actual error of the observed mean, guarantee interval coverage or validate the model against the real service counter. It quantifies uncertainty due to the draws under the stated assumptions.
