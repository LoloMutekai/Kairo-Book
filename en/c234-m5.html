Level 4 · C234·M5

Existence conditions

Your goalCheck the assumptions that guarantee an adapted solution to a backward SDE, then construct and check this solution in an affine temperature model.

Before you start
c233-m5 : verification theorem · c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c227-m5 : Ito–Tanaka · c234-m1 : state space · c234-m2 : Kalman filter · c234-m3 : nonlinear filtering · c234-m4 : backward SDEs

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional room, the temperature at the end of a period of 2 h is described by a random rule known from the start. The realized final temperature remains unknown. A backward stochastic differential equation, abbreviated backward SDE, seeks a present value Y and a noise coefficient Z that will meet this terminal rule without using the future.

Working backward from a terminal rule is not enough to produce a solution. The terminal fluctuations must have sufficiently small moments, the information must be representable by the chosen noise, and the equation’s dependence on the unknowns must remain controlled. Otherwise, a formula may seem reasonable without belonging to the stated solution class.

We will check a set of sufficient conditions in the Brownian setting, then check an explicit solution. “Sufficient” means that the conditions together provide a guarantee; a missing condition prevents us from using that guarantee, without on its own proving impossibility.

All the room’s parameters and sample path points are fictional. Y will be a value of the equation in degrees Celsius; when its generator is nonzero, it is not simply the mean final temperature conditional on the observations. We are not calibrating a sensor or a real physical model.

The rule to remember

Before solving, specify the information, the integrability class, and the conditions on the generator. Only then check the equation and its terminal condition.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Certifying a backward value for the room

Take T = 2 h, a = 20 °C, b = 2 °C/√h, and β = 0.5 /√h. The terminal rule is ξ = a + bW at T, and the generator is f(t,y,z) = βz. Construct the pair (Y,Z), then evaluate it at t = 1 h and Wₜ = 0.5 √h.

  1. Specify the information and the equation

    W is a standard real Brownian motion, with W initially equal to 0, mean 0, and variance of the increment between t and s equal to s − t. Time is measured in hours: W has unit √h. ℱt denotes all information generated by W up to t. We use this natural filtration, completed and right continuous: null events are included, and the usual convention of right continuity for information is imposed.

    ξ, the Greek letter xi, is the terminal variable, known as a rule and measurable at the terminal time T. Yt is the value sought, in °C, calculable using ℱt: it is said to be adapted. Zt, in °C/√h, is the coefficient in the Ito integral. We seek a predictable Z, meaning measurable using information from the immediate past. In our example it will be constant.

    Yt=ξ+∫tTf(s,Ys,Zs)ds−∫tTZsdWs

    s is the integration time; y and z are possible arguments of the generator f. This generator is a rate in °C/h. Its time integral and the stochastic integral are therefore both in °C. At the terminal boundary, the equation requires Y at T to equal ξ. In differential form it is dY = −f dt + Z dW: the sign of the drift will be essential to the check.

  2. State a sufficient guarantee and its scope

    Here is the setting for the guarantee we use. The horizon T is deterministic, strictly positive, and finite; the filtration is the Brownian filtration described above. ξ is measurable with respect to ℱ at T, and its square has finite expectation. 𝔼 denotes expectation under the model’s probability law, and ∞ means that there is no finite bound.

    𝔼[|ξ|2]<∞;𝔼∫0T|f(s,0,0)|2ds<∞

    For each pair of arguments, f is progressively measurable: on every interval ending at t, its joint dependence on time and outcome is measurable using ℱt. This excludes a generator that consults a realization still in the future. Its dependence on y and z satisfies a uniform bound:

    |f(t,y,z)−f(t,y′,z′)|≤Ly|y−y′|+Lz|z−z′|

    The primes distinguish other arguments; they do not indicate differentiation. L_y and L_z are finite, nonnegative deterministic constants, in /h and /√h respectively. This condition, called the Lipschitz condition, bounds the variation of f by that of the unknowns, for all their arguments; checking a few points is not enough.

    Under this set of assumptions, there is a unique pair in the following class: Y is continuous and adapted, Z is predictable, the expectation of the maximum of |Y| squared is finite, and the expectation of the integral of |Z| squared is finite. Uniqueness means identical Y paths outside an event of probability 0, and identical Z almost everywhere in time and probability. It does not cover every imaginable formal expression.

    The Brownian filtration plays a constructive role: every square integrable martingale in this filtration can be represented by an integral with respect to W. A martingale here is a process whose expected future value, conditional on present information, equals its present value. Adding independent random information may require another martingale term; continuity of W alone does not provide the guarantee.

  3. Understand how the conditions produce a solution

    The existence mechanism proceeds by approximation. For provisional processes u and v in the same class, in °C and °C/√h respectively, freeze the unknowns in f. The variable A = ξ + ∫ from 0 to T of f(s,us,vs) ds, in °C, is square integrable by the preceding bounds. Form Mt = 𝔼[A | ℱt], in °C, where the bar means “using only ℱt”. Brownian representation provides its coefficient Z.

    Yt=Mt−∫0tf(s,us,vs)ds

    This construction maps (u,v) to (Y,Z). A quadratic estimate using the Lipschitz bound makes this mapping a contraction on sufficiently short intervals: it reduces the distance between two provisional pairs. We obtain a limit, then join the intervals backward from T. The uniform constants and finite horizon allow this joining. Applying the same estimate to two solutions gives uniqueness.

    This is the mechanism behind the general guarantee, without the details of its technical estimate. In the room, we will construct the limit directly in affine form and verify every check; no numerical solver result is required.

  4. Check the room’s data

    The rule ξ = a + bW at T uses only the permitted terminal information. The generator f(t,y,z) = βz is deterministic and continuous in its arguments, hence progressively measurable. It does not depend on y: we can take L_y = 0 /h and L_z = |β| = 0.5 /√h, since its difference is exactly β(z − z′). Also, f(t,0,0) = 0 °C/h: the time integral of its square is 0.

    For the terminal value, expand the square. The cross term has expectation 0 because 𝔼[W at T] = 0; the square of the Brownian motion has expectation T, expressed in hours. This leaves:

    𝔼[ξ2]=a2+b2T=400+8=408

    The result is 408 °C squared, which is finite: 4 °C squared per hour × 2 h contributes 8 °C squared. A single finite terminal value would not have proved this property: the expectation concerns the whole probability law.

  5. Construct the pair and check the sign

    Seek a value affine in the present Brownian motion. At the terminal boundary, it must recover a + bW at T, so the noise coefficient must be b. The deterministic correction must vanish at T and provide the drift −βb. This gives:

    Yt=a+bWt+βb(T−t);Zt=b

    Y uses the present W without knowing W at T. Z is constant, hence predictable. Differentiating Y gives dY = −βb dt + b dW; the deterministic term has derivative -1 °C/h, and the noise coefficient is 2 °C/√h. This is indeed −f(t,Y,Z), since βb = 1 °C/h. The W term is linear: no second derivative contributes in Ito’s formula.

    At t = T, the term βb(T − t) is 0 °C, and Y recovers ξ. Integrating the differential between t and T, then isolating Yt, recovers exactly the backward SDE with a minus sign before its stochastic integral. This check holds at every time and for all paths outside the usual null set, rather than only at the following numerical point.

  6. Calculate in the present and check afterward

    At t = 1 h, the remaining time is T − t = 2 − 1 = 1 h. The contribution from present noise is bWt = 2 × 0.5 = 1 °C: a + bWt = 21 °C. The generator contributes βb(T − t) = 1 × 1 = 1 °C. We obtain Yt = 21 + 1 = 22 °C, together with Zt = 2 °C/√h.

    At the start, W is 0: initial Y = 20 + 1 × 2 = 22 °C. At the chosen intermediate time, noise has added 1 °C while the remaining correction has decreased by the same amount. The equality of the Y values here does not mean that Y is constant along paths.

    For a check afterward only, choose W at T equal to 1 √h. Then ξ = 20 + 2 × 1 = 22 °C. Since Z is constant, its integral between t and T is b(W at T − Wt): 2 × (1 − 0.5) = 2 × 0.5 = 1 °C.

    The right side of the equation is therefore 22 + 1 − 1 = 23 − 1 = 22 °C, matching Yt. The fictional future checks the identity; it is never used to produce the present calculation. The preceding general identity remains the proof; this later point checks its arithmetic.

  7. Check the solution class over the whole interval

    A check at time t is not enough. Here 𝔼∫ from 0 to T of |Zs| squared ds = b squared × T = 4 × 2 = 8 °C squared. The quantity is finite and has the correct unit: the square of °C/√h is integrated in hours.

    For Y, “sup” means the maximum over time on the entire interval. The quadratic maximal inequality for Brownian motion gives 𝔼 sup |W| squared ≤ 4T = 8 h. It controls the largest fluctuations rather than a single terminal fluctuation. We also use the algebraic inequality: the square of a sum of 3 terms is at most 3 times the sum of their squares.

    𝔼sup0≤t≤T|Yt|2≤3[a2+4b2T+(βbT)2]

    The noise contributes at most 4 × 8 = 32 °C squared; the total correction is 2 °C, whose square is 4 °C squared. Add: 400 + 32 = 432, then 432 + 4 = 436. The final bound is 3 × 436 = 1308 °C squared, which is finite. This is a deliberately loose upper bound, not the exact expected squared maximum.

    We therefore have a solution in the stated class, where the uniqueness guarantee applies. Nothing here proves that Brownian motion or the generator adequately describes a real room. Mathematical existence, numerical scheme accuracy, and physical suitability are distinct questions.

Without rereading, name each assumption and the problem it prevents. Then reconstruct Y and Z, explain the sign of βb(T − t), and say why a guard that rejects an infinite number does not prove a finite second moment.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I have a finite final temperature in my trial: its square is integrable.”

    A trial concerns a realization; integrability concerns the whole probability law. Calculate an expectation or give an upper bound valid for the law. For the room, the calculation gives 408 °C squared, and the check on Y concerns the maximum over time.

    Revisit the data’s moments and the solution class

  • “Backward means I can use the observed final value to calculate Y now.”

    The rule ξ is given; its future realization is not. Y must be calculable using ℱₜ. The explicit solution uses Wₜ; choosing W at T in the check is only for afterward. Revisit the directions of time and information.

    Reread the flow of information in the room

  • “If I do not prove the Lipschitz condition, no solution exists.”

    We have a sufficient guarantee, not a characterization of all solvable equations. Without this assumption, another proof is needed; existence and uniqueness may have different outcomes. Even a Lipschitz generator does not replace checking the terminal value and the filtration.

    Revisit the combined assumptions and their scope

Exercise 1 · Guided verification

Assemble the existence checks

For the same room, recover L_y, L_z, f(t,0,0), and the expectation of the square of ξ. Explain why this list of numbers is still not the complete case for existence.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Subtract βz′ from βz; the generator does not depend on y.

Hint 2

Expand the square of a + bW at T, then use the mean and variance of Brownian motion.

Compare with the solution

The absolute difference between the generator values is |β| × |z − z′|. We can therefore take L_y = 0 /h and L_z = 0.5 /√h. The generator at 0 arguments is 0 °C/h, and the time integral of its square is 0.

The expected terminal square is a squared + b squared × T = 400 + 8 = 408 °C squared. The cross term disappears because Brownian motion has mean 0, not because it is identically 0.

We still need to specify the natural Brownian filtration, terminal measurability, progressive measurability of f, the finite horizon, and the solution class. These properties give the guarantee its meaning; a short list of numerical results does not replace them.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify a Lipschitz bound for all arguments and retain the units.
  • Expand the terminal moment and state why the cross term vanishes.
  • Beyond the calculations, name at least the permitted information and the solution class.

Exercise 2 · Reconstruction on paper

Different information at the same time

Keep all the room’s parameters, but take Wₜ = -0.5 √h at t = 1 h. Calculate Yₜ and Zₜ. Compare Yₜ with the conditional expectation of ξ. Explain why Z must be retained even though the generator is deterministic.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The conditional expectation of terminal Brownian motion is the present Brownian motion; then comes the correction βb(T − t).

Hint 2

Differentiate the affine expression for Y: the coefficient of dW remains b.

Compare with the solution

The Brownian term is 2 × (-0.5) = -1 °C. The conditional expectation of ξ is a + bWt = 20 + (-1) = 19 °C. The remaining correction is still 1 °C, so Yt = 19 + 1 = 20 °C.

Zt = b = 2 °C/√h: its sign does not follow that of Wt in this affine model. The generator is a deterministic function, but Y still contains Brownian motion. Removing Z would lose the fluctuations and prevent the solution from meeting the random terminal rule.

The result clearly distinguishes Y, equal to 20 °C, from the conditional mean final temperature, equal to 19 °C. The generator adds an accumulation term; the name “backward value” alone does not give it the meaning of a measured temperature.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the product with negative W without changing the sign of the time correction.
  • Give Y and Z separately, with their units.
  • Distinguish Y from the conditional expectation of ξ and explain the role of noise.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

The room receives a surprise at the terminal time

In the same room, add to ξ a shift C independent of the entire path of W, equal to +1 °C or −1 °C with equal probabilities. C is revealed only at exactly T. Before T, the information remains Brownian; at T, it includes C. The generator remains βz. Does the preceding guarantee apply? Can a continuous solution driven only by W reach this new terminal boundary?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The square of the new terminal value remains integrable. Look instead for the information assumption that has changed.

Hint 2

The limit of a continuous process adapted before T cannot create independent information that appears exactly at T.

Compare with the solution

The generator is still Lipschitz and C is bounded, so the terminal second moment remains finite. But the filtration is no longer the stated natural Brownian filtration: it receives additional independent information at the final instant. We cannot apply the guarantee while keeping only the same equation.

If Y were continuous and adapted, its value at T would be the limit of its values at times before T. It would therefore be measurable using the Brownian information available just before T. Yet a + bW at T + C contains a nondegenerate independent surprise. This variable is not measurable using that information: a contradiction.

Here we have a precise reason for nonexistence in the continuous class and for this modified filtration. A broader formulation may allow an additional martingale with a terminal jump. That changes the equation’s setting; setting Z to 0 does not repair it.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify the filtration as the lost assumption, without wrongly blaming the second moment.
  • Explain the contradiction between adapted continuity and an independent terminal surprise.
  • Restrict the conclusion to the equation driven only by W and to the stated continuous class.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does a sufficient condition allow, and what does its failure mean?

The conditions together give the stated guarantee. A missing condition prevents using that guarantee; on its own, it does not prove that no solution exists.

Recall 2What solution class did we specify?

Y is continuous and adapted, with a finite expected maximum of its squared absolute value; Z is predictable, with a finite expected integral of its square. Uniqueness applies within this class, with the specified almost sure and almost everywhere equalities.

Recall 3What checks apply to ξ and the generator?

ξ is measurable at the terminal time and square integrable. The generator is progressively measurable, uniformly Lipschitz in y and z, and square integrable in time and expectation when these arguments are set to 0.

Recall 4Why does the choice of filtration matter as much as the moments?

In the natural Brownian filtration, square integrable martingales can be represented by an integral with respect to W. Independent information added at the terminal time may require an additional martingale term; Z integrated against W cannot carry every possible surprise.

Recall 5How can we check the affine solution without confusing Y with a terminal mean?

Reconstruct Yₜ = a + bWₜ + βb(T − t) and Zₜ = b. Check the drift −βb, the noise coefficient b, the terminal boundary ξ, adaptation, and moments over the whole interval. The term βb(T − t) separates Y from the conditional terminal mean.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without opening the answers: draw the direction of information, state the square integrability class, then the sufficient assumptions. Recover Y and Z in the room and check their terminal boundary and drift. Finish with the terminal surprise: which assumption does it lose, and what precise contradiction follows?
