Level 2 · C140·M1

Margin

Your goalCalculate the margin of a linear boundary that separates two families of points: convert the raw score to a distance in millimetres and take the smallest distance.

Before you start
c059-m6 : Deterministic discrete Bellman · c099-m5 : Probability calibration · c040-m5 : Least squares

✿01 See the idea

A fictional laboratory sorts seeds into two families using two readings: length x1 and width x2, both in millimetres. The values on this page are invented for the exercise; no real seeds have been measured. The “plus” family has the label y = +1, and the “minus” family has the label y = −1.

Here, a linear boundary is a straight line in the plane of the readings: the “plus” seeds are on one side and the “minus” seeds on the other. Several lines can separate the same seeds. Which should we prefer? A starting idea is to choose the one that leaves the most space between itself and the closest seed. This space is the margin. A seed very close to the line changes sides after a small change in its readings; a large margin requires a larger change.

This idea is an intuition, rather than a theorem: a large margin on the known seeds alone does not guarantee correct classification of future seeds. This unit calculates the margin for a given boundary. It does not search for the best boundary or cover hinge loss, the dual, or kernels, which belong to other units in the chapter.

The pitfall is the unit of measurement. The line is described by a score with no physical unit of its own, which can be multiplied by any positive factor without changing the line. The margin must be a length in millimetres, so we need to convert the score to a distance.

The rule to remember

The margin is the smallest distance, in millimetres, between the points and the boundary: each distance is the signed score y·(w·x − c) divided by the norm of w, and multiplying w and c by the same positive factor leaves it unchanged.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Four seeds and a boundary to measure

The fictional boundary is f(x) = 3·x₁ + 4·x₂ − 10. The seeds, in millimetres, are A(1 ; 3) and B(5 ; 2) for the “plus” family; C(0 ; 1) and D(2 ; 0) for the “minus” family. Let us calculate the margin of this boundary for these four seeds.

  1. Define the symbols and assumptions

    x = (x1 ; x2) is the position of a seed, in millimetres. w = (w1 ; w2) is the weight vector and c is the threshold: both are chosen by the person drawing the boundary. The score has no physical unit of its own; we call its unit “score”. The dot product w·x = w1x1 + w2x2 adds the products of corresponding components; the norm ‖w‖ is the square root of w1² + w2², giving a value in score per millimetre. The label y is +1 or −1.

    f(x)=w·x−c=w1x1+w2x2−c

    Assumptions: w is not the 0 vector; both axes are in millimetres, so the usual distance in the plane is meaningful; every seed is on the correct side, meaning that y·f(x) is strictly positive. If a seed is on the other side, the boundary does not separate the families, and this unit does not define a positive margin.

  2. Convert a score to a distance

    Two points z and z′ on the boundary have the same score of 0, so the dot product w·(z′ − z) is 0: w is perpendicular to the boundary. Let n = w/‖w‖, a vector of length one. Starting at a point z on the boundary and moving t millimetres along n changes the score by t·‖w‖, because w·w = ‖w‖².

    f(z+tn)=f(z)+t(w·n)=t‖w‖

    A movement perpendicular to w does not change the score, so the shortest path from a given score to a score of 0 follows n. A point with score f(x) is therefore at a distance |f(x)| / ‖w‖ from the boundary. With the label included, the signed distance is positive for a correctly classified seed.

    γi=yi·f(xi)‖w‖, γ=miniγi

    The numerator y·f(x) is the functional margin of a point, in score. Its smallest value, divided by ‖w‖, gives the geometric margin γ, in millimetres. We use “margin” for this last quantity. Some texts use margin for the full width of the strip, which is twice γ; here we use the half width.

  3. Calculate the norm of w

    For the sum of the squared components, we have 3 × 3 = 9 and 4 × 4 = 16, so 9 + 16 = 25.

    ‖w‖=32+42=25=5 score par mm

    Check: 5 × 5 = 25. Here we have a perfect square, so no rounding is needed; in general, the norm does not work out to an exact number, and we need to keep the square root or state that we are rounding.

  4. Calculate the signed score of each seed

    Seed A, label +1: 3 × 1 = 3 and 4 × 3 = 12, so w·x = 15; then 15 − 10 = 5 score, which the label +1 leaves unchanged.

    Seed B, label +1: 3 × 5 = 15 and 4 × 2 = 8, so w·x = 23; then 23 − 10 = 13 score.

    Seed C, label −1: 3 × 0 = 0 and 4 × 1 = 4, so w·x = 4. The score f is negative, with absolute value 10 − 4 = 6; the label −1 reverses the sign: y·f = 6 score.

    Seed D, label −1: 3 × 2 = 6 and 4 × 0 = 0, so w·x = 6. The score f is negative, with absolute value 10 − 6 = 4; with the label, y·f = 4 score.

    All four y·f values are positive: every seed is on the correct side, so the separation assumption holds. The smallest value, for D, is 4 score: this is the functional margin.

  5. Divide by the norm and take the minimum

    A: 5 / 5 = 1 mm. B: 13 / 5 = 13/5, or 2.6 mm. C: 6 / 5 = 6/5, or 1.2 mm. D: 4 / 5 = 4/5, or 0.8 mm. Dividing a score by a norm in score per millimetre does give millimetres.

    γ=miniγi=45=0.8 mm

    The margin of this boundary for these four seeds is 0.8 mm, determined by seed D. We are not claiming that this is the best possible boundary: we are only measuring this one.

  6. Check the result

    Convert back to score: 4/5 × 5 = 4, equal to the functional margin of D (4 score). The conversion is therefore reversible. The other three distances (1.0 mm, 1.2 mm and 2.6 mm) exceed 0.8 mm: the strip with half width 0.8 mm around the boundary contains no seeds inside, and D is on its edge.

    Scaling check: if we multiply w and c by the same positive factor, the line does not move, all scores are multiplied by that factor, the norm is too, and the quotient stays the same. This is why the functional margin alone does not measure anything geometric, and why the scaling exercise repeats the calculation with a factor of three.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The score w·x − c is already the distance to the boundary.”

    The score is expressed in score, and the distance in millimetres. For seed D, the signed score is 4 but the distance is 0.8 mm: dividing by the norm 5 changes the unit.

    Review the conversion from score to distance

  • “Multiplying w and c increases the margin without any effort.”

    The scores increase, the norm does too, and the line stays the same. The functional margin of D goes from 4 to 12 score for a factor of 3, but the geometric margin remains 0.8 mm.

    Repeat the exercise on scaling

  • “A positive margin guarantees that new seeds will be classified correctly.”

    The margin describes the gap between the boundary and the known seeds, here 0.8 mm for four fictional points. Without an additional assumption about how future seeds are produced, it makes no promises about them.

    Find the limitation in the recall cards

Exercise 1 · Explained application

A fifth seed

A fifth fictional seed E(3 ; 4) from the “plus” family is added, with the same boundary f(x) = 3·x₁ + 4·x₂ − 10. Calculate its signed score and then its distance in millimetres, and say whether the margin of the set changes.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate the two products, then their sum, then subtract the threshold.

Hint 2

The margin of the set is the smallest distance: compare the new distance with the margin in the example.

Compare with the solution

Products: 3 × 3 = 9 and 4 × 4 = 16. Sum: 9 + 16 = 25. Threshold subtracted: 25 − 10 = 15 score; the label +1 does not change the sign.

Distance: 15 / 5 = 3 mm. This distance exceeds 0.8 mm, so the minimum does not change: the margin remains 0.8 mm, still determined by seed D. Adding a distant seed does not reduce the margin; adding a seed closer than D would reduce it.

Check your reasoning

  • Products, sum and threshold handled in that order, with the score unit.
  • Division by the norm and result in millimetres.
  • Conclusion drawn by comparing with the minimum, without repeating all the calculations.

Exercise 2 · Guided analysis

Triple the scale, same boundary

We replace w and c with 3 times their values: the line stays the same. Calculate the new functional margin of seed D, the new norm and the geometric margin. Say what changes and what stays the same.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The score is linear in w and c: the factor applies to every score.

Hint 2

The norm of a vector multiplied by a positive factor is multiplied by that factor.

Compare with the solution

Functional margin of D: 4 × 3 = 12 score. Norm: 5 × 3 = 15 score per mm. Geometric margin: 12 / 15 = 4/5, or 0.8 mm.

All scores are multiplied by the same positive factor: the order of the distances is preserved and D remains the closest. The functional margin and the norm change; the geometric margin and the line do not. This is the reason for dividing by the norm.

Check your reasoning

  • Factor applied to the scores and the norm.
  • Quotient recalculated in millimetres and compared with the margin in the example.
  • Explicit distinction between a quantity that changes and a quantity that stays invariant.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

Remove the closest seed

We remove seed D from the set; the boundary does not move. What is the new margin, which seed determines it, and has this boundary become better? Justify your answer in one or two sentences.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Use the distances from the example: which remain after the removal?

Hint 2

Ask yourself what the margin measures: the boundary alone, or the boundary relative to the seeds retained.

Compare with the solution

Remaining distances: A at 1 mm, C at 1.2 mm, B at 2.6 mm. The smallest is 1 mm, determined by seed A: the margin goes from 0.8 mm to 1 mm.

Yet the boundary has not moved. The margin is measured relative to the seeds retained: removing the closest point automatically increases it, without improving anything for real seeds. A margin value is meaningful only for a known and specified set of points.

Check your reasoning

  • New margin obtained from the distances already calculated.
  • Seed that determines the minimum correctly identified.
  • No conclusion that the boundary is better simply because the value has increased.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do we calculate the margin of a linear boundary for labelled points?

It is the smallest distance y·(w·x − c) / ‖w‖ between the points and the boundary, when all points are on the correct side; it is expressed in the unit of the readings.

Recall 2Why do we divide the signed score by the norm of w?

Moving a distance t along the unit direction perpendicular to the boundary changes the score by t·‖w‖: the distance is therefore the score divided by the norm.

Recall 3What happens to the functional margin and the geometric margin if we multiply w and c by a positive factor?

The functional margin is multiplied by that factor; the geometric margin and the line do not change.

Recall 4Does a positive margin guarantee correct classification of new seeds?

No: it describes the gap to the known points for this boundary; without an additional assumption, it makes no promises about future seeds.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define the score f(x) = w·x − c and the labels y; calculate the signed score y·f for each point; check that all are positive; divide by the norm of w to obtain a distance in the unit of the readings; take the smallest; check using invariance under scaling; draw conclusions only about the known points.

What you understand
A margin is not a raw score: it is the smallest signed distance between known points and a boundary, obtained by dividing by the norm of the weights. Hinge loss, the dual and kernels belong to other units in the chapter and are not covered here.
