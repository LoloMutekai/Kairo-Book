Level 2 · C051·M3

Interpolation

Your goalEstimate the volume of a tank between marked heights, choose the right segment and distinguish an exact calculation from an accurate model.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You read a tank gauge at 5 cm. A calibration table gives reference point A: 2 cm for 10 L, and reference point B: 10 cm for 42 L. The volume you need is not in the table. All the data in this scene are fictional; no actual measurements are reported.

Imagine joining the reference points with a line segment. As you move up from A towards B, you cover a certain fraction of the height difference; you take the same fraction of the increase in volume. This is affine interpolation, often called linear interpolation: the chosen rule must reproduce the given values at the reference points exactly.

Here, affine means that the slope stays constant over the interval, without requiring the line to pass through the origin. This choice describes the calculation between the reference points. It does not mean that the actual tank always gains the same number of litres per centimetre: its shape could change this relationship.

Before calculating, locate the required height: it lies between A and B, closer to A. The estimate must therefore lie between the reference volumes, closer to the volume at A. Extending a rule beyond the heights that bound it is called extrapolation.

The rule to remember

Choose the reference points that bracket the height. Measure the displacement from the lower reference point, divide it by the full height difference, then apply this fraction to the change in volume.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

How many litres at 5 cm on the gauge?

Use A = (2 cm ; 10 L) and B = (10 cm ; 42 L). Treat these fictional values as exact for the calculation. Choose an affine relationship between A and B, then check the volume obtained at h = 5 cm.

  1. Name the quantities and the domain

    h is the required height; hA and hB are the heights of the reference points, all in centimetres and measured from the same reference. VA and VB are the associated volumes, in litres. I(h) denotes the volume estimated by our interpolation at height h, in litres. The parentheses mean “evaluated at this height”.

    We require hA < hB and hA ≤ h ≤ hB. The height difference must therefore be strictly positive: identical heights would prevent this division. Keep each height-volume pair together and express all heights in the same unit. The uncertainty in the reference values is not modelled in this exercise.

  2. Measure the differences from A

    Δ, the letter delta, indicates a difference. Δh = hB − hA is the full height difference between the reference points: 10 − 2 = 8 cm. ΔV = VB − VA is the corresponding change in volume: 42 − 10 = 32 L.

    The required displacement from A is d = h − hA = 5 − 2 = 3 cm. Subtracting hA is essential: we start neither at a height of 0 nor with an empty tank.

  3. Construct a relative position

    θ, the letter theta, is the fraction of the way from A to B already covered. Divide the displacement by the full height difference; the centimetres cancel, so θ has no unit.

    θ=h−hAhB−hA=38=0.375

    At A, θ equals 0; at B, θ equals 1. Between the reference points, 0 ≤ θ ≤ 1. Here, θ = 3/8 is less than 1/2: the height is closer to A, as the drawing suggests.

  4. Apply this fraction to the volume

    An affine relationship keeps a constant slope s = ΔV/Δh. Here, s = 32/8 = 4 L/cm. The volume added from A is therefore s × d, or θ × ΔV. Add this volume to VA to obtain the total volume.

    I(h)=VA+h−hAhB−hA×(VB−VA)

    The increase is (3/8) × 32 = 12 L. The result is therefore I(5 cm) = 10 + 12 = 22 L. Unit check: a number with no unit multiplies litres, then the result is added to litres.

  5. Check using weights and endpoints

    Expanding VA + θ(VB − VA) gives another form. The weights are the coefficients that multiply the volumes. Here they are 1 − θ and θ; they are nonnegative and their sum is 1.

    I(h)=(1−θ)VA+θVB

    Our check gives (5/8) × 10 = 6.25 L and (3/8) × 42 = 15.75 L. Their sum is 22.00 L, as in the first calculation; the weights total 1. This weighted average stays between 10 L and 42 L, and here is closer to 10 L.

    At h = hA, θ = 0 and I(hA) = VA; at h = hB, θ = 1 and I(hB) = VB. The model reproduces its reference values. It is the unique affine rule that does so: its slope is fixed by ΔV/Δh, then its value at A determines everything else.

  6. Choose a segment when the table grows

    Add fictional reference point C to the same table: hC = 14 cm and VC = 60 L. These letters again denote a height and its associated volume. For a requested height between B and C, the formula must use B as the start and C as the end. Recalculate the differences; do not automatically keep the slope from A to B.

    This rule is called piecewise affine interpolation: each interval between neighbouring reference points has its own segment. At the shared reference point B, both segments return VB; they meet, even if their slopes differ. A query outside the entire table requires an explicit decision: refuse to estimate it or state that you are extrapolating and give the assumptions.

  7. State exactly what the result establishes

    The fractions give 22 L exactly for the affine rule and the selected data. They do not establish that the actual volume is exactly this value. A curved relationship could pass through the same reference points and give a different volume inside the interval: the last exercise constructs one.

    The affine assumption would be physically exact if each added centimetre over this interval contributed the same volume, for example in a section of tank with a constant horizontal cross section. Our table does not supply this fact. In a tank, assuming that volume increases with height bounds it between the reference volumes. These bounds prove neither a constant slope nor a small error around 22 L. A tighter guarantee would require an additional assumption about the relationship or check measurements.

Explain it in your own words

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I just take the average of the reference volumes.”

    The ordinary average gives (10 + 42)/2 = 26 L, which is 4 L more than the interpolation at 5 cm. It gives the right weight only at the midpoint in height, where θ = 1/2. Here θ = 3/8: calculate the displacement from A and keep the corresponding weights.

    Reconstruct the fraction, then check the weights

  • “I have a formula between A and B, so I can keep using it beyond B.”

    Beyond B, this formula extends its segment: this is extrapolation of that segment. If the table provides C and the requested height is between B and C, use these neighbouring reference points. Check the bounds before any division; the second exercise compares the results.

    Choose the interval in the extended table exercise

  • “The line passes through the reference points, so it must describe the tank.”

    Passing through the reference points is a condition for interpolation, not proof of the shape between them. Distinguish the uniqueness of the affine rule from the existence of other curved rules. Look for a physical justification or a check point before claiming that the calculation is exact for the actual tank.

Exercise 2 · Method selection · piecewise interpolation

The table now includes C

The table for the same tank contains A, B and C = (14 cm ; 60 L). For 12 cm, which reference points should you choose? Calculate the volume, then calculate what extending segment A–B would give. Explain why the results correspond to different choices.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Locate the required height in the ordered list of heights. It is after B and before C.

Hint 2

In the local formula, replace starting point A with B and endpoint B with C. For comparison, then keep the old reference points A and B and observe the new value of θ.

Compare with the solution

Since 10 < 12 < 14 in centimetres, the neighbouring points that bracket the height are B and C. Their height difference is 4 cm, their increase in volume is 18 L and the displacement from B is 2 cm. The local fraction is 2/4 = 1/2.

The interpolated volume is 42 + (1/2) × 18 = 42 + 9 = 51 L. It does lie between 42 L and 60 L.

Extending A–B would give a displacement of 12 − 2 = 10 cm, so θ = 10/8 = 5/4, greater than 1. This would give 10 + (5/4) × 32 = 10 + 40 = 50 L. The arithmetic is consistent, but this is extrapolation of the first segment. The required piecewise interpolation uses reference point C and gives 51 L; it still does not guarantee the actual volume.

Check your reasoning

  • Choose B and C by checking the bounds before calculating.
  • Recalculate the fraction from B and obtain 51 L.
  • Obtain 50 L using the old line, then identify the change in domain and model.

Exercise 3 · Justification · construct a counterexample

Another curve between A and B

Return to interval A–B and h = 5 cm. I(h) is our affine interpolation. Consider another rule J(h) = I(h) + κ(h − hA)(hB − h), where κ = 1/15 litre per square centimetre, a positive value. Check that J reproduces VA and VB, then calculate J at the required height. What can you conclude about the actual volume?

J(h)=I(h)+κ(h−hA)(hB−h)
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At each reference point, a factor in the added product is 0. Check them separately.

Hint 2

At the required height, the displacement from A is 3 cm and the remaining distance to B is 5 cm. Their product is in square centimetres.

Compare with the solution

At hA, the factor h − hA equals 0; at hB, the factor hB − h equals 0. The added term is 0 at the reference points: J(hA) = I(hA) = 10 L and J(hB) = I(hB) = 42 L. Both rules therefore interpolate the values at A and B.

At 5 cm, the differences are 3 cm and 5 cm, so their product is 15 square centimetres. Multiplied by κ = 1/15 litre per square centimetre, it gives 1 L. Thus J(5 cm) = 22 + 1 = 23 L, whereas I gives 22 L.

J is a curved rule, not another affine rule: there is no contradiction with the uniqueness of the line. The reference points alone cannot determine which rule describes the tank. Here J is a mathematical counterexample; its 23 L are no more an actual measurement than the 22 L from I.

Check your reasoning

  • Check that the added term is 0 at each reference point.
  • Calculate 23 L with consistent units.
  • Distinguish the unique affine line from other possible interpolations, without claiming physical validation for J.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you reconstruct the fraction covered between height reference points? Why subtract the starting height?

θ = (h − hA)/(hB − hA). The numerator measures the displacement from A and the denominator the full A–B height difference. They have the same unit; θ has no unit. Using the raw height would change the starting point.

Recall 2Given the fraction θ, how do you find the volume and check its units?

I(h) = VA + θ(VB − VA). Take the fraction θ of the increase in litres, then add the starting volume in litres. The result is in litres; θ itself is not a volume.

Recall 3Why does the affine volume stay between the reference volumes when the height is inside the interval?

I(h) = (1 − θ)VA + θVB. Inside the interval, θ lies between 0 and 1; the weights are nonnegative and their sum is 1. This weighted average stays between VA and VB. At the endpoints, it reproduces the given values.

Recall 4When a table has several reference points, which segment should you choose? What does a request outside the table mean?

Choose the neighbouring reference points that bracket the height and recalculate the fraction in that interval. At shared reference points, the segments return the same value. Outside the table, you are no longer interpolating: extrapolation must be explicitly chosen and justified.

Recall 5What does the line passing exactly through A and B prove? What does it leave open?

It proves that this rule interpolates the values at A and B; these reference points determine a unique affine rule. They do not exclude other curves between them. A calculation with exact fractions validates neither the shape of the tank nor the estimated actual volume.
