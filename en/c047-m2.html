Level 2 · C047·M2

Existence and uniqueness

Your goalDistinguish a possible trajectory from one determined by the law and the initial condition, then check the conditions for local uniqueness.

Before you start
c045-m5 : introductory quadrature · c046-m5 : approximation error · c033-m5 : linear approximation · c047-m1 : direction field

✿01 See the idea

A tank receives water from a pump controlled by the amount already present. The fuller the tank, the higher the requested flow rate, following a square root law. With 1 L initially, drawing a curve that follows this setting proves that an evolution is possible. It does not yet prove that no other curve works.

Existence answers the question “is there a trajectory that satisfies the law and the initial condition?” Uniqueness answers the question “do all trajectories that satisfy these same data coincide?” The slope given by the direction field, even when known at every point, does not by itself answer the second question.

Keep the same tank and the same law, but start with an empty tank. The setting then requests a flow rate of 0. Yet we will check that the model also allows filling to begin after a wait. A horizontal tangent at the start can belong to several trajectories.

The pump, volumes and durations are fictional. This law is chosen to examine a mathematical difficulty; it is neither a usual draining law nor measured behaviour. Starting without any triggering input would indicate a shortcoming of the model here. The tank capacity is 4 L; we will stop all physical interpretation when it is reached.

The rule to remember

Check the initial condition and the equation to establish a solution; then state the assumptions that guarantee uniqueness, the neighbourhood where they hold and the duration over which the model remains applicable.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The same law, first with water and then with an empty tank

Volume V(t) is measured in litres and time t in minutes. Take H = 1 L, τ = 1 min and V(0) = 1 L. The flow rate is (H/τ) × 2√(V/H). We will check a trajectory, its uniqueness near the positive initial volume and its value after 0.50 min, then examine the initially empty tank.

  1. State the initial value problem precisely

    Scaled time s = t/τ and scaled volume x(s) = V(τs)/H are dimensionless. The prime on x denotes the derivative with respect to s. The physical flow rate is therefore (H/τ)x′, in L/min. The square root is the nonnegative root. We assume a pump that follows the setting exactly, without delay or fluctuation, and no leaks or water outflow before capacity is reached.

    x′(s)=f(s,x(s));x(0)=1

    Here f(s,u) = 2√max(u, 0), and x(0) = 1. The letter u denotes any state at which we evaluate the law. max selects the larger of its arguments. This extension to negative states only provides a function defined around the empty state; physical volumes remain nonnegative. For these volumes, the law is simply x′ = 2√x.

    An initial value problem combines an evolution equation with a value at a given instant. A solution is a function differentiable on an interval containing that instant, satisfying the equation throughout that interval and the initial value. Checking only one point on the curve is not enough.

  2. Distinguish the local guarantees

    Here is a sufficient criterion for a real scalar equation. If f is continuous on an open rectangle around the initial point (s⋆, x⋆), there is at least one solution on a small interval around s⋆. Continuous here means that small changes in the arguments produce small changes in the value.

    If, in addition, f is locally Lipschitz with respect to the state, this solution is locally unique: two solutions with the same initial condition coincide near that initial point. This is the Picard–Lindelöf criterion, also called the Cauchy–Lipschitz criterion. Specifically, in a sufficiently small rectangle, we need a finite constant L, independent of s, u and v, such that:

    |f(s,u)−f(s,v)|≤L|u−v|

    v denotes another state at the same instant; the bars measure an absolute value. All these quantities are dimensionless in the scaled variables. L bounds the amplification of a difference in state into a difference in slope. This criterion concerns the whole neighbourhood, rather than just the slope at the initial point. It guarantees a local interval without promising unlimited duration.

  3. Check the criterion around the positive initial volume

    Our f is continuous because maximum and square root are continuous on their domains. Around x = 1, choose states u and v between 1/4 and 9/4. Transform the difference of roots by multiplying by their sum:

    |f(s,u)−f(s,v)|=2|u−v|u+v≤2|u−v|

    Each root is at least 1/2, so their sum is at least 1. Dividing 2 by this bound gives L = 2. Since f does not depend on s, the same bound works at all instants in the rectangle. Its interior contains the initial point: the criterion applies.

    A numerical check illustrates the bound without replacing the proof for all states: at the endpoints, the roots are 1/2 and 3/2, and the slopes are 1 and 3. Their difference 2 is less than or equal to 2 × 2 = 4. The exact ratio of differences is 1; a sufficient bound does not have to be attained.

  4. Check the proposed curve and understand uniqueness

    Propose the following curve for s > −1:

    x(s)=(1+s)2

    Initially, x(0) = 1 × 1 = 1, which recovers 1. Its derivative is 2(1 + s). On the stated interval, 1 + s is positive, so √x(s) = 1 + s. The derivative is exactly f(s, x(s)): existence is verified by substitution.

    Why could another curve not start from the same point? By continuity, a solution starting from 1 stays positive sufficiently near the start. Set g(s) = √x(s). The chain rule gives g′ = x′/(2√x) = 1. Since g(0) = 1, we obtain g(s) = 1 + s: the positive curve is determined. This reasoning directly confirms the general criterion here; it does not claim to prove the theorem for all equations.

    This division is not allowed when the tank starts empty, where √x is 0. This restriction will be decisive. General solution methods come in the following units.

  5. Calculate a state and check it another way

    The requested time is t = 0.50 min. Dividing by τ = 1 min gives s = 1/2. Add 1 to obtain √x = 3/2, then square: x = 3/2 × 3/2 = 9/4. Multiplying by H gives V = 9/4 L, or 2.25 L.

    The scaled slope is 2 × 3/2 = 3. With H/τ = 1 L/min, the flow rate is 3 L/min. The derivative of the curve and the pump law therefore give the same flow rate; initially, both gave 2 in the scaled variables.

    Another check: the scaled flow rate along this curve is 2(1 + s). Its integral between 0 and s is 2s plus the square of s. Adding the initial state recovers the following identity, and hence 9/4 at the requested time:

    1+2s+s2=(1+s)2

    Over this duration, the trajectory stays between 1 and 9/4, below the capacity of 4 L after conversion back to physical units.

  6. Start with an empty tank: construct distinct solutions

    Change only the initial condition: x(0) = 0. The curve that is constantly 0 is a solution. For any delay a ≥ 0, expressed in scaled time, another possible curve is:

    xa(s)={0si s≤a(s−a)2si s≥a

    Before a, both the value and the derivative are 0: the equation is satisfied. After a, the derivative is 2(s − a) and the root of x is s − a: the equation is again satisfied. At the join s = a, the values and derivatives of both branches are 0; the entire curve is indeed differentiable and also satisfies the equation at that instant.

    The immediate start corresponds to a = 0; also choose a = 1. The two solutions have the same initial value and the same initial slope of 0, but differ whenever s is strictly positive and less than the delay. They therefore refute even local uniqueness at the empty state. The transfer exercise compares them at a later time.

  7. Identify the assumption that fails at the empty state

    Continuity of f still holds at the empty state. However, compare a state ε > 0 with 0 at the same instant, where ε is the Greek letter epsilon:

    |f(s,ε)−f(s,0)||ε−0|=2ε

    This ratio becomes arbitrarily large as ε approaches 0 through positive values. No finite constant L works in a neighbourhood of the empty state. For example, ε = 1/4 gives the ratio 4, whereas ε = 1/16 gives f(s, ε) = 1/2 and the ratio 8. These calculations illustrate the increase; the formula and its limit prove the absence of a local bound.

    Take care with the logic: if the sufficient criterion fails, it says nothing by itself about uniqueness. Here, nonuniqueness is proved by the distinct curves in the previous step. This additional proof is needed.

  8. Limit the conclusion in time and within the scenario

    For the positive initial volume, the criterion first guarantees an interval around the initial instant. The verified formula lets us continue the trajectory forward here as long as the scenario remains applicable. With capacity 4 L and H = 1 L, the limiting state is x = 4. Its root is 2, so 1 + s = 2 gives s = 1, or t = 1 min.

    Beyond that, we would need to describe overflow or the pump stopping: the same model of the volume held in the tank is no longer sufficient. A mathematical formula that can be extended does not guarantee that its extension is physically appropriate. Conversely, a numerical method that prints a single trajectory for an initially empty tank may simply have selected one of the solutions. Existence, uniqueness, approximation and agreement with the tank are separate questions.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the steps. Explain why division by √x is allowed near the positive initial volume and forbidden at the empty state. Then reconstruct a delayed start and pay particular attention to checking its join: this join prevents the curve from being dismissed as a mere drawing.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The law gives a slope at every point, so it determines a single curve.”

    All our delayed starts share the same initial slope of 0. Check their value, their derivative and the join: distinct curves satisfy the same law and the same empty initial condition.

    Return to “Start with an empty tank” and its check

  • “I have no Lipschitz bound: there must be several solutions.”

    The criterion is sufficient. Its failure removes a guarantee; it does not prove the opposite conclusion. For the empty tank, the explicitly constructed solutions establish nonuniqueness.

    Reread the assumption that fails and the conclusion it allows

  • “A unique solution exists locally: I can predict the tank indefinitely.”

    State the neighbourhood where the criterion applies, then check any extension and the limits of the scenario separately. Our capacity is reached after 1 min with the positive initial volume; another physical model is then needed.

    Return to the limits on duration and capacity

Calculate r(0) and r′(0) separately. Equal values do not mean equal derivatives.

Hint 2

The derivative of r is −2(1 − s). Compare it with the slope required by the law at the start.

Compare with the solution

The initial condition holds: r(0) = 1 = 1. But r′(0) = -2, whereas f(0, r(0)) = 2. These slopes differ, so r is not a solution of the problem, even on a small interval around the start.

The calculation checks the candidate. The guarantee of uniqueness comes separately from continuity and the local bound obtained for f. Finding an invalid candidate does not prove any nonuniqueness.

Exercise 2 · Application · check a neighbourhood

Approaching the empty tank

The initial condition remains x(0) = 1. Enlarge the neighbourhood of states: its lower bound becomes ε = 1/16, while its upper bound remains 9/4. Using the difference of roots again, calculate a sufficient constant L. Why does this proof not extend to a neighbourhood containing the empty state?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

√ε = 1/4. Find a lower bound for the sum √u + √v for two states in the new neighbourhood.

Hint 2

The bound for L is 2 divided by this minimum sum. For the empty state, instead return to the ratio comparing ε with 0.

Compare with the solution

Both roots are at least 1/4. Their sum is at least 1/2, so the same identity gives L = 2/(1/2) = 4. The neighbourhood stays strictly positive and contains the initial state: local uniqueness remains guaranteed. This bound is less tight than 2, because states closer to empty are allowed.

As the lower bound tends to 0, this estimate no longer provides a uniform finite constant. More precisely, in every neighbourhood of the empty state, the ratio 2/√ε is unbounded. This last observation rules out the Lipschitz property at the empty state; a chosen bound becoming large would not by itself rule out all other possible bounds.

Check your reasoning

  • I justify the minimum sum 1/2 before dividing.
  • I obtain 4 as a sufficient bound for all pairs of states in the neighbourhood.
  • I distinguish checking a positive neighbourhood from the case containing the empty state.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · defend a diagnosis

A single output does not demonstrate uniqueness

Now start with an empty tank. A calculator displays the trajectory that is constantly 0 and announces “this is the only solution”. Instead, compare the immediate start and the delayed start a = 1 at s = 3/2. Give their physical volumes and flow rates, check the join for the delayed start, then reply to the calculator.

Hint 1

The time elapsed since the delayed start is 3/2 − 1 = 1/2. Square this scaled duration.

Hint 2

The slopes are 2 times the time elapsed since each start. Convert back to physical units using H and τ.

Hint 3

At the join, examine the value AND the derivative on both sides. Then recall what a single calculation result can establish.

Compare with the solution

The immediate start gives x = 3/2 × 3/2 = 9/4, or 2.25 L. Its scaled slope is 3, so its flow rate is 3 L/min since H/τ = 1 L/min. The delayed start gives x = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, or 0.25 L; its slope and flow rate are respectively 1 and 1 L/min.

The volumes differ by 2 L at the same instant and stay below 4 L. At the join s = 1, the values are 0 and the left and right derivatives are 0. The law is indeed satisfied, including at the join. Both curves also have the same empty initial condition; they already differ arbitrarily close to this initial instant.

The calculator’s 0 result is a possible solution. A single output does not rule out the other trajectories we have just checked. The law and the initial condition are insufficient to choose how this fictional pump physically starts; the model would need to be specified further, without inventing an observed result.

  • I check the join using derivatives as well as values.
  • I refute uniqueness using distinct solutions, rather than just the failure of an assumption.
  • I distinguish a calculated trajectory from an observation of a real pump.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What data define the initial value problem, and what must be checked for a candidate?

An evolution equation and a value at a given instant. The candidate must be differentiable, satisfy the equation on the interval and recover the initial value. Merely passing through the right point is not enough.

Recall 2What do continuity and the local Lipschitz condition guarantee?

For our scalar equation, continuity on an open rectangle around the initial point guarantees local existence. If a single finite constant also bounds |f(s,u) − f(s,v)| by L|u − v| in this rectangle, local uniqueness is guaranteed. The total duration of validity still needs examination.

Recall 3How do you check a delayed start from an empty tank?

Take x(s) to be 0 before the delay a and equal to the square of s − a afterwards. Check the equation on each side, then the 0 value and derivative at the join. Different delays give different solutions to the same initial value problem.

Recall 4Does failure of the Lipschitz criterion demonstrate nonuniqueness?

No: it removes a sufficient guarantee. To prove nonuniqueness, you must exhibit distinct solutions to the same problem. The delayed starts do this, independently of the assessment of the criterion.

Recall 5Why is a unique curve or a single calculation output not enough to predict the real tank?

Uniqueness concerns the solutions of a model under its assumptions. Its interval, physical capacity, approximation and agreement with the system still need to be checked. A single calculation output does not even prove mathematical uniqueness.

Without rereading, reconstruct a chain: equation and initial condition → verified solution → continuity and local bound → scope of the conclusion. Add the empty case by writing two solutions and their joins, then finish with the capacity limit.
