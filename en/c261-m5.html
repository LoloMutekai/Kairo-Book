Level 4 · C261·M5

Model uncertainty

Your goalDistinguish variation within a model from disagreement between models, calculate their contributions to the variance of a mixture, and check how sensitive a conclusion is to the chosen weights.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

An analyst needs to describe tomorrow’s loss on a single hypothetical position, in euros. The available history leaves two interpretations open: model A continues the previous regime; model B assumes that a structural break persists. Both refer to the same position and the same day. A predicts a mean loss of 20 €; B predicts 80 €.

Within each model, the possible losses stay close to its own mean. Yet the means are far apart. Reducing the uncertainty calculated within A does not settle the question “should we use A or B?”. This second question is where model uncertainty arises: several descriptions of the data remain defensible and lead to different conclusions.

To make the problem calculable by hand, A assigns equal probability to losses of 10 € and 30 €; B does the same for 70 € and 90 €. These distributions, their plausibility, and all the amounts are hypothetical assumptions. We have neither fitted them to a market nor validated them against one.

We will first give each model a weight of 1/2, then change the weights. This choice sets up a sensitivity analysis; it does not measure the probability that each model is true. The mixture keeps the disagreement visible rather than hiding it behind a single mean.

The rule to remember

State the models and weights; separate variation within models from disagreement between means; change the weights before calling a conclusion robust.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Tomorrow’s loss, several models today

Let us calculate the mean and variance of the mixture with equal weights. Then let us check whether a hypothetical rule, “predicted mean loss at most 60 €”, gives the same conclusion under A and under B. This rule concerns only an expected loss.

  1. Define what is random and what is chosen

    L denotes tomorrow’s loss, in euros, which is positive in the outcomes of our example. M is a label taking the values A or B. We define a mixture distribution: choose the label using the stated weights, then a loss according to its distribution. This mathematical construction does not mean that the market actually selects a model at random.

    p is the weight of A, with no unit, between 0 and 1; the weight of B is 1 − p. The notation P(M = A) = p denotes this probability in the constructed mixture. E denotes an expectation, that is, a weighted average; Var denotes a variance, that is, an expected squared deviation. The vertical bar in E(L | M = A) means “conditional on model A”.

    For a label m equal to A or B, μm = E(L | M = m) is the mean in euros and vm = Var(L | M = m) is the variance in square euros. μ = E(L) and V = Var(L) describe the mixture. The parameters and forecast date are fixed here. The moments are finite. No independence between the predictions of A and B is required: we are mixing distributions, not adding two losses.

  2. Calculate what each model predicts

    Under A, the outcomes are equally probable. Their mean is (10 + 30)/2 = 40/2 = 20 €. The deviations from this mean are −10 € and +10 €. The average of their squares is 100 €².

    vA=(−10)2+1022=100 €²

    Under B, the same method gives (70 + 90)/2 = 160/2 = 80 €. The deviations are −10 € and +10 €, so v_B = 100 €². Identical variances do not make the models equivalent: their centers differ by 60 €.

  3. Calculate the center of the mixture

    Weight the means using the same weights as the distributions:

    μ=pμA+(1−p)μB

    For p = 1/2, μ = (20 + 80)/2 = 100/2 = 50 €. This is the mixture mean; neither model necessarily predicts this value as a possible outcome. In our example, a loss of exactly 50 € belongs to neither of the proposed distributions.

  4. Derive the variance decomposition

    With model m fixed, decompose the total deviation: L − μ = (L − μm) + (μm − μ). The first part describes variation within the model; the second is the shift in its mean. Expanding the square gives the cross term 2(L − μm)(μm − μ). Its conditional mean is 0 because E(L − μm | M = m) = 0, by the definition of μm. This cancellation requires no additional independence assumption.

    After weighting the models, W is the average of the variances within models and U is the variance of their means. W, U, and V are in square euros:

    W=pvA+(1−p)vB
    U=p(μA−μ)2+(1−p)(μB−μ)2
    V=W+U

    Here U represents disagreement between means within the chosen family. It does not quantify every form of model uncertainty: models can have the same mean and different tails. The conditional variances also exclude uncertainty in parameter estimation, since we have fixed the parameters.

  5. Apply equal weights

    The average variation within models is W = (100 + 100)/2 = 200/2 = 100 €². The addition is valid because we are combining variances in the same unit.

    The shifts in the means are 20 − 50 = -30 € and 80 − 50 = 30 €. Their squares are 900 €² and 900 €². Thus U = (900 + 900)/2 = 1800/2 = 900 €².

    The total variance is therefore V = 100 + 900 = 1000 €². Reporting only 100 €² as the mixture variance would omit the shifts in the centers. Adding the signed shifts directly would give 0 and lose precisely this disagreement.

  6. Check using all outcomes, then the limiting cases

    A second method avoids any decomposition: with equal weights, the 4 losses 10, 30, 70, and 90 € each have probability 1/4. Their mean is 50 €. Directly calculate the average of their squared deviations from 50 €:

    V=(10−50)2+(30−50)2+(70−50)2+(90−50)24=1000 €²

    The same result confirms the two components. If p equals 1, only A remains and U equals 0; if p equals 0, only B remains and U again equals 0. This expresses the exclusion of the other model, not proof that it was false.

    For two models, replacing μ with its definition gives the following form. The squared difference between centers is 3600 €² here. This form will let us change the weights without recalculating all the shifts.

    U=p(1−p)(μA−μB)2

  7. Examine the conclusion as well as the number

    Let μ(p) denote the mean obtained with weight p. The hypothetical rule accepts an expected loss of at most 60 €. The mixture with equal weights passes: its margin is 60 − 50 = 10 €. A also passes, with a margin of 40 €; B fails, with a margin of -20 €. The conclusion “the rule is satisfied” therefore depends on the model or the weighting.

    If we require it to hold under every included model, we must look at the largest expectation, here 80 €, which exceeds 60 €. This requirement is a different rule, stated explicitly. The envelope [20 €; 80 €] contains the expectations of the possible mixtures; it is not a prediction interval for L: the losses 10 € and 90 € lie outside it.

    Neither the total variance nor the envelope guarantees coverage of actual losses. A mixture of models that are all misspecified can still be misleading. To assess empirical robustness, we would still need to justify the candidates, examine other assumptions about tails or structural breaks, and compare the predictions with later observations, without selecting the preferred conclusion after the fact.

Explain it in your own words

Why does having identical variances within the two models not justify ignoring their disagreement? Reconstruct the calculation, then distinguish a mixture mean, an envelope of expectations, and an actually observed loss.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Averaging the variances is enough.”

    This average gives W. Also center each model mean around μ, square these deviations, and weight them: this gives U, then V = W + U.

    Review the decomposition and its direct check

  • “Equal weights prove that the models are equally credible.”

    Here the weights are a calculation convention. Rework the same case favoring A, then state where justified weights would come from before giving them a probabilistic interpretation about the world.

    Try changing the weights

  • “The envelope of means contains the possible losses.”

    The envelope describes expectations. Revisit the extreme outcomes predicted by A and B: they can lie outside this envelope. A prediction interval would require the distribution of L and a stated coverage probability.

    Review the scope of the conclusion

The rule fails for p below 1/3, in particular p = 0, which keeps only B. Equal weights and weighting that favors A pass, but this is not enough to claim robustness to all weights. At p = 1/3, the mean is exactly 60 €: equality passes, since the rule says “at most”.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What remains uncertain when the parameters of each model are fixed?

The choice of description: several assumptions about the regime or distribution can remain plausible and produce different predictions. Variation within a model does not resolve this choice.

Recall 2Which two parts make up the variance of our mixture?

The weighted average W of the variances within models, followed by U, the weighted variance of the model means around the common mean. V = W + U; all these quantities are in square euros.

Recall 3Why does the cross term disappear in the proof?

With the model fixed, the deviation L − μₘ has mean 0 by definition. The shift μₘ − μ is constant for that model. Their product therefore has conditional mean 0, without additional independence.

Recall 4Does changing the weights without a new observation demonstrate a reduction in empirical uncertainty?

No. It changes the constructed mixture distribution. The weights in our exercise are conventions; interpreting their credibility would require separate justification and validation.

Recall 5Why is an envelope of expectations not a guarantee about tomorrow’s loss?

It bounds the means of the included models, not the realizations of their distributions or those of the market. The family may omit an important assumption, and individual outcomes can exceed these means.
