Autocovariance

Your goalCalculate autocovariance and autocorrelation at lags 1 and 2 for a short series with regularly spaced dates, explaining the use of a single mean, the number of pairs, the divisor and the sign.

Before you start
c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c037-m5 : controlled data cleaning · c090-m1 : timestamping

✿01 See the idea

A neighbourhood library, invented for this page, records the number of books borrowed each day. We follow 5 consecutive days: 3, 4, 6, 7 and then 5 loans. These values are fictional; they do not describe any real library or establish any rules for its use.

A simple question arises: when a day is above the mean, is the next day also above it, rather than below? The mean and variance cannot answer this, because they ignore order: shuffle the days and they stay the same. Autocovariance depends on order. It compares a day’s deviation from the mean with that of the day h days later.

A deviation is positive when the day is above the mean and negative when it is below. If days h days apart are on the same side of the mean, the product of their deviations is positive; if they are on opposite sides, it is negative; if either deviation is 0, the product is 0. The average of these products is the autocovariance at lag h. Its unit is loans squared, as for a variance.

2 conditions govern this interpretation. The days must be regularly spaced, with no gap filled by mistake: this is the purpose of the timestamping and data cleaning covered earlier. We also use a single mean for the whole series. If the level kept rising, deviations from this single mean would mainly reflect that rise; trend and seasonality are covered in the following units and are not addressed here.

The rule to remember

Subtract the mean, multiply each deviation by that of the day h days later, add the n − h products, then divide by n. Dividing next by the value at lag 0 gives the autocorrelation, which is dimensionless.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

5 days of loans, from the mean to autocorrelation

Fictional data: xₜ is 3, 4, 6, 7 and then 5 loans on days 1 to 5. Let us calculate the autocovariance at lags 0 and 1, then the autocorrelation at lag 1, with their units.

  1. Define the symbols and formula

    t is the day number, from 1 to n, and is dimensionless. xt is the number of loans on day t, in loans. n is the number of days: n = 5. m is the mean of the series, in loans. et = xt − m is the deviation on day t, in loans. h is the lag in days, an integer from 0 to n − 1. γ̂(h) is the observed autocovariance at lag h, in loans squared. ρ̂(h) = γ̂(h) / γ̂(0) is the observed autocorrelation, which is dimensionless. The hat reminds us that these numbers are calculated from the available series, rather than known properties of a model.

    γ^(h)=1n∑t=1n−h(xt−m)(xt+h−m)

    Assumptions: the days are regularly spaced and there are no missing values; a single mean m is used for the whole series; the divisor is n rather than the number of pairs. This last point is a convention, which we will state each time.

  2. Calculate the single mean

    First we add the loans: 3 + 4 = 7, then 7 + 6 = 13, then 13 + 7 = 20, then 20 + 5 = 25 loans. We then divide by the number of days: 25 / 5 = 5 loans.

    Why use a single mean? Deviations only mean “above” or “below” when all days share the same reference point.

  3. Calculate and check the deviations

    Each deviation is the number of loans on that day minus the mean.

    t12345xt34675et-2-1120

    Check: since m is the mean, the deviations below and above the mean cancel exactly. Their sum must be 0, and it is indeed 0. A different result would indicate an addition or division error in the previous step.

  4. Lag 0: the observed variance

    At lag 0, each deviation is multiplied by itself: the squares are 4, 1, 1, 4 and 0 loans squared. There are n − 0 = 5 products, divided by n.

    γ^(0)=4+1+1+4+05=105=2 prêts²

    The partial sums are 5, 6, 10 and then 10. This number is the variance with divisor n; it will be the denominator of the autocorrelation. Check: it is positive or 0, as any sum of squares must be.

  5. Lag 1: multiply neighbouring deviations

    At lag 1, we pair each day with the next. There are n − 1 = 4 pairs: the last day has no following day in the series.

    (-2)×(-1)=2(-1)×(1)=-1(1)×(2)=2(2)×(0)=0

    Interpret the signs: days 1 and 2 are below the mean, so their product is positive; days 2 and 3 are on opposite sides, so it is negative; days 3 and 4 are above it, giving a positive product; the last deviation is 0, so the product is too. The partial sum goes through 1, 3 and then 3.

    γ^(1)=35=0.6 prêts²

    We divide by n = 5, even though there are only 4 products: this is the stated convention. The result is positive: over these 5 days, neighbouring days tended to be on the same side of the mean.

  6. Convert to autocorrelation and check

    To compare independently of the unit, we divide by the value at lag 0.

    ρ^(1)=γ^(1)γ^(0)=0.62=0.3

    The units of loans squared cancel: the result is dimensionless. Check the bound: with divisor n, the value stays between −1 and 1, because the sum of the lagged products cannot exceed the sum of squares (the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality). Here 0.3 satisfies this bound. Check the sign: 3 of the 4 products are positive or 0 and the sum is positive, as the diagram shows.

    Limitation: this number describes these 5 fictional days. It proves neither a cause, nor a lasting rule, nor even dependence in the series; 5 days are too few for that.

Explain it in your own words

Why does the divisor stay n when the sum at lag 1 contains only 4 products? And what happens to the number of products as the lag increases?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I can multiply the numbers of loans directly, without subtracting the mean.”

    Raw products mix the mean level with the way days vary around it: they remain large and positive even if days alternate. First subtract m, then multiply the deviations; check that their sum is 0.

    Repeat the deviation calculation

  • “At lag h, there are always n products to add.”

    Day t must have a day t + h within the series: n − h pairs remain, giving 4 at lag 1 and 3 at lag 2. The divisor n is a convention separate from the number of terms.

    Review the pairs in the diagram

  • “Positive autocorrelation proves that a day makes the next day rise.”

    It describes the observed series, rather than a mechanism. 5 fictional days, a single mean and no trend removed cannot establish a cause or a reliable forecast. Keep this wording: “over these days, neighbours tended to be on the same side of the mean”.

    Review the limits of interpretation

Hint 2

Pair day t with day t + h and count the values of t that are still possible.

Compare with the solution

There are n − 2 = 3 pairs: days 1 and 3, 2 and 4, 3 and 5. The products are -2, -2 and 0; their sum is -4 after 2 terms, then -4.

The divisor stays n = 5, rather than 3. So γ̂(2) = -4 / 5 = -0.8 loans squared. The sign is negative: over these days, 2 days with a day in between tended to be on opposite sides of the mean. This finding is limited to this short series.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the deviations already calculated, without recalculating the mean.
  • Count 3 products and divide by 5.
  • State the negative sign and the unit of loans squared.

The sum stays 3. The number of pairs is n − 1 = 4. The variant gives 3 / 4 = 0.75 loans squared, compared with 0.60 with divisor n.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

Count in half-loans

In a fictional variant, each observation is multiplied by 2 because the register now counts in half-loans. Without working through the whole series again, give the autocovariance at lags 0 and 1, then the autocorrelation at lag 1, for the transformed series. State the units.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

What happens to the mean, then to each deviation?

Hint 2

By what number is a product of two deviations multiplied? And what happens to a quotient of two numbers multiplied by the same factor?

Compare with the solution

The mean is multiplied by 2, so each deviation is too. A product of two deviations is multiplied by 2 × 2 = 4. Therefore γ̂(0) = 4 × 2 = 8 and γ̂(1) = 4 × 0.6 = 2.4, in half-loans squared.

Autocorrelation divides two numbers multiplied by the same factor 4: it stays 0.3, which is dimensionless. A change of scale therefore changes the autocovariances but not the autocorrelation.

Check your reasoning

  • Multiply the autocovariances by 4, rather than by 2.
  • Write the unit as half-loans squared.
  • Conclude that the autocorrelation stays 0.3, which is dimensionless.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does autocovariance at lag h measure?

The average of the products of a day’s deviation from the mean and that of the day h days later, using divisor n as chosen here. Its unit is the unit of the series, squared.

Recall 2At lag h, how many products do we add, and what do we divide by?

We add n − h products, because the last days have no partner. Here we divide by n, a convention that must be stated.

Recall 3What does the sign of a product of two deviations mean?

Positive: the two days are on the same side of the mean. Negative: they are on opposite sides. 0: at least one deviation is 0.

Recall 4How do we convert to autocorrelation, and what is its unit?

We divide the autocovariance by the value at lag 0. The result is dimensionless and, with divisor n, stays between minus 1 and 1.

Recall 5What does such a number calculated over a few days fail to show?

It establishes neither a cause, nor a lasting rule, nor dependence. It describes the observed series, subject to regularly spaced dates and a single mean; trend and seasonality are not addressed.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Check that the dates are regularly spaced; set a single mean; calculate the deviations and check that they cancel; for each lag h, multiply the deviations of days h days apart, add the n − h products and divide by n; divide by the value at lag 0 to obtain the autocorrelation; state the units, the divisor convention and the limits of interpretation.
