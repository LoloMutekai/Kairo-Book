Level 4 · C234·M2

Kalman filter

Your goalConstruct the Kalman recursion, justify its conditional exactness in a linear Gaussian model, and identify information counted twice.

Before you start
c233-m5 : verification theorem · c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c227-m5 : Ito-Tanaka · c234-m1 : state space

✿01 See the idea

A small cart moves along a rail marked with a scale. The previous estimate placed it 10 m from the origin. Its control input now specifies a displacement of 2 m, but the wheels may slip. An optical sensor reports 15 m: should we trust it completely or keep the expected position?

The filter keeps an estimated position and an error variance. It first propagates both using the control input and the noise in the displacement. It then compares the new reading with this prediction and moves through only part of their difference. The more uncertain the prediction is relative to the sensor, the larger this part is.

A variance describes dispersion under a probabilistic model; it is expressed in square metres, written m². It gives neither the actual error on this pass nor a probability that the sensor is correct. Positions are in metres, written m. All data in this scene are fictional and do not measure any real device.

The state space unit introduced the hidden and observed variables. Here we choose a linear Gaussian model: it allows us to replace the entire observation history with a mean–variance pair to calculate the distribution of the current position. This compression is exact under the assumptions we will state; it does not amount to guessing the true position.

The rule to remember

Predict the distribution of the current state, extract the new information from the reading, then keep the conditional mean and variance after that reading.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Locate the cart after its displacement

The previous estimate is a normal distribution with mean 10 m and variance 3 m². The commanded displacement is 2 m; the variance of the slip is Q = 1 m². The reading is z = 15 m and its noise has variance R = 2 m². We calculate a complete update, then check the mean and variance using other forms.

  1. State the model and the available information

    k is the dimensionless index of the current step. Xk is the hidden random position, Zk the random reading; x and z denote their possible or observed values. The discrete model is as follows; the index k − 1 denotes the previous step.

    Xk=aXk−1+uk+Wk
    Zk=Xk+Vk

    a = 1 is the dimensionless transition factor and uk = 2 m is the known displacement. Wk represents the unpredicted slip, with mean 0 m and variance Q; Vk represents the optical error, with mean 0 m and variance R. These noises are Gaussian, independent across steps, across noise families, and of the Gaussian initial state. Q is nonnegative and R strictly positive; the control input is known before the reading and the model has no added bias.

    I⁻ denotes the information from previous readings and known control inputs; I⁺ adds the current reading. E[· | I] means the conditional mean given I and Var(· | I) its variance. We write m⁻ = E[Xk | I⁻], P⁻ = Var(Xk | I⁻), then m⁺ and P⁺ after adding Zk. The superscript signs denote before and after the reading. m is a position in m; P, Q and R are in m². The notation N(m, P) denotes a normal distribution parameterized by its mean and variance, rather than its standard deviation.

  2. Propagate the distribution before looking at the sensor

    Let m and P denote the estimate from the previous step. The known control input creates no dispersion. The noise with mean 0 does not shift the mean but adds its variance; its independence makes its covariance with the previous error 0.

    m−=am+u;P−=a2P+Q

    Propagated mean: 1 × 10 = 10 m; with the control input, 10 + 2 = 12 m. For the error, the factor is squared: 1 × 1 = 1, then 1 × 3 = 3 m². Finally, P⁻ = 3 + 1 = 4 m². Thus Xk given I⁻ follows N(12, 4), with respective units m and m².

    The square of a comes from the squared error in the variance. Q is not a displacement to add to m⁻. Without a new reading, this predicted estimate would be the only estimate available.

  3. Isolate the new information in the reading

    The random innovation ν is Zk − m⁻; its realization is z − m⁻ = 15 − 12 = 3 m. Its conditional variance S adds P⁻ and R because the prediction error and the optical noise are independent. Cov denotes covariance: the mean of the product of deviations from the means.

    S=P−+R;K=P−S

    S = 4 + 2 = 6 m² is strictly positive. The gain K is 4/6 = 2/3, or approximately 0.6667, with no unit. The sensor has a smaller variance than the prediction, so it receives the larger weight here. Changing z alone would change the innovation, but not this gain.

    Before seeing the reading, the innovation has a conditional mean of 0 under this model. This does not require its realization to be 0: the current difference is indeed 3 m.

  4. Justify the gain using a Gaussian projection

    Condition on I⁻ and omit the time index. The prediction error e = X − m⁻ has mean 0 and variance P⁻. Then ν = e + V. For an update with coefficient g, the remaining error is e − gν = (1 − g)e − gV. e and V are independent. The error variance J(g), in m², is therefore:

    J(g)=(1−g)2P−+g2R

    Expanding and then completing the square, with S = P⁻ + R and K = P⁻/S, gives:

    J(g)=S(g−K)2+P−RS

    Since S is positive, the unique minimum is g = K. This calculation is enough to choose the best linear update with these second moments. To prove more, write ε = e − Kν. Since Cov(e, ν | I⁻) = P⁻, we have Cov(ε, ν | I⁻) = P⁻ − KS = 0 m². ε and ν are jointly Gaussian: this 0 covariance implies their conditional independence. Thus E[ε | I⁻, ν] = 0 m, and m⁻ + Kν is exactly E[X | I⁺].

    This is Gaussian closure: the conditional distribution remains normal and the recursion keeps only its parameters. Without Gaussianity, orthogonality alone gives neither this independence nor the exact conditional mean; the preceding linear minimization remains justified under its own assumptions.

  5. Calculate the updated position and check it

    m+=m−+K(z−m−)

    Apply K to the difference, then add this displacement to the prediction: (2/3) × 3 = 2 m; 12 + 2 = 14 m. This is the mean of the distribution after incorporating the sensor, rather than an additional observation of the rail.

    Another approach: m⁺ = (1 − K)m⁻ + Kz. The complement is 1/3; the contributions are (1/3) × 12 = 4 m and (2/3) × 15 = 10 m. Their sum is 14 m, as before. The weights sum to 1 and are positive here; 14 m does lie between 12 m and 15 m.

  6. Calculate the variance and check it in two ways

    At the optimal gain, J(K) gives the conditional variance after the reading. The short form is P⁺ = (1 − K)P⁻, giving (1/3) × 4 = 4/3 m², or approximately 1.3333 m². The expanded form of J, called the scalar Joseph form here, provides a first check without this simplification.

    P+=(1−K)2P−+K2R

    The squared weights are 1/9 and 4/9. Their variance contributions are 4/9 m² and 8/9 m²; the sum gives 4/3 m² again. Squaring the weights distinguishes this calculation from a weighted average of the variances.

    A second check uses precisions: for strictly positive P⁻ and R, precision is the inverse of variance. Independent precisions add. Converting back to variance gives P⁺ = P⁻R/(P⁻ + R): the numerator is 8 m⁴ and the denominator 6 m², giving 4/3 m². This quantity is positive and smaller than each of the variances 4 m² and 2 m².

    The final distribution is N(14, 4/3), with mean in m and variance in m². Its variance is independent of the particular value z in this model; it depends on the covariances and coefficients. Its decrease does not measure the actual error of the cart, whose exact position we do not know.

  7. Read the same recursion in matrix form

    For several coordinates of the same cart, X becomes a vector of size n and Z an observation of size d. The model is Xk = F times the previous X + uk + Wk, Zk = HXk + Vk. F transforms the state, H describes the sensor, and u is a known control input already expressed as a state displacement. The Gaussian and independence assumptions remain those of the scalar calculation.

    n and d count the components of the state and the reading. m and u are columns of size n; F, P and Q are n × n; H is d × n; R and S are d × d; K is n × d. The transpose T exchanges rows and columns. P and Q are symmetric positive semidefinite covariance matrices: every linear combination of errors has a nonnegative variance. R is positive definite: this variance is strictly positive for every nonzero combination of reading errors, which ensures that S is invertible. A covariance relates the errors of two components, in the product of their units. For coordinates and readings all in metres, P, Q, R and S have entries in m²; F, H and K are dimensionless.

    m−=Fm+u;P−=FPFT+Q
    ν=Z−Hm−;S=HP−HT+R
    K=P−HTS−1;m+=m−+Kν
    P+=P−−KSKT

    The conditional covariance between the predicted error and ν is P⁻Hᵀ. Choosing K to make the residual covariance 0 therefore reproduces the scalar proof. The inverse of S is a matrix inverse; K is usually calculated by solving the system SKᵀ = HP⁻, rather than by inverting entries separately. The resulting covariance recursion is a Riccati recursion. For our rail, n = d = 1, F = 1 and H = 1: it gives exactly the preceding calculation.

  8. Complete the step without adding fictitious information

    Pass (14 m; 4/3 m²) to the next step. With the same control input and the same Q, the next predicted estimate will be (16 m; 7/3 m²). It can only be updated after a new reading; the second exercise performs this step.

    The filter uses the readings available up to the current step. Recalculating a past position using future readings would be smoothing and would require a different calculation. Feeding a copy of the current reading back in does not create new information either: the last exercise checks this point.

    Innovations have a conditional mean of 0 under the correct model; their orthogonality to past observations provides a diagnostic to study over a sequence. The single difference of 3 m in our example is not enough to establish a bias, independence, or the validity of Q and R.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I can put a standard deviation in R and add the weights to update P.”

    R is a variance in m². The error weights are squared to calculate J(g); the short form is obtained only after simplification at the optimal gain. Revisit the contributions 4/9 m² and 8/9 m² in the Joseph check.

    Revisit the variance calculation and its units

  • “A 0 covariance always proves that the update is the exact conditional mean.”

    0 covariance expresses orthogonality. Here, the joint Gaussian distribution is what allows us to infer conditional independence of the residual and the innovation. Removing this assumption removes this step of the proof, while the minimization of J(g) remains justified under the stated second moments.

    Revisit the projection and Gaussian closure

  • “If the same packet arrives again, I update again: the variance decreases, so I am learning.”

    A copy carries the same noise and the same information. Treating it as an independent reading would count the precision twice. A decrease calculated under this false independence establishes no actual improvement.

    Study the duplicated packet in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Application and comparison

A noisier sensor

Start again from the prediction (12 m; 4 m²), with the reading 15 m. Replace only R with 8 m². Predict the direction of the displacement, then calculate the gain, updated mean, and variance. Compare with the estimate in the example.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The innovation does not change. The denominator P⁻ + R increases.

Hint 2

Calculate the gain exactly, then apply it to 3 m. The variance uses the complement of the gain.

Compare with the solution

The sensor receives less weight: the mean should stay closer to 12 m. S = 4 + 8 = 12 m²; K = 4/12 = 1/3.

The displacement is (1/3) × 3 = 1 m; the mean becomes 13 m. The complement of the gain is 2/3, so P⁺ = (2/3) × 4 = 8/3 m², or approximately 2.6667 m².

This variance is larger than 4/3 m²: the reading provides less information than in the example. It remains smaller than 4 m² under the model assumptions. The reading z is not enough to determine the gain: its noise variance also matters.

Check your reasoning

  • The direction of the change is stated before the update.
  • Only R changes; the innovation and prediction are retained.
  • The gain is dimensionless, the mean is in m, and the variance is in m².

Exercise 2 · Exact recursion on paper

A new reading at the next step

Restore R = 2 m² and start again from the original updated estimate (14 m; 4/3 m²). The control input is again 2 m, a = 1, Q = 1 m², and a new independent reading is 14 m. Calculate the entire step without rounding. Why must the mean move back relative to the new prediction?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First propagate the updated mean and its variance, then subtract the new prediction from the new reading.

Hint 2

Keep fractions in the gain and the update; round only the final pair.

Compare with the solution

The prediction is 14 + 2 = 16 m. Its variance is 4/3 + 1 = 7/3 m². The innovation is 14 − 16 = -2 m: it is negative, so the update moves towards smaller positions.

The innovation variance is 7/3 + 2 = 13/3 m²; the exact gain is (7/3)/(13/3) = 7/13. The displacement is (7/13) × (-2) = -14/13 m.

The updated mean is 16 + (-14/13) = 194/13 m, or approximately 14.9231 m. The complement is 6/13; P⁺ = (6/13) × (7/3) = 14/13 m², or approximately 1.0769 m².

Independent check: combining the prediction and reading using precisions gives the mean (R m⁻ + P⁻z)/(P⁻ + R), and the variance P⁻R/(P⁻ + R). Substituting the data from the new step gives the same pair. The mean stays between 14 m and 16 m; the gain has been recalculated, without automatically reusing 2/3.

Check your reasoning

  • The new step starts from both updated results, without resetting P.
  • The negative innovation leads to a negative displacement.
  • Exact results precede decimal approximations for reading.
  • A second form checks both final results.

Exercise 3 · Proof and model diagnosis

Two packets, a single piece of information

At the step in the original example, the connection transmits the same optical packet 2 times: Z and its copy Z′ = Z. The packets refer to exactly the same instant, with no new displacement. After obtaining (14 m; 4/3 m²) with the first, should you change the estimate with the second? Justify your answer using the conditioning information, then write the covariance of the two innovations calculated from the same initial prediction.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Does knowing (Z, Z) allow you to distinguish more situations than knowing Z?

Hint 2

The two innovations are identical. Their cross covariance therefore equals their variance S, rather than 0.

Compare with the solution

Adding a copy of Z to information that already contains Z does not change that information. The conditional distribution therefore remains N(14, 4/3), with mean in m and variance in m². There is no new prediction over time to perform either.

Given I⁻, both innovations equal ν. Their covariance matrix is as follows; each entry is S = 6 m².

[6666]

Its determinant is S × S − S × S = 0 m⁴: it is singular. The optical noises also have cross covariance R because the copy contains exactly the same noise V. Assuming a diagonal covariance matrix for independent noises would change the model and create fictitious precision. The correct repair in this situation is to discard the duplicate and keep the first estimate.

Two numerically equal readings taken independently would be a different experiment: equality of the numbers is not enough to prove duplication. Here, it is the identity of the packet and the noise that justifies the absence of new information.

Check your reasoning

  • The estimate remains (14 m; 4/3 m²), without adding Q.
  • The explanation concerns information already known, rather than just equality of values.
  • The cross covariances are S for the innovations and R for the noises.
  • Singularity is linked to duplication; no entrywise inversion is attempted.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the cart prediction carry, and why do the control input and Q act differently?

It carries a mean m⁻ = am + u and a variance obtained by multiplying P by the square of a, then adding Q. The known control input shifts the mean without creating dispersion; the independent noise with mean 0 adds its variance without shifting the mean.

Recall 2Which covariance does the gain make 0, and which assumption makes the updated mean exact?

With e = X − m⁻ and ν = Z − m⁻, the gain K = P⁻/S makes Cov(e − Kν, ν | I⁻) 0. The joint Gaussian distribution turns this orthogonality into conditional independence; the residual retains mean 0 after observing ν. The updated mean is then exactly the conditional mean.

Recall 3How can you check the updated variance using an approach other than its short form?

Square the weights (1 − K) and K, multiply them by P⁻ and R respectively, then add; or invert the sum of precisions. With the optimal scalar gain, these calculations give P⁻R/(P⁻ + R) again. A small variance remains a consequence of the model, without proving that the actual error is small.

Recall 4In the matrix gain, what do H, S, and the dimensions of K represent?

H transforms a state of size n into an observation of size d. S = HP⁻Hᵀ + R is the innovation covariance of size d × d. K, of size n × d, transforms this innovation into a state update. The inverse is a matrix inverse; the units of the gain connect those of the readings to those of the state.

Recall 5Why must a copy of the same packet leave the variance unchanged?

The copy adds nothing to the information already contained in the original reading and carries the same noise. Treating it as independent amounts to falsely changing the observation model. The conditional distribution must remain unchanged.
