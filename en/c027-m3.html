Identities

Your goalDerive the circle identity, calculate a missing component, and justify its sign and the divisions that are allowed.

Before you start
c005-m5 : areas and volumes · c006-m5 : composition · c027-m1 : unit circle · c027-m2 : trigonometric functions

✿01 See the idea

A rigid arm pivots in a plane about its endpoint O. It is 5 m long. Its other endpoint P is 3 m to the right of O, above the horizontal. What is its height? There is no need to know the angle first: the rigidity of the arm already links the horizontal displacement and the height.

The arm can rotate, but its endpoint stays on the same circle. The right triangle drawn beneath the arm changes shape; the sum of the squares of its horizontal and vertical sides remains equal to the square of the length of the arm. This is the Pythagorean theorem. Dividing these sides by the length of the arm gives the unit circle from the previous units.

Cosine describes the horizontal component divided by the length; sine describes the vertical component divided by that same length. These ratios have no units. Their signs can change as the arm rotates, but their squares still add up to one.

An identity is an equality that is true for every value in its domain. Our diagram explains why a relationship remains true as the angle changes; a single numerical calculation would not prove it. The scene and lengths are fictional and assumed to be exact: they are not readings from a real mechanism.

The rule to remember

On the unit circle, the squares of cosine and sine add up to one. To find a component, its square alone is not enough: the position of the arm determines its sign.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Find the height without finding the angle

The arm of length L = 5 m has a horizontal component x = 3 m and is above the pivot. Let us calculate its vertical component y, then a ratio useful to the geometric controller for the arm. All the data are exact in this fictional model.

  1. Define the symbols and the domain

    L is the positive length of the arm, and x and y are the signed coordinates of P relative to O, in metres. x is positive to the right and y is positive upwards. The plane is Euclidean: the axes are perpendicular and use the same scale. The arm is assumed to be rigid and to have no thickness.

    θ, read as “theta”, is the directed angle from the positive horizontal axis, measured anticlockwise and expressed in radians. cos θ and sin θ denote the cosine and sine of this angle, with no units: x = L × cos θ and y = L × sin θ. The angle is real; its numerical value is not needed here.

    In the following notation, the square applies to the value of sine: calculate the sine, then multiply the result by itself. It does not apply to the angle θ. The same convention applies to cosine and tangent.

    sin2θ=(sinθ)2

  2. Derive the identity before using it

    The Pythagorean theorem gives the following relationship. All three terms are in square metres. If a coordinate is negative, its square is still the square of the length of the corresponding side. The relationship therefore holds for every direction of the arm; on the axes, one side has length 0 and the same equality remains true.

    x2+y2=L2

    Since L is strictly positive, divide each term by the square of L. The units cancel: x/L is the cosine and y/L is the sine. The square of the quotient x/L is the square of x divided by the square of L, and the same holds for y/L.

    (xL)2+(yL)2=1
    cos2θ+sin2θ=1

    No particular angle was chosen in this reasoning: this proves the identity for every real angle. The calculation that follows will check a particular position.

  3. Isolate the square of the missing component

    The horizontal component gives cos θ = x/L = 3/5 = 0.60, with no units. Square this ratio: the square of cos θ = 9/25. This is not the square of the angle.

    Subtract the square of cosine from both sides of the identity to isolate the square of sine.

    sin2θ=1−925=1625

    The result 16/25 is positive and less than 1, as expected for the square of a coordinate on the unit circle. It does not yet give the sign of sine.

  4. Choose the sign and return to metres

    The square root of a positive number is its unique nonnegative root. Here, the square root of 16/25 is 4/5, since 4 × 4 = 16 and 5 × 5 = 25. But the equation specifying the square also allows the opposite value, -4/5: squaring removes the sign.

    The scene specifies that P is above O. Therefore y is positive; since L is positive, sin θ is positive too. We choose sin θ = 4/5 = 0.80, with no units.

    y=L×sinθ=5×45=4 m

    Multiplying the ratio with no units by L gives a length again. Below the pivot, the sign would have been negative, with the same square.

  5. Check using lengths

    Start again from the lengths in metres, without reusing the subtraction of ratios: 3 × 3 + 4 × 4 = 9 + 16 = 25 square metres. The square of the length of the arm is indeed 25 square metres. The positive vertical component is also less than the length of the arm.

    For the ratios, 9/25 + 16/25 = 1. These checks confirm our calculation under the stated assumptions; they do not replace the proof for all angles or validate a real arm.

  6. Derive an identity and keep its condition

    The controller also needs the vertical/horizontal ratio. When cos θ differs from 0, tangent is defined by tan θ = sin θ / cos θ = y/x; it has no units. In our position, it is 4/5 divided by 3/5, giving 4/3. This can be checked directly using 4 m / 3 m.

    Divide every term of the circle identity by the square of cos θ, which is then nonzero. The first quotient becomes 1, and the quotient of the squares of sine and cosine becomes the square of their quotient.

    1+tan2θ=1cos2θ si cos θ ≠ 0

    Exact check: the left side is 1 + 16/9 = 25/9; the right side is 1 / (9/25) = 25/9. If the arm is vertical, cos θ is 0: this division and tangent are no longer defined, while the circle identity remains valid.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The square written on the name sine applies to the angle.”

    Rewrite the square of (sin θ) with parentheses. The square applies to the value of the function, rather than its input. With our arm, it is the ratio 4/5 that is multiplied by itself to obtain 16/25.

    Reread the symbols and the step that isolates the square

  • “The root is positive, so the height is always positive.”

    The square gives the magnitude, but the values 4/5 and -4/5 have the same square. Choose the sign from the position above or below the pivot, then multiply by the positive length.

    Try placing the arm below the horizontal

  • “An identity allows division without checking the denominator.”

    Write the condition cos θ ≠ 0 before dividing by its square. The form with tangent has a more restricted domain than the circle identity; simplifying does not make division by 0 possible.

    Revisit the derivation with tangent

Exercise 1 · Check a claim

The squares the controller left out

The arm stays in the position from the worked example: cos θ = 3/5 and sin θ = 4/5. A note claims that cos θ + sin θ = 1 for every angle. Calculate this sum, correct the formula, and explain what can be concluded from this single test.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The ratios have the same denominator. Calculate their sum before introducing any squares.

Hint 2

One counterexample is enough to refute “for every angle”; an equality that holds at one point does not prove an identity.

Compare with the solution

Without squares, the sum is 3/5 + 4/5 = 7/5, or 1.40, with no units. It is not equal to 1. This position is therefore a counterexample that refutes the general claim.

The corrected formula adds the squares of cosine and sine to obtain 1. Here, its two sides are equal, because 9/25 + 16/25 = 1. Its validity for all angles comes from the reasoning about the circle, rather than this particular calculation.

Check your reasoning

  • I obtain 7/5 before adding any squares.
  • I distinguish the numerical counterexample from the general geometric proof.
  • I state that the sum of the ratios has no units.

Exercise 2 · Find and interpret

The same arm below the horizontal

The 5 m arm is now below the pivot, still 3 m to the right of O. Starting again from the identity, find sin θ and y. Explain why the squares can remain the same when the position changes.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Cosine is still x/L. The identity first gives the square of sin θ.

Hint 2

Below the pivot, y is negative. The length L remains positive.

Compare with the solution

We still have cos θ = 3/5, so the square of sin θ = 1 − 9/25 = 16/25. The candidates are 4/5 and -4/5. Below the pivot, we choose sin θ = -4/5 = -0.80, with no units.

We obtain y = 5 × (-4/5) = -4 m. Checking gives (-4/5) × (-4/5) = 16/25, then 9/25 + 16/25 = 1. The sign has changed, but the point remains on the circle with the same radius.

Without the information “below the pivot”, both signs would be possible: knowing only the horizontal displacement and the length would not determine a unique signed height.

Check your reasoning

  • I calculate the square before choosing the sign.
  • I justify the negative sign using the position, without making L negative.
  • I give a height in metres and check the squares.

Exercise 3 · Justify a transformation

Can the controller use tangent everywhere?

Reconstruct the derivation of the identity below, stating its domain, then check it in the initial position of the arm. The arm is then placed vertically upwards: cos θ = 0 and sin θ = 1. Which equalities and operations remain valid?

1+tan2θ=1cos2θ
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Divide each term of the circle identity by the square of cos θ, after stating the condition.

Hint 2

An expression that requires division by 0 has no real value. Return to the formula before division.

Compare with the solution

Under the condition cos θ ≠ 0, the square of cosine divided by itself is 1. The square of sine divided by the square of cosine is the square of the quotient sin θ/cos θ, hence the square of tangent. This gives the required identity only on this domain.

In the initial position, tan θ = 4/3. On the left: 1 + 16/9 = 25/9. On the right: 1/(9/25) = 25/9. The fractions are exact and both sides have no units.

In the vertical upward position, the circle identity gives 0 × 0 + 1 × 1 = 0 + 1 = 1. It remains true. However, tan θ would require 1/0, and the right side would require division by the square of cos θ = 0. These expressions are undefined; they are assigned neither 0 nor an enormous number. The arm still has a perfectly well-defined geometric position.

Check your reasoning

  • I state the condition before any division and transform all the terms.
  • I check both sides using the exact fractions.
  • I distinguish an undefined formula from an impossible physical position.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What distinguishes an identity from an equality checked in a single position?

An identity is true for every value in its domain. For the circle, the Pythagorean theorem followed by division by the square of L proves that the squares of cosine and sine add up to 1 for every real angle. A single calculation provides only a particular check.

Recall 2What calculation does the square of sin θ denote, and what calculation does it not denote?

It denotes the square of (sin θ): calculate the sine, then multiply it by itself. It does not denote the sine of the square of θ, where the angle would be squared before taking the sine.

Recall 3The square of sine is known. What information is missing to find the signed height of the arm?

The sign of sine, given here by the position above or below the pivot. Then y = L × sin θ, with L positive in metres and sine having no units. If the square is 0, both signs give the same 0 value.

Recall 4Under what condition can the identity with tangent be derived from the circle identity?

We need cos θ ≠ 0 to divide by the square of cosine and define tan θ = sin θ/cos θ. Then 1 plus the square of tangent equals 1 divided by the square of cosine. The circle identity still holds for all real angles.
