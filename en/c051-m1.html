Level 2 · C051·M1

Floating-point numbers

Your goalRepresent a duration with finite precision, track each rounding step in a sum and check the resulting error.

Before you start
c046-m5 : approximation error · c050-m5 : conditioning · c015-m5 : diagnosis · c036-m5 : annotated graphs

✿01 See the idea

A timer adds a long phase of 1 s, then 2 short phases of 0.125 s each. On paper, their total duration is 1.250 s. Yet a calculator that keeps very few digits can stay at 1.000 s after each addition. Where did the short durations go?

Imagine a ruler on which only certain marks can be stored. Between 1.000 s and 1.250 s, our ruler has no intermediate mark. The exact sum 1.125 s falls halfway between them; a neighbor must be chosen for storage. Some information has been lost before the next addition.

A floating-point number, or float, stores significant digits and a scale. The scale moves the decimal point; the number of digits kept remains limited. The marks are therefore farther apart for large values. Rounding means choosing a representable value, rather than just shortening its display.

The scene, durations and precision are fictional. We will use 3 significant binary digits to work through the rounding by hand. This reduced format is not the usual format of a Python program; the numerical results below belong to our model. The data represent exact durations, with no measurement noise.

The rule to remember

Distinguish the exact number, its representation and the result of each rounded operation. A short duration can be representable on its own and disappear when added to a longer duration.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Recover the durations lost by the timer

Let a = 1 s be the long phase and b = c = 1/8 s = 0.125 s the short phases. We want to compare successive additions with grouping the short phases. All operations use the same reduced format and the same rounding rule.

  1. Define the storage rule exactly

    A binary digit, called a bit, is 0 or 1. The precision p = 3 counts all significant digits, including the first nonzero digit. For a nonzero value, our format stores a sign S, an integer significand M and an integer exponent e. S is 0 for a positive number and 1 for a negative number. M is between 4 and 7 inclusive: these are the integers whose binary representation uses exactly 3 bits.

    v=(−1)S×M×2e

    v denotes the numerical value of the duration expressed in seconds; S, M, e and p are dimensionless. The factor 2 is the binary base. 0 is represented separately. The exponent range is assumed to be sufficient for this scene. Real formats also have exponent limits: they can encounter values that are too large or too small, cases we do not use here.

    fl(x) denotes rounding x to the nearest representable number. When the distances are equal, choose the neighbor whose M is even. This is the ties-to-even rule: it concerns the last significant digit, rather than the parity of the duration expressed in seconds. Finally, x ⊕ y means fl(x + y): add exactly, then round once.

  2. Check the inputs and spacings

    The numerical value of a can be written as 4 × 2^(-2) = 1; that of b as 4 × 2^(-5) = 1/8. The inputs are therefore exactly representable: no conversion error explains the result that follows.

    From 1 s inclusive to 2 s exclusive, e = -2. Increasing M by one adds 1/4 s = 0.250 s: this is the spacing h between neighbors. The neighbor immediately above a is 5 × 2^(-2) s = 1.250 s. Around b, toward larger values, the spacing is only 1/32 s = 0.03125 s.

    This shift of scale explains the word floating. From 2 s onward, the spacing toward larger values becomes 0.500 s. Precision therefore does not mean that the absolute gap between neighbors stays constant.

  3. Keep an exact reference before rounding

    The phases follow one another without overlap, so their durations add together. Expressing everything in eighths of a second gives a total numerator of 8 + 1 + 1 = 10. The exact duration t is therefore 10/8 s = 5/4 s = 1.250 s. This fraction serves as an independent check; it does not come from the result displayed by the timer.

    t=a+b+c=1+18+18=54 s

  4. Perform the first addition and decide how to round

    Before rounding, a + b = 1.125 s. The lower neighbor is 1.000 s and the upper neighbor is 1.250 s. The distances are 1.125 − 1.000 = 0.125 s and 1.250 − 1.125 = 0.125 s. There is a tie.

    The neighbors have the same exponent, with M = 4 below and M = 5 above. The first M is even: a ⊕ b = 1.000 s. The short phase has been absorbed by rounding, although it is positive and exactly representable on its own. The stored result no longer contains a hidden remainder of 0.125 s.

  5. Start from the number actually stored

    The next addition starts from 1.000 s. Its exact sum with c is 1.125 s, the same midpoint as before. The same tie-breaking rule again gives 1.000 s. Let r denote this final result: r = (a ⊕ b) ⊕ c.

    The absolute error E is the distance from the exact total: E = |r − t| = 0.250 s. The relative error R = E/|t| is 1/5 = 0.20, with no unit. It is defined here because t is nonzero. Check by another route: each addition removed 0.125 s; together they removed 2 × 0.125 s = 0.250 s.

    A normal representation keeps a nonzero first significant digit with an exponent in the allowed range, as in our notation using M. For a single rounding step in this range, the relative error can be bounded by u = 2^(−p). Here u = 1/8. The first rounding gives 1/9 ≈ 0.1111, less than u. Yet the final relative error 0.20 exceeds u: a bound for a single rounding step is not an automatic bound for a calculation with several operations.

  6. Change the intermediate values, then check

    First calculate b ⊕ c. The sum 0.125 + 0.125 = 0.250 s is representable, so it is kept exactly. Adding it to a then gives 1.000 + 0.250 = 1.250 s, which is also representable.

    (a⊕b)⊕c=1.000 s≠a⊕(b⊕c)=1.250 s

    Floating-point addition is therefore not associative: moving the parentheses can change the result. Here grouping recovers t exactly, with a difference of 0 s. This check relies on the representable intermediate values in this scene. It does not prove that grouping small values always gives an exact result.

  7. Distinguish numerical checks from measurement accuracy

    Suppose the timer's fictional specification allows an absolute difference of τ = 1/16 s = 0.0625 s for this calculation. For a candidate value y, the check passes when |y − t| ≤ τ. y and t are durations in seconds; τ is an absolute tolerance in the same unit.

    The successive calculation fails: 0.2500 s > 0.0625 s. Grouping passes: 0 s ≤ 0.0625 s. A tolerance comes from the required accuracy; widening it to accept a result does not fix the calculation. In a real test, the reference t would also need to be justified. Our checks concern only exact fictional numbers and do not establish a timer's physical accuracy.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Each duration is exact, so their sum will be exact too.”

    The set of representable values is not closed under exact addition. Here the inputs are representable, but 1.125 s is not. Fix the reasoning by checking each intermediate value before storing it.

    Review the neighbors and the first rounding step

  • “Parentheses never change an addition.”

    This property holds for exact addition, but each ⊕ includes rounding. Compare the intermediate values 1.125 s and 0.250 s, then the results 1.000 s and 1.250 s.

    Compare the panels for the two groupings

  • “I can increase the tolerance until the check passes.”

    That changes the required accuracy without recovering the lost durations. Keep the justified tolerance, measure the difference from an independent reference and change the calculation method if it fails.

Exercise 2 · Calculate input rounding

The short phase changes duration

This time, the same timer receives a short phase of exactly d = 0.1 s. Its representable neighbors are 3/32 s and 7/64 s. Calculate the exact distances, choose fl(d), then give the absolute input error. This exercise concerns only the storage of this phase.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The neighbors can be written as 6 × 2^(-6) and 7 × 2^(-6). Compare d with the lower neighbor and the upper neighbor with d.

Hint 2

Keep exact fractions to compare the distances; there will be no tie to break.

Compare with the solution

The lower neighbor is 0.09375 s and the upper neighbor is 0.109375 s. Distance downward: 1/10 − 3/32 = 1/160 s = 0.00625 s. Distance upward: 7/64 − 1/10 = 3/320 s = 0.009375 s.

The downward distance is smaller. Therefore fl(d) = 3/32 s; the absolute input error is 1/160 s. The ties-to-even rule does not come into play. This error exists before any addition to the total.

A fraction in lowest terms has a finite binary representation only if its denominator is a power of 2. The denominator 10 also contains the factor 5: the value 1/10 has no finite binary representation. Even a finite binary representation must still fit within the p available digits to be exactly representable.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does a float store, and why does the spacing change with magnitude?

A sign, a limited number of significant digits and an exponent that sets their scale. The same change in the last digit represents a larger absolute gap when the scale increases. Precision is a number of significant digits, rather than a fixed number of decimal places after the decimal point.

Recall 2How do you determine fl(x) in the model, including at the midpoint between two neighbors?

Compare the exact distances to the representable neighbors. Choose the nearest; if there is a tie, choose the one whose integer significand M is even. This tie-breaking rule does not mean choosing an even whole number of seconds.

Recall 3Why do our two groupings give different totals when all the inputs are exact?

Each addition of a short phase to a gives an intermediate sum that is not representable and is rounded to a. Grouping the short phases produces a representable intermediate value, then a representable total. Intermediate rounding makes floating-point addition non-associative.

Recall 4How do you distinguish absolute error, relative error and a bound for a single rounding step?

With r calculated and t exact, the absolute error is |r − t| in the unit of the calculation. The relative error is the same difference divided by |t|, with no unit, if t is nonzero. The bound u for a single rounding step does not automatically bound the final error of several operations.

Recall 5What must be established for a tolerance check to be useful?

A justified reference, a tolerance based on the required accuracy and consistent units. Comparing the difference with this threshold checks the stated criterion; it replaces neither the reference nor the validation of a physical measurement.
