Working memory

✿01 See the idea

In a workshop, you hear: ‘Prepare 6 pouches for the blue tray.’ The instruction card is then hidden. Before you start, the person changes the request: ‘2 fewer pouches, and put them in the yellow tray.’ To act, you need to keep the initial number available, apply the change to it and remember the new destination. Repeating the last words you heard is not enough.

Here, working memory refers to the processes that temporarily keep information available and allow it to be used for the current task. Maintenance keeps the number accessible while the card is hidden. Manipulation transforms this number through the requested subtraction. Updating also replaces ‘blue’ with ‘yellow’ in the instruction to follow.

Think of a small work surface: what you need now must stay within reach while you modify it. This image describes a function; it does not locate a physical surface in the brain. Short-term memory emphasizes temporary maintenance; the concept of working memory includes using the information in an activity. Scientific models may define this distinction in different ways.

Availability is limited: following a new instruction can make the previous one harder to keep accessible. However, an interruption does not prove that everything has been erased. The content, task, knowledge and aids all matter; the number of pouches does not measure how many pieces of information a person can maintain.

All quantities and dialogue in this workshop are fictional. We will describe the necessary operations and check the requested result. We are measuring neither an individual's capacity nor a training effect. Knowledge retained and available for later reuse belongs to long-term memory; completing this instruction does not, by itself, demonstrate such retention.

The rule to remember

Keep what is needed, transform what changes, then check the whole current instruction: quantity and destination.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Reconstruct the instruction to follow in the workshop

The fictional request was 6 pouches for the blue tray. It becomes: 2 fewer pouches, yellow tray. The card is hidden before the answer. How many should you prepare, for which tray, and how can you check this result?

  1. Establish what is requested and what is assumed

    We are looking for a final instruction consisting of a quantity and a destination. We assume that the sentence was heard correctly, that ‘fewer’ refers to the initial quantity and that ‘yellow tray’ replaces ‘blue tray’. No pouches have been moved yet: the subtraction is a change to the request, not an observation of an action already completed.

    The card is not reread during this reconstruction. This condition specifies the need for temporary maintenance. It does not isolate working memory completely: understanding the sentence, knowing the words and knowing how to subtract also contribute to the answer.

  2. Name the content to maintain and to change

    N denotes the number initially requested, R the number of pouches to subtract from this request and F the final number requested. These quantities are expressed in pouches; they are whole-number counts, with a subtraction that does not exceed the initial count. B denotes the initial tray and C the final tray: these are categories, with no numerical unit.

    Our data are N = 6 pouches, R = 2 pouches, B = blue and C = yellow. The sign = means ‘has the value’. Keeping N available is maintenance; calculating F is manipulation; replacing B with C is updating. These words describe functions, not brain processes that we have observed separately.

  3. Subtract because the requested quantity decreases

    ‘Fewer’ calls for subtraction: start with N and subtract R. The sign − expresses this subtraction. Adding would give a larger quantity, contrary to the instruction.

    F=N−R=6−2=4 pochettes

    To redo the calculation in another way, represent each pouch with a tally mark. From the initial 6 marks, cross out the last 2 and count the uncrossed marks again: 4 remain. The drawing checks the count, but if it is used to answer, it too is an external aid.

  4. Replace the destination without losing the result

    The calculated quantity is 4 pouches. You need to maintain it while choosing C, the yellow tray. The complete answer is therefore ‘4 pouches in the yellow tray’. The blue tray was relevant before the change; it must no longer guide this sorting task.

    Answering ‘4 pouches in the blue tray’ would retain the correct calculation with an outdated destination. Answering ‘6 pouches in the yellow tray’ would update the destination while forgetting the subtraction. An instruction can be partially updated.

  5. Check the quantity and then the tray

    The inverse check adds the subtracted pouches back into the requested count. The sign + indicates this addition used to check the result:

    F+R=4+2=6 pochettes=N

    We recover the initial count. This check validates the subtraction; it cannot validate a colour. For the tray, we separately compare the proposed destination with the latest instruction: yellow. A correct numerical result does not remove the need to check the content.

  6. State exactly what this reconstruction shows

    We have established the expected answer from the assumptions and the calculation. We have not observed anyone carrying out the task. Even a future correct answer would need to be accompanied by its conditions: card visible or hidden, help received, any rewording of the instruction.

    An error could arise from a misheard sentence, incorrect subtraction or content that has become difficult to keep available. It is not enough to diagnose a memory impairment. Success with the card does not demonstrate maintenance without help; immediate success without the card does not yet demonstrate lasting learning.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculation and describe what must remain available, what is transformed and what is replaced. Then explain why the inverse check does not check the choice of tray.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Working memory is just repeating the sentence.”

    Repeating can help maintain content. Here you also need to subtract and replace the destination. Name the content being kept and the required changes separately, then restate the whole request.

    Identify maintenance, manipulation and updating again

  • “Since there are 6 pouches, memory contains 6 items.”

    The number denotes pouches, not mental slots. The request includes a quantity, a destination and a rule for changing them; their difficulty also depends on their organization and on knowledge. Return to the functional definition, without converting the count into a human capacity.

    Return to the work surface analogy

  • “I answered correctly while looking at the card, so I have mastered it.”

    First record the aid used. The card lets you retrieve the instruction without maintaining it solely in your head. Try again without support on another day; an assisted answer and a reading never validate an acquired level.

    Examine success with support in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Correct the calculation without losing the tray

In the workshop, the initial request is still 6 pouches for the blue tray, changed to 2 fewer pouches for the yellow tray. Someone proposes 8 pouches in the yellow tray. Which change have they correctly incorporated, which operation needs correcting and what complete answer do you expect?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare the destination and the direction of change in the number separately.

Hint 2

‘Fewer’ must decrease the initial count; find the result by crossing out tally marks.

Compare with the solution

The yellow tray matches the new destination. However, 8 is greater than the initial count: 6 + 2 = 8 pouches corresponds to addition. The request requires 6 − 2 = 4 pouches. The expected answer is ‘4 pouches in the yellow tray’.

The calculation needs correcting while retaining the correct destination. We can describe this error in the answer; it does not prove its mental cause. An explanation of the reasoning or a check of what ‘fewer’ means would provide additional information.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify the correctly updated destination.
  • Justify subtraction through the meaning of the instruction and give the unit.
  • Provide the complete answer without assigning a definite psychological cause to the error.

Exercise 2 · Independent reconstruction

Another change arrives

After reconstructing 4 pouches in the yellow tray, you hear: ‘Actually, add 1 pouch; keep the yellow tray.’ Nothing has been put away yet. Write the new request, then check it by starting again from the initial 6 pouches. Which information remains unchanged?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The new addition starts from the latest requested quantity, not the initial quantity.

Hint 2

To check from the initial count, compare the initial subtraction with the final addition.

Compare with the solution

The latest quantity was 4 pouches. Add 1 pouch to it: 4 + 1 = 5 pouches. The request becomes ‘5 pouches in the yellow tray’. The destination remains yellow; the quantity is updated.

Another route: subtracting 2 pouches and then adding 1 back amounts to subtracting 1 pouch in total. From the initial count: 6 − 1 = 5 pouches. Both routes give the same result. Here the net subtraction expresses only the overall change in the requests, with no pouches already moved.

Maintaining 4 as available allows you to apply the addition to it. Starting again from 6 without accounting for the subtraction would use an old state. This reasoning concerns the updating needed for the task, without claiming to measure how difficult it is for a person.

Check your reasoning

  • Start from the latest quantity and keep the yellow tray.
  • Present both the successive steps and the overall calculation, using the same unit.
  • Explain which old information could cause an incorrect update.

Exercise 3 · Evidence analysis

A correct answer, but under what conditions?

Return to the final request in the example, before the addition. A visible card reads ‘4 pouches, yellow tray’. The person reads it and prepares exactly that. Someone claims: ‘They maintained the instruction without help and learned it for the long term.’ What is observed, what remains to be checked and what next attempt would you propose?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Distinguish correct sorting from the way the instruction was available.

Hint 2

Specify the conditions of a later attempt before attributing a result to it.

Compare with the solution

The sorting matches the written instruction and the person consulted the card. We therefore observe correct performance with support. This does not establish that they would have maintained the instruction without help. Nor does it show that they would still be able to reconstruct it later.

To examine maintenance, we can have the person hear a variant of this workshop instruction, hide the card and ask them to restate the quantity and tray after the change. We need to check that the request was understood and record the help actually received. The result of this attempt remains unknown until it has taken place.

To examine what remains later, a new attempt is needed after a delay, without rereading beforehand, stating what the person is being asked to recall. This would be an additional observation, not a pure measure of working memory. Neither current success with support nor a single future success automatically awards a level.

Check your reasoning

  • Name the support and limit the finding to the sorting observed.
  • Separate temporary maintenance without a card from later retention.
  • Describe a next attempt and its conditions without inventing a result for it.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does working memory refer to in the workshop scene?

The processes that temporarily keep the instruction available and allow it to be used: keeping the initial count accessible, calculating the revised quantity and following the current destination.

Recall 2What counts as maintenance, manipulation and updating?

Keeping the initial number accessible is maintenance; subtracting the requested reduction from it is manipulation; replacing the blue tray with the yellow one updates the instruction. These functions are not brain processes directly observed here.

Recall 3Why does the count of pouches not measure memory capacity?

Its unit is the pouch. It describes the quantity to prepare, without measuring how many pieces of content a person can maintain. The organization of the instruction, the task, knowledge and aids also play a role.

Recall 4What separate checks are needed on the final answer?

Reconstruct the initial count by adding the subtracted quantity to the final quantity, then separately compare the proposed tray with the latest requested destination. Addition does not check a colour.

Recall 5What does a correct answer obtained by reading the card prove?

A correct answer with that support. It proves neither maintenance without help nor lasting retention. The conditions of new attempts need to be described; a reading or an assisted answer never counts as an acquired level.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Hide this synthesis, then reconstruct the path of the instruction: content maintained, quantity transformed, destination replaced and separate checks. Then recall the distinction between content available in your head, support you can consult and knowledge retained for later. Finish by stating what the answer alone does not allow you to claim.
