Level 4 · C228·M5

Incompleteness

Your goalProve that a claim cannot be replicated, then explain why several prices remain compatible with the absence of arbitrage.

Before you start
c227-m5 : Ito-Tanaka · c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c173-m5 : completeness · c228-m1 : characteristic function · c228-m2 : Levy-Khintchine · c228-m3 : jump decomposition · c228-m4 : Ito with jumps

✿01 See the idea

A pottery workshop is considering protection against a rise in the price of a raw material certificate. The certificate is worth 100 € today. At the next settlement, its price will be 80 €, 100 € or 120 €. The protection contract must pay only the amount above 100 €: nothing in the low and middle outcomes, 20 € in the high outcome. This entire scenario and its values are fictional.

To construct this protection, the workshop has only the certificate and a cash reserve that keeps its value. A position fixed today links its payoffs across the different outcomes. It cannot adjust each box in the diagram separately: the portfolio value remains a straight line as a function of the final price.

Replication reproduces the promised payoff exactly in every possible outcome. A market is complete when every claim in the universe under consideration can be replicated using the tradable assets; it is incomplete as soon as even a single claim cannot be replicated. We will establish this failure for the protection contract, without assuming that all contracts become impossible to hedge.

Imagine a straight line through the extreme payoffs of the protection contract. In the middle, must it stay at ground level or rise above it? Answer before looking at the second diagram. This small market over a single period isolates the obstacle to replication; it does not simulate a Levy process.

The rule to remember

Choosing a price does not construct a hedge. First check the payoffs in every outcome, then ask whether the pricing constraints still leave a choice.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The workshop’s protection contract: hedging, then pricing

At the outset, no outcome is known. The workshop chooses a quantity δ of certificates and a reserve C in euros, then holds them until settlement. The low, middle and high states all have strictly positive real-world probability. Their exact probabilities are not given: they are unnecessary for the proof.

  1. Specify the available information and instruments

    S denotes the certificate price at settlement, in euros per certificate; its initial price is 100 €. The reserve C stays unchanged over the period. δ is a quantity of certificates. The terminal portfolio is worth V = C + δS, in euros, and initially costs C + δ × 100 €. A negative C means borrowing, and a negative quantity δ means a short sale.

    We allow fractional quantities, borrowing and short sales, with no fees, default, dividends or position limits. There is no intermediate trading or new information before settlement: the portfolio is self-financing after its initial purchase. A strategy cannot choose δ after seeing S.

    The universe contains exactly the 3 states shown, and all combinations of events formed from them are distinguishable at settlement. A claim is therefore a vector of 3 payoffs in euros, with a payoff for each state. Arbitrage here means: no initial cost, a final payoff that is never negative, and a strictly positive payoff in at least a single state with positive real-world probability.

  2. Write down what the protection contract promises

    K is the contractual threshold, fixed at 100 € per certificate. H is the payoff of a protection contract covering a certificate: H = max(S − K, 0), in euros. The maximum takes the larger of the amounts shown. In the order low, middle, high, the payoffs are H_b = 0 €, H_m = 0 € and H_h = 120 − 100 = 20 €; the subscripts b, m, h name these states.

    C+80δ=0C+100δ=0C+120δ=20

    These equations are simultaneous: the same C and δ must satisfy every line. Solving each outcome separately would construct a position that depends on the future.

  3. Fit the extremes and examine the middle

    Subtracting the low equation from the high equation eliminates C. The price difference is 120 − 80 = 40 € per certificate, for a payoff difference of 20 €. We therefore need δ = 20/40 = 1/2 certificate. Then C = −δ × 80 = -40 €: the position borrows 40 € and buys a fraction of a certificate.

    Check each state: in the low state, 40 + (-40) = 0 €; in the high state, 60 + (-40) = 20 €. But in the middle, 50 + (-40) = 10 €, whereas H_m = 0 €. The residual H_m − V_m is -10 €: the candidate pays too much.

    The extreme equations have a unique solution because 40 is nonzero. Any portfolio replicating H would have to be this candidate, which fails in the middle. This rules out every permitted portfolio, rather than just a poorly chosen attempt. Its initial cost is also 10 €, because the initial price here equals the middle price.

  4. Find an independent compatibility condition

    For a vector x of payoffs in euros, define the linear functional ℓ(x) = x_b − 2x_m + x_h. It produces an amount in euros. The coefficients of the reserve cancel: 1 − 2 + 1 = 0. Those of the certificate do too: 80 + 120 = 200 and 2 × 100 = 200, so ℓ(S) = 0 € per certificate.

    ℓ(V)=0;ℓ(H)=0−2×0+20=20≠0

    Thus every combination of cash reserve and certificates satisfies ℓ(V) = 0, whereas H violates this equality. This is another proof that replication is impossible. The map (C, δ) ↦ (V_b, V_m, V_h) has rank 2: the constant reserve and the nonconstant certificate are independent. Its image is a plane in the space of 3 payoffs. H lies outside this plane; the market is incomplete.

    The number of instruments alone therefore cannot decide the issue: their independence and the universe of claims also matter. Even here, a constant payoff is replicable. An approximation that reduces a residual does not turn an impossible equality into exact replication.

  5. Describe all martingale measures for the small market

    Q is a pricing measure; q_b, q_m, q_h are its dimensionless weights. A martingale measure preserves the discounted price in expectation. Since the reserve earns no interest, discounting changes nothing: the expectation under Q, denoted E_Q, must give the initial price 100 € for S. Q must be equivalent to the real-world probability law: here, this requires a strictly positive weight in each possible state.

    qb+qm+qh=180qb+100qm+120qh=100

    Subtract 100 times the normalization equation from the second equation: −20q_b + 20q_h = 0, so q_b = q_h. Calling this common value θ, all the weights are q_b = θ, q_m = 1 − 2θ, q_h = θ. The parameter θ is dimensionless and must satisfy 0 < θ < 1/2. These conditions describe all solutions, with a degree of freedom remaining.

    The existence of these positive weights establishes the absence of arbitrage here: every portfolio with no initial cost has terminal expectation 0 under Q. A payoff that is never negative and is positive in a state would instead have strictly positive expectation. Pricing weights do not estimate the real-world frequencies of the outcomes.

  6. Calculate and check two compatible prices

    For Q_A, take θ = 1/4. Then 2θ = 1/2, q_m = 1/2, and the weights are (1/4, 1/2, 1/4). They sum to 1. The expectation of the certificate is 20 + 50 + 30 = 70 + 30 = 100 €, matching its initial price. The protection price under this choice is c_A = E_Q_A[H] = 1/4 × 20 = 5 €.

    For Q_B, take θ = 1/3. Then 2θ = 2/3 and q_m = 1/3: the weights are (1/3, 1/3, 1/3), again strictly positive and summing to 1. The expectation of the certificate is 80/3 + 100/3 + 40 = 60 + 40 = 100 €. This time c_B = 1/3 × 20 = 20/3 €, or approximately 6.67 €.

    The exact difference c_B − c_A is 5/3 €, or approximately 1.67 €. Both measures respect the same tradable assets, but value H differently. This is consistent with its failure of replication: if H = C + δS, its expectation under every martingale measure would necessarily be the same cost C + δ × 100 €.

  7. Separate the price interval from the choice of a price

    For the whole family, the price c is E_Q[H] = 20θ euros. Since 0 < θ < 1/2, the compatible prices are exactly 0 € < c < 10 €. This exact interval applies when adding a protection contract that can be bought and sold at the same price, with the same freedom to take positions as for the other instruments.

    Why does every interior price work? Setting θ = c/20 gives positive weights that simultaneously price the reserve, certificate and protection contract. Every combination of these instruments with no initial cost still has terminal expectation 0; the preceding argument therefore still rules out arbitrage. The endpoints will be tested in the last exercise.

    The absence of arbitrage does not impose any particular point in this interval. Choosing 5 € rather than 20/3 € requires an additional justified rule, such as a risk preference or market information. This rule selects a price; it does not change the instruments’ payoffs and therefore does not resolve incompleteness.

  8. Connect the obstacle to jumps without generalizing too quickly

    The link with a jump model is the position chosen before the outcome. Write ΔS for the change in the certificate price during a jump, in euros per certificate, and ΔV for the change in the portfolio value in euros. The unchanged reserve imposes ΔV = δΔS. The different possible jump sizes must therefore be hedged with the same δ, decided before the jump.

    In a continuous-time model, we must examine whether the risks of claims can be represented by strategies using the tradable assets. Simply counting our states proves no theorem for a general Levy model. The presence of jumps is insufficient to conclude that a market is incomplete: we must study the risks, available information and instruments actually permitted.

    Our proven conclusion remains precise: in this finite market, the tradable payoffs do not span H and the martingale measure is not unique. We have neither constructed a complete Levy model nor chosen an approximate hedging method.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Why is the portfolio that succeeds at the extremes the only possible candidate? Then recover the freedom in θ and explain why choosing it does not change the failure in the middle. An answer obtained after a hint remains an assisted answer.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I fitted the extremes, so I have hedged the contract.”

    Substitute the middle price for S in the same portfolio: it pays 10 € instead of 0 €. Check every state, then explain why the extreme equations already determine C and δ. The functional ℓ provides a check independent of this fitting.

    Review the fitting and compatibility condition

  • “A martingale measure exists, so the market is complete.”

    The existence of positive weights rules out arbitrage here. Completeness requires every claim to be replicable. Our measures Q_A and Q_B satisfy the existing prices, but assign 5 € and 20/3 € to H: existence and uniqueness are different properties.

    Review the family of measures and the two valuations

  • “I choose equal weights: I have found the true price.”

    Equal weights define Q_B in our scenario, without establishing real-world frequencies or a compulsory choice of price. Q_A is also compatible with the assets. State the selection rule and keep the interval open; the endpoints produce arbitrages that the last exercise asks you to construct.

    Test the endpoints and the role of a chosen price

Exercise 1 · Application · another elimination

Start with the low and middle outcomes

In the same scenario, first impose the payoffs of H in the low and middle states. Find C and δ, then test the high outcome. Why does this new attempt confirm the proof rather than contradict it?

Hint 1

Subtract the two equations whose right-hand sides are 0 €.

Hint 2

Their price difference is nonzero. Then test the payoff of the resulting position at S = 120 €.

Compare with the solution

Subtraction gives (100 − 80)δ = 0, or 20δ = 0. Thus δ = 0 certificate, then C = 0 €. This portfolio pays 0 € in the high outcome; H requires 20 € there.

The pairs of equations give different candidates, but no position satisfies all the equations. This contradiction is precisely the failure of replication. Changing the equations used for fitting moves the error; it does not remove it.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify δ = 0 using a nonzero price difference.
  • Test the outcome that was not used for fitting, including the units.
  • Explain why C and δ must remain identical in every state.

Exercise 2 · Reconstruction · measure and price

A proposal from the workshop

The workshop proposes q_h = 1/3, then announces 6.67 € as a rounded price. Without copying the family of measures, recover q_b and q_m from the constraints. Check the sum, the certificate price and the exact price of H. Is the rounded price enough to identify an exact value?

Hint 1

Subtract the initial price multiplied by the sum of the weights from the certificate’s expectation equation.

Hint 2

Only the high outcome contributes to the expectation of H. Keep the fraction until the last line.

Compare with the solution

The differences from the initial price are −20, 0, 20 € per certificate. The constraint gives q_b = q_h = 1/3, then q_m = 1 − 2/3 = 1/3. The weights are positive and sum to 1.

Checking the asset gives 80/3 + 100/3 + 40 = 100 €. The protection contract is worth exactly 20 × 1/3 = 20/3 €, of which 6.67 € is merely a rounded display value. The problem fixes the weights, hence the exact fraction; a rounded decimal amount on its own would leave several possible exact values.

This calculation verifies a compatible price. It establishes neither that Q_B is the real-world probability law nor that the absence of arbitrage imposes this price on everyone.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover the weights from the constraints and check their strict positivity.
  • Check the asset price before the protection price.
  • Distinguish the exact fraction, rounding and the fact that the measure is chosen.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · explicit arbitrages

Why exclude the endpoints?

The protection contract becomes tradable at the same buying and selling price. Construct an arbitrage if it costs 0 €, then if it costs 10 €. For the upper endpoint, reuse the portfolio fitted to the extremes. State the initial cost and every net payoff. Finally, distinguish a merely quoted price from actually adding H to the tradable instruments: which market remains incomplete?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At the lower endpoint, buying a protection contract for free is enough. At the upper endpoint, compare selling a protection contract with buying the portfolio that costs 10 €.

Hint 2

Calculate V − H in the order low, middle, high; remember to check the initial financing.

Compare with the solution

At 0 €, buy a protection contract: initial cost 0 €, payoffs (0, 0, 20) €. They are never negative and are positive in the possible high outcome. This is an arbitrage.

At 10 €, sell a protection contract for 10 € and buy the portfolio (C = -40 €, δ = 1/2 certificate), which costs 10 €. The net initial cost is 0 €. Its net payoffs V − H are (0 − 0, 10 − 0, 20 − 20) = (0, 10, 0) €. They are positive in the possible middle outcome and never negative. This is another arbitrage.

Inside the interval, choosing a positive θ with 1 − 2θ positive rules out these arbitrages and all others through the expectation argument. Fixing c selects a compatible pricing measure for H. As long as only the reserve and certificate are used for replication, H remains outside their attainable payoffs: a merely quoted price creates no additional instrument.

In the exact situation described in this problem, H is actually added to the tradable instruments. The reserve, certificate and H have independent payoffs: ℓ vanishes on the first two, but not on H. They therefore span all payoffs across the 3 states, and the extended market is complete. The initial market, with only the reserve and certificate, remains incomplete. Always name the instruments in the market being discussed.

Check your reasoning

  • Present the bought and sold positions, then check that the initial cost is 0.
  • Give the net payoffs in every state, including a state where the payoff is strictly positive.
  • Explain the role of positive real-world probabilities.
  • Distinguish choosing a price for H from adding H to the instruments permitted for hedging other claims.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be specified before declaring a market incomplete?

The available information, trading dates, permitted instruments and strategies, then the universe of claims. It is then enough to exhibit a claim that these strategies cannot replicate in every possible state.

Recall 2Which payoff equality rules out replication of our protection contract?

Every value V = C + δS satisfies V_b − 2V_m + V_h = 0, because the prices are evenly spaced and the reserve is constant. For H, this expression equals 20 €: the payoff is outside the attainable space.

Recall 3Why do the pricing constraints leave a family of weights?

Normalization and the certificate price impose q_b = q_h = θ and q_m = 1 − 2θ. Every θ strictly between 0 and 1/2 gives positive weights and prices the same assets; they do not all price H at the same value.

Recall 4Why does choosing Q_A fail to fix the hedge?

Q_A assigns 5 € to H but changes none of the reserve’s or certificate’s payoffs. The compatibility obstacle remains. A chosen price requires additional justification; it proves no replication.

Recall 5Which aspect of the example prepares us to study incompleteness with jumps?

The position must be decided before the outcome. The same number of certificates imposes a relationship between payoffs for every possible jump size. We must examine the risks spanned by the assets; the presence of jumps alone does not prove incompleteness.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without support: name the instruments and information; write the required payoffs; demonstrate the failure of replication and then the condition involving ℓ; recover the martingale weights and their free parameter; deduce the possible prices and justify excluding the endpoints. Finish with the difference between choosing a price and having a hedging instrument. Reading and an assisted answer do not establish attainment of any level.

Progress reference · no automatic assessment

A chosen result does not prove an exact solution

What you understand
A method can choose a result in a constrained problem without establishing that all required equalities hold. Here, incompleteness concerns attainable payoffs; in numerical fitting, exactness and uniqueness must also be examined separately. This comparison does not turn a fitting problem into a financial market.
