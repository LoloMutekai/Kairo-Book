Level 1 · C039·M3

Bases

Your goalRecognize a basis by checking that it allows every vector in the space to be expressed uniquely, then calculate the coefficients of a displacement.

Before you start
c032-m5 : calculating rank · c014-m5 : invariants · c039-m1 : subspaces · c039-m2 : independence

✿01 See the idea

A small carriage moves a tip across a table. Its command u moves the tip 2 mm to the right and 1 mm upwards; its command v moves it 1 mm to the right and 2 mm upwards. The arrows are diagonal. Can we still use them to describe any displacement of the tip?

We are working with a fictional model: displacements are exact, commands can be multiplied by any real number, and travel is unlimited. A negative coefficient reverses a command; a fractional coefficient takes a fraction of it. This is therefore more than counting whole presses of physical buttons.

A combination a u + b v means multiplying each displacement by its coefficient, then adding their components. A family spans the chosen space if every relative destination can be reached this way. It is linearly independent if no combination gives the 0 displacement unless all the coefficients are 0: this is the independence reviewed in the previous unit.

A basis combines these 2 properties. Spanning gives an expression for every displacement; linear independence makes that expression unique. We keep the order B = (u, v): the first coefficient goes with u and the second goes with v. These coefficients are the coordinates in B. They are distinct from the horizontal and vertical components of the arrow.

The rule to remember

Specify the space, show that the family reaches every vector, then check that no command is redundant. A basis then provides a unique expression in the chosen order.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Reach the target and justify the word “basis”

The tip must undergo the displacement t = (5 ; 4) mm. Let us find a and b such that t = a u + b v, then check that B = (u, v) is indeed a basis of the entire plane. Finding the commands for this target would not, by itself, prove this.

  1. Define the space, symbols, and units

    E denotes the space of all displacements in the plane of the table, equipped with componentwise addition and multiplication by a real number. The symbol ℝ denotes the real numbers. With components expressed in millimetres, each displacement in E is represented by a pair of real numbers.

    E≃ℝ2;u=(2;1) mm;v=(1;2) mm

    The sign ≃ indicates this identification through components. The letters u, v, and t denote displacement vectors in millimetres. The letters a and b denote real multipliers with no unit. Later, x and y will be the numerical values of the components of an arbitrary displacement expressed in millimetres: (x ; y) mm, rather than coefficients in B that are already known. The 0 vector, denoted 0, does not move the tip.

  2. Translate the destination into a system of equations

    In the horizontal direction, a u contributes 2a mm and b v contributes 1b mm. In the vertical direction, they contribute 1a mm and 2b mm. We add these contributions to match the target. After expressing each component in the same unit, we solve the equations between their numerical values:

    {2a+b=5a+2b=4

    The unit factors before b in the first line and before a in the second are omitted from the notation, since vx = 1 and uy = 1. The values 5 and 4 describe the destination along the axes; we are still looking for a and b.

  3. Eliminate b to find a

    Multiply the first equation by 2, then subtract the second. The terms in b become equal before subtraction, so they cancel. The remaining coefficient of a is 2 × 2 − 1 × 1 = 4 − 1 = 3.

    2(2a+b)−(a+2b)=2×5−4

    On the right: 2 × 5 = 10, then 10 − 4 = 6. The remaining equation is therefore 3a = 6. Since 3 is nonzero, division is allowed: a = 6/3 = 2. This coefficient has no unit.

  4. Find b by substitution

    Return to the first equation, since a is now known. The horizontal contribution of a u is 2 × 2 = 4 mm. We still need to produce 5 − 4 = 1 mm horizontally. Each unit of v contributes 1 mm horizontally: b = 1/1 = 1.

    b=5−2×21=1

    When reasoning with units, this division is a length divided by a length, so b has no unit. The coordinates of t in the order B = (u, v) are (2 ; 1).

  5. Reconstruct and check each component

    Also multiply the vertical components: 2 × 1 = 2 mm and 1 × 2 = 2 mm. The horizontal contributions are 4 mm and 1 × 1 = 1 mm. The full reconstruction gives:

    2u+1v=(4;2)+(1;2)=(5;4) mm

    Each pair of components in this equality is expressed in millimetres. We recover the target’s horizontal component 5 mm and vertical component 4 mm separately. This reconstruction check can detect a sign or calculation error in the elimination. It does not yet prove that all other targets can be reached.

  6. Show that the family spans the space

    Now take an arbitrary displacement (x ; y) mm. The same elimination suggests the following coefficients; the denominator 3 does not depend on the destination and is nonzero:

    a=2x−y3;b=2y−x3

    Let us check their effect for any real numbers x and y. The reconstructed horizontal component is x, and the vertical component is y:

    2a+b=2(2x−y)+2y−x3=x
    a+2b=2x−y+2(2y−x)3=y

    These coefficients are real and allowed by our model. Every vector in E therefore has an expression using u and v: the family spans E. Choosing an arbitrary displacement here replaces an accumulation of individual trials.

  7. Show independence, then uniqueness

    To check that the family is linearly independent, suppose αu + βv = 0. The Greek letters α and β are real coefficients with no unit. All components must be 0:

    2α+β=0;α+2β=0

    As before, multiplying the first equation by 2 and subtracting the second gives 3α = 0. Thus α = 0, then the first equation forces β = 0. This is exactly the criterion for linear independence.

    Why does this guarantee uniqueness? Suppose the same displacement has coefficients a, b and also a′, b′. The prime only distinguishes the second expression. Subtracting the expressions gives (a − a′)u + (b − b′)v = 0. Independence forces a − a′ = 0 and b − b′ = 0. The expressions coincide.

  8. Conclude in the stated space

    The ordered family B = (u, v) spans E and is linearly independent in E: it is a basis of E. For our target, the coordinates in B are (2 ; 1), while the components along the axes are (5 ; 4) mm. These pairs answer different questions: how much of each command, and what horizontal and vertical displacement.

    The conclusion concerns the ideal plane of our model. With commands restricted to positive whole numbers or a table with bounded travel, it would no longer necessarily be possible to reach every destination. The algebraic proof of a basis validates neither the carriage mechanism nor the precision of a real machine.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the system of equations. Explain why reaching the target does not prove that the family spans the space, then why independence rules out competing coordinates. Finally, reconstruct the target without confusing the command coefficients with the components in millimetres.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I reached the target, so I have a basis.”

    A single target only gives an expression for that vector. Return to an arbitrary displacement (x ; y) mm to establish that the family spans the entire plane, then check independence using the 0 combination. The 2 proofs have distinct roles.

    Review spanning and independence

  • “Diagonal commands cannot form a basis.”

    The word basis does not require a right angle. It requires spanning and independence. The formulas in the lesson establish these properties for our diagonal commands; the diagram helps us see them, and the equations prove them.

    Review what the arrows must guarantee

  • “The coordinates in B must be (5 ; 4).”

    This pair gives the component values along the axes, in millimetres. In B = (u, v), the coefficients are (2 ; 1), with no unit. Reconstruct a u + b v: you should recover the destination’s components, rather than copy their pair as a command.

    Review the reconstruction check

Exercise 1 · Direct application

From coefficients to the destination

The carriage keeps commands u and v. We are given a = 1 and b = 2 in the order B = (u, v). What displacement do we obtain? Give its components with their unit and explain how this pair differs from the pair of coefficients.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Multiply the horizontal and vertical components of each command separately.

Hint 2

Only add contributions along the same axis.

Compare with the solution

The term a u is (2 ; 1) mm, since 1 × 2 = 2 and 1 × 1 = 1. The term b v is (2 ; 4) mm, since 2 × 1 = 2 and 2 × 2 = 4.

Addition gives (2 + 2 ; 1 + 4) mm = (4 ; 5) mm. The coordinates in B remain (1 ; 2), with no unit: they indicate the multipliers, while (4 ; 5) mm describes the resulting effect.

Inverse check: the rule a = (2x − y)/3 gives 1 again for x = 4, y = 5, and b = (2y − x)/3 gives 2 again.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the contributions of each command before adding them.
  • Recover (4 ; 5) mm, keeping the axes in the correct order.
  • Distinguish multipliers with no unit from displacement components in millimetres.

Exercise 2 · Independent problem solving

Move horizontally using diagonal commands

Using the same commands, find the coefficients of the displacement (1 ; 0) mm. Can the carriage in the model reach it? Justify any negative coefficient and check the vertical component.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First write the equations whose right sides are 1 and 0.

Hint 2

Cancelling the vertical component requires a + 2b = 0; one command will have to compensate for the other.

Compare with the solution

The system is 2a + b = 1 and a + 2b = 0. Multiplying the first line by 2 then subtracting the second gives 3a = 2. Thus a = 2/3. The first line, or the general formula, gives b = -1/3.

23u−13v=(1;0) mm

Horizontal check: 4/3 + (-1/3) = 1 mm. Vertical check: 2/3 + (-2/3) = 0 mm. The negative coefficient b reverses a fraction of v; it does not mean a negative travel distance.

The destination can therefore be reached in the model with real coefficients. The same conclusion would not hold for a device that prohibited any negative or fractional command.

Check your reasoning

  • Find a = 2/3 and b = -1/3, without rounding the fractions.
  • Check that the vertical component is exactly 0.
  • Relate the negative command to the assumption of real coefficients.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and proof

Replace a command with an enlarged copy

We replace v with w = 2u = (4 ; 2) mm, keeping the same space E. Is the family (u, w) a basis of E? Find out whether it reaches our target (5 ; 4) mm, then give distinct expressions for u using this family.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Every combination c u + d w can be rewritten as (c + 2d)u. The letters c and d denote real multipliers.

Hint 2

Compare the required vertical component when the horizontal component is 5 mm. To examine redundancy, write u as w − u.

Compare with the solution

Set s = c + 2d, a real number with no unit. Every combination is s u = (2s ; 1s) mm and stays on the line spanned by u. Reaching the horizontal component 5 mm forces s = 5/2 = 5/2, with no unit. Since uy = 1, the vertical component would be 2.5 mm, instead of 4 mm. The target is missed: the family does not span E.

Independence also fails: u = 1u + 0w, but also u = (-1)u + 1w, since w = 2u. The pairs (1 ; 0) and (-1 ; 1) are different and describe the same displacement. A basis does not allow this ambiguity.

This family is therefore not a basis of the plane. It does span a line, but it remains redundant on that line. The family consisting of the single nonzero vector u would be a basis of that line. The stated space is part of the question.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify why the target cannot be reached using an equality between components.
  • Exhibit distinct coefficients for the same vector.
  • Conclude in E, without confusing the plane with the line spanned.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What properties define a basis of a vector space?

The family spans that space and is linearly independent. It therefore allows every vector to be written as a combination of its elements, with unique coefficients once their order is fixed.

Recall 2Why is reaching several destinations insufficient to prove spanning?

The trials only concern the chosen destinations. We must establish that every vector in the space has an expression, for example by giving and checking a formula for an arbitrary destination.

Recall 3How does independence rule out competing expressions?

Subtracting the expressions produces a 0 combination. Linear independence then forces each difference between coefficients to be 0: the expressions are identical.

Recall 4What do the coordinates of our target in B = (u, v) mean?

The pair (2 ; 1) indicates the multipliers with no unit before u and v. Their combination recovers the displacement (5 ; 4) mm along the axes. The order of the family determines the role of each coefficient.

Recall 5Why specify the space before talking about a basis?

Spanning depends on the intended space. The single vector u is a basis of its line, but does not span the plane. Adding a copy of u creates no new direction and destroys uniqueness of the coefficients.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the equations, reconstruct this sequence: state the space and the allowed coefficients; translate a combination into components; solve, then reconstruct; move from a target to an arbitrary vector to prove spanning; use the 0 combination for independence and uniqueness. Finish by explaining why command coefficients are distinct from components in millimetres.

Reading milestone · column independence

What you understand
A basis is linearly independent and spans the stated space. Independent columns form a basis of the subspace they span; they do not necessarily form a basis of the entire ambient space, the space of vectors with as many components as the matrix has rows.
