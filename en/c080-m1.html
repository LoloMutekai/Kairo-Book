Biomolecules

Your goalRecognize the main families of biomolecules in a fictional neuron extract, calculate a share of the mass, and distinguish composition from function.

Before you start
c079-m5 : function and mechanism · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales

✿01 See the idea

On the laboratory bench, a preparation of neurons has yielded separate fractions, placed in vials labelled proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids. A neuron is a cell of the nervous system. Reading these labels lets us recognize materials found in living organisms; weighing the vials alone will not tell us how this cell works.

This entire scene is fictional, including the identification and the masses. We assume that the fractions are purified, dry, recovered without loss, and counted without overlap. The mass balance covers only these vials, not all the matter in a neuron. Water and salts are excluded. This is neither an experimental procedure nor a typical composition of the brain.

A molecule is an assembly of bonded atoms; atoms are its chemical constituents. Biomolecules are the molecules of living organisms. Here, we study 4 main organic families, whose structures are based on carbon atoms. They do not form an exhaustive inventory: water is also a molecule essential to the cell.

Carbohydrates include simple sugars, such as glucose, and assemblies of sugars. Glucose can supply energy that the cell can use after chemical transformation. Lipids form a diverse family of molecules with low solubility in water; some, called phospholipids, help form the membrane, the thin boundary surrounding the cell. Not all lipids are long chains of repeating building blocks.

Proteins contain one or more chains of amino acids: an amino acid is a molecular building block of these chains. The order of these building blocks and the folding of the chain contribute to the function of the protein. Some proteins are enzymes, which speed up chemical reactions; others provide support or transport. The family name alone therefore does not identify a specific role.

Nucleic acids, DNA and RNA, are chains of nucleotides. A nucleotide is a building block containing a sugar, a phosphate group, and a base; this base is a part of the building block whose identity can vary along the chain. DNA stores information in the order of its bases; some RNAs help use this information to produce proteins, among other roles. Containing a sugar does not make DNA a carbohydrate: we classify the whole molecule by its organization.

The rule to remember

Identify the molecular organization, suggest a compatible role, then specify what the measurement establishes. Family, mass, and function answer different questions.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What share of the recovered mass is protein?

The vials contain 12 mg of proteins, 5 mg of lipids, 2 mg of carbohydrates, and 1 mg of nucleic acids. Let us find the protein share in the sum of these masses. mg means milligram, the unit of mass used here.

  1. Define what is counted

    The letter m denotes a mass in mg; its subscripts P, L, G, and A identify proteins, lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids, respectively. M is their total mass, in mg. The subscripts serve only as family labels.

    We add masses of dry matter that has already been identified. The vials do not overlap: no mass is counted in more than one family. A real molecule can combine several types of constituents; assuming purified fractions avoids classification ambiguities here. The total includes no other fraction of the preparation.

  2. Connect the labels to the structure

    The protein vial contains chains of amino acids. The carbohydrate vial contains sugars and their assemblies; the lipid vial contains lipids. The nucleic acid vial contains chains of nucleotides. Our scene provides these labels: the balance alone would not allow us to assign them.

    A protein could speed up a reaction, a phospholipid help form a boundary, a carbohydrate supply a resource, and a nucleic acid carry information. These possible roles guide our interpretation; none is measured by the masses in the table.

  3. Form the correct total

    The question concerns all the mass recovered in the vials. We must therefore add the families and keep the milligram as the common unit.

    M=mP+mL+mG+mA

    Proteins and lipids: 12 + 5 = 17 mg. Carbohydrates and nucleic acids: 2 + 1 = 3 mg. The total is therefore 17 + 3 = 20 mg.

  4. Divide the part by the whole

    The letter w with subscript P denotes the mass fraction of proteins: the share of M that they account for. It has no unit, because a mass in mg is divided by a mass in mg. It is defined if M is strictly positive.

    wP=mPM=1220=0.60

    The exact simplified fraction is 3/5. Its decimal form 0.60 is exact here, with no rounding.

    The numerator is the mass of the family being studied; the denominator is the chosen total. Dividing by the mass of lipids alone would answer a different question, that of the protein/lipid ratio.

  5. Express the share as a percentage

    The letter p with subscript P denotes the numerical value of this share expressed as a percentage. To read it as “per 100”, we multiply the fraction by 100; we then add the % sign to this value.

    pP=100×wP=100×0.60=60

    Proteins therefore account for 60 % of the dry mass in our vials. This result applies only to this fictional inventory. It expresses neither a percentage of molecules nor a share of the activity of the neuron.

  6. Check by reconstruction and by the remainder

    Reconstruction: 0.60 × 20 mg = 12 mg. This recovers the given protein mass. Independent remainder check: 20 − 12 = 8 mg; the other vials do indeed give 5 + 2 + 1 = 8 mg.

    A positive partial mass does not exceed its total: the fraction must lie between 0 and 1, and the percentage between 0 % and 100 %. Our result respects these bounds. To count molecules, we would also need to know the masses of the molecules present. To identify a specific function, we would need to identify the molecules and observe their action.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculation. Explain how the organization of the building blocks distinguishes the families, then reconstruct the protein share, naming the chosen whole. Why does the same balance give neither a number of molecules nor an activity?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “All biomolecules are long chains made of the same building block.”

    Distinguish the building blocks: amino acids for proteins, nucleotides for nucleic acids, and sugars for carbohydrate assemblies. Glucose is a simple sugar, and lipids do not form a family of polymers with a single building block. A polymer is specifically a large molecule made of many linked building blocks.

    Find the families and the chain drawing again

  • “60 % of the mass means 60 % of the molecules.”

    The calculation divides masses. Molecules from different families can have very different masses: the same mass can contain different numbers of molecules. Keep the expression “mass percentage” and name the total of the vials.

    Reread the division and the unit check

  • “The family ranking highest by mass must do the largest share of the work.”

    The balance measures a quantity of matter. The order of the building blocks, folding, location, and conditions of action also matter. The last exercise lets you change all the masses while preserving the composition: this mass balance still provides no measurement of activity.

    Examine what the change in quantity allows you to conclude

Exercise 2 · Application and checking

Account for the whole composition

Start again with the initial vials. Calculate the mass fraction and then the percentage of lipids, carbohydrates, and nucleic acids. Together with the protein share from the example, check that the sum of the percentages represents all the mass being accounted for.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Each family has the same denominator: M = 20 mg.

Hint 2

The fraction is a mass divided by M; the percentage is 100 times this fraction.

Compare with the solution

Lipids: 5/20 = 1/4 = 0.25, hence 25 %. Carbohydrates: 2/20 = 1/10 = 0.10, hence 10 %. Nucleic acids: 1/20 = 1/20 = 0.05, hence 5 %. The fractions have no unit, and the initial masses are in mg.

Proteins and lipids: 60 + 25 = 85 %. Carbohydrates and nucleic acids: 10 + 5 = 15 %. Total: 85 + 15 = 100 %. This check confirms that our accounting of the mass shares is complete, under the assumptions of separation and no loss.

It does not demonstrate that the inventory describes the entire neuron. Even an incomplete breakdown of the matter in a living organism could total 100 % if its denominator covers only the selected vials.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the common total as the denominator.
  • Show the fractions, conversion, and sum used for checking.
  • Specify that the whole consists of the vials, not the entire neuron.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and justification

More matter: what can we conclude?

We have 2 times as much of each fraction from the same preparation, with the same molecules in the same proportions. Recalculate the masses and the protein share. A classmate concludes: “The proportion of proteins increases, so neural activity increases.” Evaluate these claims separately.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The factor multiplies every mass, including the total.

Hint 2

Write the new quotient before concluding; then look for the measurement of activity in the data.

Compare with the solution

Proteins: 2 × 12 = 24 mg; lipids: 2 × 5 = 10 mg; carbohydrates: 2 × 2 = 4 mg; nucleic acids: 2 × 1 = 2 mg. The total is 2 × 20 = 40 mg, as confirmed by the sum of the new masses.

The protein fraction is 24/40 = 3/5 = 0.60, or 60 %. The same factor in the numerator and denominator cancels. The protein mass increases, but its proportion stays the same: the first claim is false.

Neural activity has not been measured, and the vials are separate fractions. The second claim is not established by this mass balance. An appropriate observation of functioning cells would be needed to examine their activity; no increase in activity is inferred here.

Check your reasoning

  • Multiply all the masses and check their sum.
  • Justify the unchanged fraction using the quotient.
  • Distinguish a claim refuted by the calculation from a claim not established because a measurement is missing.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which organic families have we distinguished, and by what identifying features?

Carbohydrates: sugars and assemblies of sugars; lipids: a diverse family of molecules with low solubility in water; proteins: chains of amino acids; nucleic acids: chains of nucleotides, including DNA and RNA. This classification does not cover all molecules in living organisms.

Recall 2Why is knowing a mass of proteins not enough to know their function?

Their identity, the order of their amino acids, their folding, and their conditions of action matter. Mass indicates how much matter is present, without identifying the proteins or measuring their action.

Recall 3Why is DNA not classified as a carbohydrate even though its building blocks contain a sugar?

The whole molecule is a chain of nucleotides. Each nucleotide contains a sugar, a phosphate group, and a base. We classify the complete organization of the molecule, not an isolated constituent of its building block.

Recall 4How can you reconstruct a mass fraction and its percentage, and under what assumptions?

Divide the mass of the family by the chosen total, which must be strictly positive and in the same unit; multiply the fraction by 100 to express it as a percentage. Our fractions are separate and do not overlap. You must name what the total covers and check the mass balance.

Recall 5What can we conclude from a protein share of 60 % in our vials?

Proteins account for this share of the selected dry mass. This gives neither their share in the number of molecules, nor a universal proportion for a neuron, nor a measurement of activity. The scene and its masses are fictional.
