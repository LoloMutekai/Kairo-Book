Level 1 · C029·M2

Axioms

Your goalConstruct and check a probability measure on a finite sample space, then add events without counting their shared part more than once.

Before you start
c028-m5 : choice trees · c024-m5 : measurement quality · c029-m1 : events

✿01 See the idea

You draw a ticket from a box and record only its letter. Some letters appear on several tickets, so they have more chances of being drawn. To discuss the chances of obtaining a letter, you therefore need to look at the contents of the box, as well as the list of possible letters.

Our fictional box contains 10 tickets: A, B, C, C, D, D, E, E, E, F. By assumption, each ticket has the same chance of being drawn. The 6 possible letters therefore do not all have the same chance: A appears on 1 ticket, E on 3 tickets. These numbers describe a model chosen for the lesson; they are never measured frequencies.

We keep the properties of the letters from the previous unit: A and B are pink and marked with a star, C is pink with a circle, D is blue and marked with a star, and E and F are blue with a circle. Copies of the same letter have the same properties. The first diagram groups tickets by letter; the second places them in regions that do not overlap.

Imagine all the available probability as a total quantity equal to 1. An event receives the share of tickets for which it occurs. This share cannot be negative; all possibilities together must give the total; regions with no ticket in common can be added. These requirements for consistency are the axioms. By themselves, they do not tell us whether our drawing assumption represents a real box.

The rule to remember

Assign a non-negative mass to each outcome and check the total. For an event, add the masses of the selected outcomes, counting each outcome only once.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The chance of drawing a pink letter or a letter marked with a star

Draw 1 ticket from the 10 without looking, assuming that each ticket has the same chance. Record only its letter. The contents are still those of the fictional box; repeated draws are not studied. Let us construct a probability measure on the letters, then calculate the chance of meeting the condition “pink or marked with a star”.

  1. Define the recorded outcome and its masses

    Ω, uppercase omega, is the sample space of the results we record: Ω = {A, B, C, D, E, F}. An outcome ω, lowercase omega, is a letter here. Copies of a letter do not create new outcomes in this sample space, since we do not record the physical identity of the ticket. An event U is a set of letters from Ω; the symbol ⊆ means “is a subset of”.

    P is the rule that assigns a real number P(U), its probability, to each event U. This number has no physical unit. In the table, p with a letter as a subscript denotes the mass of that outcome: p with subscript A equals P({A}), for example. The braces in {A} form the event containing only A; A alone names an outcome. In the diagram, P below a letter denotes this mass.

    R is the event “pink”: R = {A, B, C}. S is the event “marked with a star”: S = {A, B, D}. The symbol ∩ selects the shared letters; the symbol ∪ combines the letters, keeping each only once. Thus R ∩ S = {A, B}. The complement Rᶜ contains the letters of Ω that are not in R; the superscript c is a label, not a numerical power.

    The chance of each ticket is 1/10. Copies of a letter cannot be drawn together in this draw, so their chances add. This gives the masses below. The numbers of tickets are counts; dividing gives probabilities with no unit.

    pA=pB=pF=110pC=pD=210pE=310

  2. State the axioms before calculating

    An axiom is a starting rule. Non-negativity: for every event U, P(U) is greater than or equal to 0. Normalization: P(Ω) = 1, since every observed outcome is in Ω. Additivity: if events are mutually exclusive, meaning they have no outcome in common, the probability of their union is the sum of their probabilities.

    For the events U and V below, ∅ denotes the empty set. The assumption U ∩ V = ∅ rules out any double counting; it must be checked before using the last equality.

    P(U)≥0;P(Ω)=1
    U∩V=∅⇒P(U∪V)=P(U)+P(V)

    The symbol ⇒ is read as “implies”. In its general form, the additivity axiom also applies to an infinite sequence of mutually exclusive events: this is called countable additivity. A countable sequence can be arranged in a numbered list. In our finite sample space, such a list can contain only finitely many non-empty events, so repeating the addition above is enough for our calculations.

    These rules do not require equal masses. On a finite sample space, non-negative outcome masses that sum to 1 define a probability measure: for each event, add the masses of the letters it contains. The events {A}, {B}, {C} are mutually exclusive, even though all their letters are pink.

  3. Check the distribution of all the probability

    Each mass is non-negative. Add the numerators: 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 1 = 10 tickets. The common denominator counts the entire box and does not change from letter to letter.

    P(Ω)=1+1+2+2+3+110=1

    The masses therefore define a consistent probability measure on Ω. They do not prove that tickets in a real box would actually be equally likely: this property remains an assumption about the drawing mechanism.

  4. Calculate a colour probability without assuming equally likely letters

    For R, add the masses of A, B and C. The part {A, B} has mass 1/10 + 1/10 = 1/5; adding C gives 1/5 + 1/5 = 2/5. We add because the draw cannot produce several different letters at once.

    P(R)=1+1+210=2/5=0.40

    Direct check: the tickets marked A, B or C make up exactly this share of the box. The decimal value 0.40 is a probability with no unit, rather than a number of tickets.

    Counting 3 pink letters out of 6 letters would give 1/2. This division would be justified only if the letters were equally likely, each with mass 1/6. Here A has mass 1/10 and E has mass 3/10: that assumption is false. Division by the total number of outcomes is not an axiom.

  5. Derive the empty set probability, complement rule and bounds

    The empty set adds no outcome to Ω and is mutually exclusive with it. Additivity gives P(Ω) = P(Ω) + P(∅). Subtracting P(Ω) from both sides requires P(∅) = 0. This is a consequence of the axioms, rather than a forgotten mass to add to the total.

    U and its complement Uᶜ are mutually exclusive and together cover all of Ω. Therefore P(U) + P(Uᶜ) = 1. Isolating the second term gives the complement rule. Since P(Uᶜ) is non-negative, P(U) cannot exceed 1.

    P(Uc)=1−P(U);0≤P(U)≤1

    In the box, Rᶜ = {D, E, F}. Using the complement gives 1 − 2/5 = 3/5. Calculating from the letters first gives 3/10 + 1/10 = 2/5, then 1/5 + 2/5 = 3/5. The results agree; the total for pink and not pink is 2/5 + 3/5 = 1.

    The symbol ∅ describes impossibility through the absence of outcomes. The equality P(U) = 0 alone does not allow us to conclude U = ∅ in every model: an outcome can be assigned 0 mass. In this particular box, all letters have strictly positive mass.

  6. Handle “or” when the conditions overlap

    S contains A, B and D: P(S) = 1/5 + 1/5 = 2/5. R and S have the letters A and B in common; they are not mutually exclusive. Adding their probabilities directly gives 2/5 + 2/5 = 4/5 and counts each shared mass more than once.

    The sum has counted the region R ∩ S = {A, B} in R and then in S. Its extra copy, with mass 1/5, must be removed. We keep the letters in the union and remove only their double counting.

    P(R∪S)=P(R)+P(S)−P(R∩S)

    The result is 4/5 − 1/5 = 3/5, or 0.60 with no unit. The formula follows from additivity over the disjoint regions in the second diagram; it does not permit adding all conditions indiscriminately.

  7. Check using a different partition and explain what it establishes

    R ∪ S = {A, B, C, D}. Take all of R, then D alone: these regions are mutually exclusive. Their sum is 2/5 + 1/5 = 3/5, matching the result obtained by subtraction. The diagram also gives 1/5 + 1/5 + 1/5 = 3/5: each letter is included only once.

    The complement of this union is {E, F}. Its mass is 1 − 3/5 = 2/5, equal to 3/10 + 1/10 = 2/5. Together with the mass of the union, this gives 1. The masses are within the bounds and all tickets are covered.

    Yet the incorrect value 4/5 was also between 0 and 1. Checking the bounds is therefore not enough to detect double counting. Similarly, checking the total sum does not remove the need to check that each mass is non-negative. These checks establish mathematical consistency; they do not measure the quality of a model of a real draw.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the fractions and start again from the box. Explain what is assumed equally likely and why the letters are not, then reconstruct the axioms, complement rule and union calculation. Justify the subtraction using the regions in the diagram, going beyond simply reciting a formula.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “There are 6 letters, so each has a chance of 1/6.”

    Equal chances are assumed for the physical tickets. The letters do not all have the same number of copies. Look again at A and E: their masses are 1/10 and 3/10, even though each appears only once in the list of possible letters.

    Reread the contents of the box and the first diagram

  • “For “R or S”, I always add P(R) and P(S).”

    Check the intersection first. The letters A and B meet both conditions: their masses would be counted in each event. Remove their extra copy, or partition the union into mutually exclusive regions before adding.

    Revisit “or” and the check using a partition

  • “The sum is 1, so my list must define a probability measure.”

    A negative mass can be offset by an excessive mass elsewhere. Check non-negativity and the total separately, then define event probabilities by adding without duplicates. The diagnostic exercise provides a list with the correct total that is still invalid.

    Examine the proposed distribution and its repair

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Combine distinct letters, then take the complement

Keep the original box. T is the event “the letter is E or F”, so T = {E, F}. Calculate P(T) and P(Tᶜ). Why is addition allowed for E and F? Give the letters for which the complement occurs.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The draw cannot produce the letter E and the letter F at the same time. Add their masses, rather than their names.

Hint 2

The complement contains all letters of Ω that are neither E nor F. Its mass completes the total 1.

Compare with the solution

The events {E} and {F} are mutually exclusive. P(T) = 3/10 + 1/10 = 2/5. We add the masses because the events have no outcome in common.

Tᶜ = {A, B, C, D} and P(Tᶜ) = 1 − 2/5 = 3/5. Direct check: this is the list for R ∪ S, whose mass is 3/5. The mass of T and that of its complement give 1.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify the addition by the absence of a shared letter.
  • Obtain 2/5 and 3/5, with no physical unit.
  • Name the letters in the complement and check the total.

Exercise 2 · Reconstruction on paper

Compare addition with a partition

Without reopening the example, calculate P(R ∪ S) for R = {A, B, C} and S = {A, B, D}. First use the probabilities of R and S, then check by taking R and only the letters of S that are missing from R. Is a proposed value P(R ∪ S) = 4/5 justified by the fact that it stays between 0 and 1?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write down the shared letters before using the probabilities. One extra copy of their mass must be removed from the sum.

Hint 2

Among the letters of S, only D is missing from R. R and {D} are mutually exclusive.

Compare with the solution

R ∩ S = {A, B}, with mass 1/5. P(R) = 2/5 and P(S) = 2/5. Therefore P(R ∪ S) = 2/5 + 2/5 − 1/5 = 4/5 − 1/5 = 3/5.

Another approach: R ∪ S is the disjoint union of R and {D}. Its mass is 2/5 + 1/5 = 3/5. This calculation confirms the first without counting A or B more than once.

The proposed value 4/5 satisfies the bounds but violates the rule for adding the masses of the letters in the union. A bound is a necessary condition, rather than proof of a correct calculation.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify {A, B} before subtracting its mass 1/5.
  • Obtain 3/5 using both partitions.
  • Explain why checking the bounds alone misses double counting.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and repair

A correct sum can hide an invalid mass

For the same sample space of letters, someone proposes replacing the original distribution with: A → 1/10, B → 1/10, C → 1/5, D → 1/5, E → -1/10, F → 1/2. They claim: “The total is enough to validate the distribution.” Check the total and their claim. To return to the masses of the box, can we correct only E? Explain the complete repair.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the masses from A to D, then those of E and F. Next inspect the sign of each mass.

Hint 2

Replacing the mass of E with 3/10 adds an amount to the total. To recover the original box, the same amount must be subtracted from F.

Compare with the solution

The masses from A to D sum to 3/5. For E and F, -1/10 + 1/2 = 2/5. The proposed total is therefore 3/5 + 2/5 = 1. Normalization holds, but E is assigned a negative mass: non-negativity fails. This list does not define a probability measure.

Correcting E alone by replacing its mass with 3/10 would give 3/5 + 3/10 = 9/10 for A to E, then 9/10 + 1/2 = 7/5 for the total. This time, normalization fails.

The increase for E is 3/10 − (-1/10) = 2/5. We therefore subtract 2/5 from F: 1/2 − 2/5 = 1/10. This recovers the original masses of E and F, all non-negative, and a total of 9/10 + 1/10 = 1.

This repair recovers the distribution for our box under the assumption of equally likely tickets. Other distributions could satisfy the axioms without representing these contents. Mathematical consistency and agreement with the mechanism are separate questions.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the total 1 without accepting the mass -1/10 as valid.
  • Name the exact axiom violated in the proposal, then in the repair of E alone.
  • Justify the compensating change to F and check the final total 1.
  • Distinguish a consistent distribution from one suited to the described box.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What rules must a probability measure satisfy, and when can probabilities be added directly?

Probabilities are non-negative, P(Ω) = 1, and the probability of a union of mutually exclusive events is the sum of their probabilities. Mutually exclusive means having no outcome in common. The general axiom allows a countable sequence; on our finite sample space, finite additions are enough.

Recall 2How can we derive P(∅), the complement rule and the upper bound without learning them separately?

Adding ∅ to Ω does not change Ω: additivity requires P(∅) = 0. U and Uᶜ are mutually exclusive and cover Ω, so P(Uᶜ) = 1 − P(U). Its non-negativity requires P(U) ≤ 1.

Recall 3Why does counting 3 pink letters out of 6 fail to give the correct probability in our box?

The letters are not equally likely: they do not have the same number of copies. The physical tickets are equally likely by assumption. We must add the masses of A, B and C: this gives 2/5, instead of 1/2.

Recall 4Why must P(R ∩ S) be subtracted in the probability of R or S?

P(R) + P(S) counts each shared mass in each event. We remove its extra copy while keeping these outcomes in the union: P(R ∪ S) = P(R) + P(S) − P(R ∩ S). The disjoint regions provide an independent check.

Recall 5What does a list of non-negative masses summing to 1 prove, and what does it fail to prove?

It defines a consistent probability measure on the finite sample space if events receive the sum of their outcome masses. It does not prove that these masses describe the real draw. The total alone is not enough: a negative mass can be offset elsewhere.

Recognise normalization without treating it as evidence of use

What you understand
A list of non-negative masses must have a total mass equal to 1. Dividing finite non-negative weights by their strictly positive sum provides this normalization in exact arithmetic. This does not select the right outcomes or validate the model that produced the weights.
