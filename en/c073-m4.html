Level 2 · C073·M4

Factorial design

Your goalBuild a complete, balanced factorial design, count its trials and calculate differences between means while keeping the factors separate.

Before you start
c056-m5 : effect size · c065-m5 : prediction interval · c073-m1 : ANOVA · c073-m2 : multiple comparisons · c073-m3 : nonparametric tests

✿01 See the idea

A workshop wants to compare paint drying times. It can set the temperature to 20 °C or 30 °C, and keep the air still or ventilate it. If the only trials compare the lower temperature without ventilation against the higher temperature with ventilation, a shorter drying time does not tell us which setting made the difference.

A factor is a characteristic that we deliberately vary; its levels are the chosen settings. Here, temperature and ventilation are the 2 factors. A complete factorial design tests each temperature level with each ventilation level. Each combination forms a cell of the design.

The table therefore also includes cells away from its diagonal: low temperature with ventilation, high temperature without ventilation. We can then compare temperatures while keeping ventilation the same, and compare ventilation settings while keeping temperature the same. Complete refers to all the chosen combinations, rather than every possible temperature.

We plan for 2 separate panels per cell: the design is balanced because every cell has the same number of panels. The measured response will be drying time, in minutes. The situation, settings and all the times below are fictional; they do not describe a real paint.

The rule to remember

Cross all the levels, count the combinations and then the independent replicates; keep track of each factor throughout the comparisons.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Prepare the design, then examine the fictional times

The workshop has 8 comparable panels. For each panel, timing starts when paint application ends and stops at the same criterion for a dry surface. We want to cover every combination and compare settings with equal weight given to each level of the other factor.

  1. Define what varies, what is measured and what stays fixed

    A denotes the temperature factor, in °C, with low and high levels: 20 °C and 30 °C. B denotes the ventilation factor, which is categorical, with still and ventilated levels; it has no numerical unit. Ventilation is defined by the same fan setting for all relevant trials.

    The response y is a time, in min. Within each cell, p and q identify the 2 separate panels; the labels p and q do not link panels across cells. We keep the same paint, coating thickness, substrate and target humidity. The stopping criterion is set before taking measurements.

    Each panel goes through the chamber separately, with the planned conditions restored before the next panel. We assume the trials are independent, with no influence from the previous panel. Several panels drying together in the same run would share an exposure: we would need to reconsider the experimental unit before counting them as independent replicates.

  2. Count the cells, then the trials

    L_A and L_B denote the numbers of levels of A and B; R is the number of replicates per cell. C counts the cells and N the trials. These are counts, with no physical unit. Every ventilation setting is planned for each temperature, so the number of cells is a product.

    C=LA×LB=2×2=4

    Each cell then requires R new panels:

    N=C×R=4×2=8

    Check using a row: each temperature receives 2 × 2 = 4 panels; the 2 temperatures require 8 in total. Using a column: each ventilation setting receives 2 × 2 = 4 panels.

  3. Assign treatments before observing responses

    Prepare 2 labels per combination and assign them at random to the panels; also randomize the trial order before taking measurements. The table organizes the combinations; it does not require running an entire row before the next. Otherwise, a drift in humidity as work progresses could systematically coincide with a temperature.

    For each trial, record its panel, temperature, ventilation setting, position in the sequence and time. Random assignment reduces systematic associations with uncontrolled causes; it does not guarantee that all imbalances disappear in a small sample. Our design assumes the chamber can return to the required conditions between trials.

  4. Calculate cell means while retaining observations

    The 8 fictional times are those in the first diagram. For a cell, m denotes the observed mean, in min: add the R times and divide by R. The sum is in min; dividing it by a count still gives min.

    At 20 °C, still air: (60 + 64)/2 = 124/2 = 62 min. At the same temperature, ventilated air: (50 + 54)/2 = 104/2 = 52 min.

    At 30 °C, still air: (44 + 48)/2 = 92/2 = 46 min. At the same temperature, ventilated air: (34 + 38)/2 = 72/2 = 36 min.

    Each mean lies between the times of its panels: this is a simple check for an error in the sum or denominator. Means summarize the cells; retaining individual times is still necessary to examine their variability.

  5. Compare temperatures with ventilation levels equally represented

    m_basse and m_haute are the marginal means for temperature: each row is summarized by giving equal weight to the ventilation levels. The balanced design also gives these means by pooling all the panels in the row directly.

    The marginal means are: m_basse = (62 + 52)/2 = 114/2 = 57 min. m_haute = (46 + 36)/2 = 82/2 = 41 min.

    Δ_A denotes the descriptive main effect of temperature here, defined as high minus low, with equal weight for each ventilation setting. It is expressed in min:

    ΔA=mhaute−mbasse=41−57=-16 min

    The negative sign indicates a shorter mean time at high temperature in these data. It represents neither a percentage nor a gain measured in a real workshop. This mean alone does not tell us whether the contrast is the same for each ventilation setting.

  6. Compare ventilation settings with temperatures equally represented

    m_immobile and m_ventilé are the marginal means for ventilation, obtained by summarizing the columns. m_immobile = (62 + 46)/2 = 108/2 = 54 min. m_ventilé = (52 + 36)/2 = 88/2 = 44 min.

    Δ_B is the difference ventilated minus still, in min, with equal weight for each temperature:

    ΔB=mventilé−mimmobile=44−54=-10 min

    We fixed the direction of each subtraction before interpreting it. These differences are observed summaries: no test or uncertainty interval has been calculated here. The design organizes the experiment; analyzing its uncertainty would require additional assumptions and calculations.

  7. Check calculations against the individual data

    The sum of the times at low temperature is 124 + 104 = 228 min; at high temperature, 92 + 72 = 164 min. The overall sum is 392 min. The grand mean for the 8 panels is therefore 392/8 = 49 min.

    Another approach: the cell means sum to 62 + 52 + 46 + 36 = 196 min. Their mean is 196/4 = 49 min: the results agree because all cells have the same number of panels.

    Check the marginal means: 228/4 = 57 min and 164/4 = 41 min directly recover the temperature means. With different numbers of panels, the mean of all panels and the mean giving equal weight to each cell do not necessarily coincide; the intended comparison would need to be specified.

  8. State what the design allows us to conclude

    Keeping only the diagonal would compare 36 − 62 = -26 min. This difference changes temperature and ventilation together: it cannot attribute the change to a particular factor. The complete design provides exactly the combinations needed to compare them separately.

    Complete means all cells are present; balanced means they have equal numbers of panels; randomly assigned describes the assignment procedure. None of these properties replaces the others. Calculating means assumes neither a normal distribution nor additive effects. A causal interpretation would require, among other things, following the planned assignment, no interference and compliance with the measurement conditions.

    We stop at constructing the design and interpreting it descriptively. The next unit will study interaction, meaning the possibility that a contrast changes depending on the other factor, as well as repeated measures.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results. Redraw the cells, explain the products that give their number and the number of panels, then recover each marginal difference. Why does comparing only the diagonal answer a different question?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I have tried each setting at least once, so my design is complete.”

    Having each temperature and each ventilation setting somewhere is not enough. Every combination is needed. With only low temperature in still air and high temperature in ventilated air, the factors change together; reconstruct the 4 cells and find the missing ones.

    Review the table of combinations

  • “I measure the same panel 2 times and get my replicates.”

    You are repeating the reading of the same treated object. For the design described here, R counts panels subjected to the treatment separately. Reconsider the experimental unit and also check whether several panels share the same chamber run.

    Review the assumptions for each trial

  • “The mean decreases, so every ventilation setting gives exactly the same temperature effect.”

    The marginal difference summarizes ventilation settings with equal weights. It does not guarantee that every local contrast is identical. Keep the cells and their observations; interaction will be studied next. Do not turn this fictional difference into statistical evidence either.

    Reread the definition of marginal differences

The diagonal difference is 36 − 62 = -26 min. The temperature and ventilation factors change together; attributing this difference to temperature alone is unjustified.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What makes the drying design factorial and complete?

Several factors are crossed and every combination of the chosen levels is represented. Here, each temperature is tested with each ventilation setting; having each level somewhere is not enough.

Recall 2How can you recover the numbers of cells and trials without confusing them?

For factors A and B, C = L_A × L_B counts the combinations. With R replicates per cell, N = C × R counts the trials. Here, 2 × 2 = 4 cells, then 4 × 2 = 8 panels.

Recall 3Why does remeasuring a panel not provide a new independent replicate?

The same panel retains the same application of the treatment. A new replicate in the design requires a new unit treated separately; panels in the same run may also share an exposure.

Recall 4What does the marginal temperature difference summarize, and what limitation remains?

It compares the high and low means with equal weight for each ventilation setting: 41 − 57 = -16 min. It guarantees neither the same contrast at each ventilation setting nor a statistically established difference.

Recall 5Why do complete, balanced and randomly assigned describe different properties?

Complete concerns the presence of all cells; balanced concerns their equal numbers of panels; randomly assigned concerns the assignment procedure. A complete table can be unbalanced or run in an order that coincides with a drift.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the drying table and define factors, levels, cell, response and experimental unit. Recover C and then N, explain random assignment and balancing, then calculate a marginal difference, stating its direction and weights. Finish by explaining what the diagonal fails to separate.
