Weights and missing data

Your goalCalculate a weighted average using the available ratings, report the weight covered, and distinguish a missing value from a 0 rating.

Before you start
c004-m5 : rational equations · c013-m5 : recursion · c012-m5 : counterexamples · c009-m5 : reading notes · c020-m1 : score, unit and normalization

✿01 See the idea

You are assessing a room for a workshop. The assessment sheet lists lighting, tables and quietness. Lighting receives 8 points out of 10, and tables receive 6 points out of 10. The quietness box stays blank: no one has assessed it yet.

Before any visit, the group assigned weights of 3, 2 and 5 to these criteria. A weight represents the importance chosen for the calculation; it measures neither the number of people nor the reliability of a rating. Here, quietness was therefore intended to carry as much weight as the other criteria combined.

A blank box does not describe a poor room. To summarize what is known, we choose a precise rule: calculate the weighted average of only the available ratings, using their known weights. The quietness rating remains missing, and its planned weight stays visible in the report.

This scene and all its values are fictional. The ratings are assumed to be comparable, on the same scale, with differences in points that we accept as suitable for combining. The result will summarize the criteria that have ratings; nothing guarantees that the missing criterion resembles them.

The rule to remember

Select the criteria with available ratings, keep their weights in both the numerator and the denominator, then report the weight covered separately. Without a strictly positive sum of available weights, the average cannot be calculated.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A summary of the room with one box still blank

Fictional data: L denotes lighting, with a rating of 8 points and a weight of 3; T denotes tables, with a rating of 6 points and a weight of 2; C denotes quietness, with a planned weight of 5 and a missing rating. All ratings use the scale from 0 to 10 points. Let us calculate what can be reported using only the criteria with available ratings.

  1. Define the symbols and the scope of the calculation

    For a criterion indexed by i, xᵢ is its rating in points and wᵢ is its weight, with no unit. The weights are known, nonnegative and fixed before the ratings are seen. D denotes the set of selected criteria: those whose rating and weight are available. On this sheet, D contains L and T.

    N denotes the sum of the products wᵢ × xᵢ over D; its unit is the point, because the weights have no unit. W is the sum of those same weights, with no unit. If W is strictly greater than 0, the available score S is N divided by W, in points.

    S=NW pour W>0

    This formula is our convention for summarizing the available data. It does not claim that the full rating of the room would be identical. An unknown weight would also prevent us from calculating the contribution of that criterion; replacing it with 1 or 0 would add a rule that would need justification.

  2. Establish the two weight references

    For the average of the available ratings, add the weights of L and T: W = 3 + 2 = 5, with no unit. The weight for quietness does not enter this sum because its rating is missing.

    To describe what is missing, also keep the planned total Wp: 5 + 5 = 10, with no unit. The small p stands for “planned”. Excluding quietness from the calculation using available data therefore erases neither its existence nor its original importance.

  3. Apply each weight to its rating

    Lighting contributes 3 × 8 = 24 points. Tables contribute 2 × 6 = 12 points. Multiplication includes each rating with the importance chosen for it.

    Add these contributions: N = 24 + 12 = 36 points. N is not yet a rating out of 10: it is a weighted sum that still needs to be divided.

    N=wLxL+wTxT=36 points

  4. Divide by the weight actually used

    The numerator uses L and T, so the denominator must use L and T as well. Dividing N by W brings the weighted sum back to the common scale of the ratings.

    S=365=7.2 points

    The fraction 36/5 is exact; the expression 7.2 points is also exact here. The score lies between the selected ratings of 6 and 8 points, as required when weights are nonnegative and have a positive sum.

  5. Check by normalizing the weights first

    We can carry out the division before the multiplications. The normalized weight αᵢ, using the Greek letter alpha, is wᵢ/W for a selected criterion. It has no unit. For L, it is 3/5; for T, it is 2/5.

    αL+αT=3/5+2/5=1

    The normalized contributions are 3/5 × 8 = 24/5 points and 2/5 × 6 = 12/5 points. Their sum gives 36/5 = 7.2 points: we obtain S again by calculating in a different order.

    An additional concrete check: with these integer weights, imagine 3 copies of the rating 8 and 2 copies of the rating 6. Their total is 36 points for 5 copies. This count checks the average; it creates no new opinions.

  6. Report coverage along with the result

    Coverage K compares the available weight with the planned weight: K = W/Wp. It has no unit. Here K = 5/10 = 1/2. This means that the summary uses half the weight originally planned.

    Write: “7.2 points out of 10 for lighting and tables; weight covered 1/2; quietness not assessed.” A high average for this subset does not certify the quality of the whole room.

    K is neither the proportion of criteria counted with equal weights nor a probability that S is correct. Without knowing all the planned weights, we could not put a number on this coverage.

  7. Check what an actual 0 rating would change

    Now suppose that a quietness assessment arrives with a rating of 0 points. This is a variation of the same sheet: its product is 5 × 0 = 0 points, but its weight of 5 does enter the denominator.

    The numerator becomes 36 points and the denominator 10. The complete score is 18/5 = 3.6 points, with coverage of 1. Dividing 36 by 10 from the start would have given this result by silently treating the missing rating as a 0 rating.

    If all ratings are missing, the sums for the available data are N = 0 points and W = 0. The division is undefined: display “score cannot be calculated”. Do not turn this missing result into a rating of 0.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why removing a rating from the summary also requires removing its weight from the denominator. Then reconstruct the difference between coverage, the available score and an actual 0 rating.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A blank box must mean 0.”

    Missing data provide no rating. For the average of the available ratings, remove the criterion from both sums and report what is missing. An actually observed rating of 0 keeps its weight in the denominator, even if its product is 0.

    Compare missing quietness with assessed quietness

  • “I keep the total planned weight so that the average is complete.”

    Your numerator describes only the ratings received. Here, dividing it by the weight of all criteria implicitly assigns a 0 rating to quietness. Use the selected weight for the available score, and the planned weight for coverage.

    Review the two weight references

  • “The available score is enough to judge the whole room.”

    It summarizes lighting and tables. Coverage of 1/2 indicates that quietness, which is important in the original rule, remains unknown. Keep this scope in the statement of the result; normalization does not create the missing data.

Compare with the solution

The simple average gives (8 + 6)/2 = 14/2 = 7 points. It gives lighting and tables equal importance, whereas their weights are 3 and 2.

The normalized weights are 3/5 and 2/5; their sum is 1. The intended score is 3/5 × 8 + 2/5 × 6 = 24/5 + 12/5 = 36/5 = 7.2 points.

Check: both scores stay between 6 and 8 points. The weighted score is closer to the lighting rating, which has the larger weight. Checking this position does not remove the need to check the weights.

  • You check that the normalized weights total 1.
  • You explain that changing the weights changes the rule, without claiming that the quietness rating has been recovered.

A 0 value is available. Its weighted product is 0, but its weight enters the sum of weights.

Coverage now compares the weights of all criteria with available ratings with those of all planned criteria.

Compare with the solution

The contribution of quietness is 5 × 0 = 0 points. So N = 24 + 12 + 0 = 36 points: the numerator is unchanged.

The sum of weights becomes 3 + 2 + 5 = 10, with no unit. The score is 36/10 = 18/5 = 3.6 points. Coverage is 10/10 = 1.

The result falls because a low rating actually enters the average with its weight. It lies between 0 and 8 points. The initial score of 7.2 points and this new rating do not describe the same set of available data.

Hint 1

For the first sheet, the numerator is 0, but the sum of selected weights equals the known weight for quietness.

Hint 2

For the empty sheet, the sum of available weights is 0. Does the rule S = N/W still make sense?

Hint 3

A display can show a known value or indicate that no value can be calculated. It must allow this difference to be identified.

Compare with the solution

With quietness alone, N = 0 points and W = 5. The division is defined: S = 0/5 = 0 points. Coverage is 5/10 = 1/2. Report: “Quietness rated at 0 points; available score 0 points; coverage 1/2; lighting and tables ratings missing.”

With the empty sheet, N = 0 points and W = 0. Division by 0 is impossible. Coverage is 0/10 = 0, but no score can be calculated. Report: “No ratings available; coverage 0; score cannot be calculated.”

The same 0 numerator therefore does not lead to the same status: you need to look at the denominator. A rating of 0 says something about the criterion assessed; the absence of a score says nothing about the quality of the room. The page must preserve this difference.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What changes when a rating of 0 has actually been observed, compared with a blank box?

The observed rating enters the selected set: its product is 0, but its weight stays in the denominator. A missing rating enters neither of the two sums in the summary of available data.

Recall 2How can you reconstruct the score from the available data?

Select the criteria whose rating and weight are known. Add their rating × weight products, then divide by the sum of those same weights, provided it is strictly positive. Keep the unit of the ratings.

Recall 3Why report coverage with the score, and what does it measure?

It is the ratio of the weight used to the total planned weight, when that total is known and positive. It indicates the scope of the available score, without guaranteeing that the missing criteria resemble it or that the score is true.

Recall 4Can a missing weight automatically be replaced with 1?

That would change the calculation rule. You must report the missing weight or have an explicitly justified fallback. The same replacement value is not suitable for every quantity.

Recall 5When does a 0 numerator give a 0 score, and when does it give no score?

If the available weight is positive, a 0 numerator gives a 0 score. If the available weight is 0, the division is undefined: the score still cannot be calculated, even if 0 coverage can be reported.
