Level 1 · C045·M2

Partial fractions

Your goalDecompose a fraction with distinct linear factors, integrate it, and check the resulting volume for a fictional pump.

Before you start
c034-m5 : average value · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c045-m1 : integration by parts

✿01 See the idea

A pump fills a container, but its flow rate decreases as it fills. You want to know the volume delivered between the start and 2 min. Multiplying the initial flow rate by the duration would assume that the pump always ran at the same rate: you need to accumulate the small volumes over time.

The flow rate law in our fictional scenario has a product in the denominator. In this form, its antiderivative is not immediately apparent. The idea of partial fractions is to rewrite this fraction exactly as a sum of simpler fractions. Each will have a single factor in the denominator, which will lead to logarithms.

The second diagram already shows the form we want. The negative term is needed to reconstruct the law: it does not represent a second pump removing water. This is an algebraic separation of the same flow rate, which stays positive throughout the scenario.

The durations and flow rate are entirely fictional; no real pump measurement is used. We consider only a fraction whose numerator has a lower degree than its denominator, with two distinct linear factors. Repeated factors and quadratic factors require other forms of decomposition.

The rule to remember

Find the coefficients, combine the fractions over a common denominator to prove the identity, then integrate the simple fractions over an interval where they are defined.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

How much has the pump delivered?

The fictional pump follows q(t) = Q f(t/τ), with Q = 6 L/min, τ = 1 min and f(x) = 1/[(x + 1)(x + 3)]. Let us calculate the volume between t = 0 min and t = 2 min, without replacing the varying flow rate with a constant.

  1. Define the quantities, units, and interval

    t is the elapsed time, in minutes. τ is a strictly positive reference duration, so x = t/τ is dimensionless. Q is the flow rate scale in litres per minute; f is a dimensionless function; q(t) is the actual flow rate. V will denote the volume delivered, in litres. In this example, x runs from 0 to 2.

    We assume that the law is exact in this scenario, that the pump runs continuously, and that there are no leaks. The factors x + 1 and x + 3 are strictly positive on this interval. The fraction is undefined at x = −1 and at x = −3, values outside the scenario.

    With t = τx, a small duration dt equals τ dx: the conversion factor τ is needed so that flow rate multiplied by duration gives a volume. The symbol ∫ denotes continuous accumulation between the limits; dx specifies the variable of integration.

    V=∫02τq(t)dt=Qτ∫02f(x)dx

  2. Find an identity rather than agreement at a single point

    A and B are the unknown real coefficients, with no units. We want an equality that holds for every x where the fractions are defined. Since the factors in the denominator are linear and distinct, we try a constant over each one.

    1(x+1)(x+3)=Ax+1+Bx+3

    Multiply by the common denominator, which is nonzero on the domain. The first term receives the factor x + 3; the second receives x + 1. The identity we need becomes:

    1=A(x+3)+B(x+1)

    Expanding the right side gives (A + B)x + (3A + 1B). To recover the constant numerator 1, we therefore need A + B = 0 and 3A + 1B = 1. This comparison also proves that the proposed form is sufficient if these conditions hold.

  3. Isolate the coefficients using factors that vanish

    In the polynomial identity obtained after multiplication, set x = −1: the term containing B vanishes. We get 1 = A(−1 + 3) = 2A, so A = 1/2 = 1/2.

    Next, set x = −3 in this same identity: the term containing A vanishes. This leaves 1 = B(−3 + 1) = (-2)B, so B = -1/2. The coefficients are opposites.

    These substitutions apply to the polynomial, which is defined for every real number. We have never evaluated the original fraction at the excluded values. We now check that the coefficients we found do give an identity.

    f(x)=1/2x+1+-1/2x+3

  4. Combine the fractions and check the flow rate

    The coefficient of x is 1/2 + (-1/2) = 0. The constant term is 3 × 1/2 + 1 × (-1/2) = 3/2 + (-1/2) = 1. The reconstructed numerator is therefore exactly 1, for every allowed x: this verifies the decomposition.

    An additional check at x = 1: the original fraction equals 1/(2 × 4) = 1/8. The simple fractions give 1/4 + (-1/8) = 1/8. The corresponding flow rate is Q × f(x) = 6 × 1/8 = 3/4 L/min, or 0.75 L/min.

    Agreement at this point alone would not have proved the identity. The preceding expansion proves it; the check at a single point helps detect a sign or copying error.

  5. Integrate the simple terms

    A useful reminder: ln is the natural logarithm. For a real constant c, dimensionless here, an antiderivative of 1/(x + c) is ln|x + c| on any interval where x + c is nonzero. The bars denote absolute value; they make the argument of the logarithm positive. The derivative of ln|x + c| is indeed 1/(x + c).

    On our interval, the arguments are already positive. F denotes a dimensionless antiderivative of f, and C is a dimensionless constant of integration. We can therefore write:

    F(x)=12ln(x+1)−12ln(x+3)+C

    By linearity of differentiation, F′ gives back the simple fractions, hence f. The prime denotes the derivative with respect to x. There is no need to multiply the logarithms: we decomposed the fraction into a sum, which we integrate term by term.

  6. Evaluate at the limits and keep the exact result

    The integral equals F(2) − F(0); C cancels in this difference. Using ln u − ln v = ln(u/v) for strictly positive u and v, the ratio at the upper limit is 3/5 = 3/5; at the start it is 1/3 = 1/3. Here, the letters u and v denote any positive arguments of the logarithm.

    The factor Qτ equals 6 L/min × 1 min = 6 L. With the coefficient A, this gives 6 × 1/2 = 3 L in front of the logarithm. The quotient of the ratios is (3/5) ÷ (1/3) = 9/5.

    V=3 L×ln(3513)=3ln(9/5) L

    This is an exact volume. The argument of the logarithm is a dimensionless ratio; the litres come from the outside factor. Keeping the logarithm avoids unnecessary rounding.

  7. Check the physical meaning and the antiderivative

    On the interval, each positive factor in the denominator increases with x, so the flow rate decreases. It goes from 2 L/min to 2/5 L/min. Over 2 min, a flow rate that always lies between these values gives a volume between 2/5 × 2 = 4/5 L and 2 × 2 = 4 L. These are bounds, not a second exact value.

    The argument 9/5 is greater than 1: its logarithm is positive, as the volume delivered must be. To check the bounds without a calculator, we can use (u − 1)/u ≤ ln u ≤ u − 1 for u ≥ 1. Here, u − 1 = 4/5, then (u − 1)/u = 4/9. After multiplying by 3 L, we get 4/3 L ≤ V ≤ 12/5 L, well within the previous bounds. This inequality comes from bounding 1/s between 1/u and 1 when integrating it from 1 to u; s is simply the dimensionless variable of integration.

    Finally, differentiating QτF(t/τ) with respect to time gives Qτ × f(t/τ)/τ = q(t). This check recovers the flow rate law with its unit L/min and also checks the conversion factor. No pole is crossed; no question of an improper integral arises here.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the coefficients, explain why a product in the denominator becomes a difference of fractions here. Reconstruct the polynomial identity, then explain why the negative substitutions are allowed at this stage and why the final volume is in litres.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I split the product in the denominator while keeping the numerator over each factor.”

    You need to find A and B. Putting 1 over each factor would produce a numerator 2x + (1 + 3), which depends on x. Reconstruct the numerator, then require A + B = 0 and 3A + 1B = 1.

    Revisit the identity and combine its fractions

  • “To find A, I evaluate the fraction at x = −1.”

    The fraction is undefined at this point. First multiply by the common denominator on the allowed domain, then look for a polynomial identity. It is in this polynomial, defined everywhere, that you replace x with −1. Then verify the identity and keep the excluded values of the fraction.

    Reread why the polynomial allows the substitution

  • “I subtract the arguments of the logarithms, or keep only the upper limit.”

    A difference of logarithms becomes the logarithm of a quotient if the arguments are positive. The integral requires F(end) − F(start): the final ratio 3/5 must be divided by the initial ratio 1/3, giving 9/5.

    Evaluate at the limits again for a shorter duration

Exercise 1 · Identification and justification

Choose the coefficients for the same law

For the pump in the example, one person proposes A = 1/2 and B = 1/2, another A = 1/2 and B = -1/2. Choose by expanding the numerator after combining the fractions over a common denominator. Explain why a single numerical check would not be enough.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The coefficient of x in the reconstructed numerator is A + B.

Hint 2

The original numerator contains no term in x: its contribution must be 0. Check the constant term too.

Compare with the solution

With coefficients of the same sign, A + B = 1: a term in x remains, so the identity is false. With A = 1/2 and B = -1/2, their sum is 0.

The constant term is 3 × 1/2 + 1 × (-1/2) = 1. The numerator is therefore exactly 1, as required. This equality of polynomials holds for all x; the combined fraction remains subject to the exclusions x = −1 and x = −3. A check at a single point does not replace this proof.

Check your reasoning

  • You reject the proposal with coefficients of the same sign using the coefficient of x.
  • You explicitly check the coefficient of x and the constant term for the proposal you choose.
  • You distinguish an identity from a check at a single point, while keeping the domain restrictions.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and checking

Recover the antiderivative without the lesson

Hide the example. Recover an antiderivative F of f on x ≥ 0, then calculate F′(1). Deduce the flow rate of the pump at t = 1 min. Justify removing the absolute values on this interval.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

An antiderivative of 1/(x + c) is ln|x + c| on an interval that excludes −c.

Hint 2

The coefficients multiply the logarithms. To check, differentiate, then combine the terms over a common denominator.

Compare with the solution

We get F(x) = (1/2) ln(x + 1) + (-1/2) ln(x + 3) + C. The arguments are strictly positive for x ≥ 0, so the absolute values are unnecessary.

The derivative is (1/2)/(x + 1) + (-1/2)/(x + 3). At x = 1, it gives 1/4 + (-1/8) = 1/8, with no units. Since τ = 1 min, t = 1 min does correspond to x = 1.

The flow rate is Q × f(1) = 6 × 1/8 = 3/4 L/min, or 0.75 L/min. Do not give F(1) as the flow rate: F is an antiderivative, and the flow rate uses f.

Check your reasoning

  • You keep the signs of the coefficients and add a constant of integration.
  • You justify the positive arguments and verify F′ = f.
  • You distinguish F′(1) = 1/8 from the flow rate 3/4 L/min.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same scenario

The pump stops earlier

The pump follows the same law but stops at t = 1 min. Recover the volume delivered since the start. Someone proposes 3 ln(1/2) L. Identify what is missing, correct the calculation, and compare with the volume delivered over the full duration.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate the ratio (x + 1)/(x + 3) at the new upper limit and at the start.

Hint 2

You still need to subtract the logarithm at the lower limit from the logarithm at the upper limit. Their difference becomes the logarithm of a quotient.

Compare with the solution

At x = 1, the ratio is 2/4 = 1/2. At the start, it is still 1/3. The proposal omits subtracting F(0); it even gives a negative logarithm for a positive volume delivered.

The quotient of the ratios is (1/2) ÷ (1/3) = 3/2. The corrected volume is therefore 3 ln(3/2) L. The factor 3 L has not changed, since Q, τ, and the coefficients are the same.

We have 1 < 3/2 < 9/5. The logarithm is increasing on the positive real numbers: this volume is positive and smaller than the full volume 3 ln(9/5) L. This is consistent with a positive flow rate and an earlier stop.

Check your reasoning

  • You write the difference between the values of the antiderivative at the two limits.
  • You obtain the quotient 3/2 and the exact volume 3 ln(3/2) L.
  • You check the sign and the comparison with the full duration.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the purpose of partial fractions, and how do you prove the decomposition for our pump?

To rewrite a fraction exactly as a sum of simpler fractions. After combining them over a common denominator, you must recover the original numerator as a polynomial identity; a single numerical check is not enough.

Recall 2Why can you use x = −1 to find A without evaluating the fraction there?

After multiplying by the denominator on the allowed domain, we seek a polynomial identity defined for every real number. We substitute −1 into it to isolate A, then verify the identity. The fraction remains undefined at −1 and at −3.

Recall 3What antiderivative works for 1/(x + c), and when can you remove the absolute values?

ln|x + c| + C on an interval that excludes −c, where c and C are constants. You can write ln(x + c) when x + c is strictly positive. Differentiating must give back the fraction.

Recall 4Why does the full volume involve ln(9/5) and litres?

We subtract the values of the antiderivative at the limits: the logarithms give the quotient (3/5)/(1/3) = 9/5. QτA = 3 L supplies the unit; the logarithm takes a dimensionless ratio as its argument.

Recall 5What checks complement the algebraic identity in this scenario?

Differentiate the expression for the volume to recover the flow rate with the factor τ, then check the sign and the bounds between 4/5 L and 4 L. A correct formula does not validate the law for a real pump.
