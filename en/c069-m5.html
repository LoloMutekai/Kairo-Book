Level 1 · C069·M5

Congruences and Euclid

Your goalSolve a simple congruence using the Euclidean algorithm, then verify the smallest number of presses that reaches a space on the board.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

An imaginary circular board has 12 spaces, numbered from 0 to 11. A marker starts on space 0. Each button press moves it forward by 5 spaces in the same direction, wrapping around to the start after a full turn. What is the smallest number of presses needed to reach space 7?

The movement counter and the visible space give different information. After 3 presses, the total movement is 15 spaces; the marker is on space 3, because it has completed a turn of 12 spaces. Discarding full turns preserves the position but loses the number of turns.

Saying that integers are congruent modulo 12 means that they have the same remainder when divided by 12. They therefore represent the same space. The word modulo specifies the size of the cycle, called the modulus. Ordinary equality would also preserve the total number of spaces travelled.

To find the number of presses without trying every possibility, we will look for an integer coefficient that turns the step into a movement equivalent to 1 space. The Euclidean algorithm will let us construct this coefficient. The board is a setting for calculation, with no actual measurement or assumption about speed.

The rule to remember

Removing a full turn preserves the space. To undo a step by integer multiplication, first find its greatest common divisor with the modulus.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Reaching space 7 with step 5

The start is 0, the modulus n = 12, the step a = 5, and the target b = 7. We seek the smallest nonnegative integer x such that ax is congruent to b modulo n. Each press applies exactly the same step; no press is lost and the direction never changes.

  1. Define remainders and congruence

    All symbols here denote integers, with no physical unit. n counts the spaces on the board and is greater than 1; a is strictly positive and counts the spaces advanced per press; x is nonnegative and counts the presses. b and r are space indices. The calculations use these counts, without converting lengths or durations.

    For a nonnegative total A and a strictly positive divisor B, Euclidean division gives A = qB + r with 0 ≤ r < B. q is the integer quotient, and r is the remainder. The bound on r prevents us from retaining an extra full turn. This expression is unique: distinct remainders in this interval cannot differ by a nonzero multiple of B.

    u≡v(mod n)⇔u−v=kn

    u and v are arbitrary integers; k is an integer that may be negative. The symbol ≡ means “is congruent to”, and ⇔ means that each statement implies the other. This definition is equivalent to requiring the same canonical remainder between 0 and n − 1. In the board setting, 15 − 3 = 12: the total movement and the visible space are indeed congruent.

  2. Express the target without dividing too soon

    5x≡7(mod 12)

    The product ax counts all the spaces advanced. We therefore allow ax = b + kn: the full turns are accounted for by k. Dividing b by a using ordinary arithmetic does not give an integer number of presses and ignores these turns.

    Adding the same integer to both sides or multiplying both sides by the same integer preserves a congruence: if u − v = kn, then c(u − v) = (ck)n for every integer c. This property will justify our multiplication. Division by an arbitrary integer has no such guarantee.

  3. Run the Euclidean algorithm down to the last remainder

    A divisor of A is a positive integer that divides A with no remainder. The greatest common divisor, written GCD(A, B), is the largest positive integer that divides both A and B. If A = qB + r, the pairs (A, B) and (B, r) have the same common divisors: a divisor of A and B divides A − qB = r; conversely, a divisor of B and r divides qB + r = A. This is the invariant of the Euclidean algorithm.

    Divide 12 by 5: the quotient 2 leaves 12 − 10 = 2, which is less than 5. Then divide 5 by 2: the quotient 2 leaves 5 − 4 = 1. Finally, 2 = 2 × 1 + 0. The last nonzero remainder is 1, so GCD(12, 5) = 1.

    The nonzero remainders are strictly decreasing positive integers, so the process stops. When the remainder is 0, the common divisors are those of the last divisor. Here, the GCD is 1: we say that the step and the modulus are coprime. This does not mean that each number is itself prime.

  4. Work backwards through the subtractions to produce the unit

    The line that produced 1 can be written as 1 = 5 − 2 × 2. Replace the remainder 2 with its expression 12 − 2 × 5. This substitution connects the result to the original data.

    1=5−2×(12−2×5)

    Distributing the sign and the factor gives 5 − 2 × 12 + 4 × 5. Collecting the terms in 5 gives the following identity, called Bezout’s identity:

    1=5×5−2×12

    The coefficients are therefore 5 for the step and -2 for the modulus. An independent check of the substitution gives 25 + (-24) = 1. This equality of integers is more precise than simply landing on the same space.

  5. Identify the modular inverse

    The term 2 × 12 is an integer number of turns. The preceding identity therefore says that 5 × 5 ≡ 1 (mod 12). An integer c such that ca ≡ 1 (mod n) is called an inverse of a modulo n; c is an integer coefficient, rather than the ordinary fraction 1/a.

    Why check the GCD before looking for c? If ca − 1 is a multiple of n, every common divisor of a and n must also divide 1: the GCD must equal 1. Conversely, working backwards through the Euclidean algorithm when the GCD is 1 gives integers c and d such that ca + dn = 1, and hence an inverse c. Here c = 5 and d = -2; these letters are coefficients with no unit.

  6. Multiply by the inverse, then remove full turns

    Multiply the target congruence by 5. The coefficient of x becomes congruent to 1, leaving x ≡ 5 × 7 = 35 (mod 12). The multiplication is reversible here: multiplying this new congruence by the step recovers the previous one, using the inverse identity.

    35=2×12+11

    To obtain the number of presses in the canonical interval, remove 2 turns in the calculation of this number: 35 − 24 = 11. Here these turns describe the reduction of an integer modulo 12; the marker’s physical turns will be counted in the next check.

  7. Check the space and justify the minimum

    With 11 presses, the marker travels 5 × 11 = 55 spaces. Now 55 = 4 × 12 + 7: removing 48 spaces corresponding to full turns leaves space 7, which equals the target 7. We check the press count in the original equation, rather than only in an intermediate transformation.

    Every solution satisfies x ≡ 11 (mod 12), so it can be written as x = 11 + kn with integer k. Since 0 ≤ 11 < 12, a negative k makes x negative and a positive k increases it. The smallest possible number of presses is indeed 11. Other numbers of presses reach this space, but none is smaller and nonnegative.

    This calculation teaches only how to solve a linear congruence with an invertible step, and how to detect an obstruction when the GCD does not divide the target. Systems of several congruences and cryptographic applications would require another course.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The total movement must be the space number.”

    The counter preserves the turns, while the space number discards them. After 3 presses, travelling 15 spaces gives space 3, because 15 = 12 + 3. Find the remainder in the allowed interval.

    Review the path that wraps around to the start of the board

  • “I can divide a congruence as if it were an ordinary equality.”

    You must justify the operation for the integers and the modulus. Here the Euclidean algorithm supplies an integer inverse because the GCD is 1. Multiplication by this inverse replaces division; the ordinary quotient of the target by the step does not answer the question.

    Review the Euclidean algorithm, the identity, and then the inverse

  • “The Euclidean algorithm ends at 0, so the GCD is 0.”

    A 0 remainder signals the stopping point. The GCD is the last nonzero remainder: 1 for step 5, and 4 for step 8. Start from the last division and ask which integer still divides the values in the pair.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and proof of impossibility

Changing the step can make the target unreachable

On the same board, the step becomes 8 and the target returns to 7, starting at 0. Can it be reached? Calculate the GCD using the Euclidean algorithm and explain why no number of presses works. Does the absence of an inverse alone prove impossibility for an arbitrary target?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with 12 = 1 × 8 + 4.

Hint 2

If 8x − 7 = k × 12, every common divisor of the step and the modulus must divide the target.

Compare with the solution

The Euclidean algorithm gives 12 = 1 × 8 + 4, then 8 = 2 × 4 + 0. The GCD is 4.

The total 8x is divisible by 4, and removing a multiple of 12 preserves this divisibility. A target that can be reached must therefore be divisible by 4. But 7 = 1 × 4 + 3, with a nonzero remainder. Space 7 is unreachable.

The absence of an inverse does not make every target unreachable: space 8 is reached after 1 press. To rule out our target, the decisive proof is that the GCD does not divide the target, rather than simply that it differs from 1.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify the last nonzero remainder, 4, as the GCD.
  • Justify why this GCD would have to divide the target.
  • Show the remainder 3 and distinguish the absence of an inverse from the impossibility of reaching a particular target.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does u ≡ v (mod n) mean, and what information is discarded on our board?

The difference u − v is an integer multiple of n; u and v have the same canonical remainder. The space is preserved, while the number of turns is discarded. The modulus n must be specified.

Recall 2Why can we replace the pair (A, B) with (B, r) if A = qB + r? Why does the Euclidean algorithm stop?

A common divisor of A and B divides A − qB = r; a divisor of B and r divides qB + r = A. The common divisors are therefore preserved. The nonzero remainders decrease among the positive integers; at the stopping point, the last nonzero remainder is the GCD.

Recall 3How does Bezout’s identity provide an inverse, and what condition must be checked?

When GCD(a, n) = 1, working backwards through the Euclidean algorithm gives ca + dn = 1 with integers c and d. Then ca ≡ 1 (mod n), so c is an inverse of a. If the GCD is greater than 1, it would divide ca and dn but not 1: no inverse exists.

Recall 4After finding the inverse c of the step a, how do you solve ax ≡ b (mod n) and verify the smallest number of presses?

Multiplying by c gives x ≡ cb (mod n). Reducing cb to the interval from 0 to n − 1 gives the smallest nonnegative number of presses. Then check that ax − b is a multiple of n. With a step that is not invertible, the GCD must at least divide b; otherwise the target is impossible.
