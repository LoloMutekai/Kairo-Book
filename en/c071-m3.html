Level 2 · C071·M3

Degrees and percolation thresholds

Your goalCount the appropriate degrees, reconstruct a local percolation threshold and explain the limited implications of exceeding it.

Before you start
c070-m5 : minimum spanning tree · c029-m5 : Bayes · c030-m5 : Poisson · c063-m5 : synthetic dataset and external validity · c071-m1 : paths and multiplicative propagation · c071-m2 : Noisy-OR and independence

✿01 See the idea

An alert can reach relay V through A or B. Looking only at the links entering V gives an incomplete picture: A and B themselves receive links. Their degrees describe this neighbourhood, while the transmission probabilities describe what passes along the paths.

The in-degree of a vertex is the number of arrows pointing towards it. Its out-degree counts the arrows leaving it. In our fictional scenario, V has 2 incoming links; its incoming neighbours A and B have 2 and 4 respectively. The diagram shows only the immediate neighbourhood of V.

A threshold is a comparison value. Percolation seeks to characterise the conditions that allow large-scale propagation in a network model. Here, we study a candidate local rule that summarises the degrees around V. Exceeding its threshold does not demonstrate a cascade across the network.

All values are fictional. We keep the same scenario and vary its parameters in the exercises. None of the probabilities is a measured frequency or a setting validated for real relays.

The rule to remember

Count the incoming arrows, calculate the mean of the squared degrees of the neighbours, then apply the stated local rule. Distinguish this threshold from the transmitted risk and from proof of a cascade.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The same relay, with a distinct threshold and risk

The fictional directed graph has exactly the links S→A, U→A, S→B, W→B, X→B, Y→B, A→V and B→V. It has neither loops nor multiple links. S is certain to emit the alert; U, W, X and Y remain inactive. Each link leaving S transmits with probability p = 0.50; those from A and B to V transmit with probability w = 0.50. The transmissions are independent, given that S has emitted the alert. Let us calculate the threshold for the neighbourhood of V, then compare it with the risk of the alert arriving.

  1. Define the counts and their scope

    For any vertex u, d⁻(u) denotes its in-degree; the sign − is a label for “incoming”, rather than a subtraction. The incoming neighbours of V are the vertices connected to V by an arrow pointing towards V: here A and B. Let k denote their number. We have k = d⁻(V) = 2, d⁻(A) = 2 and d⁻(B) = 4.

    The degree counts all links in the defined graph, including those from parents that are currently inactive. It counts neither just the alerts received nor just the active paths. These counts have no physical unit; their square is not a probability.

  2. Calculate the mean of the squares

    Let M denote the mean of the squared in-degrees of the neighbours of V. The sum runs over these neighbours, each counted once. In our simple graph, there are k of them. M is a second moment, meaning a mean of squares; a variance would require first centring the degrees around their mean.

    M=d−(A)2+d−(B)2k

    The squares are 2 × 2 = 4 and 4 × 4 = 16. Their sum is 20. Divide by the number 2 of neighbours: M = 20 / 2 = 10. Squaring gives more weight here to the high degree of B.

  3. Construct the denominator of the local rule

    We choose the following candidate rule: D = M − k, then t = k / D when D is clearly positive. D is the auxiliary denominator; t is the raw threshold, with no unit. Here D = 10 − 2 = 8, so t = 2 / 8 = 1/4.

    D=M−k;t=kD

    This rule uses the degree of V in the numerator and the mean of the squared degrees of its neighbours in M. These are different populations. We therefore do not call both of them “mean degree”. We study this choice as a local mechanism; it provides no proof of a global percolation threshold.

  4. Apply the bounds and check the division

    The convention sets a lower bound L = 0.01 and an upper bound H = 0.99. The retained threshold τ, pronounced “tau”, is t clamped to this interval: replace it with L if it is too small, with H if it is too large, and keep t otherwise. Here 0.01 < 0.25 < 0.99; τ = 1/4 = 0.25 with no unit.

    τ=min(H,max(L,t))

    min takes the smallest value; max takes the largest. Reverse check: 1/4 × 8 = 2, recovering the numerator. If D were non-positive or numerically too close to 0, division would be avoided and this convention would return H. A neighbourhood with no incoming links would also return H. This fallback value is a calculation choice, rather than proof of network resistance.

  5. Reconstruct the propagation already studied

    q_A and q_B denote the probabilities of arrival at V through A and B, conditional on S being certain to emit the alert. Each is a product of transmission probabilities: q_A = 0.50 × 0.50 = 1/4; q_B = 1/4. All these probabilities have no unit and lie between 0 and 1.

    R denotes the probability of at least one arrival. Since the transmissions along the paths are independent in this scenario, we multiply their failure probabilities, then take the complement. This is the Noisy-OR aggregation from the previous unit.

    R=1−(1−qA)(1−qB)

    The complements are 3/4 and 3/4. The probability of no arrival is 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16. Thus R = 1 − 9/16 = 7/16, or 0.4375 with no unit.

  6. Check the risk using another operation

    We can also add the arrival probabilities, then subtract the case counted twice. The probability of simultaneous arrival is q_A × q_B = 1/16 under our independence assumption. The sum of the probabilities is 1/2; subtracting the intersection gives 1/2 − 1/16 = 7/16, exactly the previous R.

    Consistency check: R is at least as large as the probability 1/4 for a single path, and at most equal to the sum 1/2. This check would detect, in particular, adding without subtracting the overlap.

  7. Interpret the threshold crossing without adding a guarantee

    The risk 0.4375 exceeds the local threshold 0.25. Our comparison R > τ therefore flags the neighbourhood. This means neither that V is certain to receive the alert nor that a specific fraction of the network will be affected. Here τ is a reference value calculated from the degrees; R also depends on the transmissions.

    Independence is an assumption of our scenario, rather than a consequence of having distinct paths. We fixed the emission by S and assumed that the remaining transmissions were independent. A common failure affecting these transmissions would change the calculation of R. Analysis of cycles and double counting belongs to the later unit devoted to them.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results. Explain why the degree of V, the second moment M, the risk R and the threshold τ describe different things. Then reconstruct the calculation of τ and state exactly what R > τ allows you to report.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I use the out-degree of V or the number of alerts received for k.”

    Return to the list of arrows: k counts those entering V, whether or not they transmit. The degrees of A and B come from their own incoming links. The direction of the arrows and the scope of the count must be defined before any calculation.

    Review the neighbourhood and the direction of the arrows

  • “The mean of the squares is the square of the mean, or the variance.”

    You must square each degree before adding. To obtain the variance, then subtract the square of the mean. The first exercise compares these quantities using the same degrees.

    Compare the means in the first exercise

  • “The threshold has been exceeded, so a real cascade is proven.”

    The result is an alert from the chosen local rule. It depends on the graph, the weights and the assumptions. Neither the upper bound nor the name “percolation” proves how the network behaves. The milestone separates the code that is present from the missing proof.

Exercise 1 · Distinguish and calculate

Three summaries of the same neighbourhood

Using the degrees 2 and 4 of A and B, calculate the mean m, its square and the mean of the squares M. Then calculate the variance as the mean of the squared deviations from m. Which quantity enters the threshold in the example?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For M, square the degrees before taking the mean. For the variance, first centre each degree around m.

Compare with the solution

The sum of the degrees is 6; m = 6 / 2 = 3. Its square is 9. The mean of the squares is M = (4 + 16) / 2 = 10. These results are distinct.

The variance is the mean of the squared deviations d⁻(A) − m and d⁻(B) − m; it is 1. Another check: M minus the square of m gives 10 − 9 = 1. The threshold uses M = 10, without subtracting the square of m. Confusing M with the variance would change the denominator and even its sign.

Check your reasoning

  • Square at the correct stage.
  • Distinguish the mean, the square of the mean, the second moment and the variance.
  • Identify M as an input to the local rule.

Exercise 2 · Recalculate a variant

Neighbour B loses incoming links

In the same graph, remove X→B and Y→B. B keeps S and W as parents; its degree becomes 2. Nothing else changes. Calculate M, D, the raw threshold, then the clamped threshold. Does the risk R from the example still exceed this threshold?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The degree of V remains 2. Recalculate the squares of the degrees of its neighbours.

Hint 2

A raw threshold above H must be clamped to H.

Compare with the solution

The squares are now 4 and 4. Their sum is 8, giving M = 4 and D = 4 − 2 = 2. The raw threshold is 2 / 2 = 1.

This threshold exceeds H = 0.99: the retained threshold is therefore 0.99. The risk remains 7/16, because the active paths S→A→V and S→B→V keep their weights; the removed links came from inactive parents. R is below the new threshold: our rule no longer flags it. This illustrates its sensitivity to the structural neighbourhood; this result proves no real improvement in safety.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the degree of V distinct from that of B.
  • Present the raw threshold, then apply the bound.
  • Explain why the risk and the threshold do not change together.

Exercise 3 · Calculate and challenge a conclusion

Same degrees, weaker transmissions

Return to the initial graph. The transmissions from S to A and B keep the probability 0.50. Those from A and B to V change to 0.25, still independently. Someone claims: “The degrees are identical, so the threshold will still be exceeded.” Calculate the new risk and assess this claim.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The threshold depends on the degrees and remains unchanged; the path contributions depend on the weights.

Hint 2

Compare the exact fractions before rounding the risk.

Compare with the solution

Each path now contributes 1/2 × 1/4 = 1/8. Its complement is 7/8. The product of the complements is 49/64; the risk becomes 1 − 49/64 = 15/64, or approximately 0.2344 with no unit.

The threshold remains 1/4. The difference τ − R is 1/64, which is strictly positive: the risk is below the threshold. The claim is false. The degrees determine the threshold of this rule, rather than the transmission probability. The exact calculation settles the comparison even when rounded decimals seem close.

Check your reasoning

  • Recalculate the path products and the aggregation of the complements.
  • Keep the threshold from the initial example.
  • Refute the claim using an exact comparison and name the independence assumption.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the in-degree count, and why is counting alerts insufficient?

It counts all arrows pointing towards the vertex in the defined graph. An inactive link still counts towards the degree; an alert received is a propagation event.

Recall 2How do you reconstruct M, and why is it different from a variance?

Square each degree of the incoming neighbours, add the squares, then divide by the number of neighbours. The degrees are not centred. The variance subtracts the square of their mean from this mean of the squares.

Recall 3How do you get the local threshold from M and the degree k, and when should you avoid division?

Calculate D = M − k, then t = k / D if D is clearly positive, and clamp t to the stated bounds. Under the convention studied, a denominator that is non-positive or too close to 0, or an empty neighbourhood, returns the upper bound. This fallback does not prove real resistance.

Recall 4Why does R exceeding the local threshold fail to demonstrate a real cascade?

R depends on a transmission model and independence assumptions; the threshold is a local rule based on degrees. Comparing them produces a signal from this model. It demonstrates neither large-scale propagation, nor execution of a program, nor validity on a real network.
