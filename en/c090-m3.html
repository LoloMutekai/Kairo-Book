Level 2 · C090·M3

Trend

Your goalDescribe a linear trend over a dated time window, calculate its slope, and distinguish this summary from fluctuations and a forecast.

Before you start
c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c037-m5 : controlled cleaning · c090-m1 : timestamping · c090-m2 : autocovariance

✿01 See the idea

The water level in a tank is recorded each day at the same time. It goes from 10 cm to 15 cm, drops back to 12 cm, then reaches 16 cm and 17 cm. Is the drop in the middle enough to cancel out the overall rise?

A trend describes how the underlying level changes at the chosen time scale. Fluctuations are the deviations around this change. For this short window, we choose a straight line as a summary: it can rise even if some readings fall. A trend therefore does not require a rise at every step.

Choosing a straight line is a descriptive assumption, rather than the discovery of a law governing how the tank fills. A different window or persistent curvature may call for a different summary. Here we will learn to calculate and critically examine this linear summary, without covering the seasonality or stationarity discussed in the following units.

The readings and the tank are fictional. All heights use the same reference level and the same instrument, assumed to give comparable readings. Days are elapsed durations of 24 h; there are no missing readings or changes of procedure in this scene.

The rule to remember

State the window, fit the underlying level, then examine the deviations: a positive slope summarizes a rise without dictating every change or guaranteeing the future.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What rise summarizes the tank’s time window?

The pairs (time in days; level in cm) are (0; 10), (1; 15), (2; 12), (3; 16), (4; 17). Let us fit a straight line to the 5 readings, giving them equal weight.

  1. Define the summary and its limits

    The index i identifies a reading, from 0 to 4. tᵢ is its elapsed time, in days; yᵢ is its observed level, in cm. n = 5 is the number of readings and has no unit. T(t) = a + bt denotes the fitted line: a is its level at time 0, in cm, and b is its slope, in cm/d. Its residual eᵢ = yᵢ − T(tᵢ), in cm, measures the reading’s deviation from this line.

    We choose a and b by least squares: minimize the sum of the squared residuals eᵢ, in cm². This defines precisely what “fit” means. The window and times are given, with distinct dates and comparable readings. An event changing the tank’s operating regime would make this single summary questionable.

    The descriptive calculation requires neither normality nor independence of the readings. However, interpreting the line as an underlying mean level requires a model in which the deviations contain no omitted change in the mean. Temporal dependence must also be considered before a test or an uncertainty interval; we calculate neither here.

  2. Locate the centre of the window

    t̄, read as “t bar”, is the mean of the times; ȳ, read as “y bar”, is the mean of the levels. The symbol Σ means to add over all the readings. The mean time is 2 days, at the centre of the regularly spaced dates. The sum of the levels is 10 + 15 + 12 + 16 + 17 = 70 cm, so ȳ = 70/5 = 14 cm.

    With a constant a in the fit, the line passes through the centre (t̄; ȳ). We therefore seek its slope about (2 days; 14 cm), rather than a line determined solely by the endpoint readings.

  3. Calculate the slope using deviations from the centre

    b=∑i(ti−t¯)(yi−y¯)∑i(ti−t¯)2

    In the numerator, a height above the mean contributes positively if it occurs after the centre, and negatively if it occurs before. The denominator measures the spread of the times; it is strictly positive because the dates differ. Their units are cm·d and d²: the quotient is indeed in cm/d.

    The centred times are −2, −1, 0, 1, 2 days. Their sum is 0: the term containing ȳ cancels out in the numerator. Grouping the symmetric dates gives 2 × (17 − 10) + (16 − 15) = 14 + 1 = 15 cm·d. The height of the central reading affects the mean level, even though its weight in this slope is 0.

    The denominator adds the squared centred times. The endpoints each contribute 4 d², their neighbours each contribute 1 d², and the centre contributes 0 d²: 4 + 1 + 0 + 1 + 4 = 2 × (4 + 1) = 10 d². We obtain b = 15/10 = 3/2 cm/d, or 1.50 cm/d. The line therefore describes a rise over this window.

  4. Recover the line’s starting level

    a=y¯−bt¯=14−32×2=11 cm

    The product b × t̄ represents a rise of 3 cm between time 0 and the centre. Subtracting it from ȳ gives a = 11 cm. This fitted level differs from the first reading, 10 cm: minimizing deviations over the window does not require the line to pass through its first point.

    T(t)=11+32t

    In this numerical expression, t must be given in days and T(t) is obtained in cm. The slope is constant in the chosen line; the readings are not assumed to change by exactly this amount each day.

  5. Subtract without erasing the readings

    At the successive dates, the line gives 11; 12.5; 14; 15.5; 17 cm. For example, on day 3: 11 + (3/2) × 3 = 11 + 4.5 = 15.5 cm.

    Next, subtract the fitted value from each reading. The residuals are -1; 2.5; -2; 0.5; 0 cm. On day 1, 15 − 12.5 = 2.5 cm: the reading is above the line. A residual compares with the fitted level at the same date, rather than with the previous day.

    The decomposition is exact for each reading: yᵢ = T(tᵢ) + eᵢ. Keeping the residuals allows us to examine what the line does not describe. Calling them “noise” would not be enough to establish that they are random or independent.

  6. Check the result using another property

    The line must pass through the centre: 11 + (3/2) × 2 = 14 cm. The sum of the residuals must also be 0: (-1) + 2.5 + (-2) + 0.5 + 0 = 0 cm.

    Let us also check the sum of the products centred time × residual: (−2) × (-1) + (−1) × 2.5 + 0 × (-2) + 1 × 0.5 + 2 × 0 = 0 cm·d. The linear component associated with time has indeed been removed from this sample.

    These equalities check the fit, rather than the model’s relevance: they follow from least squares with a constant. They do not guarantee the absence of curvature, dependence between residuals, or measurement error. A straight line does not reveal the cause of the change in water level either.

  7. State a conclusion with the right scope

    Conclusion: over days 0 to 4, the fitted linear trend rises by 1.50 cm per day, despite the fall observed between days 1 and 2. This sentence states the model, the window, and the unit.

    Extending the line beyond the last reading would be an extrapolation: assuming that this summary remains valid for dates not yet observed. Here we have neither future observations nor an evaluation of such a forecast. A slope calculated from these readings is not a measured gain or a guarantee for tomorrow.

Explain it in your own words

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The level fell once, so there is no upward trend.”

    The change between readings concerns a single step; the slope summarizes the window. Compare the lavender points with the line, then distinguish a change between dates from a residual at a given date.

    Compare the readings and the line

  • “The slope must be the last height minus the first, divided by the elapsed time.”

    That quotient defines the slope between the endpoints. Here it is 1.75 cm/d; least squares gives 1.50 cm/d. Revisit the centred products: the interior readings contribute to the fitting criterion.

    Recalculate the slope about the centre

  • “The residuals are centred, so the line must be right for what comes next.”

    Centring is a property imposed by the fitting procedure on the readings used. Examine the deviations and test any extrapolation separately. An answer obtained after reading the solution remains an assisted answer; reading it validates neither the model nor an attained level of mastery.

Exercise 1 · Application

A fall and a positive trend

For the same tank, compare the level on day 2 with the level on day 1, then with the line on day 2. Give the change between readings and the residual, with their unit. Does this contradict the positive slope?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A change subtracts the previous reading; a residual subtracts the fitted level at the same date.

Hint 2

On day 2, the line passes through the centre of the window, at 14 cm.

Compare with the solution

Observed change: 12 − 15 = -3 cm over the step of one day. Residual: 12 − 14 = -2 cm. The first compares two dates; the second compares the observation and the line at the same instant.

The slope remains 1.50 cm/d over the window. An increasing linear trend allows deviations that make some readings fall; the observed fall does not make the calculation contradictory. It should also remain in the report.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain -3 cm and -2 cm using the correct reference for each.
  • Distinguish change, residual, and slope, without replacing each reading with a value on the line.

Exercise 2 · Change of unit

The tank does not speed up when the clock changes

Express time in hours elapsed since the same starting point. One day lasts 24 h. Calculate the slope in cm/h, keep the intercept at time 0, then recover the fitted level on day 3. Explain why the numerical slope changes while the readings stay the same.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

If h denotes the number of elapsed hours, t = h/24 in the formula written using days.

Hint 2

Spread the rise in one day over its 24 hours, then replace day 3 with 72 h.

Compare with the solution

The hourly slope is (3/2)/24 = 1/16 cm/h, or 0.0625 cm/h. The constant remains 11 cm because time 0 does not change. Let h be the number of elapsed hours. The same line can therefore be written as 11 + (1/16)h, with the result in cm.

Day 3 corresponds to 3 × 24 = 72 h. The fitted rise is (1/16) × 72 = 4.5 cm; the level is 15.5 cm, identical to 15.5 cm in the calculation using days.

The coefficient describes a rise per unit of time. A shorter time unit gives a smaller number. Comparing slopes without their units could wrongly suggest that the tank is changing differently.

Check your reasoning

  • Divide the daily slope by 24 and state cm/h.
  • Keep 11 cm at the start and recover 15.5 cm at the same physical instant.
  • Explain the equivalence through the change of unit, without changing the heights.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

Does a positive indicator mean that the tank level is rising?

For these same readings, someone calculates the mean of the absolute values of successive changes, then calls it a “trend”. Calculate this indicator and the slope between the endpoints. Compare them with our fitted slope. Why can the indicator based on absolute values not tell whether the tank level is rising or falling?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The absolute value keeps the magnitude and removes the sign: a fall contributes positively.

Hint 2

The 5 readings give 4 transitions. For the quotient between endpoints, keep the sign and divide by the total duration.

Compare with the solution

The changes are 5, -3, 4 and 1 cm. Their absolute values are 5, 3, 4 and 1 cm. The indicator is therefore (5 + 3 + 4 + 1)/4 = 13/4 = 3.25 cm per transition.

The slope between the endpoints is (17 − 10)/(4 − 0) = 1.75 cm/d. Our fitted slope is 1.50 cm/d. These quantities answer different questions: the mean magnitude of the steps, the net change per duration, then the best straight line according to the stated criterion.

Reading the same heights in reverse order would preserve all the step magnitudes while reversing the overall direction. The absolute indicator therefore does not convey that direction. Since the intervals here all last one day, its number is also a mean magnitude in cm per day; with irregular dates, a mean per transition would no longer be a rate per day.

Check your reasoning

  • Count 4 transitions and obtain 3.25 cm per transition.
  • Obtain 1.75 cm/d between endpoints and distinguish it from the fitted 1.50 cm/d.
  • Explain the loss of direction through the removal of signs, and specify the role of the time intervals.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Does an isolated fall contradict an increasing linear trend?

No. The trend summarizes how the underlying level changes over a window; fluctuations can make some readings fall. State the model and the window.

Recall 2How do you recover the fitted slope, and what is its unit?

Add the products of the centred times and centred levels, then divide by the sum of the squared centred times. For this tank: cm·d divided by d², giving cm/d. The constant is then the mean level minus the slope times the mean time.

Recall 3What does a residual compare, and what check does its centring provide?

It subtracts the line from the reading at the same date. Its 0 sum checks the fit with a constant on the data used; it proves neither independence, absence of curvature, nor future validity.

Recall 4Why does the mean of absolute changes not give the direction of a trend?

The absolute value removes the signs. Reversing the order of the readings keeps the magnitudes but reverses the direction. With irregular dates, distinguish change per transition from change per unit of time as well.

Recall 5What is missing before a fitted line can become a defensible forecast?

A justification for extending the model, checks appropriate to temporal dependence, and an evaluation on separate future observations. The descriptive slope alone provides neither that evidence nor the cause of the change.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Start again from the dated readings and their units. State the window and the choice of a linear model; reconstruct the centre, slope, constant, and residuals. Then explain why a local fall, a magnitude of change, and a future forecast are distinct from the fitted trend.
