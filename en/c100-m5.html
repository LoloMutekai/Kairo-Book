Level 2 · C100·M5

Learning curves

Your goalConstruct training and validation errors as a function of the number of examples, then propose a next experiment without turning the curve into a promise.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You want to predict the duration of a bike ride along the same route. Your model starts with a few recorded rides. It looks excellent when you assess it on those rides, but makes larger errors on the ones you set aside. What happens to this gap when you give it more examples?

A learning curve answers this by changing the number of training examples. At each size, the model is fitted again, then its error is measured on its own examples and on a separate validation set. A lower error is better. The horizontal axis counts examples: it counts neither optimization iterations nor computing time.

In our fictional example, the model predicts a single constant duration: the mean of the rides used to fit it. It ignores wind and traffic. This deliberately simple choice lets us check every point by hand. The durations and errors on this page are exercise data, with no personal measurements or claims of real benefits.

We keep the same validation rides throughout the comparison. The thin line in the second diagram represents training; the thick line represents validation. Read their heights and their separation together. A small separation is not enough if both errors remain too large for the intended use.

The rule to remember

Change the training size, refit using the same procedure, evaluate separately, then read both errors and their gap. The points guide an experiment; they do not guarantee how the curve continues.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Recalculate every point for the bike rides

Set aside V = (18, 20, 22, 24) minutes. Training starts with (18, 18), adds (22, 22), then another 4 rides of 22 minutes. The sets are nested: each size keeps the observations from the previous one. Repeated durations refer to distinct rides, never the same ride shared with V.

  1. Fix what is being compared

    n is the number of rides used to fit the model, with no physical unit. Tn is their set; V is the validation set, of size m = 4. yᵢ is the duration of the ride indexed by i, in minutes. μn is the duration predicted by the model fitted on Tn, in minutes. The symbol ∑ means to add the terms for all rides in the indicated set; |S| denotes the number of observations in S.

    The fitting rule is the arithmetic mean. It minimizes the sum of squared errors among constant predictions, with no penalty on that constant. At each n, this mean is recalculated using only the durations in Tn. The mean from the previous fit is not retained.

    μn=1n∑i∈Tnyi

    To avoid any ambiguity, read the mean as: the sum of training durations divided by n. The protocol keeps the model family, fitting rule, error measure and V fixed. For a ridge or lasso model, the regularization settings would also be fixed throughout this curve.

  2. Fit using the available durations

    With n = 2, the sum is 18 + 18 = 36 minutes, so μn = 36/2 = 18 minutes. With n = 4, the added durations contribute 2 × 22 = 44 minutes: sum 80, mean 20 minutes.

    With n = 8, training contains 2 rides of 18 minutes and 6 of 22 minutes. Their sum is 36 + 132 = 168 minutes. The prediction becomes μn = 168/8 = 21 minutes. The model size stays the same: only the amount of data has changed.

  3. Define an error that is comparable across sizes

    En(S) is the mean squared error of model μn on the nonempty set S. Here S denotes Tn or V. Subtract the prediction from each duration, square each difference, add the results, then divide by the number of durations evaluated. Squaring prevents errors with opposite signs from cancelling out.

    En(S)=1|S|∑i∈S(yi−μn)2

    En(S) is in square minutes. It is neither a mean duration nor a percentage of correct predictions. The denominator is n for training and m for validation. Comparing raw sums would also change the number of terms: we compare means.

  4. Work through the intermediate size in full

    For n = 4, the prediction is 20 minutes. The training differences, in ride order, are (-2, -2, 2, 2) minutes. The squares are (4, 4, 4, 4) square minutes. Their sum is 16 square minutes; En(Tn) = 16/4 = 4.00 square minutes.

    On V, reuse this same prediction without any further fitting. The differences are (-2, 0, 2, 4) minutes; the squares are (4, 0, 4, 16). Their sum is 24 square minutes; En(V) = 24/4 = 6.00 square minutes.

    The validation minus training gap is 6 − 4 = 2 square minutes. An independent check of training: all durations are the same distance of 2 minutes from the mean, so each square is 4. The mean of these squares must give 4.

  5. Assemble the other points and check them

    At n = 2, the training durations equal the prediction: sum of squares 0, mean error 0. Validation gives a sum of squares of 56, so 56/4 = 14 square minutes. At n = 8, the sums are 24 and 20 respectively: the means are 3.00 and 5.00 square minutes. The exercises let you reconstruct these sums.

    nentraînementvalidation2014446835

    The error columns are in square minutes. As another check of the last validation result, its set is symmetric around 21 minutes: opposite differences have the same square. Adding them in pairs must give 20. Finally, each mean error must be positive or equal to 0 and recover its sum when multiplied by the number of observations evaluated.

  6. Interpret without blindly extending the curve

    Validation drops from 14 to 6, then to 5 square minutes. The observed decreases are 8 then 1 square minutes, over intervals of different sizes. This slowdown suggests measuring other sizes; it does not demonstrate a plateau. The gap between the curves is 14, 2, then 2 square minutes: it does not decrease at every point.

    Training rises from 0 to 4, then falls back to 3. With finite samples, no monotonicity of the observed curves is guaranteed. The initial error was 0 only because the durations in a tiny sample were identical.

    In general, low training error and clearly higher validation error, with a gap that persists across repetitions, suggest high variance: more representative data or stronger regularization are options to compare. Two errors that are close but too high point towards an overly limited family, missing variables or noise. These are diagnostic hypotheses, not a measured decomposition of bias and variance. Our constant model cannot use wind information, even with more rides.

    Generalizing beyond the exercise would require observations and validation that represent future use. Repeating with several admissible samples reveals the spread of the points. If the rides have temporal dependence, respect time order and avoid duplicates across sets. Any preprocessing is fitted only on Tn. The final test set remains reserved: using it to choose sizes or settings would remove its role as an independent assessment.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the points. Reconstruct the procedure for a given size, explain why the model must be refitted and why V remains separate. Then distinguish what the observed decrease establishes from what it leaves unknown.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The horizontal axis counts optimizer iterations.”

    Name what varies: here n counts distinct rides used for fitting. Adding iterations on the same rides studies optimization convergence; it does not provide new points on this curve of error against sample size.

    Reread the axes and the protocol in the first diagram

  • “I recalculate the mean using validation to predict it better.”

    Validation would no longer be kept out of fitting. Keep the mean calculated on Tₙ, then evaluate errors on V using that unchanged mean. Apply the same separation to preprocessing.

    Revisit the validation calculation at the intermediate size

  • “The curves get closer: the model must be good and more data will be enough.”

    Also look at their heights and compare them with a stated requirement. Two high errors can meet. The observed curve alone does not distinguish noise, missing variables and an unsuitable family; test a hypothesis using the same protocol.

What should you measure before deciding?

Hint 1

Separate the numerical finding, the extrapolation and the criterion for acceptable error.

Hint 2

Start by repeating the curve with a fixed procedure. Then compare another procedure using the same admissible splits.

Compare with the solution

The observed decreases are 8 then 1 square minutes, but they cover different changes in sample size. We have neither a demonstrated limit, nor a defined threshold for acceptable error, nor a measure of the variability of the points. The next point is unknown. The segment in the diagram does not predict it.

First define an acceptable error for the intended use, using the same measure. To study the contribution of data, keep the constant model and evaluate additional sizes across several representative selections, with separate validation. Compare the spread and heights of the errors, respecting time order if the intended use requires it.

To study the limits of the constant model, prepare a second procedure that uses information available at prediction time, then compare its curves using the same splits. Fit transformations only on each training set. Do not change the data, measure and splitting rule at the same time: you would no longer be able to attribute the difference. Reserve the final test for the chosen procedure.

If a persistent gap remains with low training error, the variance hypothesis warrants an experiment. If both errors plateau at an excessive height, also examine the limits of the variables and family, and the noise in the durations. Our few points alone cannot settle the question.

  • Distinguish an observed decrease from an unknown future error.
  • Require a criterion for the intended use and repetitions before claiming that the data are sufficient.
  • Propose a comparison that keeps validation and test out of fitting.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do the curves in this unit vary, and what do their heights represent?

They vary the number of training examples. Each height is the mean squared error of the refitted model, evaluated on its training set or on separate validation. Here, the error unit is the square minute.

Recall 2What must be done again and what must stay fixed at each size?

Refit the model using the training subset. Keep the procedure, its settings, the measure and the validation protocol; here V is fixed. Preprocessing learns only from the current subset.

Recall 3How should you read the heights of the errors and their separation together?

Low training error with higher validation error and a persistent gap suggests variance. Two errors that are close but excessive suggest looking for a limited family, missing variables or noise. The curve alone does not demonstrate their cause.

Recall 4Must the observed curves always move in the same direction?

No. Finite samples can make the means fluctuate. In the example, training error rises then falls again; a trend guarantees neither every point nor a future error.

Recall 5Why does repeated validation not replace a reserved final test?

Choices guided by validation progressively use its information. The final test kept aside allows an independent assessment of those choices, provided its sample and protocol match the intended use.

Reconstruct the reasoning
