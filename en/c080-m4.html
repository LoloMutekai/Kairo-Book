Action potential

Your goalExplain the rise and fall of an action potential, calculate voltage changes, and distinguish a return to rest from readiness for a new signal.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a small region of an axon, the extension of a neuron that carries an electrical signal. One electrode is inside, the other in the fluid outside. They compare the electrical potentials on either side of the membrane: this difference is the membrane voltage. At rest, the inside is at -70 mV relative to the outside in this example. The millivolt, abbreviated mV, is a unit of voltage.

The membrane has channels, proteins that allow certain ions to pass through. An ion carries an electrical charge. Sodium, written Na⁺, and potassium, written K⁺, each carry a positive charge. Concentration gradients and electrical forces determine the direction of their movement when their channels open. Voltage-gated channels change state according to the membrane voltage.

A disturbance, which we will call a stimulus, first makes the inside less negative: this is depolarization. If it is enough to initiate regenerative opening of sodium channels, sodium enters, causing further depolarization and promoting more channel opening. This feedback produces the rapid rise of an action potential, a brief excursion in voltage. Then sodium entry decreases and potassium exit becomes dominant: the voltage falls.

We will follow a single fictional trace in an axon whose action potential relies mainly on these sodium and potassium channels. The resting voltage, threshold, peak, and times are chosen for reading and calculation; they are neither constants for all neurons nor a reading taken from a person. Time t will be expressed in milliseconds, abbreviated ms. The first diagram shows voltage at the same location over time, rather than the path of an ion along the axon.

The rule to remember

Sodium entry sustains the rise; sodium channel inactivation and potassium exit allow the fall. Channel availability matters as much as voltage when triggering a new action potential.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Read the trace and explain each change

Our fictional electrode gives the following successive readings: at t = 0 ms, -70 mV; at 1 ms, -55 mV; at 2 ms, +30 mV; at 3 ms, -70 mV; at 4 ms, -80 mV; at 5 ms, -70 mV. The membrane has initially recovered. Only in this state do we set the firing threshold at -55 mV. The ionic gradients are assumed to be sufficiently maintained during this event.

  1. Set the convention before calculating

    V denotes electrical potential, and the subscript m means “membrane”. V for the inside and V for the outside are the electrode potentials. We choose the outside as the reference at 0 mV. All these quantities are expressed in mV. A negative membrane potential means that the inside potential is lower than the outside potential; it does not mean that the entire contents of the cell are negatively charged.

    Vm=Vintérieur−Vextérieur

    For any change ΔV, the Greek letter Δ is read as “change”: subtract the starting value from the final value. The words for starting and final values serve as labels, and ΔV remains a voltage in mV.

    ΔV=Varrivée−Vdépart

  2. Measure the gap from rest to threshold

    Initially, the potential is -70 mV; at threshold, it is -55 mV. We want the increase needed, so we use final value minus starting value. Subtracting a negative value here amounts to adding its opposite.

    ΔV=-55−(-70)=15 mV

    Reverse check: -70 + 15 = -55 mV. The voltage increases while remaining negative: depolarizing does not necessarily mean becoming positive. The threshold corresponds to the onset of regenerative feedback, rather than crossing 0 mV. In a real cell, its value also depends on the state of the channels and on stimulation.

  3. Link the rise to sodium entry

    Between threshold and peak, voltage-gated sodium channels activate rapidly. The electrochemical gradient favours sodium entry here: positive charges enter and depolarization sustains activation. Electrochemistry combines the effects of concentrations and voltage.

    Change from threshold to peak: 30 − (-55) = 85 mV. To obtain the amplitude from rest, use a different starting value: 30 − (-70) = 100 mV. The peak at +30 mV and the amplitude of 100 mV therefore answer different questions.

    Check by adding the parts: 15 + 85 = 100 mV. Under comparable conditions, a triggered action potential has an approximately characteristic amplitude: this is the all-or-none principle. Doubling the initial disturbance does not double this amplitude. This rule does not claim that signals from different cells or states are identical.

  4. Explain the fall and its sign

    Sodium channels become inactivated: they are temporarily unavailable, even if the voltage favours their activation. Voltage-gated potassium channels open more slowly. Potassium leaving carries positive charges out; the membrane repolarizes, meaning that it returns towards more negative voltages.

    From the peak to the first return to rest: -70 − 30 = -100 mV. The negative sign indicates a decrease. Its magnitude is 100 mV. Check: 30 + (-100) = -70 mV.

    The sodium-potassium pump maintains the gradients over time by consuming energy. The rapid fall during this event is mainly due to changes in channel conductance: how easily the channels let ions pass through. It does not require the pump to suddenly exchange all the ions that moved.

  5. Go below rest, then recover

    Potassium channels do not all close again instantly. Their prolonged opening can lead here to -80 mV, a voltage below rest: this is the hyperpolarization that follows the action potential. Its difference from rest is -80 − (-70) = -10 mV.

    As the potassium channels activated by depolarization close again, the voltage returns towards its resting value. From the trough to rest, the change is -70 − (-80) = 10 mV. Channels open at rest continue to let ions pass through: these background exchanges and the maintained gradients allow this return. The trace gives neither the number of ions that moved nor the energy consumed.

  6. Distinguish voltage from channel state

    Just after firing, enough sodium channels remain inactivated to prevent a new action potential: this is the absolute refractory period. In this physiological regime, increasing the stimulus is not enough. Their return to an available state requires repolarization and time.

    Next, some sodium channels have recovered, while potassium channel opening may remain high: a new signal becomes possible but requires stronger stimulation. This is the relative refractory period. The trough can contribute to it; its depth is not the only cause.

    The first passage through -70 mV, at t = 3 ms, therefore does not prove that all channels have recovered. Our voltage values alone do not establish when the refractory periods end. The biological delay cannot be read from numerical equality with the resting voltage.

  7. Check the trace and explain where propagation occurs

    From rest to peak and then to the first return, 100 + (-100) = 0 mV. From rest to trough and then to the final resting value, -10 + 10 = 0 mV. The net change is therefore 0 + 0 = 0 mV, the same as the final voltage minus the initial voltage. This 0 net change cancels neither the past event nor its ionic exchanges.

    In an axon, movements of charge create local electrical currents. Those from a depolarized region can cause an available neighbouring region to trigger an action potential in turn. The signal thus regenerates from region to region; the same sodium ions do not travel the entire length of the axon. The region that has just fired is refractory, which limits its immediate re-excitation during ongoing propagation. Our recording at a single location measures no propagation speed.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “As long as the voltage is negative, the membrane is not depolarizing.”

    From -70 to -55 mV, it gains 15 mV. Depolarizing here means making the inside less negative. The sodium entering carries a positive charge; the sign of the voltage does not give the sign of the ions entering.

    Review the convention and the threshold calculation

  • “The pump immediately returns the voltage to rest after the peak.”

    Find sodium channel inactivation and potassium exit in the falling phase. The pump maintains the gradients over time; it does not replace the rapid change in channel opening during this signal.

    Reread the sodium and potassium flows

  • “A stronger stimulus necessarily produces an action potential 2 times as high.”

    Separate the depolarization that leads to firing from the amplitude of the triggered signal. The all-or-none principle concerns this signal in the same membrane state. Also check its availability: during the absolute refractory period, strengthening the stimulus is not enough.

Exercise 3 · Critique a conclusion

Same voltage, same response?

Compare the initial resting state at t = 0 ms with the first return to -70 mV, at t = 3 ms. An additional piece of information specifies that, at this first return, enough sodium channels remain inactivated to prevent firing again. Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Voltage is a quantity; the available or inactivated state of a channel is a separate piece of information.

Hint 2

The additional information lets you name a period. The curve alone does not supply this information.

Compare with the solution

The same potential of -70 mV is compatible with different channel states. Initially, they are available. At the first return, the added information establishes an absolute refractory period: the stimulus does not trigger another action potential under the stated conditions.

With the curve alone, availability must remain unknown. You would need information on channel recovery or a test of re-excitability, meaning the ability of this region to produce a signal again after stimulation. A voltage that has returned to rest does not replace this evidence.

After partial recovery, a relative refractory period may still require a stronger stimulus. Neither its duration nor the required stimulus can be calculated from the voltages in our trace alone.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does a negative membrane voltage mean, and how do you calculate its change?

With the inside-minus-outside convention, the inside potential is lower than the outside potential. The change is final value minus starting value, in mV. A voltage can increase while remaining negative.

Recall 2Why can sodium entry produce a self-sustaining rise?

Depolarization activates voltage-gated sodium channels. Positive charges entering cause further depolarization, which promotes their activation. The threshold marks the onset of this feedback in a given membrane state.

Recall 3What allows the fall and the trough after the peak?

Sodium channels become inactivated, and the delayed opening of potassium channels favours positive charges leaving. Prolonged potassium channel opening can bring the voltage below rest. The pump maintains the gradients over time.

Recall 4What does the all-or-none principle say, and what limit must be stated?

Under comparable conditions, a triggered firing event has an approximately characteristic amplitude; it is not proportional to stimulus strength. Channel state, gradients, and cell properties limit this comparison.

Recall 5Why is returning to the resting voltage not enough to prove recovery?

Some sodium channels may remain inactivated. The absolute period prevents firing again; during the relative period, firing becomes possible again with stronger stimulation. Voltage alone does not fully describe channel state.
