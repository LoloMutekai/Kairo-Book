Level 3 · C170·M4

Reflection

Your goalUse reflection of a path after its first hit of a level to count exactly the probability of hitting that level on a symmetric random walk of 6 steps, then read the analogous Brownian statement and identify what is still assumed without proof.

Before you start
c169-m5 : optional stopping under assumptions · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c170-m1 : construction and properties · c170-m2 : increments · c170-m3 : quadratic variation

✿01 See the idea

An imaginary counter starts at the 0 mark on a ruler with arbitrary units. At each clock tick, it moves up one unit or down one unit, each with the same probability 1/2, independently of the previous ticks. After 6 ticks, we check an imaginary alarm: has the counter hit level 2 at any time? This "at any time" is hard to count path by path, whereas the final position is easy to count.

Reflection connects these two questions. Take a path that hits the level and then ends below it. Starting from its first visit to the level, replace each upward step with a downward step and each downward step with an upward step. The new path is the mirror image of the original path across the level: it ends as far above the level as the original ended below it. The two paths have the same number of steps, so they have the same probability.

Each path that hits the level and ends below it therefore corresponds to exactly one path that ends above it. Counting the former is equivalent to counting the latter, and the final position is enough to describe them. This walk is a discrete working model for the Brownian motion studied in this chapter. We do not prove the passage to the limit here: the final Brownian statement is accepted without proof, with its assumption.

The values in this scene are fictional. They serve to count paths, rather than to describe a real alarm or a validated setting.

The rule to remember

After the first hit of the level, reflect the rest of the path across it: hitting the level and then ending below it corresponds to ending above it. For the walk, endpoints exactly on the level are their own mirror images and must not be counted twice.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

An alarm on a walk of six steps

The imaginary counter takes 6 independent steps of 1 unit upward or downward, with equal probabilities. What is the probability that it hits level 2 at least once? We count this using reflection, then check the result.

  1. Set out the symbols, units and assumptions

    Xᵢ is the displacement at tick i, in ruler units: it equals 1 for an upward step or −1 for a downward step. Sk is the position after k ticks, starting at 0. Mn is the highest position reached during the n ticks. The level a is a strictly positive integer; here a equals 2 units. The time τ, in ticks, is the first time at which Sk equals a.

    Sk=X1+⋯+Xk,Mn=max{Sk:0≤k≤n},τ=min{k:Sk=a}

    Assumptions: the displacements are independent, each equal to 1 or −1 with probability 1/2; the number of steps n is fixed in advance. A path is a sequence of 6 signs; there are 64 such paths, because each step doubles their number: 8 paths after three steps, then 8 × 8 = 64 after six. They are all equally likely, each with probability 1 out of 64. The event "τ = k" can be determined by looking only at S up to k: τ is a stopping time, in the sense of the unit on optional stopping. This property will be essential at the reflection step.

  2. Count where the paths end

    A path with j upward steps and n − j downward steps ends at 2j − n. The number of such paths is the number of ways to choose the upward steps among the n steps. With 6 steps: endpoint 6 for 1 path; endpoint 4 for 6 × 1 = 6 paths (a single downward step, 6 possible places); endpoint 2 for 6 × 5 divided by 2, giving 15 paths (choose the 2 downward steps among 6); endpoint 0 for 6 × 5 × 4 divided by the product 3 × 2 = 6, giving 20 paths (choose the 3 upward steps among 6). The rest follows by symmetry.

    fin-6-4-20246chemins1615201561

    Immediate check: endpoints below level 2 account for 1 + 6 + 15 + 20 = 42 paths, and those at or above the level account for 22 (next step). The total 42 + 22 must give 64.

  3. Add what the final position already shows

    A path that starts at 0, moves in steps of 1 unit and ends at level 2 or higher must pass through level 2: it cannot jump over it. All these paths therefore hit the level. Their number is 15 + 6 + 1 = 22.

    P(Sn≥a)=2264

    Of these, 7 end strictly above the level: this is 22 − 15, because 15 paths end exactly on it.

  4. Reflect a path after its first hit

    Take the path with successive positions 0, 1, 2, 1, 0, 1, 0. It hits level 2 for the first time at tick 2 and ends at 0. Define its reflection: the same initial section up to τ, then positions symmetric about the level.

    S~k=Sksi k ≤ τS~k=2a−Sksi k > τ

    After τ, the positions 1, 0, 1, 0 become 2 × 2 minus the position, giving 3, 4, 3, 4. The reflected path therefore ends at 2 × 2 − 0 = 4: above the level, as stated. Three properties make the operation useful. It preserves the first hit, since the reflection still equals 2 at tick τ. It is its own inverse, because 2a − (2a − s) = s. Finally, it preserves probability: since τ is determined from the past, the subsequent steps remain independent and equally likely, and reversing their signs exchanges equally likely sequences.

  5. Count paths that hit the level and then end below it

    A path that hits the level and ends at b < 2 is reflected into a path ending at 2a − b, strictly above it. Conversely, every path that ends above the level hits it, so its reflection ends below the level. We therefore only need to count the mirror endpoints. Endpoint 0: mirror 4, giving 6 paths. Endpoint -2: mirror 6, giving 1 path. Endpoints -4 and -6: their mirrors are beyond 6 and no path reaches them, giving 0 paths.

    In total: 6 + 1 = 7 paths hit the level and then end below it. This number is the number of paths ending strictly above it, 7: this is what reflection establishes. Of the 20 paths ending at 0, only 6 have hit the level; the other 14 have never been there.

  6. Conclude and adjust the doubled count

    The paths that hit the level consist of those ending at or above it, numbering 22, plus those hitting it and then ending below it, numbering 7: 22 + 7 = 29. The required probability is therefore 29 out of 64, or approximately 0.4531.

    P(Mn≥a)=2P(Sn≥a)−P(Sn=a)=2×22−1564=2964

    Why subtract P(Sn = a)? An endpoint exactly on the level is its own mirror image: doubling its count would count it twice. Simply doubling would give 44 out of 64, or 0.6875, which is too large for the walk. The remaining 35 paths never hit the level.

  7. Check, then move on to Brownian motion

    Two checks. First, two methods give 29: the sum 22 + 7 and the formula 44 − 15. Second, the paths that do not hit the level, 35, together with the 29 that do, give the total 64. These checks confirm the counting, rather than the choice of model for the scene.

    Brownian version. For Brownian motion B and a strictly positive level a, the probability that B reaches a by time t is twice the probability that B is above a at time t. The term subtracted for the walk disappears because B has a continuous distribution, and ending exactly at a has probability 0.

    P(max0≤s≤tBs≥a)=2P(Bt≥a)

    Here this statement is accepted without proof. It assumes that the first hitting time is a stopping time and that, after it, the motion starts again as a fresh Brownian motion, independent of the past: this is the strong Markov property, the subject of the next unit in the chapter. We have also not calculated the numerical value, which requires the cumulative distribution function of the normal distribution.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Hitting the level is twice as likely as ending at or above it, including for the walk.”

    For the walk, endpoints exactly on the level are their own mirror images and would be counted twice. For level 2, doubling 22 would give 44 paths; we must subtract 15 to obtain 29. Doubling without adjustment applies only to the Brownian statement, where ending exactly on the level has probability 0.

    Go through the count in the example again

  • “We can reflect the entire path from its start.”

    Reflecting from the start changes each position s to −s: a path ending at 0 still ends at 0, and the level is no longer hit. Reflection keeps the initial section up to the first hit, then reverses the rest: the endpoint becomes 4.

    Review the mirror image

  • “We can reflect from the time when the path reaches its maximum.”

    The time of the maximum can only be known by looking at the entire remaining sequence: it is not a stopping time. Reflection would change the maximum and the operation would no longer be reversible. We choose the first hit of the level, determined from the past alone.

    Practice identifying τ

Exercise 3 · Reasoning to justify

Had it hit the level?

We observe that the counter ends strictly below level 2 after 6 steps. What proportion of these paths had hit the level? Justify each count, then compare with the proportion among paths ending at 0 and explain the difference.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Paths ending below the level are all those whose endpoint is below level 2 in the table.

Hint 2

Use reflection for those that have hit the level, then divide the two counts.

Compare with the solution

Endpoints below the level: 1 + 6 + 15 + 20 = 42 paths. Among these, those that have hit the level have mirror endpoints 4 and 6: 6 + 1 = 7 paths. The proportion is 7 out of 42, or approximately 0.1667.

Among paths ending at 0, 6 out of 20 have hit the level, giving 0.30. This proportion is greater than 0.1667, because the "below the level" group also includes distant endpoints, as far as -6, where a visit to the level is rare; ending near the level makes that visit more likely. Both calculations assume the equally likely walk in the fictional scene.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What path do we construct from a path that hits level a, and what does it preserve?

Keep the path up to the first hit τ, then replace each position s with 2a − s. The operation is reversible and preserves the number of steps and the probability.

Recall 2Why is the first hit a suitable starting point for reflection?

It is determined from the past alone: it is a stopping time. The subsequent steps remain independent and symmetric, so reversing their signs preserves equal probabilities. The time of the maximum would look into the future.

Recall 3What exact rule gives P(Mₙ ≥ a) for the symmetric random walk?

P(Mₙ ≥ a) = 2 P(Sₙ ≥ a) − P(Sₙ = a). Subtract the endpoints exactly on the level, which are their own mirror images.

Recall 4What Brownian statement corresponds to this rule, and what is accepted without proof here?

For a > 0, P(max of B over [0, t] ≥ a) = 2 P(B_t ≥ a). The restart of the motion after τ, that is, the strong Markov property, is accepted without proof here.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define the walk, the level and the first hit; check that this hit is determined from the past; reflect the rest after τ; deduce that hitting the level and then ending below it corresponds to ending above it; avoid counting endpoints exactly on the level twice; read the Brownian statement as accepted without proof until the strong Markov property is available.

Milestone · reflection versus clamping in the reviewed code

What you understand
Reflecting a path after τ reverses the remaining section to the other side of the level and preserves the number of steps and the probability. Bringing an estimate back to a bound is a different operation: the value is forced onto the bound, without symmetry or preservation of probability.
