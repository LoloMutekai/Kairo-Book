Roots

Your goalFind the side length of a square photo print from its area, then check the root, its sign and its unit.

Before you start
c006-m5 : composition · c010-m1 : exponents

✿01 See the idea

You are preparing a square photo print. Its information sheet gives the printed area, but not the side length. Imagine covering the photo with tiles, each with a side length of 1 centimetre: their number measures the area in square centimetres. To find the side length, look for an arrangement with as many rows as there are tiles in each row.

In the drawing, 6 tiles along a side give 6 rows: 6 × 6 = 36 tiles. Going from the side length to the area means multiplying the side length by itself. Going back from the area to the side length means finding which length produces that square: this operation is called taking the square root.

The photo dimensions are fictional and chosen to allow calculations with a pencil. We assume an exact square, with no margin around the image and no measurement error. The side length of a general rectangle would not be determined by its area alone.

The rule to remember

Find the nonnegative number that, when multiplied by itself, gives the area measurement. Then check the square and the unit: the side length is a length.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A known area, a side length to find

The square print covers 36 square centimetres. Find its side length and explain how you can check the answer without relying on the √ symbol alone.

  1. Name what is given and what is sought

    A denotes the area of the print, in square centimetres; c denotes its side length, in centimetres. The product c × c, written as c squared, is the area. The √ symbol denotes the principal square root: the nonnegative root. The ≥ symbol is read as “greater than or equal to”.

    A=c2,c≥0

    We therefore seek c = √A, the unique nonnegative side length whose square equals A, where A is nonnegative. For numerical values, take the root of the area measurement, then express the length in centimetres.

  2. Propose a side length and count the whole square again

    Try 6 cm. A row contains 6 tiles and the square contains 6 rows. Multiplying these counts lets us compare the covered area with the required area.

    6×6=36=36

    The difference is 36 − 36 = 0 square centimetres: the coverage is exact. Since 6 is positive, √36 = 6. The side length is 6 cm. We have checked the multiplication and the square shape, rather than simply recognizing a familiar result.

  3. Reject division as an incorrect inverse operation

    The exponent 2 does not mean “multiply by 2”: it means taking 2 identical factors. Dividing 36 by 2 gives 18; this number does not answer the question.

    If the side length were 18 cm, the area would be 18 × 18 = 324 square centimetres. Squaring therefore rejects this candidate.

  4. Distinguish a root from an equation

    To compare signs without discussing a negative length, y now denotes a real number with no unit. Here, real means a number on the number line, positive, 0 or negative. The equation y squared = 36 asks for all numbers whose square equals 36.

    (−6)×(−6)=36

    The equation has the solutions 6 and −6: the product of 2 negative numbers is positive. However, √36 denotes only 6. The ± symbol, read as “plus or minus”, is shorthand for the 2 choices in an equation; it is not part of the value of this root. For the photo, only the positive length is suitable.

  5. Check the domain before looking for a value

    The square of a real number is always greater than or equal to 0. A real square root therefore requires an input greater than or equal to 0. A 0 input is allowed: 0 × 0 = 0, so √0 = 0; however, it does not describe a print with a positive printed area.

    An information sheet stating -9 square centimetres is incompatible with our square. No real number has a square equal to -9. We must report an impossible value in this model, without replacing the missing answer with 0. Complex numbers are outside the scope of this unit.

  6. Add areas without adding their roots

    Change only the target area of the same print: it is given as 16 + 9 square centimetres. Add first: this gives 25. Since 5 × 5 = 25, the side length of the required square is 5 cm.

    16+9=25=5

    Taking the roots separately would give 4 + 3 = 7 cm. This side length would cover 49 square centimetres, an excess of 24. The square root therefore does not distribute over a sum in general. This counterexample is enough to reject the proposed rule.

  7. Find bounds when no whole-number side length works

    An area of 20 square centimetres is still possible. Now 4 × 4 = 16 and 5 × 5 = 25; 20 is strictly between these areas. Among nonnegative side lengths, a greater side length gives a greater area: extending the side adds strips to the square.

    16<20<25⇒4<20<5

    The < symbol is read as “strictly less than”; ⇒ means “therefore”. √20 is the exact expression for the measurement we seek. Giving an interval does not assert equality with a decimal value: the final exercise will narrow these bounds.

  8. Read the connection with exponents

    Taking a root reverses raising to a power under the stated sign conditions. For a strictly positive number x with no unit, x to the power 1/2 is another way to write √x.

    x12=x,x>0

    More generally, k is an integer greater than or equal to 2. The kth root of positive x is the unique positive number r such that r to the power k gives x; it is written as x to the power 1/k. If p is an integer, x to the power p/k means: take this root, then raise the result to the power p. The letters x and r have no unit here, and neither do the exponents p and k.

    For an even root order, negative inputs have no real root; for an odd root order, they have a negative root. We do not calculate them here. For our photo, the useful operation remains taking the square root of a nonnegative area.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Taking the square root means dividing by 2.”

    Square the candidate. 36 ÷ 2 gives 18, but 18 × 18 gives 324, not 36. You need to reverse a product of identical factors.

    Revisit the incorrect inverse operation and its check

  • “√36 equals ±6; a negative input also works.”

    Separate the domain from the choice of result: the real square root takes a nonnegative number and returns the nonnegative number. The equation y squared = 36 has 2 solutions; the symbol √36 denotes only a single solution. A length is expressed in centimetres, with no negative sign.

    Review the sign and the domain

  • “I can take the root of each term in a sum.”

    For the photo with area 16 + 9, calculate the sum, then its root: the side length is 5 cm. Adding 4 and 3 would produce a square of 49 square centimetres. Compare the areas to correct the reasoning.

    Repeat the diagnostic task in the areas exercise

Exercise 2 · Application and diagnosis

Check a proposed sum

The information sheet gives 16 + 9 square centimetres for the print. Someone claims a side length of 7 cm by adding the roots. Find the correct side length, calculate the area of their candidate and find the numerical excess. What would you say if the sheet then listed -9 square centimetres?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with the total area; then square each proposed side length.

Hint 2

Can the square of a real number be negative? Declining to calculate does not mean the length is 0.

Compare with the solution

The area is 16 + 9 = 25 square centimetres. Then 5 × 5 = 25 gives the correct side length: 5 cm.

The candidate 7 cm would produce 7 × 7 = 49 square centimetres. Its excess would be 49 − 25 = 24 square centimetres. Adding the areas therefore does not justify adding the roots.

For -9, no real square root exists. The sheet would be incompatible with a photo area; we would ask for the value to be corrected, without claiming a 0 side length.

Check your reasoning

  • Add before taking the root and obtain 5 cm.
  • Check 7 cm by its area and obtain an excess of 24 square centimetres.
  • Explain why the negative input is rejected without inventing a replacement value.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and justification

Give an accurate interval

The target area is 20 square centimetres. Try the side lengths 4.4 cm and 4.5 cm. Calculate their exact areas and deduce bounds for the true side length. Can we write that the side length is exactly 4.5 cm?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write each decimal as a fraction with denominator 10, then multiply this fraction by itself.

Hint 2

Compare each area with 20. For nonnegative side lengths, squaring preserves order.

Compare with the solution

4.4 = 44/10; its square is 19.36. Similarly, 4.5 = 45/10; its square is 20.25. These areas are in square centimetres and the decimals written here are exact.

The first area is 0.64 square centimetres below the target; the second exceeds it by 0.25 square centimetres. The required side length is therefore strictly between the side lengths tested.

4.4<20<4.5

The true side length is between 4.4 cm and 4.5 cm. It equals neither bound: their squares do not equal 20. Writing √20 cm preserves the exact value. We have established an interval, not a rounding rule.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate 19.36 and 20.25 square centimetres without rounding the products.
  • Justify the direction of the inequalities by comparing the areas and using the nonnegativity of the side lengths.
  • Distinguish bounds, an exact equality and a possible approximation.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you find the side length of a square from its area, and how can you check the answer?

Take the nonnegative square root of the area measurement. The side length is in centimetres if the area is in square centimetres. Multiply the side length by itself and compare the resulting area with the given area.

Recall 2Why do √36 and “solve y squared = 36” give different answers?

The principal root equals 6. The equation seeks all possible real numbers: 6 and −6 have the same square 36. A photo side length uses only the positive value.

Recall 3What should you do with a square root whose input is negative? What if the input is 0?

A negative input has no real square root: no real square is negative. For a 0 input, √0 = 0. A nonexistent result cannot be replaced with 0.

Recall 4How can you use the photo to disprove the rule “the root of a sum is the sum of the roots”?

For the area 16 + 9 = 25, the side length is 5. The separate roots give 4 + 3 = 7, whose square is 49. The areas differ: the rule fails.

Recall 5For positive x, what does x to the power 1/k denote? What does the case k = 2 mean?

For an integer k greater than or equal to 2, it is the positive number r whose kth power gives x. With k = 2, this is √x, also written as x to the power 1/2. The sign conditions are part of this interpretation.
