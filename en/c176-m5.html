Level 3 · C176·M5

Correlated simulation

Your goalCompare two settings using the same random draws, calculate the variance of their difference exactly, and state when sharing draws reduces or increases the number of simulations needed.

Before you start
c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c051-m5 : error diagnostics · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c017-m5 : reproducibility protocol · c176-m1 : generators and seeds · c176-m2 : Monte Carlo error · c176-m3 : antithetic variates · c176-m4 : control variates

✿01 See the idea

A fictional service counter receives a queue of varying length each morning. Two settings, A and B, each produce a waiting time that we want to compare. The numbers on this page are invented so that the calculation is exact; they do not describe any real service counter.

First approach: simulate A over a series of mornings, then B over another series. The observed difference then mixes the effect of the setting with the luck of the mornings drawn. Second approach: draw the workload for each morning once, then let A and B read that same morning. A seed makes a generator’s sequence of numbers reproducible (c176-m1); here, what matters is that the two settings share the same draws, as well as being reproducible.

When both waiting times rise and fall together with the workload, their difference changes little: the shared component cancels out. The exact tool is covariance, which measures whether two quantities deviate from their respective means in the same direction. Positive covariance lowers the variance of the difference when draws are shared; negative covariance raises it.

We extend the work on Monte Carlo error (c176-m2). We do not revisit antithetic variates (c176-m3) or control variates (c176-m4): the aim is to compare two settings rather than estimate a single mean. In an Euler-Maruyama simulation (c172-m5), the shared draws would be the noise increments; this page does not simulate any trajectory.

Assumptions on this page: the workload takes four equally likely values; each pair of simulations draws its workload independently of the other pairs; each waiting time is an exact function of the workload. Without these assumptions, the formulas below must be reconsidered.

The rule to remember

To estimate the difference between two settings, share the draws and calculate the variance of the difference as Var(a) + Var(b) − twice Cov(a, b): covariance can help or hurt depending on its sign.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Two service-counter settings, the same simulated morning

The workload z, in people, is 2, 4, 6 or 8, each with the same probability. Setting A gives a = 3 z minutes; setting B gives b = 2 z + 2 minutes. These values are fictional. We seek the mean difference d = a − b, then the number of pairs of simulations needed for the observed mean of d to have a variance of at most 1 min².

  1. Define the quantities, their units and the assumptions

    z is the workload in people; its mean satisfies the following formula.

    2+4+6+84=5 personnes

    a and b are waiting times in minutes, and d = a − b is their difference in minutes. Var denotes the mean of the squared deviations from the mean, in square minutes. Cov(a, b) is the mean of the products of the deviations of a and b from their respective means, also in square minutes. A coefficient such as 3 is in minutes per person.

    Assumptions: in separate mode, B reads a workload z′ independent of A’s workload; in shared mode, A and B read the same workload within each pair. The pairs are independent of one another, so the variance of the mean of n pairs is the variance of one pair divided by n, as in Monte Carlo error. This is the formula to be demonstrated.

    Var(a−b)=Var(a)+Var(b)−2Cov(a,b)

  2. Calculate the variance of the workload

    The deviations from the mean are ±3 and ±1 people: they come in opposite pairs, so the mean of the four squares is the mean of two squares. The squares are 9 and 1 people², and their sum is 10.

    Var(z)=9+12=5 personnes²

  3. Propagate this variance to both settings

    Multiplying the workload by a coefficient multiplies its variance by the square of that coefficient; adding a fixed offset shifts the mean without changing the spread. The offset of 2 minutes therefore does not enter the variances.

    Var(a)=32Var(z)=9×5=45 min²
    Var(b)=22Var(z)=4×5=20 min²

    The covariance is calculated using both coefficients, because a and b are multiples of the same workload.

    Cov(a,b)=3×2×Var(z)=6×5=30 min²

  4. Compare separate draws and shared draws

    With separate workloads, a and b are independent: their covariance is 0 and the variances add. With a shared workload, twice the calculated covariance must be subtracted.

    séparés:45+20=65 min²
    communs:65−2×30=65−60=5 min²

    The ratio of the two variances is 65 / 5 = 13 in this model.

  5. Check using the direct difference

    Another approach, without covariance: d = (3 − 2) z − 2, that is, 1 × z − 2. The offset does not change the variance, and the coefficient 1 is squared.

    Var(d)=12Var(z)=1×5=5 min²

    We obtain 5 min² again; agreement between the two approaches validates the covariance calculation. The mean difference is 1 × 5 − 2 = 5 − 2 = 3 minutes: the waiting time for setting A averages 3 minutes longer than for B in this model.

  6. Work through two draws by hand

    For a workload of 2 people, A gives 6 minutes and B gives 2 × 2 + 2 = 6 minutes: the difference is 0 minute. For 8 people, A gives 24 minutes, B gives 18 minutes, and the difference is 6.

    If A had read the large workload and B the small one, the difference would be 24 − 6 = 18 minutes, instead of 6: part of that difference would come from the draw rather than the setting. Two draws illustrate the mechanism; they do not measure a variance, which describes the spread of the whole distribution.

  7. Convert the variance into a number of pairs

    The variance of the mean of n pairs is Var(d) / n. For it to be at most 1 min², n must be at least Var(d) divided by 1.

    n≥51=5;n≥651=65

    With a shared workload, 5 pairs suffice; with separate workloads, 65 are needed. This ratio is exact for this distribution over four workloads and these linear settings. It does not predict the gain in a real simulation: there, correlation is measured from the draws produced.

Explain it in your own words

Why does the fixed offset shift the mean of the difference without changing its variance? And why does covariance disappear when B reads a different workload from A?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Sharing draws always reduces the variance of the difference.”

    The subtracted term is twice the covariance, and covariance can be negative. If one setting decreases as the workload rises, sharing draws increases the variance: the exercise with the decreasing setting shows this.

    Go to the exercises

  • “The variance of a difference is the difference of the variances.”

    The variances add, then the covariance term is subtracted. With 45 and 20, subtraction would give 45 − 20 instead of 5 min²: work through both calculation approaches in the example again.

    Review the example

  • “Setting a seed for each setting is enough to correlate the simulations.”

    A seed makes each simulation reproducible; it does not link them. Two generators initialized with different seeds produce distinct draws. A and B must read the same values, at the same positions in the sequence, for the same workload. Compare this with the code passage in the milestone.

    Go to the milestone

Exercise 1 · Guided application

A less sensitive setting

Keep the workloads and setting A. Setting B′ adds 1 minute of waiting time per person, plus a fixed offset that does not affect the variances (a fictional value). Calculate Var(a − b′) with separate workloads, then with a shared workload, and check using the direct difference.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A coefficient of one minute per person leaves the workload variance unchanged: Var(b′) is 5 min².

Hint 2

The covariance of a and b′ is the product of both coefficients and the workload variance.

Compare with the solution

Separate: Var(a) + Var(b′) = 45 + 5 = 50 min². Covariance: 3 × 1 × 5 = 15 min².

Shared: 50 − 2 × 15 = 50 − 30 = 20 min².

Check: the slope of the difference is 3 − 1 = 2; its square is 4, and 4 × 5 = 20 min². Both approaches agree, and sharing draws does reduce the variance.

Check your reasoning

  • Var(b′) equals the workload variance, in square minutes.
  • Covariance calculated using both coefficients.
  • Result for shared draws recovered using the direct difference.

Exercise 2 · Transfer

A setting that decreases as the workload rises

Setting C frees up staff: its waiting time decreases by 1 minute per additional person, starting from a fictional fixed offset. Compare Var(a − c) with separate and shared workloads. Does sharing help here?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The coefficient for C is negative: the covariance of a and c is the negative of the covariance in the previous exercise.

Hint 2

Subtracting twice a negative covariance amounts to adding.

Compare with the solution

Var(c) is 5 min², so separate workloads give 45 + 5 = 50 min². The covariance is minus 15 min².

Shared workload: 50 − 2 × (minus 15) = 50 + 30 = 80 min², greater than 50.

Check: the difference a − c has slope 3 + 1, or 4 minutes per person; its variance is the square of this slope times 5, giving 80 min². Here sharing hurts: the most common error is forgetting the sign of the covariance.

Check your reasoning

  • Negative sign of the covariance recognized.
  • Variance with shared draws greater than with separate draws, with both numbers given.
  • Conclusion limited to this model.

Exercise 3 · Limiting case

Same slope, fixed offset

Setting E has the same slope as A, 3 minutes per person, and adds a fixed 2 minutes. Give the variance of a − e with a shared workload and with separate workloads, the number of separate pairs for a target variance of 1 min², then discuss the shared case.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write the difference a − e as a function of z: what happens to the term in z?

Hint 2

With separate workloads, the variances of a and e are equal.

Compare with the solution

The difference is minus 2 minutes for every workload: its variance with a shared workload is 0 min² and its mean is minus 2 minutes.

Separate: 45 + 45 = 90 min², so at least 90 / 1 = 90 pairs.

In the shared case, one pair suffices here, but only because the model is exactly linear and A and E read the same workload. 0 variance in the model establishes nothing about a real simulation containing other sources of randomness.

Check your reasoning

  • Term in z eliminated from the difference.
  • Variance with separate draws obtained by addition.
  • Limitation of the exact model recognized.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What formula gives the variance of a − b?

Var(a) + Var(b) − 2 Cov(a, b), in square minutes if a and b are in minutes.

Recall 2When do shared draws reduce the variance of the difference?

When the covariance of the two outputs is positive, because the variance of the difference then falls below the sum of the variances. Negative covariance raises it.

Recall 3Why is a seed for each setting insufficient?

It makes each simulation reproducible but does not correlate them: A and B must read the same draws, at the same positions in the sequence.

Recall 4Does adding a fixed offset to an output change its variance?

No: it only shifts the mean. Variance depends only on the spread around the mean.

Recall 5How do you go from the variance of a difference to the number of pairs?

The mean of n independent pairs has variance Var(d) / n; n must be at least Var(d) divided by the target variance, under this independence assumption.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Share the draws between the two settings; define a, b and d = a − b with their units; calculate Var(a), Var(b) and Cov(a, b); apply Var(a − b) = Var(a) + Var(b) − twice Cov(a, b); check using the direct difference; divide Var(d) by the target variance to obtain the number of pairs; check the sign of the covariance and the independence assumptions.
