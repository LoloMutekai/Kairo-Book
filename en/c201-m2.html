Level 3 · C201·M2

Point-in-time universe

Your goalReconstruct, for a given decision date, the list of securities that a simulation engine was actually allowed to know about, then measure the effect of using a list read later.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

Let us replay the past to test an investment rule. At the decision date, which we denote by t, the engine must choose from the securities in an index. With a simulation clock, date t separates the usable past from the forbidden future: nothing that happens after t may be used. This unit asks a single question: which securities were we allowed to consider on that day?

It is tempting to start from the list we read today, since it is easy to obtain. But it contains only the securities still present. Securities that have left are missing, and securities that joined later appear even though nobody knew at date t that they would join. The missing securities produce the survivorship bias already encountered in a backtest audit; later additions introduce information from the future.

A point-in-time universe, meaning "as it was known at date t", is reconstructed using two dates per security. The effective date e states when membership begins in the real world. The publication date p states when this information became available. They differ when a provider adds or corrects a row after the fact: the row is then effective before it is known, and including it amounts to reading the future.

A security leaves the universe on its exit date s, if it has one. An exit after t must never be used to exclude the security: at date t, it has not happened yet.

All values on this page are fictional: six imaginary securities from A to F, numbered days in the scenario, and textbook prices. They refer to no real index and promise no gain.

The rule to remember

At date t, a security belongs to the universe only if its membership is already effective, already published, and has not yet ended; a list read later never replaces this reconstruction.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Six fictional securities, one decision day, two lists

Six fictional securities, named A to F, make up the history of an imaginary index. For each one, e is the effective entry day, p the day when that entry was published, and s the exit day, if any. A: e = 1, p = 1, no exit. B: e = 2, p = 2, s = 14. C: e = 3, p = 3, s = 8. D: e = 6, p = 12, no exit; the provider added the row for D after the fact. E: e = 9, p = 9, no exit. F: e = 11, p = 11, no exit. The engine decides on day t = 10. It invests 100 fictional monetary units per selected security, with equal weighting, then reads the value of each position on day 15, which is 5 days later: A is then worth 110, B 60 (liquidated on day 14), D 130, E 106 and F 120 units. A list of securities was read on day 20. What is the difference between the universe reconstructed on day 10 and this list, and how does it change the average value obtained?

  1. Define the symbols, units and assumptions

    Days are integer session numbers in the fictional scenario, with no other unit. For security i, e denotes the effective entry day, p the publication day of that entry, and s the exit day, if any; t is the decision day. The value v of a position is expressed in fictional monetary units, for an investment of 100 units per security on day t.

    Explicit assumptions: information published on day p can be used for the decision on that same day; a security with an exit on day s no longer belongs to the universe from that day onward; weighting is equal; the value of a security that has left remains fixed at its liquidation value until day 15; there are no costs, dividends or reinvestment. These choices simplify the scenario; they do not describe a market.

  2. Write the membership rule

    On day t, the engine is allowed to consider only securities for which all three conditions hold at the same time.

    U(t)={i:pi≤t,ei≤t,t<si}

    The first two conditions concern what we already know: the effective date and the publication date are both at most t. The third states that the security has not yet left. If the security has no exit, it is always true. The symbol |U| will later denote the number of securities in the set.

  3. Apply the rule to each security on the decision day

    We compare each date with t = 10. A: effective on day 1, published on day 1, never left, so it belongs to the universe. B: effective and published from day 2; its exit falls on day 14, which is 4 days after t. This exit is in the future and unknown at the decision: it must not exclude B, which remains in the universe.

    C: the exit on day 8 precedes t by 2 days, so C has already left. D: the row has been effective for 4 days, but is published on day 12, which is 2 days after t; at the decision it does not yet exist for the engine, so D is excluded. E: effective and published on day 9, in the universe. F: effective entry on day 11, which is 1 day after t, so F is excluded.

    The reconstructed universe is {A, B, E}. It contains 3 securities. No security's membership depended on an event after t: the exit of B was used only to establish that it had not yet happened.

  4. Compare with the list read after the fact

    The list read on day 20, which is 10 days after the decision, keeps only the securities present on that day. B (exit on day 14) and C (exit on day 8) are missing. D is now published and F has joined. The list is therefore {A, D, E, F} and contains 4 securities.

    The securities in common are A and E: 2 securities. The reconstructed universe contains 1 security that the list omits, B, which is precisely the one that ended badly. The list contains 2 securities that the engine could not have known about on day t, D and F. An error in only one direction would not describe the difference: here one security is lost and two securities come from the future.

  5. Measure the effect of the choice of universe

    For each universe U, we calculate the average value of the positions on day 15, for 100 units invested in each selected security.

    VU=1|U|∑i∈Uvi

    Reconstructed universe: 110 + 60 = 170, then 170 + 106 = 276. Dividing by 3 securities gives the average.

    VPIT=2763=92

    Later list: 110 + 130 + 106 + 120 = 466 units, divided by 4 securities.

    Vliste=4664=116.5

    The bias B is the difference between the average obtained with the later list and that of the reconstructed universe.

    B=Vliste−VPIT=116.5−92=24.5

    Reconstruction leads to an average value below the investment of 100 units; the later list places it above. This reversal comes entirely from the fictional scenario. It illustrates a mechanism; it measures no real bias.

  6. Check the partition and the result

    Ranking check: each security must fall into exactly one category on day t. In the universe, 3 securities; already left, 1 (C); effective but unpublished, 1 (D); not yet joined, 1 (F).

    3+1+1+1=6=6

    Difference check: 1 + 2 = 3 securities in the reconstructed universe and 2 + 2 = 4 securities in the later list, recovering both counts. Order-of-magnitude check: the list average (116.5) exceeds the universe average (92), because the added securities are worth 130 and 120 units while the lost security is worth 60 units.

Explain it in your own words

Why exclude D at date t when its row is already effective, and why keep B when we know today that it will leave? Which date justifies each answer?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A security belongs to the universe as soon as its effective date has passed.”

    The effective date describes the real world; the publication date describes what the engine can know. For D, effective for 4 days, the row is not published until 2 days after t: including it reads the future. Always apply both conditions.

    Review D in the example

  • “The current index list is sufficient, since it is up to date.”

    Up to date today does not mean known at date t. The list for day 20 loses B, which will leave, and adds D and F. Reconstruct the universe at the decision date, then compare.

    Review the diagram of the two lists

  • “A buffer of a few days after the effective date makes the publication date unnecessary.”

    A guessed buffer is not data. With a buffer of 3 days, D would appear to be known on day 9, although it is published on day 12. Keep the publication date, or do not claim to have a point-in-time universe.

Exercise 1 · Application

Same history, different decision day

Use the six securities from the example again, but decide on day t = 13. Give the universe membership and size, then check the classification of all securities.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First compare p with t for D, then s with t for B and C.

Hint 2

A security that left before t leaves the universe; a security that will leave later stays in it.

Compare with the solution

A and E remain in the universe. D is now published: 13 − 12 = 1 day since it became known, so it joins. F is effective and published on day 11, so it also joins. B has not yet left: 14 − 13 = 1 day remaining, so it stays. C has been out since day 8. The universe is {A, B, D, E, F}, a total of 5 securities.

Check: 5 securities in the universe + 1 security already out = 6, recovering the 6 securities. No event after t determined membership: the exit of B had not yet happened.

Check your reasoning

  • Compare each security with the decision date using the rule with three conditions.
  • Keep B despite its future exit and exclude C, which has already left.
  • Complete the check with the total number of securities.

Exercise 2 · Decomposition

Separate survival from future information

Build an intermediate list: the later list A, D, E, F with only security B added back, which we could have found in a history of exits. Calculate its average value on day 15, then split the total bias into a part due to the absence of B and a part due to D and F.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The intermediate list contains one more security than the later list; also add the value of B.

Hint 2

One part is the difference between the later list average and the intermediate average, the other between the intermediate average and that of the reconstructed universe.

Compare with the solution

Sum: 466 + 60 = 526 units for 4 + 1 = 5 securities. Intermediate average: 526 ÷ 5 = 105.2 units.

Part due to the absence of B: 116.5 − 105.2 = 11.3 units. Part due to D and F: 105.2 − 92 = 13.2 units. These two parts add up to 24.5 units, the total bias in the example.

Interpretation: even after restoring the missing security, the list still has a difference, because it still contains securities from the future. This breakdown applies to the fictional scenario, not to a real market.

Check your reasoning

  • Recalculate the sum and the count before dividing.
  • Recover the total bias by adding the two parts.
  • Do not generalize the numerical breakdown beyond the fictional scenario.

Exercise 3 · Rule critique

Can a safety buffer replace publication?

A colleague proposes never reading the publication date: they assume an entry is known 3 days after its effective date. Apply this rule to D and E on day t = 10, give the membership and size of the universe it would produce, then state whether it is reliable.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For each security, calculate the day when the rule assumes the entry is known, then compare it with t.

Hint 2

Then compare these days with the actual publication dates.

Compare with the solution

For D, the rule assumes the entry is known on day 6 + 3 = 9. This day precedes t = 10, so D is admitted, although it is published on day 12: the rule is wrong by 12 − 9 = 3 days. For E, it assumes the entry is known on day 9 + 3 = 12, which is 2 days after t, so E is excluded, although it is published from day 9.

The rule produces {A, B, D}: the same count as the reconstructed universe, 3 securities, but different membership. Two errors cancel out in the count, hiding the flaw if only the sizes are compared.

For D, a buffer of at least 12 − 6 = 6 days would be needed; for E, none. Nothing in the effective dates indicates this: only the publication date tells us. A buffer can serve as a documented precaution, never as proof of a point-in-time universe.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the assumed day of knowledge for D and E and compare it with t.
  • Compare membership, not just the count, with the reconstructed universe.
  • Conclude on the status of a buffer: an assumption, not data.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the point-in-time universe at decision date t?

The set of securities whose membership is already effective, already published and has not yet ended at t, reconstructed without using any later date.

Recall 2Why distinguish the effective date from the publication date?

The first describes the real world, the second what was known. A row added after the fact is effective before it is published; including it at t reads the future.

Recall 3What does a list of securities read after date t do?

It loses securities that have left, which is survivorship bias, and contains securities that joined or were published after t, which is information from the future.

Recall 4What does a universe declaration in the reviewed code fail to prove?

It does not certify complete historical membership: the DatasetManifest documentation states this in the reviewed lines.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Set decision date t; read the effective date, publication date and exit date, if any, for each security; keep only securities whose membership is effective, published and has not yet ended at t, without using a future exit; compare with the list read later; measure the difference under the same assumptions; check the partition of securities.
