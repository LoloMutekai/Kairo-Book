Level 4 · C263·M5

Distribution shift

Your goalDistinguish a change in the distribution of signals from a change in their relationship with the outcome, quantify the effect of each on the daily utility of a fixed rule, and know when reweighting past data remains valid.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A decision rule has been tuned on past data and is then used over a period it has never seen. For its past utility to predict its future utility, the period of use must resemble the tuning period. This resemblance can fail in two very different ways, which require different corrections.

Our single scenario is fictional. Each day, a rule reads a signal, high or low. If the signal is high, it takes a position; if it is low, it does nothing. The day later turns out to be an up day or a down day, but only after the horizon of the temporal labels: the outcome arrives with a delay. A position gains 3 m.u. on an up day and loses 4 m.u. on a down day, with decision costs already deducted. The unit “m.u.” is a fictional monetary unit, unrelated to any real currency.

The first kind of change affects the inputs. High signals become rarer, but when the signal is high, the day still ends higher as often as before. The rule remains valid; it applies less often. The second kind affects the relationship. High signals remain just as frequent, but a high signal no longer predicts a rise as well. A dashboard that only looks at signals cannot distinguish these two situations.

This lesson quantifies both effects on average utility per day and shows when reweighting past days is sufficient and when it misleads. It does not seek the date of a change or set any threshold for real use. All values on the page are fictional: no market data, no observed returns, no promised gains.

The rule to remember

Separate the share of signals from the relationship between signal and outcome; reweight past data only if the relationship has not changed; confirm with outcomes that have matured.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The same rule, one past and two futures

Window A: 200 days, including 120 with a high signal; among these, 84 are up days. Gain 3 m.u., loss 4 m.u. per position. Window B₁: 100 days, including 30 with a high signal and 21 up days among them. Window B₂: 100 days, including 60 with a high signal and 30 up days among them. Let us calculate the average utility per day of the same rule, then see what reweighting and an alert can tell us about it. Fictional values.

  1. Define the quantities and assumptions

    a denotes the share of days with a high signal and has no unit. p denotes the rise rate among these high signal days and has no unit. g is the gain from a winning position and c the loss from a losing position, in m.u. The subscript A or B identifies the window. The rule, g and c are assumed to remain fixed across windows, and the outcome of each day is assumed to have matured.

    A low signal day earns nothing. A high signal day earns 3 m.u. with probability p and costs 4 m.u. with the complementary probability, giving the following average utility per high signal day.

    u(p)=p⋅g−(1−p)⋅c=p(g+c)−c

    The average utility per day in the window is the high signal share times this utility, in m.u. per day.

  2. Measure utility in window A

    UA=aA⋅u(pA)

    High signal share: 120 / 200 = 0.60. Rise rate: 84 / 120 = 0.70. Sum g + c = 7 m.u. Then p (g + c) = 0.70 × 7 = 4.90, followed by u = 4.90 − 4 = 0.90 m.u. per high signal day. Average utility: 0.60 × 0.90 = 0.54 m.u. per day.

    Check using the threshold. Utility on a high signal day is 0 at the following rate; above it, the rule gains on average, and below it, the rule loses.

    p*=cg+c

    Here p* = 4 / 7 = 0.571. The rate 0.70 exceeds this threshold by 9/70 (exactly), and 9/70 × 7 = 9/10, or 0.90: this gives the utility per high signal day by another route.

  3. First future B₁: only the inputs change

    High signal share: 30 / 100 = 0.30. Rise rate: 21 / 30 = 0.70, identical to that of A. Utility per high signal day therefore remains 0.90 m.u., and measured utility is 0.30 × 0.90 = 0.27 m.u. per day: too few positions, rather than poor positions.

    Let us reweight the past. Each high signal day in A receives weight w, the ratio of the high signal shares in the two windows; the estimate for B is the average of the utilities in A using these weights.

    w=aBaA⇒UB≈w⋅UA

    Low signal days have 0 utility: their weight does not change the sum. Here w = 0.30 / 0.60 = 0.50, giving 0.50 × 0.54 = 0.27 m.u. per day. The reweighted estimate equals the direct measurement because p has not changed: this is precisely the assumption behind reweighting.

  4. Raise an alert on inputs, stating the assumptions

    To judge whether a difference in shares is plausible as a simple fluctuation, compare it with the variance of a share observed over n days: the reference share y times its complement to one, divided by n. The squared difference divided by this variance gives Z², which has no unit: the square of the difference measured in standard deviations.

    Z2=(x−y)2y(1−y)n

    Assumptions: independent days and a reference known without error. These are false in practice — overlapping labels make days dependent, and the share in A is itself estimated — so Z² underestimates the noise and does not constitute a test. By a fictional exercise convention with no statistical justification, an alert is raised above 9, or 3 standard deviations.

    For B1: difference 0.60 − 0.30 = 0.30, squared difference 0.09. Reference variance: 0.60 × 0.40 / 100 = 0.24 / 100 = 0.0024. Thus Z² = 0.09 / 0.0024 = 37.5, above 9: the input alert is triggered, and it correctly describes an actual change in the share.

  5. Second future B₂: the relationship changes, the inputs stay the same

    High signal share: 60 / 100 = 0.60, as in A. The difference is 0, so Z² is 0: no input alert. Yet 30 of the 60 high signal days are up days, giving a rate of 0.50.

    Then p (g + c) = 0.50 × 7 = 3.50, followed by u = 3.50 − 4 = -0.50 m.u. per high signal day, and average utility is 0.60 times (-0.50) = -0.30 m.u. per day. The rate 0.50 is below the threshold 0.571: the rule loses on average on each position.

    Reweighting fails: the shares are equal, so all weights are 1 and the prediction remains 0.54 m.u. per day. The difference between this prediction and the measurement (-0.30) is 0.84 m.u. per day.

  6. Raise an alert on the relationship once labels have matured

    Only outcomes that have matured reveal the new rate: this alert therefore lags behind the inputs. Apply the same statistic using the rise rate in A as the reference, over the 60 high signal days in B2.

    Reference variance: 0.70 × 0.30 / 60 = 0.21 / 60 = 0.0035. Difference: 0.70 − 0.50 = 0.20, squared difference 0.04. Thus Z² = 0.04 / 0.0035 = 11.43, above 9.

    The alert is triggered, but by a modest margin, and only because 60 high signal days are available. With fewer high signal days, the same difference would fall below the threshold (final exercise).

  7. Check boundary cases and state the limits

    Boundary case: if p were exactly 0.571, u would be 0, and some high signal days would gain as much as the others lose. Another check: by construction, the average weight over A is 1; otherwise, we would not be comparing averages per day.

    Limits. Reweighting requires every signal possible in B to also exist in A, which is true here. The utility estimated on A is itself uncertain (model uncertainty): this calculation ignores that uncertainty. The values are fictional and say nothing about a real market.

Explain it in your own words

Explain why reweighting predicts B₁ exactly but is wrong for B₂, then why the input alert stays silent in B₂ even though utility becomes negative.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If the distribution of signals has not changed, the model is still valid.”

    B₂ keeps exactly the high signal share of A, but its rise rate falls to 0.50, below the threshold 0.571, and utility becomes -0.30 m.u. per day. Monitoring signals does not reveal the relationship: outcomes that have matured must also be compared.

    Review the second future in the example

  • “An input alert proves that the rule has become poor.”

    In B₁, the alert is triggered (Z² is 37.5), but the rate remains 0.70 and daily utility remains 0.27 m.u., which is positive. The rule applies less often; it does not lose. Always compare with utility.

    Review the first future in the example

  • “Reweighting compensates for all distribution shifts.”

    It assumes the same relationship between signal and outcome. Otherwise, it reproduces the utility of A (0.54 m.u. per day) while B₂ gives -0.30. First check the rise rate for each signal, then redo the last exercise.

    Test this in the exercise on combined changes

Exercise 1 · Explained application

Reweight a third window

In a window C of 100 days, 40 days have a high signal and 28 of them are up days. The rule, gain and loss remain those of A. Check whether reweighting is valid, calculate the weight of the high signal days in A and the reweighted utility, then compare with direct measurement.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The rise rate in C is 28 / 40: compare it with that in A before reweighting.

Hint 2

The weight is the ratio of the high signal share in C to that in A.

Compare with the solution

High signal share in C: 40 / 100 = 0.40. Rate in C: 28 / 40 = 0.70, as in A: reweighting is valid.

Weight: 0.40 / 0.60 = 0.667 (exactly 2/3). Reweighted utility: 2/3 × 0.54 = 0.36 m.u. per day.

Direct measurement: 0.40 × 0.90 = 0.36 m.u. per day. The two routes agree because only the share has changed.

Check your reasoning

  • Check that the rise rate has not changed before reweighting.
  • Weight has no unit and equals the ratio of the high signal shares.
  • Utility is expressed in m.u. per day and compared with direct measurement.

Exercise 2 · Guided reasoning

How many up days are needed?

In window B₂, 60 days have a high signal. With a gain of 3 m.u. and a loss of 4 m.u., what is the minimum number of these days that must be up days for the total utility of the window to be nonnegative? Compare with the 30 observed.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Utility is nonnegative when the rate p is at least c / (g + c).

Hint 2

Multiply by the number of high signal days, then remember that a number of days is an integer: round up.

Compare with the solution

Rate threshold: 4 / 7 = 0.571. In days: 60 × 4 / 7 = 34.29.

An integer at least as large as this number is needed: 35 up days, rather than 34, since 34 is less than 34.29.

Check: the 30 observed days are far below 35, confirming the negative utility of -0.30 m.u. per day.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the threshold c / (g + c) without confusing it with an alert probability.
  • Round up because the number of days is an integer.
  • Compare the minimum with the observed count.

Exercise 3 · Critical analysis

Both changes at once

Window D: 100 days, including 30 with a high signal, among which 15 are up days. (a) What daily utility does reweighting predict, and what utility is measured? (b) Calculate Z² for the rise rate using A as the reference: is the alert at 9 triggered? (c) What can you conclude?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The high signal share in D is that of B₁; its rise rate is that of B₂.

Hint 2

The reference variance of the rate is now divided by the number of high signal days in D.

Hint 3

A Z² below the threshold does not prove the absence of change.

Compare with the solution

(a) Weight 0.50, prediction 0.50 × 0.54 = 0.27 m.u. per day. Measurement: rate 15 / 30 = 0.50, u = -0.50 m.u. per high signal day, utility 0.30 times (-0.50) = -0.15 m.u. per day. Reweighting even gets the sign wrong.

(b) Variance: 0.21 / 30 = 0.007. Z² = 0.04 / 0.007 = 5.71, below 9: no alert on the relationship.

(c) A silent alert means “not detected with this convention and 30 high signal days”, rather than “absent”: measured utility is negative and the rate 0.50 remains below the threshold 0.571. More days with mature outcomes are needed, or utility must be examined directly, while retaining the uncertainty of these estimates.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish the effect of the share from that of the rise rate.
  • Divide the variance of the rate by the number of high signal days in D.
  • Conclude “not detected” rather than “absent”, also comparing with measured utility.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is a change in inputs, and what is it assumed to preserve?

The distribution of signals changes, here the share of high signal days, while the outcome rate for a given signal stays the same. Reweighting past data by the ratio of the shares can then predict utility.

Recall 2Why can a signal alert stay silent while utility becomes negative?

If the relationship between signal and outcome changes, the distribution of signals can remain identical. Only outcomes that have matured show the new rise rate.

Recall 3What rise rate makes the utility of a high signal day 0?

The loss divided by the sum of the gain and the loss. Above it, the rule gains on average; below it, the rule loses, with these fixed costs.

Recall 4When is the weight given by the share in B divided by the share in A valid?

When the outcome rate for a given signal has not changed and every signal in B exists in A. It does not correct a change in the relationship.

Recall 5What does a Z² below the threshold tell us?

That no difference is detected with this convention and this window size, rather than that no difference exists: independent days and a known reference are assumptions.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Fix the rule, costs and reference window; write daily utility as the high signal share times the utility of a high signal day; decide whether the change affects the share of signals or the rise rate; reweight only if this rate has not changed; confirm with mature outcomes; quantify the alert using Z² with its assumptions; conclude “detected” or “not detected”, never “proven” or “absent”.
