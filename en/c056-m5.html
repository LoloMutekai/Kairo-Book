Level 1 · C056·M5

Effect size

Your goalCalculate a difference in means and the corresponding Cohen's d, then distinguish magnitude, precision, and practical importance.

Before you start
c055-m5 : confidence interval · c056-m1 : hypotheses · c056-m2 : decision errors · c056-m3 : power · c056-m4 : independent and paired comparisons

✿01 See the idea

In a workshop, two sets of instructions for assembling the same small box are compared. Each person sees only one set of instructions and completes only one timed assembly. Group A takes a mean of 12 s; group B takes a mean of 10 s. B is therefore faster by 2 s on average in this sample. This difference describes a magnitude: it is already an effect size, expressed in the unit of the problem.

Is this a large difference relative to the variation between people? To answer, we need a measuring scale. Here, each group has a standard deviation of 4 s. The standard deviation describes the spread of individual times around their mean. We will use a measure of spread common to the groups: the pooled standard deviation. The difference in means is half of it.

Cohen's d measures this difference in units of pooled standard deviation. It gives a number without a unit, which should be reported alongside the difference in seconds. It gives neither the probability that one set of instructions is better nor the fraction of people who will be faster. The statistical test question, "Are these data incompatible with the null hypothesis?", remains separate from "By how much do the means differ?"

All times and sample sizes in this scene are fictional. We assume that people are independent, timing conditions are comparable, and the populations have a common, positive spread. We do not assume that the difference in means alone proves a causal effect of the instructions. The calculations use sample summaries rather than a real study.

The rule to remember

State the direction of the comparison, keep the difference in its original unit, then divide it by an explicitly chosen measure of spread. Discuss uncertainty and practical importance separately.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Two sets of instructions, a difference to measure

Fictional summaries: A has 32 people, a mean of 12 s, and a sample standard deviation of 4 s; B has 32 other people, a mean of 10 s, and a sample standard deviation of 4 s. We choose the direction A minus B: a positive difference here indicates that B takes less time on average.

    This version of d compares independent groups by expressing their difference relative to a common spread. No normal distribution is needed to carry out this descriptive calculation; inference requires additional assumptions and a method.

  2. Keep the seconds first

    Subtract in the stated direction: Δ = 12 − 10 = 2 s. This result allows us to discuss the actual time at issue. Keep it even if you then calculate a measure without a unit.

    The result is positive because A takes more time. This favors B only because our criterion is a time to be reduced. A positive sign does not universally mean an improvement.

  6. Interpret the estimate without turning it into a verdict

    The descriptive report is: 'In this fictional sample, B takes 2 s less than A on average; Cohen's d, in the direction A minus B, is 0.50, with a pooled standard deviation of 4 s.' An estimated d varies from sample to sample; it is not a known population value.

    Standardized magnitude does not determine usefulness. Suppose that before the comparison the workshop chose 3 s as the minimum useful reduction, a fictional criterion specific to this task. The estimate of 2 s remains below this criterion. However, it does not suffice to prove that the population effect is too small: its uncertainty has not been quantified.

    A small p-value would not automatically make this difference useful; a p-value that is not small would not prove a 0 effect either. A confidence interval, with its assumptions, would provide information about uncertainty. We do not calculate one here or report any test result. No universal verbal threshold for 'small' or 'large' replaces the assembly context.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “d equals 0.50, so it is a proportion of people helped.”

    Name the numerator and denominator: a difference in means in seconds divided by a spread in seconds. The result counts standard deviations. It does not count people who save time or provide a probability.

    Review the diagram using spread as a measuring scale

  • “I divide by the standard error to obtain the effect size.”

    Return to the definition of the chosen d: its denominator is the pooled standard deviation of individual times. The standard error measures the uncertainty of the estimated difference; using it would answer another question. An effect measured more precisely can retain the same magnitude.

    Identify the denominator and its units again

  • “More people will necessarily make d larger and the instructions useful.”

    With identical means and standard deviations, equal group sample sizes can increase without changing d. This does not remove uncertainty or change the practical criterion set by the workshop. Separate these three questions in the last exercise.

    Examine the larger study

Exercise 3 · Transfer and reasoned judgment

More people, the same estimated difference

Another fictional study of the same assembly task observes 128 independent people per set of instructions, with exactly the same means and standard deviations as the example. These are new observations rather than copied rows. The practical criterion remains 3 s. Recalculate the pooled variance and d. Respond to: "The sample size is multiplied by 4, so the effect is multiplied by 4 and we must adopt B." What conclusions do the supplied data allow?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In the pooled variance formula, replace both sample sizes. The variances within the groups remain identical.

Hint 2

Separate the observed difference, estimation precision, and the minimum useful reduction; no p-value or interval is provided.

Compare with the solution

Each weight becomes 128 − 1 = 127; their sum is 254. Each contribution is 127 × 16 = 2032. The pooled variance is 4064 / 254 = 16 seconds squared. Its positive square root remains 4 s. Thus d = 2 / 4 = 0.50, rather than an effect multiplied by 4.

Under the same model of independent observations, more observations normally provide a more precise estimate; this does not multiply the observed difference. Here, it remains 2 s, which is 1 s below the practical criterion of 3 s. The standardized ratio and the time target answer different questions.

We can describe the difference in this study. We cannot report statistical significance, demonstrate a population effect below the criterion, or decide to adopt the instructions based on this d alone. We would need, among other things, an uncertainty analysis, an examination of the comparison design, and a clear account of the practical consequences of the choice. These fictional values measure no real improvement.

Check your reasoning

  • Repeat the pooling with 128 people per group and recover 16 seconds squared.
  • Keep d = 0.50 and the raw difference of 2 s.
  • Distinguish precision, magnitude, and the practical criterion without inventing a test result or concluding that there is no effect.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What effect size can we report before any standardization?

The difference in means in its original unit: here A minus B is 2 s. It directly expresses the time at issue in the assembly task.

Recall 2What does the denominator of our Cohen's d measure?

The pooled standard deviation describes the spread of individual times within the groups. It is not the standard error of the difference in means. The choice of this reference must be appropriate.

Recall 3How can we recover the pooled spread without arbitrarily averaging the standard deviations?

Square the standard deviations, weight each variance by its sample size minus 1, divide by the sum of the weights, then take the positive square root. Here, the variances are equal, so we recover their common standard deviation.

Recall 4What changes when the groups are reversed and when time units are converted consistently?

Reversing the groups changes the sign of d and preserves its magnitude. Converting all times by the same positive factor preserves d. The favorable direction depends on the criterion measured.

Recall 5Why is d = 0.50 not enough to adopt instructions B?

It is an estimate of magnitude, with no guarantee of causality, precision, or practical importance. We need to keep the difference in seconds, quantify its uncertainty, and compare it with the workshop criterion. A p-value answers another question.
