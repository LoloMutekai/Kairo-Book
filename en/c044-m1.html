Level 2 · C044·M1

Tokens and text segmentation

Your goalSplit a message using an explicit rule, find its identifiers and check the count without confusing words, characters and tokens.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You are preparing the message “My aunt murmured.” in a fictional workshop. To you, it contains 3 words. Now imagine a machine that receives numbered pieces: it has the piece “My”, but does not have the whole verb. It must assemble several pieces to represent it. Before counting, you therefore need to know which pieces exist and how to choose them.

A token is an element of a vocabulary used by a language model. An identifier is the number used to refer to that element. Tokenization transforms text into an ordered sequence of these identifiers. Depending on the vocabulary and rule used, a word can take up several tokens; spaces and punctuation also contribute to the representation.

Our small tokenizer is entirely fictional, with a vocabulary and identifiers chosen for this exercise. In the boxes, ␠ represents exactly 1 ordinary space; this symbol only makes it visible. The vertical bars separate the boxes and are not part of the message. No space should be added between the pieces when joining them back together.

The boxes in the diagram show readable fragments. For a real model, an individual token cannot necessarily be decoded into a readable fragment on its own: this depends on its encoding. Here, we work only with the letters, spaces and full stop in the displayed message, without changing letter case or removing punctuation.

The rule to remember

Fix the exact text, vocabulary and rule; record the pieces in order, then count their occurrences and check that joining them reconstructs the message.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Split, number, then reassemble the message

The text is exactly “My aunt murmured.”, without quotation marks or a line break. Our initial vocabulary contains only My ↔ 11, ␠aunt ↔ 24, ␠mur ↔ 35, mur ↔ 36, ed ↔ 47 and . ↔ 8. These numbers are fictional; no measurement from a real model is presented.

  1. Set a rule that determines the choice

    Starting at the beginning of the remaining text, take the longest piece in the vocabulary that exactly matches that beginning. Write down its identifier, remove the recognized piece, then repeat. For equal lengths, an identical piece has the same identifier; our table contains no duplicates. If no piece matches, tokenization fails: you must not ignore the character causing the problem.

    This longest prefix rule is the rule for our exercise. It gives an unambiguous choice with our table, but does not describe every real tokenizer. The vocabulary here contains no special tokens, meaning elements reserved for a function such as marking a message boundary. We count only the displayed text.

  2. Follow each remaining fragment

    At the start, My matches: write down 11. The remainder starts with ␠aunt: write down 24. It then starts with ␠mur: write down 35. What remains of murmured is now mured: mur matches and gives 36, then ed gives 47. Finally, the full stop gives 8; nothing remains.

    The fragment sequence is therefore My | ␠aunt | ␠mur | mur | ed | . ; its identifier sequence is [11, 24, 35, 36, 47, 8]. ␠mur and mur are not interchangeable: only the first piece starts with a space. Our token boundaries do not have to be word boundaries.

  3. Count positions without adding the identifiers

    Let s be the exact text and T our fixed tokenizer. T(s) is the identifier sequence; n is its length, measured in tokens. Here, the symbol |T(s)| means “number of elements in the sequence”. Each position counts as 1, even if an identifier appears several times. Identifiers are labels with no physical unit: adding them has no meaning as a measure of length.

    n=|T(s)|=2+3+1=6 tokens

    We added 2 tokens for My and ␠aunt, 3 tokens for the verb with its leading space, then 1 for the full stop. The result is 6, whereas the word count is 3. The difference 6 − 3 = 3 is not a general rule; it comes from the pieces in this table.

  4. Check the text and its length another way

    To check the result, replace each identifier with its piece, in order, then join the pieces without adding a separator. You must recover exactly “My aunt murmured.”, with 2 spaces and the final full stop. Preserving the meaning while losing the full stop would not pass this check.

    Let c be the number of characters in the chosen text. The lengths of the pieces, including spaces, are respectively 2, 5, 4, 3, 2 and 1 characters. Adding them checks that no character has been lost or added.

    c=2+5+4+3+2+1=17 caractères

    Independent check using words: My contributes 2 letters; aunt contributes 5 − 1 letters; murmured contributes 9 − 1 letters. Then add 2 spaces and 1 punctuation character: you recover 17 characters. Checking the total is useful, but only exact reconstruction also checks the order.

  5. Measure a ratio without treating it as a constant

    Let r be the average number of characters per token for this text and this tokenizer, with n strictly positive. Divide the number of characters by the number of positions in the sequence. This ratio describes the average length of the pieces here, rather than the tokenization of any sentence.

    r=cn=176≈2.83 caractères par token

    The exact ratio is 17/6 characters per token. The check r × n = c gives 17 characters using the exact fraction; the displayed decimal is rounded. A different text or tokenizer may give a different ratio. A real count requires the tokenizer corresponding to the model and the exact scope of the text being counted.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A token must be a word.”

    Find the pieces of the verb: ␠mur, mur and ed occupy distinct positions. The full stop also occupies a position. Start again from the declared vocabulary rather than the spaces between words.

    Compare pieces, words and characters

  • “Spaces do not count, and the numbers give the size.”

    Here, the space belongs to ␠aunt or ␠mur. Removing these spaces prevents reconstruction of the message. The numbers identify the pieces; their values give no length. Count the positions, then join the pieces to check.

    Review counting and reconstruction

  • “I know the number of tokens as soon as I see the text.”

    The vocabulary and rule for the target model are missing. Adding a whole piece in the last exercise changes the count for the same message. An average calculated from our example cannot replace this information.

    Try changing the vocabulary

Same text, new vocabulary

Compare with the solution

The new piece ␠murmured, which is 9 characters long, is chosen before ␠mur, which is 4 characters long. The sequence becomes My | ␠aunt | ␠murmured | ., giving [11, 24, 58, 8]: 4 tokens.

The count decreases by 6 − 4 = 2 tokens, because the new piece replaces the 3 pieces of the verb with 1 single piece. The text retains its 17 characters and 3 words. Join the new pieces to check.

The claim does not follow from the calculation. We have proved a result for this table and this rule. For the real model, we must identify its tokenizer and the text actually encoded, including any reserved elements. The new count is neither a measured performance gain nor a recommendation to modify a model.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why is a word not a reliable unit for counting tokens?

The tokenizer uses a vocabulary and a rule: a word can occupy several pieces, and spaces or punctuation marks can contribute to tokenization. In our table, the verb and the full stop explain the difference between the counts.

Recall 2What does a token identifier measure? What should you count instead to find the length of the sequence?

It measures no length: it identifies an element of the vocabulary. Count the positions in the sequence, including all repeated occurrences.

Recall 3How can you check our sequence beyond its token count?

Replace the identifiers with their pieces, then join these pieces in order without adding spaces. Compare with the exact text, including punctuation; an identical count can hide an error in order or content.

Recall 4What information must you fix before stating a token count?

The exact text, vocabulary and rule of the tokenizer corresponding to the model. Also specify whether you are counting only the text or a representation containing reserved elements. An average number of characters per token does not provide an exact count.
