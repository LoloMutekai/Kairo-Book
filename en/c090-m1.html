Level 2 · C090·M1

Timestamping

Your goalReconstruct the order of timestamped readings, convert their time reference and calculate the durations between them without confusing measurement with reception.

Before you start
c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c037-m5 : controlled cleaning

✿01 See the idea

A sensor on a shelf sends readings to a data logger. Reading B waits while C has already reached its destination. If you read the file in arrival order A, C, B, the last row therefore does not contain the last temperature measured.

Timestamping means attaching a date and time to an event in a stated time reference. You must also name the event: “the sensor took a reading” and “the data logger received it” do not refer to the same instant. A temperature without this reference does not tell you when it was observed.

In this entirely fictional scene, A, B and C are reading identifiers. Their values are 20 °C, 20.5 °C and 21 °C respectively. They were measured 0, 1 and 4 min after the first reading. The drawing respects these durations: the last interval is longer.

A row number is only a position in a sequence. Adjacent rows may be separated by a long wait. We will keep each temperature together with its measurement time, then measure the time gaps on a common clock. We will not yet try to explain the change in temperature.

The rule to remember

Before comparing readings, name the timestamped event, convert the timestamps to a common reference, sort the time–value pairs and calculate their time gaps.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Place the sensor readings in time

On 20/10/2026 (day/month/year), the sensor measures A at 9 h 0 min, B at 9 h 1 min and C at 9 h 4 min. The seconds are 0. The display uses UTC + 2 h. The delays between measurement and reception are 5 s, 210 s and 8 s respectively. Let us calculate the chronology, intervals and reception order.

  1. Define what the timestamps mean and state the assumptions

    UTC stands for Coordinated Universal Time, our common reference. An offset of UTC + 2 h means that the local display is 2 h ahead of this reference. The numerical offset applies to the stated date; a country name or a time without a date would not be enough to determine it.

    We assume synchronised clocks, a resolution of 1 s and no change of offset or clock adjustment during this short period. Resolution indicates the smallest displayed step; it does not guarantee clock accuracy. In this scene, we treat the fictional times as exact.

    The pair (tᵢ, yᵢ) links a reading to its timestamp: i is its position after sorting and has no unit, tᵢ is its time in seconds since the start of the UTC day, and yᵢ is the temperature in °C. The symbol Δ means “difference”. Δtᵢ is the duration between the reading at position i and the previous reading. A position counts readings, not seconds.

  2. Convert the times to a common reference

    To convert the local display back to UTC, subtract the amount it is ahead: 9 − 2 = 7 h. Here, this conversion keeps the same date. If it crossed midnight, the date would also need to change; subtracting hours while ignoring the day would distort the order.

    Let sh be the number of seconds in an hour, and d the offset in seconds. We have sh = 60 × 60 = 3600 s/h, then d = 2 h × 3600 s/h = 7200 s. The displayed time of the first reading is 9 × 3600 = 32400 s since the start of the local day. Its time within the UTC day is therefore 32400 − 7200 = 25200 s.

    The minutes for B and C add 1 × 60 = 60 s and 4 × 60 = 240 s respectively to this time. We obtain t_A = 25200 s, t_B = 25260 s and t_C = 25440 s since the start of the same UTC day. Here, the subscripts A, B, C identify the named readings rather than their positions.

  3. Sort the pairs while keeping the temperatures attached

    The times satisfy 25200 < 25260 < 25440 s. The reading sequence is therefore A, B, C: (25200 s ; 20 °C), (25260 s ; 20.5 °C), (25440 s ; 21 °C). Move each complete row: sorting only the timestamp column would assign some temperatures to the wrong times.

    We can also place A at 0 s and subtract t_A from the other timestamps. This gives 60 s for B and 240 s for C. Changing the origin makes the numbers smaller without changing the order or durations.

  4. Subtract times, not positions

    Δti=ti−ti−1

    Positions start at 1; this formula applies from position 2 onwards. It subtracts the previous time from the current time, using the same unit and origin. A to B: 25260 − 25200 = 60 s. B to C: 25440 − 25260 = 180 s.

    The last interval is 180/60 = 3 times the first. Sampling, meaning the choice of times at which sensor readings are taken, is therefore irregular. The 3 readings do not form a grid with constant spacing. Do not infer from the gap that a reading must have been lost: the intended schedule is not provided.

  5. Calculate reception times separately

    Let rᵢ denote the reception time and ℓᵢ the delay, both in seconds; rᵢ and tᵢ have the same origin. The delay is the elapsed time between the events “taking a reading” and “receiving it”.

    ℓi=ri−ti;ri=ti+ℓi

    To simplify the calculation, subtract the time t_A from both measurement and reception times. Measured from reading A, the reception of A occurs at 0 + 5 = 5 s; that of B at 60 + 210 = 270 s; that of C at 240 + 8 = 248 s. Since 5 < 248 < 270 s, the arrival order is A, C, B. The delay of B is enough to reverse these arrivals without changing the order of the readings.

  6. Check the results and preserve what remains uncertain

    First check, using another method: from the start A to the end C, we can read directly 4 − 0 = 4 min, or 240 s. The sum of the intervals is also 60 + 180 = 240 s. The two methods agree.

    Second check: converting back to the initial display adds the offset. 7 + 2 = 9 h. The minutes remain those of each reading. Third check: all delays are positive, as required by our scene in which the data logger receives each reading after it is taken.

    Before any further analysis, keep the identifier, original measurement timestamp, standardised time reference, temperature and reception timestamp. A missing timestamp remains unknown; a repeated timestamp calls for an examination of identifiers, resolution and provenance. Neither row order nor formatting alone can fix an inaccurate clock.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations and redraw the readings, then the arrivals. Explain why B keeps its place between A and C despite arriving late. Find the total duration in two ways and name the assumptions that make your subtractions valid.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The last row received is the last reading taken.”

    B is received 270 s after A, after C is received at 248 s, even though B was measured before C. First name the timestamped event; then sort the sequence by the timestamp of that event, keeping complete rows together.

    Compare the chronology with the arrivals

  • “The same amount of time always passes between adjacent rows.”

    The gaps between readings are 60 s, then 180 s. Subtract the standardised timestamps; do not replace these gaps with a difference in row positions. A gap does not prove a loss unless the intended measurement schedule is known.

    Revisit the interval calculations

  • “The timestamp format guarantees that the clock is correct.”

    A precise display can come from a clock that is offset. The UTC reference makes timestamps comparable if their conversion is correct; it does not prove their accuracy. Check the date, offset and synchronisation before interpreting a delay.

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Find an actual time gap

The timestamps of B and C are 25260 s and 25440 s since the start of the same UTC day. Calculate the duration from B to C, then compare it with A to B, which is 60 s. What can you say about the spacing of the readings?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Subtract the starting time from the ending time; the two numbers have the same origin.

Hint 2

To compare the durations, divide B to C by A to B. No row position appears in either calculation.

Compare with the solution

B to C is 25440 − 25260 = 180 s. The ratio is 180/60 = 3. The last interval therefore lasts 3 times as long as the first: the readings are irregularly spaced.

This calculation proves neither a sensor failure nor a missing temperature reading. It shows only that the observed timestamps do not form a grid with constant spacing.

Check your reasoning

  • Write a subtraction in the correct direction and obtain 180 s.
  • Justify the irregular spacing by comparing 60 s and 180 s.
  • Leave the reason for the larger interval unknown.

Exercise 2 · Change of representation

C changes its display, not its time

A copy of the same reading C is displayed at 8 h 4 min in UTC + 1 h, on the same day. B is still displayed at 9 h 1 min in UTC + 2 h. The displayed hour for C looks smaller: has it become earlier than B? Convert and check.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Subtract each time’s own UTC offset from it.

Hint 2

Then convert the hours and minutes to seconds since the start of the UTC day, as in the example.

Compare with the solution

For C: 8 − 1 = 7 h UTC. In seconds, (8 × 3600) − (1 × 3600) + (4 × 60) = 28800 − 3600 + 240 = 25440 s. C returns to exactly its original time, 25440 s.

B remains at 7 h 1 min UTC, or 25260 s. C is therefore still 180 s later than B. Comparing only the displayed hours would have compared two different time references.

The reverse check adds 1 h to the UTC hour of C to recover 8 h. Keeping the original representation lets you check the conversion instead of overwriting its provenance.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the offset specific to each timestamp.
  • Recover 25440 s and recognise the same instant as C.
  • Conclude that B comes before C using the UTC timestamps, with a reverse check.

Exercise 3 · Reasoned diagnosis

Assess a proposed file repair

The data logger receives A, C, B. Someone proposes replacing all measurement timestamps with reception timestamps to “put time back in order”, then deleting a row if the timestamp of B appears twice. Respond using the delays and durations in our scene. What must be checked before deleting a row?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Sorting by reception answers “when was it available?”. Sorting by measurement answers a different question.

Hint 2

Calculate the delay of B from its position 60 s and its reception at 270 s. Identical timestamps can also result from the clock’s resolution.

Compare with the solution

B was measured 60 s after A, then received at 270 s: its delay is 270 − 60 = 210 s, or exactly 7/2 min, or 3.50 min in decimal form. It arrives 270 − 248 = 22 s after C. This is a late arrival, not a shift of the reading in time.

Replacing the measurement timestamp with the reception timestamp would make the temperature series describe arrivals. Keep both fields and choose the sorting order that answers the question. For the chronology of the temperature, the order remains A, B, C.

A repetition of t_B would give a gap of 25260 − 25260 = 0 s between these rows. This may indicate a copy, but it may also indicate distinct events that cannot be distinguished at second resolution. Check identifiers, values, precision and provenance; keep the original records. An identical timestamp is not enough to justify deletion or to impose a physical order on these events.

Check your reasoning

  • Find the delay of 210 s for B and the gap of 22 s after the reception of C.
  • Distinguish reordering by measurement time from a sequence of arrivals.
  • Explain at least two possible causes of a repeated timestamp and require evidence before deleting.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must you specify before saying that a reading is timestamped?

The timestamped event, the full date, the time and its reference. In our scene, measurement time and reception time are separate fields; they do not answer the same question.

Recall 2Why do we subtract a positive offset to return to UTC?

The displayed time is ahead of UTC: subtract this advance. Then check by adding the offset back, and preserve any date change if the conversion crosses midnight.

Recall 3How do you distinguish a row’s position from the duration between readings?

The position counts rows and has no unit of time. Calculate the duration by subtracting their times in the same reference. Here, the intervals are 60 s, then 180 s despite consecutive positions.

Recall 4Does a timestamp with second resolution prove clock accuracy and event uniqueness?

No. Resolution indicates the displayed step; the clock may be offset. Distinct events can share the same displayed timestamp. You must check synchronisation, identifiers and provenance to go further.

Recall 5Why does B remain between A and C in the reading sequence?

Its measurement timestamp precedes that of C. Its delay of 210 s explains why it is received after C; this wait does not change the instant when the sensor measured the temperature.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the example, reconstruct the chain: timestamped event → known date and offset → common reference → sorted time–value pairs → checked time differences. Then explain why the arrivals A, C, B do not impose that order on the readings, and name what a repeated timestamp leaves unknown.
