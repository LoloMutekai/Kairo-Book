Level 2 · C137·M2

Covariance

Your goalCalculate and interpret a covariance from paired measurements, then construct their matrix while keeping the units and the division convention explicit.

Before you start
c048-m5 : positivity · c050-m5 : conditioning · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c036-m5 : annotated graphs · c137-m1 : centering

✿01 See the idea

On a workshop table, you compare 4 empty pouches, labeled A, B, C and D. For each one, you record its width in centimeters and its mass in grams. The measurements are fictional: they help explain the calculation and do not describe a real product.

Is a pouch that is wider than the mean also heavier than the mean? To answer, look at each pair of deviations. If the deviations have the same sign, their product is positive; if they have opposite signs, it is negative. The covariance is the mean of these signed products.

In the scatter plot, A is below both means and D is above both. These pouches contribute positively. C is wider but lighter than the means: it goes against this trend. B is exactly at the mean mass and contributes nothing to the product.

This interpretation uses the means as reference points, rather than alphabetical order or the passage of time. A positive result does not require every pair to contribute positively. It also does not prove that width causes mass: materials and manufacturing are not studied here.

The rule to remember

Keep the pairs, center each column, multiply the deviations for the same pouch, then take their mean. State the divisor and the product unit.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From paired measurements to the covariance matrix

Here is the complete fictional dataset: A = (10 cm; 20 g), B = (12 cm; 30 g), C = (14 cm; 25 g), D = (16 cm; 45 g). We describe these 4 pouches with the same weight for each. No values are missing, and the two measurements in a row refer to the same pouch.

  1. Choose exactly what to calculate

    n is the number of pouches and has no unit. The index i identifies a row in the dataset; xᵢ is its width in cm and yᵢ its mass in g. The bar in x̄ and ȳ denotes the column mean. Let aᵢ = xᵢ − x̄ be the width deviation, in cm, and bᵢ = yᵢ − ȳ the mass deviation, in g.

    cxᵧ denotes the descriptive covariance here, in cm·g. The symbol ∑ means to add the indicated term over all rows, from i = 1 to i = n. The product aᵢbᵢ keeps the pairing through i.

    cxy=1n∑i=1naibi

    We divide by n to describe the complete dataset with equal weights. This definition requires neither a normal distribution nor independence between rows. Estimating a population covariance from a sample is a different question: in that context, the divisor n − 1 is commonly used. Results must use the same convention to be compared; we keep n throughout this page.

  2. Calculate the means to use as origins

    For width: 10 + 12 = 22 cm and 14 + 16 = 30 cm; the sum is 52 cm. The mean is therefore 52 ÷ 4 = 13 cm.

    For mass: 20 + 30 = 50 g and 25 + 45 = 70 g; the sum is 120 g. The mean is 120 ÷ 4 = 30 g. These separate origins allow us to compare relative positions despite the different units.

  3. Center each measurement while keeping the pairs

    For A: a = 10 − 13 = -3 cm ; b = 20 − 30 = -10 g. For B: a = 12 − 13 = -1 cm ; b = 30 − 30 = 0 g.

    For C: a = 14 − 13 = 1 cm ; b = 25 − 30 = -5 g. For D: a = 16 − 13 = 3 cm ; b = 45 − 30 = 15 g.

    Check the centering: -3 + (-1) + 1 + 3 = 0 cm and -10 + 0 + (-5) + 15 = 0 g. A nonzero sum of deviations here would signal a mean calculation or subtraction that needs to be checked.

  4. Form the signed products row by row

    The contribution of A is (-3) × (-10) = 30 cm·g: it is below both means. B gives (-1) × 0 = 0 cm·g, since its mass is exactly at the mean.

    C gives 1 × (-5) = -5 cm·g: width above the mean, mass below. D gives 3 × 15 = 45 cm·g: both deviations are positive.

    Keep the sign. Taking the absolute value of the product for C would remove precisely the opposition that the covariance must account for. Multiplying all the widths together would not measure their relationship with mass.

  5. Take the mean and state exactly what the result describes

    First add A and B: 30 + 0 = 30 cm·g. Then C and D: (-5) + 45 = 40 cm·g. The sum of all contributions is 70 cm·g.

    cxy=704=17.5 cm·g

    The mean of the products is positive: in this dataset, contributions from deviations with the same sign outweigh contributions from deviations with opposite signs. C remains a visible exception. The result says neither how many grams to add per centimeter nor how a new pouch would behave.

    A 0 covariance would mean that the sum of centered products is 0. Opposing contributions can cancel each other out: that would not be enough to conclude that the quantities are independent.

  6. Place the variances and covariance in a matrix

    A descriptive variance is the mean of the squared deviations in a single column. For x, the squares of the extreme deviations are each 9 cm² and the others are each 1 cm². Their sum is 2 × (9 + 1) = 2 × 10 = 20 cm². Therefore cxx = 20 ÷ 4 = 5 cm².

    For y, the squares are 100, 0, 25 and 225 g². The contributions of A and C give 125 g²; with that of D and the 0 square for B, the sum is 350 g². Thus cᵧᵧ = 350 ÷ 4 = 87.5 g².

    The matrix C orders the columns as width, mass. The entries cxx and cᵧᵧ are the variances; the off-diagonal entries cxᵧ and cᵧx are equal because aᵢbᵢ = bᵢaᵢ in every row. This equality is called the symmetry of C.

    C=[cxxcxycyxcyy]=[517.517.587.5]

    The matrix has no single common unit: the width entry is in cm², the mass entry in g², and the off-diagonal entries in cm·g. The displayed numbers assume these fixed units.

  7. Check the consistency of the matrix

    A covariance matrix constructed from the same rows and the same weights is positive semidefinite: the variance of any linear combination of the columns remains nonnegative. Indeed, for fixed coefficients u and v, this variance is the mean of the squares of the sums u aᵢ + v bᵢ. The units of u and v must make the two terms compatible for addition.

    For our symmetric 2 × 2 matrix, check the diagonal entries and the determinant. The diagonal entries 5 and 87.5 are positive. The determinant, written det(C), is the product of the diagonal entries minus the product of the off-diagonal entries:

    det(C)=5×87.5−17.52=437.50−306.25=131.25

    Its unit is cm²·g². With a positive first diagonal entry and this positive determinant, the matrix is even positive definite in these coordinates. A negative determinant would contradict an exact covariance matrix constructed in this way. A 0 determinant, however, could be valid.

    These checks detect some calculation errors; they do not prove that the measurements were correctly paired. The evidence for the pairing remains the label on each pouch, retained from the original dataset.

  8. Connect the scale of the numbers to the scale of the units

    Now express the same masses in kilograms. Since 1 kg = 1000 g, each mass and each mass deviation is divided by 1000. The width deviations remain in centimeters; each cross product is therefore divided by the same factor.

    The covariance becomes 35/2 ÷ 1000 = 7/400 cm·kg, or exactly 0.0175 cm·kg. Its numerical value changes without any change to the pouches or their relationship. A covariance therefore does not measure a strength that can be compared across all units.

    The matrix brings together the dispersions and joint variations that PCA will use later. Choosing an optimal projection belongs to the next unit; this calculation does not yet choose any axis or perform any compression.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results. Explain why A contributes positively despite its two negative deviations, why C contributes negatively, and why B contributes nothing. Then reconstruct the matrix, naming the unit of each entry.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The widths and masses are positive, so the covariance must be positive.”

    The deviations from the means are what we multiply. Those of C have opposite signs and give -5 cm·g. Repeating the centering prevents confusion between the mean of the raw products and the covariance.

    Review the centering and signed contributions

  • “I can sort the widths and masses separately before calculating.”

    Sorting the columns separately can pair the mass of one pouch with the width of another. The means would be preserved, but the cross products would no longer describe the dataset. If you reorder the data, always move the entire row with its label.

    Identify the pouches behind the points in the scatter plot

  • “A numerically larger covariance proves a stronger relationship.”

    The same pouches give 17.5 cm·g or 0.0175 cm·kg depending on the mass unit. Start by checking the units, centering and divisor before making any comparison. Covariance is not a coefficient without units.

    Test the change of units in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Read, then calculate

The pouch that goes against the trend

Without rereading the calculation, find the deviations for C from its width 14 cm, its mass 25 g and the means 13 cm and 30 g. Calculate its contribution. Why is the contribution of B, with mass 30 g, different?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Subtract the mean of the correct column from each measurement for C.

Hint 2

The product keeps the signs. For B, first examine the mass deviation.

Compare with the solution

C has deviations 14 − 13 = 1 cm and 25 − 30 = -5 g. Their product is -5 cm·g. A width above the mean accompanies a mass below the mean here: its contribution is negative.

B has a mass deviation of 30 − 30 = 0 g. Its product is therefore 0 cm·g, even though its width deviation -1 cm is nonzero. Neither C alone nor B alone gives the covariance of the dataset: all contributions must be averaged.

Check your reasoning

  • Subtract the means before multiplying.
  • Obtain -5 cm·g for C and 0 cm·g for B, explaining the signs.
  • Distinguish a single contribution from the mean of all contributions.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

Do the pouches change when the unit changes?

A colleague expresses the masses of the same pouches in kg and keeps the widths in cm. They announce: “The covariance has decreased, so the relationship has become weaker.” Predict the sign, calculate the new covariance and answer the objection. Then explain whether adding the same constant to all widths would change the covariance.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The mean mass and each mass deviation are divided by 1000.

Hint 2

When a constant is added to a column, its mean increases by the same constant: examine what remains in the subtraction.

Compare with the solution

The conversion factor is positive: the sign stays positive. Each cross product and their mean are divided by 1000. The covariance is therefore 35/2 ÷ 1000 = 7/400 cm·kg, or 0.0175 cm·kg exactly.

The number has changed because the unit has changed. It describes the same pairs of measurements for the pouches; no change in their relationship has been established. The units must be stated and made consistent before comparing covariances.

Let h be the constant added to all widths, in cm. The new mean is x̄ + h. The deviation (xᵢ + h) − (x̄ + h) is still xᵢ − x̄: the products and covariance are preserved. Shifting the origin by addition and changing the scale by multiplication therefore have different effects.

Check your reasoning

  • Predict that the sign stays the same and justify the conversion factor.
  • Give 0.0175 cm·kg with its unit.
  • Answer the objection without attributing a physical change to the pouches.
  • Show how the added constant cancels in the centered deviation.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why must the two columns stay paired?

Each product must pair the width and mass of the same pouch. Sorting one column alone changes these pairings, even if the means stay the same.

Recall 2How do you reconstruct our covariance, and which convention must you state?

Subtract the mean of each column, multiply the deviations in each row, then average these signed products. We divide by n to describe the dataset with equal weights; the divisor n − 1 belongs to another convention that must be made explicit.

Recall 3What does a positive covariance mean, and what does it fail to prove?

The sum of centered products is positive: contributions from deviations with the same sign outweigh those from deviations with opposite signs. This does not make all contributions positive and does not prove any causality.

Recall 4What do the diagonal and off-diagonal entries of C contain? Why is it symmetric?

The diagonal entries are means of squared deviations, so they are variances. The off-diagonal entries are covariances; they are equal because the order of the factors does not change the products in a row. The units depend on the entry.

Recall 5Why does switching from grams to kilograms change the covariance, while adding a constant to the widths preserves it?

The conversion divides all mass deviations and all products by 1000. A constant added to the width is also added to its mean and cancels during centering. The covariance carries the product unit cm·g or cm·kg, rather than a universal strength without units.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the formulas, reconstruct the process: identify the pairs and their units; fix the weights and divisor; calculate the means; center; form and then average the signed products; place variances and covariances in C; check symmetry and consistency; limit the interpretation to the dataset and its units.
