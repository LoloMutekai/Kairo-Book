Level 2 · C046·M4

Remainder

Your goalExpress what a Taylor polynomial leaves out, then bound this remainder over the entire interval of the expansion.

Before you start
c031-m5 : asymptotes · c045-m5 : introductory quadrature · c046-m1 : series tests · c046-m2 : power series · c046-m3 : Taylor

✿01 See the idea

A workshop draws a square plate with side length 8 cm, then increases its area from 64 cm² to 80 cm². What will the new side length be? The square root gives the exact length; the Taylor polynomial provides a shorter calculation. We still need to know what this shortcut has left out.

Imagine the distance between the polynomial result and the exact length. This signed difference is the remainder: it gives the displacement to add to the polynomial to recover the function value. A bound on the remainder is an upper limit on the size of this displacement. Knowing this limit does not mean knowing the exact displacement.

The lower diagram shows a permitted range for the side length. Its left endpoint comes from the polynomial; its right endpoint adds a proven margin. The exact value lies inside. We will justify both the margin and which side of the polynomial result contains the exact value.

The dimensions are fictional. We assume a perfect square and exact areas in the model. The remainder studied here comes only from replacing the square root with a polynomial; it represents neither a measurement error nor a manufacturing tolerance.

The rule to remember

The remainder is the exact difference between the function and the polynomial. To bound it, bound the next derivative over the entire interval between the center and the target point.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Bound the side length without calculating a decimal square root

The initial side length is 8 cm and the final area is 80 cm². Let us use the Taylor polynomial of degree 2 for the normalized square root, centered at 0. We will find exact bounds before displaying any decimal values.

  1. Express the change in area as a dimensionless number

    Let L be the initial side length in cm, A the final area in cm², and ℓ the final side length in cm. The initial area is the square of L. The relative change in area h is the added area divided by the initial area: the cm² units cancel. Here the initial area is 8 × 8 = 64 cm², then h = (80 − 64)/64 = 16/64 = 1/4.

    h=A−L2L2;ℓ=L1+h

    Define f(t) = √(1 + t), where t is a dimensionless argument greater than -1. The square root denotes the positive root. f is dimensionless, and ℓ = L f(h). Our radicand 1 + h is 5/4. The center of the expansion is a = 0; the target point is a + h = h.

  2. Separate the polynomial from the exact remainder

    Pn is the Taylor polynomial constructed at the center a through degree n; n is a nonnegative integer. Rn denotes what this polynomial leaves out. The following definition is an exact equality, even when we do not know the value of the remainder.

    Rn(a+h)=f(a+h)−Pn(a+h)

    Here n = 2. The derivatives at the center give f(0) = 1, f′(0) = 1/2 and f″(0) = -1/4. The quadratic coefficient is f″(0)/2, because 2! = 2 × 1. The prime marks indicate successive differentiations with respect to t.

    P2(t)=1+t2−t28

    At the point h, the linear term is 1/4 ÷ 2 = 1/8; the square of h is 1/16, so the term to subtract is 1/16 ÷ 8 = 1/128. Thus P of degree 2 at h is 1 + 1/8 − 1/128 = 9/8 − 1/128 = 143/128. This result is dimensionless.

  3. Give the remainder a form that allows it to be bounded

    Taylor’s theorem with the Lagrange remainder applies if f has continuous derivatives through order n + 1 on an open interval containing the entire interval between a and a + h. For nonzero h, there then exists a point ξ strictly between these endpoints such that:

    Rn(a+h)=f(n+1)(ξ)(n+1)!hn+1

    f with a parenthesized superscript denotes a derivative of order n + 1, rather than a power. The factorial m!, for a positive integer m, multiplies the integers from 1 to m; 0! is 1. In our case, the next order is 3 and 3! = 3 × 2 × 1 = 6.

    ξ is not an input we choose: the theorem guarantees its existence and does not give its value. The remainder therefore cannot be obtained by simply replacing ξ with the center or an endpoint. For h = 0, the polynomial and the function agree at the center, so the remainder there is 0.

  4. Bound the required derivative over the entire interval

    The normalized square root has the following derivatives for t > -1. The fractional exponents describe powers of the positive quantity 1 + t.

    f′(t)=121+t
    f″(t)=−14(1+t)32
    f‴(t)=38(1+t)52

    Over the interval from 0 to 1/4, 1 + t is at least 1. The denominator of f‴ is therefore at least 8, and the derivative is positive and at most 3/8. We take M = 3/8, where M is an upper bound on the absolute value of the next derivative over this entire interval. The continuity assumptions are satisfied because this interval lies within the domain t > -1.

    All the quantities f, t, P, R and M for this normalized function are dimensionless. Only the final multiplication by L will give a length.

  5. Calculate the bound, then use the sign

    Take absolute values in the remainder formula. If the absolute value of the next derivative is bounded everywhere by M, we obtain:

    |Rn(a+h)|≤M(n+1)!|h|n+1

    Here the cube of h is 1/16 × 1/4 = 1/64. Multiplying by M gives 3/8 × 1/64 = 3/512; dividing by 3! gives 3/512 ÷ 6 = 1/1024. The factorial is part of the theorem: omitting it gives a different bound, which is larger in this example.

    0<R2(h)≤1/1024

    The absolute value bound alone would allow a remainder of either sign. Here f‴(ξ) and the cube of h are strictly positive: the remainder is positive. The function value therefore exceeds the polynomial value. The number 1/1024 is a proven upper limit, rather than the exact value of R.

  6. Return to centimeters while keeping track of the remainder

    Let ℓa = L times P of degree 2 at h be the approximate side length, and Δ = ℓ − ℓa the remainder in length. Since L is positive, multiplying the bounds by L preserves the direction of the inequalities. The approximate side length is 8 × 143/128 = 143/16 cm. Its maximum margin is 8 × 1/1024 = 1/128 cm.

    143/16 cm<ℓ≤1145/128 cm

    The right endpoint is the sum 143/16 + 1/128 = 1145/128 cm. Displayed as a decimal, ℓa = 8.9375 cm. We keep the margin in its exact form 1/128 cm: rounding a bound down would invalidate the stated guarantee.

  7. Check the bounds by another method

    The exact length is positive and its square is 80 cm². For positive lengths, squaring preserves order: we can therefore check the bounds without Taylor and without calculating a decimal square root.

    The square of the left endpoint is 143/16 × 143/16 = 20449/256 cm². It falls short of the required area by 80 − 20449/256 = 31/256 cm²: the left endpoint is too small.

    The square of the right endpoint is 1145/128 × 1145/128 = 1311025/16384 cm². It exceeds the required area by 1311025/16384 − 80 = 305/16384 cm²: the right endpoint is large enough. The signs of these differences confirm the bounds. They still do not give the exact remainder.

    We have proved a guarantee for this point and this model. No infinite series or limit as the degree increases was needed. The next unit will distinguish ways of expressing approximation error.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I found 1/1024, so that is the exact remainder.”

    Start again from R = f − P: this equality defines the remainder. The calculation with M gives only an inequality. For our square, the sign of f‴ proves that the remainder is positive; it does not turn its bound into an exact value.

    Reread the definition of the remainder and the calculation of its bound

  • “I can use the next derivative at the target point as an upper bound.”

    The point ξ can lie anywhere inside the interval. Here f‴ decreases as t increases: its value at the point h is smaller than some intermediate values. Use the bound 3/8 obtained over the entire interval.

    Review the bound on the derivative

  • “The remainder is at most 1/1024 cm.”

    The number 1/1024 bounds R for the normalized square root, which is dimensionless. The remainder in length is Δ = L R: its bound is 8 × 1/1024 = 1/128 cm.

    Return to the step on centimeters

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · distinguish the quantities

What has actually been calculated?

For the same square, keep h = 1/4 and M = 3/8. Recalculate the bound on R for degree 2. Someone then writes “R = 1/1024”. Correct this claim and state the sign of the remainder.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The cube of h is 1/64; the factorial of the next order is 6.

Hint 2

The formula contains the derivative at ξ; M gives only an upper bound on this derivative.

Compare with the solution

The bound is M multiplied by the cube of h, then divided by 6: 3/8 × 1/64 ÷ 6 = 3/512 ÷ 6 = 1/1024. This is a bound on a dimensionless number.

We must write 0 < R ≤ 1/1024. Strict positivity follows from f‴(ξ) > 0 and h > 0 in the Lagrange formula. The claimed equality does not follow from the calculation: we have calculated neither ξ nor f(h) − P(h).

  • You write an inequality and justify the sign separately.
  • You assign no unit of length to R.

Exercise 2 · Application · convert the bounds

The drawing needs dimensions in millimeters

The workshop now wants the side length in mm, with 10 mm per cm. Using the same polynomial and its remainder, give the approximate side length, the remainder bound in mm, and exact bounds on the final side length. The plate and its area do not change.

Hint 1

First multiply the dimensionless remainder by L, then convert cm to mm.

Hint 2

The approximate side length and the margin must undergo the same conversion. The remainder here is positive.

Compare with the solution

The approximate side length is 143/16 cm, which is 10 × 143/16 = 715/8 mm. The bound in length is 1/128 cm, which is 10 × 1/128 = 5/64 mm.

The upper endpoint is 715/8 + 5/64 = 5725/64 mm. We obtain 715/8 mm < ℓ ≤ 5725/64 mm. Displayed as decimals, the approximate side length is 89.375 mm and the maximum margin is 0.078125 mm; these terminating decimals are exact.

Changing the unit increases the displayed numbers by a factor of 10, without changing the physical guarantee. Converting only the approximate side length would leave a margin expressed in a different unit.

Exercise 3 · Analysis · repair a proof

Was the point ξ chosen?

For the same plate, someone claims: “I take ξ = h in the Lagrange formula; the derivative there is small, so my upper limit is guaranteed.” Identify the flaw, repair the proof with a valid upper bound, then propose a check using the areas of the squares at the endpoints.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The theorem says “there exists ξ”. It does not allow us to choose an endpoint.

Hint 2

As t increases over our interval, the denominator of f‴ increases. To check positive lengths, compare their squares with the target area.

Compare with the solution

ξ must be an interior point whose value is unknown. In addition, f‴ is decreasing over the interval: its value at h is less than the values taken before h. This value is therefore not an upper bound over the entire interval. A smaller upper limit is useful only if its proof is valid.

Repair the proof by observing that 1 + t ≥ 1 for 0 ≤ t ≤ 1/4. It follows that 0 < f‴(t) ≤ 3/8. The formula again gives 0 < R ≤ 1/1024, then 0 cm < Δ ≤ 1/128 cm after multiplication by L.

The independent check uses 143/16 × 143/16 = 20449/256 < 80 and 1145/128 × 1145/128 = 1311025/16384 > 80, in cm². The differences 31/256 cm² and 305/16384 cm² are positive. Since the bounds are positive lengths, these comparisons do place the exact side length between them.

Check your reasoning

  • You distinguish the existence of ξ from a choice of its value.
  • You justify the upper bound at every point of the interval.
  • You present another way to check the bounds and recall that the lengths are positive.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do we define the remainder of a Taylor polynomial? Is it already a bound?

The remainder is the signed difference Rₙ = f − Pₙ at the target point. It is an exact quantity. An inequality on its absolute value then gives a bound, which is not necessarily attained.

Recall 2What does the Lagrange form say, and what do we know about ξ?

With continuous derivatives through order n + 1 on an open interval containing the interval between the endpoints, the remainder equals the derivative of order n + 1 at ξ, multiplied by h to the power n + 1, then divided by (n + 1)!. For nonzero h, ξ lies strictly between a and a + h; its value remains unknown.

Recall 3Over what region must the upper bound M apply?

Over the entire interval connecting the center to the target point: the absolute value of the next derivative must be at most M throughout. A derivative value at just one endpoint is not sufficient without justification.

Recall 4How do we go from the remainder for the normalized square root to the remainder in the side length of the square?

Multiply by the initial side length L: Δ = L R. Here the dimensionless bound 1/1024 becomes 8 × 1/1024 = 1/128 cm. The positive sign of the remainder follows separately from the next derivative and the cube of h.

Recall 5How can we check the bounds on the side length without using a decimal square root?

Check that both bounds are positive, then square them. The square of the lower bound is less than the target area; that of the upper bound is greater. The squaring function preserves order for positive lengths.
