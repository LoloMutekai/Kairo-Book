Level 2 · C062·M1

Measurement, score and probability

Your goalDistinguish a sensor reading, a constructed score and a probability for a specific event, then justify each number.

Before you start
c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c056-m5 : effect size · c009-m5 : reading notes

✿01 See the idea

The probe in a workshop room displays 28 °C. Beside it, a monitoring display shows a heat score of 0.80. A forecast gives a 50 % probability of exceeding 30 °C in 10 minutes. These numbers concern the same room, but answer different questions.

The measurement reports what the sensor has just read, in a physical unit. The score places this reading on a scale we have chosen. The probability describes the possibilities for a future event, according to a model. The sensor does not directly read the probability of that event.

The whole scene is fictional: the reading, scale and possible futures are chosen for this exercise. The calculated percentages belong to the fictional model; they are neither an observed success rate nor a validated forecast for a real workshop.

Before calculating, ask yourself what would need to be specified below each display for another reader to understand it. A value must come with its unit or scale and the question it answers.

The rule to remember

To read a number, identify its origin: an observation with a unit, a scoring rule, or an event and a probability model. A change of scale provides no evidence of reliability.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the probe reading to a clearly defined forecast

The current reading is z = 28 °C. We choose a score origin a = 20 °C and a reference difference d = 10 °C. To forecast the temperature in 10 minutes, the fictional model includes only 26, 29, 31 and 34 °C, assumed equally likely after this reading. Let us calculate the score and the probability of strictly exceeding 30 °C.

  1. Identify the measurement, future quantity and assumptions

    Z denotes the sensor reading before we observe it; z is the value actually displayed now. Both are expressed in degrees Celsius. This measurement may contain an error: we do not claim that it equals the actual temperature. No model of sensor error is provided here; we can construct a score from the reading without resolving this question.

    T denotes the actual future temperature, in °C, at the time horizon h = 10 minutes. E denotes the event "T exceeds 30 °C at that instant". Strictly exceeding excludes a temperature equal to the threshold. The event does not mean exceeding the threshold at any time before that instant.

    The model is conditional on the current reading of 28 °C: its possible futures are given after receiving this information. They are mutually exclusive, cover all the possibilities in this simplified model and have equal weight. Equal probability is a stated assumption, rather than a conclusion drawn from the sensor.

  2. Construct a score from an explicit rule

    s denotes the score, with no unit. We choose s = (z − a)/d. a is the origin temperature for the score, in °C; d is a strictly positive temperature difference, in °C. Subtracting a identifies how far the reading is from the chosen origin; dividing by d expresses this displacement in reference differences.

    s=z−ad=28−2010=810=0.80

    The numerator is 8 °C, the difference between two temperatures. The ratio of differences with the same unit has no unit. The score increases as z increases, since d is positive: it allows us to rank readings according to this rule.

    Reverse check: a + ds = 20 + 10 × 0.80 = 28 °C. At z = a, the score would be 0; a difference d above a would give it 1. The rule can also produce numbers outside this interval: it is unbounded.

  3. Define the probability before counting

    p is the probability of E under the stated model, after observing the reading z. P is the symbol that assigns a probability to an event. The vertical bar is read as "given"; it recalls the information already received. This notation describes our conditional model, rather than validation against real observations.

    p=P(T>30 °C∣Z=28 °C)

    Each future has weight 1. Of the 4 futures, 26 and 29 °C do not exceed 30 °C; 31 and 34 °C exceed it. The number of favorable cases is therefore 1 + 1 = 2; the total is 2 + 2 = 4.

    Counting cases and dividing by their total number is justified by equal probability. With different weights, we would need to add the favorable weights and divide by the total weight, as in the last exercise.

  4. Calculate and check the forecast

    p=cas favorablescas possibles=24=1/2=0.50

    Probability has no physical unit. To express it as a percentage, multiply by 100: 0.50 × 100 = 50 %. This conversion changes the presentation of the same probability value.

    Check using the complement: not exceeding the threshold covers 2 cases out of 4, giving 1/2. The two events are mutually exclusive and cover all cases: 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. Each probability lies between 0 and 1.

    This check verifies that the calculation is consistent with the assumptions. It does not demonstrate that the chosen futures are sufficient for a real room or that their weights are appropriate.

  5. Read the results without adding a promise

    The measurement is 28 °C now. The score is 0.80 on our scale. The probability is 0.50 for the defined event and time horizon. There is no contradiction: each number answers its own question.

    Multiplying the score by 100 gives 80 points on the expanded scale. Writing 80 % probability from this calculation alone would add an unjustified interpretation. Having no unit, or even lying between 0 and 1, is not enough to define a probability.

    A score can be designed to estimate a probability, provided that the event and the justification for this interpretation are specified. Here, p comes from an explicit model. To call it calibrated in practice, we would need to compare its forecasts with outcomes actually observed in relevant situations. Calibration means this agreement between stated probabilities and observed frequencies; a detailed check belongs to a later unit.

    Finally, a probability is not yet an instruction to act. Deciding whether to intervene requires an additional decision criterion, which we do not set in this unit.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Recall what each display means, then explain why the reading determines the score but does not by itself determine the weights of the futures. Finish with the check using the complement.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The score is between 0 and 1, so it is a probability.”

    Recall the rule (z − a)/d and look for the event whose probability it would give. Here, it locates a temperature difference; it supplies no weights for futures. The score calculation and the calculation of p have separate justifications.

    Review the construction of the score and the model of futures

  • “The probe displays 28 °C: I know the temperature in 10 minutes.”

    The display is a present reading. For the future, our model proposes several possible values after this reading. State the time horizon and the event before making any probability forecast; even the present reading may contain an error.

    Reread the measurement and probability cards

  • “I count the possible temperatures; their weights do not matter.”

    The ratio of the numbers of cases assumes that each case has equal weight. If the weights change, the same list of temperatures can give a different probability, while the score for the reading stays the same.

    Redo the exercise on the weights of the futures

Exercise 2 · Calculation and limits of interpretation

Another reading on the same scale

The same probe now displays 32 °C. Keep a = 20 °C and d = 10 °C. Calculate the new score, check it in reverse and interpret its value. The model of futures in the example was given only after the reading 28 °C: can the same probability automatically be assigned after this new reading?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First subtract the origin, then divide by the reference difference.

Hint 2

The upper bound of a probability is not a bound on our scoring rule. The conditional model must correspond to the information received.

Compare with the solution

The difference is 32 − 20 = 12 °C. The score is 12/10 = 6/5 = 1.20, with no unit. Reverse check: 20 + 10 × 1.20 = 32 °C.

The score exceeds 1 because the reading is more than one difference d above a. This is allowed on this scale; 120 points after expansion by 100 do not constitute 120 % probability.

The distribution of futures was specified after 28 °C, rather than after 32 °C. Without an additional assumption linking the futures to this new reading, the corresponding probability is not determined by the exercise statement. Neither 1/2 nor 0 is assigned by default.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 6/5 and verify the reconstruction of the reading.
  • Explain why this score can exceed 1.
  • Identify the conditional scope of the model without inventing a new probability.

Exercise 3 · Reasoned transfer

The same reading, different weights

Start again from z = 28 °C and the same futures at 10 minutes. Another model assigns them relative weights 1, 1, 1 and 3, respectively for 26, 29, 31 and 34 °C. A weight expresses relative importance, with no unit; it must be divided by the total to obtain a probability. Recalculate p, check its complement and answer the objection "the measurement and score have not changed, so p cannot change".

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The favorable futures remain the same; add their weights before dividing by the sum of all the weights.

Hint 2

Compare what changed in the model with what stayed the same in the score calculation.

Compare with the solution

The favorable weights total 1 + 3 = 4. The unfavorable weights total 1 + 1 = 2. The total is 2 + 4 = 6. Therefore p = 4/6 = 2/3 ≈ 0.6667, or approximately 66.67 %.

The complement is 2/6 = 1/3. Exact check: 2/3 + 1/3 = 1. Keep the fractions until the end: the displayed decimals are rounded.

The score remains 0.80, because z, a and d are unchanged. The probability changes because the model weights change. Neither the initial model nor this weighted model is claimed to be more faithful to the real world: their weights are fictional, and no observed outcome distinguishes between them here.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the sum of the favorable weights divided by the sum of all the weights.
  • Obtain 2/3 and check the complement with exact fractions.
  • Link the difference in probability to the change in model, without changing the score or claiming a real gain.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the measurement 28 °C tell us, and what does it leave open?

It reports the current probe display in degrees Celsius. It guarantees neither the actual present temperature nor its value in 10 minutes.

Recall 2Why does our score have no unit, and why does that not make it a probability?

s = (z − a)/d divides a temperature difference by a difference with the same unit. The rule constructs a scale; it does not give the weights of the outcomes of an event. It can exceed 1.

Recall 3What details make our probability interpretable?

The event is T > 30 °C at the time horizon 10 minutes; the model of futures is given after the reading z = 28 °C. The cases are exhaustive, mutually exclusive and equally likely in the initial model. The 2 favorable cases out of 4 give 1/2.

Recall 4How can the same futures give a different probability with the same score?

The score depends on the reading and its rule. The probability also depends on the weights of the futures. Weights of 1, 1, 1, 3 give 2/3 instead of 1/2, without changing the reading or the score.

Recall 5What does the sum of p and its complement check? What else would be needed to claim real reliability?

The total 1 checks the consistency of the model and calculation for these complementary events. It does not prove that the weights are appropriate. The probabilities would need to be compared with observed outcomes in relevant situations; reading the material or giving an assisted answer never constitutes an attained level.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the figures, reconstruct the chain of justifications: what the probe read and in which unit; the rule that produces the score; the future event, its time horizon and the model that assigns weights to its outcomes; the favorable/total ratio and its check using the complement. Finally, explain why a consistent calculation still needs validation against reality.
