Synthetic datasets and external validity

Your goalBuild a small ticket dataset, compare rules for predicting time to completion, and explain what is missing before their ranking can be applied outside the simulation.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

At a workshop service counter, a screen announces how much time remains until service for your ticket is complete. Rule A always announces 2 min; rule B always announces 5 min. To compare them, we create tickets whose course of service we know. The data, durations, and mixtures on this page are entirely fictional.

In the quiet case, nobody is ahead of our ticket. In the peak case, 3 people are waiting ahead of it. Each service takes 2 min. A seems well chosen when the counter is empty; when a queue is waiting, its announced time is too short. The question is then: what share did we give peak periods in our dataset?

A synthetic dataset is a set of cases created according to explicit rules. It can be used to practise a calculation, compare methods, or look for a weakness. Its ground truth is what those rules produce. External validity concerns how far the result applies in a target situation, such as the same counter on a Friday evening.

A ranking that is exact for our tickets can depend on their distribution. Recreating the same dataset very carefully does not show that this distribution, the service durations, and the interruptions resemble those on Friday. We will isolate this difficulty by changing only the proportion of peak cases.

The rule to remember

Always link a score to how its cases were generated. A synthetic comparison prepares for a check in the target situation; it does not provide that check.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Which mixture of tickets have we actually evaluated?

The initial dataset combines 8 quiet cases and 2 peak cases. We fix A and B before the calculation, then give them exactly the same tickets. The criterion is the mean absolute error in the time from arrival to the end of service; announcing too early or too late has the same cost.

  1. Write the generation specification

    Each row of the dataset describes a separate short episode. When our ticket arrives, the counter is available, but q people must be served ahead of it. Service follows the queue order, with no break and no new priority. One staff member serves at a time; no service is already under way. All service durations are s = 2 min. Time since arrival starts at 0 min.

    q is a count of people, with no physical unit; s is the duration per service; d(q) is the total time to completion in minutes produced by the generator. The letter q in parentheses indicates that this time depends on the queue. We must serve q people, then our ticket, giving:

    d(q)=(q+1)s

    Quiet: q = 0, waiting time 0 × 2 = 0 min, then 0 + 2 = 2 min. Peak: q = 3, waiting time 3 × 2 = 6 min, then 6 + 2 = 8 min. This reference is known because we created it.

  2. Compare errors in each situation

    The letter r denotes the chosen rule, A or B; aᵣ is its announced time, in minutes. Here a_A = 2 min and a_B = 5 min. Eᵣ(q) is the distance between the announced time and the reference time to completion. The vertical bars take the absolute value: they make a negative difference positive.

    Er(q)=|ar−d(q)|

    For A, the quiet-case error is |2 − 2| = 0 min; the peak-case error is |2 − 8| = 6 min. For B, we get |5 − 2| = 3 min and |5 − 8| = 3 min.

    A is therefore better in quiet cases, B in peak cases. The overall score must state how many tickets belong to each situation. An average that hides these groups conceals this dependence.

  3. Add before dividing

    The total N counts the tickets, giving each the same weight: N = 8 + 2 = 10. For A, quiet tickets contribute 8 × 0 = 0 min of cumulative error; peak tickets contribute 2 × 6 = 12 min. We add, then divide by the number of tickets:

    LA=0+1210=1.20 min

    Lᵣ denotes the mean error of rule r, in minutes per evaluated ticket. For B, the contributions are 24 min and 6 min: (24 + 6)/10 = 30/10 = 3.00 min. A obtains the lower score in this dataset. This result fully describes our list; there is no random sampling here.

  4. Reveal the hidden weight

    p is the proportion of peak tickets, with no unit; it lies between 0 and 1. In our list, p = 2/10 = 1/5. The quiet proportion is 1 − p. Grouping the terms of the average gives the same operation:

    Lr(p)=(1−p)Er(0)+pEr(3)

    Thus L_A(p) = 6p min and L_B(p) = 3 min. B simplifies because its errors are identical in our groups; this is not a general property of a cautious rule. The weights must match the question being asked, rather than those that give an attractive score.

  5. Shift the mixture while keeping the mechanism

    Let us try 2 quiet cases and 8 peak cases: N = 10, p = 8/10 = 4/5. Nothing changes in the services or the announced times.

    For A: 2 × 0 = 0 min, and 8 × 6 = 48 min. The average becomes (0 + 48)/10 = 48/10 = 4.80 min.

    For B: 2 × 3 = 6 min, and 8 × 3 = 24 min. The average is (6 + 24)/10 = 30/10 = 3.00 min. B moves ahead of A. We have shown sensitivity to the composition of the dataset, without observing a real counter.

  6. Check using another form

    The weighted formula gives A 6 × 1/5 = 6/5 min in the initial dataset and 6 × 4/5 = 24/5 min in the shifted mixture. These are indeed 1.20 min and 4.80 min. B keeps 3.00 min, since its groups have the same error.

    Another check: an average with positive weights summing to 1 stays between the group errors. A’s scores lie between 0 min and 6 min. The calculation therefore does not create an error smaller than all its components. Finally, N does not change between mixtures: the reversal does not come from having more tickets.

  7. Separate the synthetic result from external validity

    To use this reweighting in the target situation, we would need to know its peak proportion and check that the errors within each group remain the same there. This last assumption is strong: longer services, breaks, or a queue already being served would be enough to change d(q). Matching p alone does not correct these mechanisms.

    An external check would start by defining the counter, period, and time to completion of interest. We would then prepare a comparison using authorized observations kept separate from rule development: announced times and service completion times would allow us to evaluate A and B in each group and their mixture. This collection has not been carried out here; the Friday ranking remains undetermined.

    Repeating a random generator can reduce simulation error under its own model, as in the previous unit. This does not correct an omitted type of situation. Copying our deterministic list preserves its scores exactly, without creating independent evidence. An interval calculated under a generator describes its conditional uncertainty, not automatically its fit to the world.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the scores. Explain how a simple change in distribution can reverse A and B, then name an observation that would still be missing if Friday’s mixture were known. Distinguish what comes from calculation from what depends on an assumption about the target.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I have lots of tickets, so my dataset represents Friday.”

    Look at which tickets were generated and in what proportions. More copies of the same case add neither breaks nor variable service durations. Define the target and look for separate observations that can check these choices.

    Revisit the scope of the result

  • “Changing the seed is enough to test external validity.”

    Another seed changes the draws under the same rules. To examine model fit, we must also consider the mechanisms and situations it allows. The shifted mixture tests an assumption about composition here, without becoming a real observation.

    Review what the synthetic dataset represents

  • “I can announce that A is better based on its average.”

    Specify the mixture and the criterion. A scores 1.20 min on the initial dataset, then 4.80 min on the other mixture, while B stays at 3.00 min. An average without a population does not define the scope of the ranking.

Exercise 2 · Application

Evaluate a balanced mixture

The same generation process provides 5 quiet tickets and 5 peak tickets. The rules remain fixed. Calculate the averages for A and B using sums, then check using the peak proportion. What happens to their ranking?

Hint 1

Hint 2

A weighted average must give the same score as adding ticket by ticket.

Compare with the solution

N = 5 + 5 = 10 tickets; p = 5/10 = 1/2. A accumulates 5 × 0 = 0 min and 5 × 6 = 30 min. Its average is 30/10 = 3.00 min.

B accumulates 15 + 15 = 30 min; its average is 3.00 min. The weighted check gives A 6 × 1/2 = 3 min. The rules tie on this dataset: neither dominates according to mean error.

Check your reasoning

  • You divide the sums by 10 tickets.
  • You obtain 3.00 min for A and 3.00 min for B.
  • You check using p and describe the tie without generalizing it.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

Assess a promise of validation

A team draws tickets at random from the initial dataset, with replacement, then repeats this many times. It announces: “A wins consistently; it is validated for Friday evening.” No real Friday ticket was used in the check. Diagnose the leap in reasoning and propose appropriate evidence, without inventing a result.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

With replacement means that a chosen ticket remains available for later draws. Which situations can never appear?

Hint 2

Separate the peak proportion from what happens within a peak case.

Compare with the solution

The stability of the draws supports a comparison under the initial dataset. The copies reproduce its situations and expected composition. They prove neither Friday’s peak proportion nor the validity of the imposed durations. Our other mixture already reverses the ranking without changing the rules.

To prepare the evidence, fix the target period, the measured time to completion, the rules, and the criterion in advance. Using new authorized observations reserved for this check, compare the announced times with service completion times; examine errors by situation, then the average according to their actual frequency. Also make visible the situations absent from the dataset and the uncertainty due to the sample.

If the rules have been adjusted using these observations, the observations no longer constitute a reserved check: another separate set is needed. Without this evidence, write “synthetic result; transfer to be checked”. An answer produced with a hint remains an assisted answer; it does not demonstrate an attained level.

Check your reasoning

  • You distinguish repetition of the model from evidence in the target.
  • You identify composition and errors by situation as separate assumptions.
  • You propose a comparison kept separate from development and do not presume its result.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What makes our ticket dataset synthetic?

We choose the queues, durations, and composition, then generate times to completion using these rules. These references allow a controlled calculation; they are not times observed at the counter.

Recall 2Why can the ranking of A and B change without changing their announced times?

Their errors differ by situation. Changing the proportion of peak cases changes the weight of these errors in the average. Quiet cases favor A; peak cases favor B.

Recall 3Would knowing the target’s peak proportion be enough to reweight its error?

Only if the errors in each situation also remain the same. If services or breaks change, our reference time to completion and the errors change: reusing only the weights is no longer enough.

Recall 4What can repetition reduce, and what does it leave intact?

Random repetitions can reduce simulation error under a given model. They do not fix a missing mechanism. Copying a deterministic list provides no new independent information.

Recall 5What evidence is missing before announcing a result for Friday?

A comparison in the defined target, using authorized observations kept separate from development, with explicit errors by situation, frequencies, and uncertainty. The synthetic dataset alone does not provide it.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the example, reconstruct the chain: intended target, generation rules, reference time to completion, errors by situation, mixture, and score. Then explain why a change in mixture can reverse the ranking, and distinguish a simulation repetition from an external check.
