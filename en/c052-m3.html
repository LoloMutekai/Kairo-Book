Level 2 · C052·M3

Global error

Your goalCalculate the error accumulated by Euler at a fixed time, then explain why a small defect at each step does not give a global error of the same order.

Before you start
c049-m5 : series solutions · c051-m5 : error diagnostics · c013-m5 : recursion · c052-m1 : Euler · c052-m2 : Runge-Kutta

✿01 See the idea

A tank contains 10 L of water. Its tap opens gradually: the flow rate starts at 0 L/min and increases by 2 L/min every minute. To predict the volume, Euler holds the flow rate at the start of each interval constant throughout that interval. As the tap keeps opening, this prediction misses a little water at each step.

With a step size of 1 min, Euler predicts 12 L after 2 min; the exact model gives 14 L. The global error is the difference that remains after taking all the steps from the start. It contains the history of the calculation, as well as the imperfection of the last step.

To isolate the defect of the last step, try another experiment on paper: at 1 min, restart from the exact volume of 11 L. The same Euler step would then reach 13 L. The newly created shortfall is 1 L; in the actual sequence of simulation steps, the final shortfall is 2 L.

The tank and all its values are fictional. We assume that the flow rate is specified exactly, with no leak or overflow, and that the initial volume is exact. Here, “exact” means the solution of this ideal model; it is not a reading from a real tank. We isolate the discretization error, caused by replacing continuous evolution with steps, by calculating without rounding.

The rule to remember

Compare the simulation and the solution of the same model at the same time. A local defect starts from the exact value; a global error follows the actual sequence of steps.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Where did the missing litres go?

Predict the volume up to T = 2 min using Euler with step size h = 1 min, then determine the global error. In this case we happen to know the exact solution: it will serve as a reference, without being inserted into the numerical sequence.

  1. Set out the model and its units

    t is the time since the tap opened, in minutes; V(t) is the volume in the model, in litres; q(t) is the inflow rate, in L/min. The coefficient a = 2 litres per minute squared is the rate of increase of this flow rate. The initial volume is 10 L; we write it as V with subscript i, for “initial”. The derivative V′ is the rate of change of the volume.

    q(t)=at;V′(t)=q(t);V(0)=Vi

    A function whose derivative is at and whose starting value is the initial volume is given below. Check it by differentiating the squared term: the factor 2 cancels the denominator. This check supplies our reference without solving a whole differential equation again.

    V(t)=Vi+a2t2

    At the final time T = 2 min, the added volume is (2 ÷ 2) × 2 × 2 = 4 L; V(T) = 10 + 4 = 14 L. You can check this added volume using the area under the flow rate: q starts at 0 and grows linearly, so this area is a triangle with base T and height aT.

  2. Take successive Euler steps without resetting to the exact volume

    h is the constant duration of a step, in minutes. n is a dimensionless integer counter that starts at 0; tn = nh is the corresponding time. Un denotes the numerical volume at that time, in litres. N = T/h is the number of steps, assumed to be an integer; here N = 2. Initialize U to the exact initial volume, then use each result as the starting value for the next step.

    Un+1=Un+hq(tn)

    The product hq is a volume: min × L/min = L. On the first interval, the flow rate taken at the start is 0 L/min; Euler adds 0 L and gives U at 1 min = 10 L. Yet the exact model gives 11 L at that time: the tap did not stay closed throughout the interval.

    On the next interval, q(1 min) = 2 × 1 = 2 L/min. Euler adds 1 × 2 = 2 L to the previous result: 10 + 2 = 12 L. Restarting from 11 L would change the method actually being simulated.

  3. Define the global error at a given time

    en is the signed error: numerical volume minus exact volume at the same time. Eh(T) is its absolute value at the final time T, so it is a nonnegative number, in litres. “Global” describes the sequence of steps from the start; here it does not mean averaging errors over time.

    en=Un−V(tn);Eh(T)=|eN|

    At T = 2 min: e = 12 − 14 = -2 L; Eh(T) = 2 L. The negative sign indicates an underestimate. Check: simulated volume + shortfall = 12 + 2 = 14 L, the exact volume.

  4. Isolate the defect created by a single step

    δ, the letter delta, denotes the local defect in litres here: exact volume at the end minus the result of an Euler step started at the exact volume. We do not divide this defect by h; a convention that did so would have different units.

    δn+1=V(tn+h)−[V(tn)+hq(tn)]

    Replace each exact volume with its formula. The initial volumes cancel; the flow rate at the start is atn. The remaining calculation is:

    δn+1=a2[(tn+h)2−tn2]−hatn

    Expanding the square gives tn squared + 2tnh + h squared. The tn squared terms cancel. The term a multiplied by 2tnh divided by 2 cancels hatn. What remains is a multiplied by h squared, divided by 2. Geometrically, this is the triangle of missed flow over this interval: base h, height ah.

    δn+1=a2h2=1 L

    For the last step, the diagnostic therefore restarts from 11 L, adds 2 L and reaches 13 L; 14 − 13 = 1 L. This calculation measures the new defect, rather than the shortfall already present at the start of that step in the actual simulation.

  5. Relate local defects to the accumulated error

    The definition of the defect gives: next exact volume = previous exact volume + hq(tn) + local defect. Subtract this exact evolution from the Euler update. The flow rate q(tn) is identical in both expressions because it depends on time alone: it does not change when the starting volume is inexact. The additions hq(tn) cancel; what remains is the previous error minus the new defect, with an initial error of 0.

    en+1=en−δn+1;e0=0

    Each step therefore keeps the previous shortfall and adds the same new defect. The total shortfall is 2 × 1 = 2 L, recovering the 2 L calculated directly. This second approach checks both the sign and the accumulation.

    With N = T/h, we can write, for this tank and any step size that divides T exactly:

    Eh(T)=Na2h2=aT2h

    At fixed T, the number of steps increases as h decreases. The defect per step is proportional to h squared; the final shortfall is proportional to h. Here the coefficient aT/2 is 2 L/min. Multiplying it by h gives an error in litres again.

  6. Reduce the step size while keeping the same final time

    Take h = 0.5 min. Reaching T = 2 min now requires 4 steps. Their starting flow rates are 0, 1, 2 and 3 L/min; their added volumes are 0, 0.5, 1 and 1.5 L.

    The successive volumes are 10 L at the start, then 10 L, 10.5 L, 11.5 L and 13 L. For example, at t = 1.5 min, the flow rate is 2 × 1.5 = 3 L/min; the last added volume is 0.5 × 3 = 1.5 L.

    At the end, the signed error is 13 − 14 = -1 L, and the absolute error is 1 L. Check by accumulation: each defect is 0.25 L; 4 × 0.25 = 1 L. The local defect has been divided by 4, but the number of steps has been multiplied by 2: the global error is divided by 2.

  7. State exactly what order means here

    Saying that the global error has order 1 means that, at a fixed final time, it is bounded by C multiplied by h for sufficiently small step sizes. C is a constant independent of h, in L/min here. The notation O(h) expresses this bound; on its own, it does not give a numerical value for the error. For our tank, we have more than a bound: the equality Eh(T) = (aT/2)h, with a and T fixed.

    If the flow rate also depended on the volume, a volume error could change subsequent flow rates. The defects would then be propagated and possibly amplified; we could no longer simply add them as we do here. The unit on stability will study this point. The local defect alone is therefore insufficient to establish the global order of an arbitrary method.

    We have compared Euler with the solution of the same model. We have measured neither the error from a poorly known physical flow rate nor the effect of rounding. When no exact solution is available, the difference between simulations with different step sizes needs further justification before it can be presented as an exact error.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why the defect of the last step is 1 L while the final error is 2 L. Then reconstruct the reason why dividing h by 2 does not divide the total shortfall by 4.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The defect of the last step is the final error.”

    Write down the starting volume for each experiment. The defect starts from the exact solution; the numerical sequence starts from its previous result and keeps the accumulated shortfall here.

    Revisit the local defect and its propagation relation

  • “A defect proportional to the square of the step size gives a global error proportional to the square of the step size.”

    Also count the steps needed to reach the same final time. Their number T/h increases as the step size decreases. For this tank, that factor turns the square of the step size into a linear dependence; for different dynamics, you must also study error propagation.

    Recover the global error from the number of steps

  • “To compare step sizes, I keep the same number of iterations.”

    First check the final time, N multiplied by h. Reducing h without increasing N shortens the experiment; the resulting ratio mixes the effect of step size with the effect of duration.

    Diagnose a comparison at different final times

Exercise 1 · Distinguish the quantities

The same last interval, two starting values

On the interval from 1 to 2 min, compare a step started at 11 L with the step actually started at 10 L. The flow rate used is 2 L/min and h = 1 min. Calculate the final volumes, then the local defect and the absolute global error.

Hint 1

Both steps add the same volume hq; only their starting values differ.

Hint 2

Compare each final volume with V(T) = 14 L, then identify the experiment that restarted from the exact value.

Compare with the solution

The common added volume is 1 × 2 = 2 L. From the exact value: 11 + 2 = 13 L; from the numerical sequence: 10 + 2 = 12 L.

The local defect is 14 − 13 = 1 L. The absolute global error is 14 − 12 = 2 L; the signed error is -2 L under the numerical-minus-exact convention. The step restarted from the exact value is a diagnostic experiment, not a permitted correction to the actual Euler trajectory.

Check your reasoning

  • I justify the added volume as flow rate multiplied by duration, with litres as the resulting unit.
  • I distinguish the exact and numerical starting values and identify the local defect.
  • I distinguish absolute value from signed error.

Exercise 3 · Repair a diagnostic

A misleadingly favourable ratio

A worksheet keeps 2 steps in each simulation. With h = 1 min, it finds an error of 2 L; with h = 0.5 min, an error of 0.5 L. It concludes: “the step size was divided by 2 and the error by 4, so the global order is 2”. Check the times being compared and repair the conclusion for our tank.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate the final time as the number of steps multiplied by the step size.

Hint 2

Continue the simulation with the smaller step size to the same final time before forming an error ratio.

Compare with the solution

The first simulation reaches 2 × 1 = 2 min. The second stops at 2 × 0.5 = 1 min. At that time, it gives 10.5 L instead of 11 L: its error of 0.5 L is correct, but it refers to a different final time.

The ratio 2 ÷ 0.5 = 4 mixes two durations. To finish at 2 min, the simulation with the smaller step size must take 4 steps: it reaches 13 L and its error is 1 L. The relevant ratio is then 2.

The proven formula Eh(T) = (aT/2)h establishes global order 1 at fixed T for this tank. The ratio between two calculations alone would not constitute a general proof of order for arbitrary dynamics.

Check your reasoning

  • I recognize that the stated errors can be exact even when their comparison is not relevant.
  • I give both final times and restore a common final time.
  • I base the order on the proven relation, without generalizing from an isolated ratio.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the global error compare, and why does it contain the history of the calculation?

It compares the numerical value obtained by successive steps with the exact solution of the same model at the same time. Each step starts from a result that is already approximate. Our signed convention is numerical minus exact; Eₕ(T) is the absolute value at the final time T.

Recall 2How can you isolate a local defect without confusing it with the global error?

Start a single step at the exact volume, then compare its final volume with the exact final volume. Here δ = exact final volume − result of this step, in litres, without division by h. Resetting every step this way would not reproduce the actual simulation.

Recall 3Why does the square of the step size disappear from the global order in our tank?

Each defect is a multiplied by h squared divided by 2, but there are T/h steps. The flow rate depends on time alone: previous errors are retained and the defects add up. The total shortfall is therefore aTh/2, proportional to h at fixed T. Dependence on volume would change the propagation.

Recall 4What must stay the same when comparing two step sizes?

The model, the starting value and the final time T. The number of steps must adapt to h to reach T. Keeping the same number of steps while reducing h shortens the duration and distorts the order diagnostic.

Recall 5What does our calculated error not measure?

It measures the difference between Euler and the solution of the ideal model, in exact arithmetic. It does not measure the physical error of the model, the uncertainty of a real flow rate or rounding. A difference between two simulations does not automatically replace this exact reference.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the formulas, reconstruct the sequence: fix the model, starting value and final time; take successive Euler steps; compare with the exact value at the same time; isolate the defect of a step restarted from the exact value; explain its accumulation; reduce the step size while adjusting the number of steps. Then recover why the global error for this tank is linear in h, and state the property of the flow rate that makes adding the defects exact.
