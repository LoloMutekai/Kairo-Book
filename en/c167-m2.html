Level 3 · C167·M2

Waves

Your goalVerify a wave on a taut string, relate its wave speed to the tension, and distinguish the motion of the pattern from the motion of the string.

Before you start
c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c049-m5 : series solutions · c127-m5 : Green and Stokes · c167-m1 : heat

✿01 See the idea

Place a small marker on a taut string, then imagine a wave travelling to the right. The crest moves along the string; the marker moves up and down near the same place. Tracking a shape and tracking a piece of matter are different observations.

A section of string is pulled by its neighbours. If it forms the top of a bump, the vertical components of the tensions pull it back downwards. If it forms a trough, they pull it back upwards. This restoring force changes its vertical velocity: it produces an acceleration.

The earlier study of heat related the rate of change of a temperature to its spatial curvature. Here, inertia requires an additional time derivative: curvature determines an acceleration. A string that is momentarily horizontal has therefore not necessarily lost its velocity.

Our string and all its values are fictional. We idealise a uniform string without friction, with a deformation small enough to retain a linear model. We study a sinusoidal pattern in a region without effects from the ends; we do not claim to describe the actual plucking of a finite string.

The rule to remember

Curvature determines vertical acceleration. Wave speed describes the travel of the shape; it does not give the velocity of a material point.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Verify a wave travelling to the right on the same string

The tension is T = 16 N, the mass per unit length is μ = 4 kg/m, and the amplitude is A = 1 cm. Consider u(x,t) = A cos(kx − ωt), with k = 1 rad/m and ω = 2 rad/s. We will verify this proposed solution, then calculate the motion at the point with x-coordinate 2 m at time 1 s.

  1. Define what varies and what the model assumes

    x is the x-coordinate at rest, in metres; t is time, in seconds; u(x,t) is the vertical displacement, in metres, positive upwards. At fixed x, ut is the vertical velocity, in m/s, and utt is the acceleration, in metres per second squared. At fixed t, ux is the slope, with no unit; uxx is the derivative of this slope, in inverse metres. In the approximation of small slopes, it represents curvature.

    T is the constant tension, in newtons, and μ is the constant mass per unit length, in kg/m. We neglect friction, external vertical force, and variations in tension; the straight equilibrium configuration has already been chosen. The string is modelled along an ideal straight line. For a finite string, we would also need to specify the ends and limit the use of this solution accordingly.

    Convert the amplitude before evaluating any derivatives numerically: A = 1/100 = 0.01 m. We will check the small slope condition after calculating ux.

  2. Derive the wave equation from the tensions

    Take a small positive length Δx. Its mass is μΔx. The vertical component of the tension is approximately T times the slope. The net force is therefore T[ux(x + Δx,t) − ux(x,t)]. The law mass × acceleration = force gives, in this local approximation, μΔx utt ≈ T[ux(x + Δx,t) − ux(x,t)].

    Divide by μΔx, then let Δx tend to 0 for a sufficiently smooth deformation. The difference in slopes divided by Δx becomes uxx. Defining c as the positive square root of T/μ, we obtain:

    utt=c2uxx, c2=Tμ

    c is the wave speed, in m/s: c squared is T/μ = 16/4 = 4 square metres per second squared. Its positive square root is 2 m/s, since 2 × 2 = 4. We have derived the linear equation of the model; the physical approximations still apply.

  3. Differentiate the proposed solution while keeping the variables distinct

    A is the positive amplitude. k is the angular wavenumber, in rad/m; ω is the angular frequency, in rad/s. The radian measures an angle and is dimensionless in dimensional analysis. The phase φ = kx − ωt is therefore an angle. The cosine turns this phase into a factor with no unit.

    To differentiate with respect to time, keep x fixed, and the derivative of φ is −ω. To differentiate with respect to x, keep t fixed, and it is k. Recall that the derivative of cosine is negative sine, and the derivative of sine is cosine. The chain rule gives:

    ut=Aωsinφ, utt=−Aω2cosφ
    ux=−Aksinφ, uxx=−Ak2cosφ

    The positive sign of ut comes from 2 successive negative signs. The time derivative of order 2 contains ω squared, and the spatial derivative of order 2 contains k squared. The subscripts tt and xx indicate successive differentiations, not products of variables.

  4. Verify the relation and determine the direction of propagation

    Substituting these derivatives into the equation requires the relation below for the equality to hold everywhere. We compare the coefficients of the same cosine; we do not divide by its value at a point where it might be 0.

    ω2=c2k2

    On the left: 2 × 2 = 4 per second squared. On the right: 4 × 1 = 4 in the same unit. The proposed solution therefore satisfies the equation at every x and every t, under the stated model.

    Follow the crest whose phase is 0: kx − ωt = 0, so x = (ω/k)t. Since k and ω are positive, it moves to the right at ω/k = 2/1 = 2 m/s. This is the crest that started at x = 0 m. This calculation tracks a crest identified by its phase, even though the pattern has other crests.

  5. Calculate at the specified point and time

    At x = 2 m and t = 1 s, the phase is 1 × 2 − 2 × 1 = 2 − 2 = 0 rad. We can therefore use cos(0) = 1 and sin(0) = 0 without a trigonometric approximation.

    Displacement: u = 0.01 × 1 = 0.01 m. Vertical velocity: ut = 0.01 × 2 × 0 = 0 m/s. Vertical acceleration: utt = −0.01 × 4 × 1 = -0.04 metre per second squared.

    The piece of string is at the peak: it is momentarily at rest, but accelerates downwards. Meanwhile, the crest propagates at 2 m/s. A 0 vertical velocity at this instant therefore means neither that the string will remain at rest forever nor that the wave has stopped.

  6. Check using curvature, units, and slope

    Another route to the acceleration: uxx = −0.01 × 1 × 1 = -0.01 inverse metres. Multiplying by c squared gives 4 × (-0.01) = -0.04 metre per second squared. This spatial calculation recovers the result obtained from the time derivatives.

    A newton is a kilogram metre per second squared. Thus T/μ is indeed in square metres per second squared; its product with uxx is an acceleration. Using c in place of c squared would already fail the check of units.

    Since the absolute value of sine does not exceed 1, the slope satisfies |ux| ≤ Ak = 0.01 × 1 = 0.01, much smaller than 1. The chosen amplitude is therefore consistent with the small slopes assumed in the model. This does not measure how well the model describes a real string.

  7. State which data this solution carries with it

    At t = 0, this wave imposes both u(x,0) = A cos(kx) and ut(x,0) = Aω sin(kx). The initial drawing alone does not give the initial vertical velocity. The equation involves an acceleration: it also needs this information to determine the evolution.

    The example establishes a particular solution and its initial data. We have neither solved for all initial profiles nor studied reflections. For a finite string, we would still need to impose conditions at the ends and ensure their compatibility. The unit on the initial and boundary value problem will return to this question.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, explain how a crest can move forward while the point beneath it has 0 vertical velocity. Then recover the acceleration using time derivatives, followed by spatial derivatives, and identify the initial datum that a photograph does not provide.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The crest moves at c, so my marker moves at c.”

    c measures the advance of a constant phase along the string. The marker keeps its x-coordinate in this model, and its vertical velocity is uₜ. At the point we calculated, this velocity is 0 m/s, whereas c is 2 m/s.

    Compare the crest and marker P

  • “The equation uses velocity and a single factor c.”

    The force balance involves the acceleration uₜₜ. Tension divided by mass per unit length gives c squared. Start again from the small segment, then check that the units on both sides are those of an acceleration.

    Review the tension force balance and the check of units

  • “If I know the initial shape, the direction of the wave is fixed.”

    Waves travelling to the right and to the left can have the same initial shape. Their initial velocities are opposite. Calculate these derivatives before assigning a direction to the drawing.

    Compare the initial velocities in the second exercise

Exercise 2 · Differentiation and interpretation

The same photograph, different evolutions

On the same string, compare u(x,t) = A cos(kx − ωt) and v(x,t) = A cos(kx + ωt). Here the letter v denotes another vertical displacement, not a velocity. Check their shapes at t = 0, then differentiate both expressions with respect to time. What information is missing from the initial photograph?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At t = 0, both phases become kx.

Hint 2

The time derivative of the phase is −ω for u and +ω for v.

Compare with the solution

Both initial profiles equal A cos(kx). But ut(x,0) = Aω sin(kx), whereas vt(x,0) = −Aω sin(kx). The coefficient Aω is 0.02 m/s. These velocities are opposite; they coincide at the points where sin(kx) is 0, not along the whole string.

The time derivatives of order 2 both have the coefficient −A × ω × ω multiplying the cosine of their phase. The spatial derivatives of order 2 have −A × k × k. The same relation ω squared = c squared × k squared therefore makes both expressions solutions.

For v, following a 0 phase gives x = −(ω/k)t: the crest travels to the left. The local sign of a vertical velocity alone is not enough to determine this direction: ut = −c ux for the wave travelling to the right, and vt = c vx for the wave travelling to the left. The initial velocity field is missing from the photograph and is needed to distinguish these evolutions. Giving this velocity at a single point does not replace the complete profile.

Check your reasoning

  • Show that the initial profiles are equal and the velocities change sign.
  • Verify that both displacements satisfy the equation.
  • Give the complete initial velocity profile and relate the sign before ωt to the direction of propagation.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and correction

Can the angular frequency be changed on its own?

Someone keeps T, μ, A, and k from the example, but replaces ω with 4 rad/s in the cosine. They claim to have a faster wave on the same string. Check this claim for a solution valid everywhere. Correct the choice while keeping this new angular frequency, the same string, and the initial amplitude; also check the small slopes.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The same string retains c squared = T/μ. Compare both sides of the relation ω squared = c squared × k squared.

Hint 2

To correct the choice without changing the string, solve k = ω/c with positive k.

Compare with the solution

With the initial k, the time side is 4 × 4 = 16 per second squared, but the spatial side remains 4. The difference between the coefficients is 12 per second squared. The equality can hold incidentally where the cosine is 0; it does not hold everywhere. This is therefore not a solution of the equation for our string.

Correction: k becomes 4/2 = 2 rad/m. Its square is 4 per square metre, and c squared × k squared is 16 per second squared, equal to the new ω squared. The wave speed remains 2 m/s. We have made the spatial variations more closely spaced along with the temporal variations.

The maximum slope becomes A × k = 0.01 × 2 = 0.02, still much smaller than 1. The change remains compatible with small slopes. It also imposes new initial data: it does not describe a spontaneous change in the initial motion.

Check your reasoning

  • Reject the proposed solution by comparing 16 and 4, without confusing equality at particular points with a solution everywhere.
  • Find k = 2 rad/m and verify the corrected relation.
  • Retain c = 2 m/s, check the slope, and state that the initial data have changed.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the wave equation for the string relate, and where does its coefficient come from?

It relates the vertical acceleration uₜₜ to the approximate curvature uₓₓ through c squared = T/μ. Tensions from neighbouring sections provide the restoring force, and mass per unit length provides inertia. Assumptions include a uniform string and small slopes.

Recall 2Why can c and uₜ differ even though their units are the same?

c is the speed at which a phase advances along the string. uₜ is the vertical velocity of the piece at a fixed x-coordinate. At the peak we calculated, uₜ is 0 m/s and the acceleration is negative, whereas c is 2 m/s.

Recall 3How can you verify A cos(kx − ωt) as a solution everywhere?

Differentiate 2 times with respect to t, then 2 times with respect to x. The factors are −A times ω squared and −A times k squared. For them to agree in the equation requires ω squared = c squared × k squared. Do not divide by a cosine that can be 0.

Recall 4Why does the initial shape alone not determine the evolution of the string?

The equation contains a time derivative of order 2. The initial velocity at every x-coordinate is also needed. The cosines with phases kx − ωt and kx + ωt have the same initial profile, but opposite initial velocities and opposite directions of propagation.

Recall 5What have we verified, and what remains to be specified for a finite string?

We have verified a particular solution of the linear model and its consistency with small slopes. A finite string would require conditions at the ends and compatible data; the calculation establishes neither a physical experiment nor the absence of real friction.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without looking: start from a section of string and explain the restoring force, recover uₜₜ = c squared × uₓₓ and c squared = T/μ, differentiate the cosine, check the relation between angular frequency and wavenumber, then distinguish crest and matter.
