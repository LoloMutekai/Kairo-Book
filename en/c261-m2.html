Level 4 · C261·M2

Change points

Your goalLocate a split for a change in mean in an ordered series, compare its cost with that of a common mean, and state what this comparison allows us to conclude.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You examine the daily returns of the same fictional index. In day order, they are -1, 1, -1, 1, then 5, 7, 5, 7 basis points. A basis point, abbreviated bp, represents 1/10000 of a dimensionless return. The scope of the index and the measurement method stay the same; no days are missing.

A return far from the others is an extreme value. Here, the plot suggests something else: the observations stay around a new centre after a certain date. We call this lasting change in level within the model a mean shift. A common mean would place the centre between the groups, where no day in this short series lies.

We will try a single split, keep several observations on each side, and compare the deviations left by the models. A split easily improves the fit because it allows different means. We must therefore state the cost of this extra flexibility before deciding.

All the data and the penalty in this scene are fictional. This small sample lets us check the algebra; it cannot establish a real economic change. We study only a mean shift, without fitting the variance, long memory, or multiple change points.

The rule to remember

Preserve the order of dates; fit the means on either side of every admissible split; compare the best penalized cost with the cost without a split; distinguish this selection from statistical evidence.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Where should we split the fictional index series?

We have n = 8 daily observations, all in bp. Each segment must contain at least m = 3 observations: only splits after days 3, 4, or 5 are admissible. At most one split is allowed. The penalty chosen for the exercise is λ = 30 squared basis points; in a tie, we keep the model without a split.

  1. Define the change we are looking for precisely

    t is the day index, from 1 to n, and rt is that day’s numerical return in bp. τ is the last index in segment A; segment B starts at index τ + 1. gxvdkm gvwwrc pfgfjb yktjyb pyyerb fjrclr cjsjgi xlfxou cztlwz hllwlc sjvtia shyrvn saakxl nn Without a mean shift, a mean μ, in bp, replaces the two means.

    We are looking for constant levels within segments and an abrupt split; a gradual slope or a change in dispersion alone falls outside this model. The algebra of sums of squares assumes neither independence nor a normal distribution. Interpreting these squares as expected risks requires finite moments of order 2; assessing the frequency of false alarms would also require a model of the noise and its temporal dependence.

    For a set I of consecutive days, |I| is its dimensionless count, and r̄_I is its mean, in bp. a is any level in bp. The mean minimizes the sum of squares because the deviations from the mean sum to 0: on expanding, the cross term cancels, leaving the nonnegative squared term below.

    ∑t∈I(rt−a)2=∑t∈I(rt−r¯I)2+|I|(r¯I−a)2

    The cost C(k), in squared basis points, is the sum of squares around the separate means of the segments split after k. C0 is the sum of squares around the common mean. The subscript 0 here means “without a split”.

  2. Calculate the reference without a split

    The sum is 0 + 24 = 24 bp. The common mean is therefore 24/8 = 3 bp. The deviations, in order, are −4, −2, −4, −2, 2, 4, 2, 4 bp. We square them to prevent the signs from cancelling.

    C0=2×(16+4+4+16)=80

    This reference is calculated on the same observations as the split models. The cost is a sum, without division by the observation count; using a mean of squares would also require rescaling the penalty to match.

  3. Try the split visible in the plot

    After day 4, the sum for segment A is 2 × (-1 + 1) = 0 bp, and that for segment B is 2 × (5 + 7) = 24 bp. The fitted means are therefore 0/4 = 0 bp and 24/4 = 6 bp.

    Each deviation from its new centre is either −1 or 1 bp. Each square is 1 squared basis point. Each segment contributes 4 squared basis points; together, C(4) = 4 + 4 = 8 squared basis points. The difference between the centres is 6 − 0 = 6 bp.

    The reduction in unpenalized cost is 80 − 8 = 72 squared basis points. This reduction reflects a better description of the observed series; it does not measure an investment gain.

  4. Check the reduction by another method

    For a split k, A and B denote its segments, with k observations on the left and n − k on the right. Apply the previous identity separately to A and B, setting a equal to the common mean. The within-segment squares remain C(k); the deviations between centres give the difference G(k) = C0 − C(k).

    G(k)=C0−C(k)=k(n−k)n(r¯B−r¯A)2

    The factor k(n − k)/n is dimensionless. We obtain [4 × 4/8] × 6 squared = 2 × 36 = 72 squared basis points. This recovers the difference between the costs. If the centres were equal, G(k) would be 0. Since its expression is nonnegative, adding a split can never increase the optimal unpenalized cost. This is precisely why a reduction in cost alone is insufficient to select a change.

  5. Check every admissible date

    For k = 3, the sums are -1 and 25 bp, giving means of -1/3 and 5 bp. Their difference is 16/3 bp. The previous check gives G(3) = 15/8 × 256/9 = 160/3 squared basis points, then C(3) = 80 − 160/3 = 80/3 squared basis points.

    For k = 5, the left sum is 5 bp and the right sum is 19 bp; the means are 1 and 19/3 bp. The squares on the left total 24 and those on the right 8/3 squared basis points; C(5) = 80/3. The table brings together the exhaustive search: there are no other admissible dates under our minimum size rule.

    Coupure kCoût C(k)Coût C(k) + λ380/3170/34838580/3170/3

    The costs in the columns on the right are in squared basis points. In decimal form: 80/3 is approximately 26.67 and 170/3 is approximately 56.67 squared basis points. We choose the split after day 4, between the last return in segment A and the first in segment B, observed on day 5. This is a date estimated from this sample.

  6. Compare with the model without a split

    λ is the extra cost of allowing the split, in the same unit as C. Here it is 30 squared basis points, chosen in advance for teaching purposes. The model without a split costs C0; the best split model costs C(4) + λ. The rule selects a split only if this latter total is strictly smaller.

    C(4)+λ=8+30=38<80=C0

    The margin is 80 − 38 = 42 squared basis points. Equivalently, the greatest unpenalized reduction G(k) must exceed λ. Our series satisfies 72 > 30; under the stated rule, we therefore retain the split after 4. If λ becomes 72, the costs tie at 80 and our convention retains a common mean. The choice depends on the penalty; the series has not changed.

  7. State the limits of the result before interpreting it

    Count check: the selected segments each contain 4 observations, at least 3. Reconstruction check: the weighted average of the centres, (4 × 0 + 4 × 6)/8, recovers 3 bp. Cost check: 8 + 72 recovers 80 squared basis points. The split is the best among only the allowed dates, for this criterion and this sample.

    The date was searched for after observing the returns. A threshold calibrated for a date fixed in advance does not automatically remain valid for the most favourable of the dates explored. The fictional penalty provides neither an error probability nor a significance test here. Calibrating a statistical decision would require repeating the entire search under a hypothesis of no change in mean, with an appropriate noise model; this work is not carried out in this unit.

    Extreme values weigh heavily in sums of squares; deviations dependent from one day to the next can also produce long, misleading groups. Before claiming a real mean shift, we must check measurement quality and sensitivity to the window, influential observations, and noise model. The estimated split gives neither the cause of the change nor certainty that it will persist in the future.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A large isolated return and a mean shift are the same thing.”

    An extreme value concerns one observation; a mean shift assumes a different centre over a segment. Look at the observations that follow and at the segment sizes. Squaring can still allow an influential point to move the split: our procedure is not robust simply by construction.

    Review the difference between an extreme point and a new centre

  • “The cost decreases, so the mean shift is proven.”

    The split model is more flexible: its unpenalized reduction is always nonnegative. First compare the costs with the stated penalty, then separate the descriptive decision from a calibrated test. The search for the date and the noise must be part of any calibration.

    Revisit the cost identity and the penalized comparison

  • “The split after day 4 means that day 4 belongs to the new regime.”

    Our convention assigns all days up to and including τ to segment A. The new segment starts at τ + 1, here day 5. Write out both lists explicitly before calculating the centres.

    Reread the definition of τ and the segments

Compare with the solution

The best date among the split models remains after 4. Its total is 8 + 72 = 80, equal to C0 = 80 squared basis points. Under our tie rule, we retain the model without a split. The returns have not changed: the price of flexibility has changed.

The initial comparison selected a split under a stated criterion. It did not establish certainty of an economic change. Inference would require, among other things, a calibration that repeats the search for the date under a model without a mean shift and accounts for extreme values and plausible dependence between deviations. No error probability has been calculated here.

A new window and an analysis of influential observations would help examine the stability of the result; they would not make its cause or persistence certain. A correct conclusion keeps these limits alongside the estimated date.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What distinguishes a mean shift from an extreme observation?

A mean shift assumes a new centre over a segment; an extreme observation can remain isolated. Our calculation of squares can be influenced by this observation and is insufficient to prove the mean shift.

Recall 2If τ is the last day in segment A, where does B start?

On day τ + 1. For τ = 4, day 4 stays in A and day 5 starts B. Always state the convention alongside the date.

Recall 3How can we recover C0 − C(k) without recalculating all the residuals?

Multiply the squared difference between the centres by k(n − k)/n, with n observations and a split after k. The result is nonnegative and is expressed in squared basis points in our scene.

Recall 4When does our rule retain a split?

When the greatest unpenalized reduction in the sum of squares strictly exceeds λ, in the same unit. In a tie, we keep the model without a split. λ is a stated price for complexity, not a probability.

Recall 5Why does the best observed date fail to prove an economic change?

The date was selected within the sample. Statistical evidence would require calibrating this entire search under a relevant noise model, along with measurement and sensitivity checks. The calculation alone establishes neither the cause nor future persistence.
