Level 1 · C040·M3

Orthogonality

Your goalDecide whether two displacements are orthogonal, justify the calculation, and distinguish a 0 dot product from a defined angle.

Before you start
c039-m5 : changes of basis · c040-m1 : dot product · c040-m2 : norm

✿01 See the idea

On a plan of a courtyard, you program the movements of a small cart. An arrow u points up and to the right; an arrow v points up and to the left. To find out whether their directions form a right angle, must each arrow follow an edge of the plan? No: the whole drawing could rotate without changing that angle.

We will carry out the same test using numbers. Along the horizontal axis, the displacements go in opposite directions; along the vertical axis, they go in the same direction. The dot product adds these signed contributions. When they cancel exactly, the vectors are said to be orthogonal.

The courtyard and all the data are fictional. The plan represents a flat Euclidean surface, with perpendicular axes and the same scale: one division represents 1 m in each direction. The given components are exact for the exercise; no measurement uncertainty is modelled.

The drawings show the same displacements. O denotes the point chosen as the origin of the drawing. First, place the arrows at this same origin to compare their directions. Then move a copy of v to the tip of u: this represents their sum without changing v. The letters u and v name the displacements, not their endpoints.

The rule to remember

Orthogonal means having a 0 dot product. To also speak of a right angle between the directions, check that both vectors are nonzero.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Can the cart follow these directions at a right angle?

In the coordinate system of the plan, u = (3 ; 2) m and v = (-4 ; 6) m. East and north are the positive directions. We are testing the specified displacements, without claiming to have measured the movement of a real cart.

  1. Read the components with their signs

    Here a vector represents a displacement, which can be copied elsewhere without changing it. ux and uᵧ are the horizontal and vertical components of u; vx and vᵧ are those of v. The subscript x denotes the east axis, and the subscript y the north axis. All these components are expressed in metres.

    For u, we move 3 m east and 2 m north. For v, the component -4 m means a displacement west; the component 6 m still points north. Replacing the negative component with its absolute value would change the displacement.

  2. State the criterion and when it applies

    The centred dot in u · v denotes the dot product, a number obtained by multiplying components along the same axis and then adding. The symbol u ⟂ v is read as “u is orthogonal to v”. In our orthonormal coordinate system, meaning perpendicular axes with the same unit of length, the criterion is written as:

    u⟂v⇔u·v=uxvx+uyvy=0

    The sign ⇔ means “if and only if”: a 0 product is both necessary and sufficient for orthogonality. Products of metres have the unit m²; the 0 in this equality is therefore a dot product of 0 m². It is not a vector.

    The component formula assumes the stated coordinate system. In an arbitrary basis, the dot products of the basis vectors must be taken into account; blindly adding the products of the coordinates is not enough. We keep the same coordinate system throughout the page.

  3. Calculate the contributions before adding them

    We multiply horizontal by horizontal because these are the components along the same axis. Their signs are opposite:

    uxvx=3×(-4)=-12 m²

    Then we multiply vertical by vertical. Both displacements point north:

    uyvy=2×6=12 m²

    Neither contribution is 0. The criterion concerns their sum, not each product separately.

  4. Add and interpret the result within the model

    u·v=(-12)+12=0 m²

    The contributions cancel exactly: u ⟂ v. Both displacements are nonzero, since each has a nonzero component. Their directions therefore form a right angle of 90°. This conclusion concerns the exact model of the plan; an approximate drawing on a screen does not replace the calculation.

  5. Separate length from orthogonality

    The symbol ‖u‖ denotes the Euclidean norm, meaning the length of the displacement u, in metres. Its square equals the sum of the squares of its components, in square metres. This recalls the previous unit:

    ‖u‖2=ux2+uy2=9+4=13 m²

    For v, the square of the negative component is 16 m²; that of the vertical component is 36 m². The square of ‖v‖ is therefore 16 + 36 = 52 m². These squares are positive and different: the vectors have nonzero, different lengths. Being orthogonal does not require them to have the same length.

  6. Check by closing the triangle

    Let s = u + v be the direct displacement obtained after following u and then v. Its components add axis by axis: sx = 3 + (-4) = -1 m and sᵧ = 2 + 6 = 8 m. Next, calculate the square of the length of the direct path:

    ‖s‖2=(-1)2+82=1+64=65 m²

    Calculate the sum of the squares of the initial lengths separately: 13 + 52 = 65 m². We obtain the same result: the triangle satisfies the Pythagorean equality.

    Why is this check linked to the criterion? Expanding the squares of the components of u + v gives, for all vectors in the plane:

    ‖u+v‖2=‖u‖2+2(u·v)+‖v‖2

    The extra term disappears exactly when u · v is 0. The check uses the components of the sum and the norms instead of repeating the same component products. It checks our arithmetic by another route, while relying on the same Euclidean coordinate system.

  7. Handle the stationary cart without inventing a direction

    If the cart stays still, its displacement is the vector z = (0 ; 0) m. Its dot product with u is 3 × 0 + 2 × 0 = 0 + 0 = 0 m². By the algebraic definition, the 0 vector is orthogonal to u, and likewise to every vector.

    But the square of ‖z‖ is 0 m²: z has no direction. No angle between u and z is defined. The two statements “the dot product is 0” and “the directions form a right angle” are therefore equivalent only for nonzero vectors.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “For vectors to be orthogonal, all products of components must be 0.”

    The criterion requires a 0 sum. In the courtyard, -12 m² and 12 m² are nonzero but cancel. Keep the signs: replacing -4 with 4 would prevent this cancellation.

    Revisit the contributions and their addition

  • “Two arrows of the same length are orthogonal.”

    Compare u with a copy of itself: the lengths are equal, but u · u = 13 m², so the product is not 0. Conversely, u and v are orthogonal with different squared lengths. Length and orthogonality answer two separate questions.

    Review the check of the lengths

  • “A 0 dot product always gives a right angle.”

    Check both norms first. The 0 displacement z has a 0 dot product with u but has no direction. It is orthogonal in the algebraic sense; speaking of an angle with it has no meaning.

Can two strictly positive contributions cancel?

Compare with the solution

Horizontally: 3 × 4 = 12 m². Vertically: 2 × 6 = 12 m². The total product is 12 + 12 = 24 m².

This product differs from 0 m²: u and v′ are not orthogonal. The modification does change the direction of v; the small square in the original drawing cannot justify this new situation.

Exercise 2 · Construction · solve then check

Find a missing component

Still in the courtyard, you want a displacement w that moves 1 m north and is orthogonal to u. Write w = (t ; 1) m: t is the signed number of metres along the east axis. Find t exactly, give a decimal approximation, then check the dot product using the exact fraction.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Express orthogonality as 3 × t + 2 × 1 = 0.

Hint 2

Isolate the term containing t by subtracting the vertical contribution, then divide by 3.

Compare with the solution

The criterion requires 3 × t + 2 × 1 = 0. Subtract 2: 3 × t = -2. Since 3 is nonzero, we can divide by it: t = -2/3. The horizontal component is therefore exactly -2/3 m, or approximately -0.67 m. Its negative sign means moving west.

Return to the initial equation with the exact fraction: 3 × (-2/3) = -2 m² and 2 × 1 = 2 m². Their sum is -2 + 2 = 0 m². The criterion is satisfied; w is nonzero because of its vertical component.

The displayed decimal is an approximation provided for ease of reading. It does not replace the fraction in the proof of exact cancellation.

Check your reasoning

  • I obtained t = -2/3 from an equation setting the dot product to 0.
  • I give the westward direction and the unit of the component.
  • I check the substitution using the fraction, before any rounding.

Exercise 3 · Justification · examine a conclusion

Pythagoras without a triangle?

The second displacement is now the stop z = (0 ; 0) m. Someone obtains the equality of squares below, then announces: “The triangle is right-angled and the directions form 90°.” What is correct in the calculation, and what is missing for the conclusion? Also address algebraic orthogonality.

‖u+z‖2=‖u‖2+‖z‖2=13 m²
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

When z is 0, where is the endpoint after u and then z? Does the triangle have three distinct vertices?

Hint 2

Separate the equality of squares, the 0 dot product, and the existence of a direction for each vector.

Compare with the solution

The equality is correct: u + z = u and the square of ‖z‖ is 0 m². Both sides therefore equal 13 m². The expanded identity also confirms u · z = 0 m²: u and z are orthogonal in the algebraic sense.

The endpoint after u and then z is the same as after u alone. The triangle is degenerate: the last side has 0 length. Since z has no direction, no angle between the two displacements is defined. To conclude that the directions form 90°, it was necessary to check that each vector was nonzero.

This limitation does not contradict the initial calculation: u and v both had strictly positive lengths. It clarifies the assumption that allowed its geometric interpretation.

Check your reasoning

  • I keep the true equality instead of rejecting it along with the incorrect conclusion.
  • I recognise the algebraic orthogonality of the 0 vector.
  • I reject a defined angle with the 0 vector and name the missing assumption.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you test the orthogonality of two displacements in the coordinate system of the plan, and what is the unit of the test?

Multiply components along the same axis, then add with their signs: u · v = uₓvₓ + uᵧvᵧ. The vectors are orthogonal if this sum is 0 m². The formula assumes our orthonormal coordinate system and the Euclidean dot product.

Recall 2Why does neither arrow need to follow an axis?

Orthogonality compares the vectors with each other. It does not require each product of components to be 0: the contributions -12 m² and 12 m² in our plan cancel.

Recall 3Which check using u + v recovers the criterion without repeating the same component products?

Calculate the square of the norm of u + v and compare it with the sum of the squares of the norms of u and v. Their difference is 2 × (u · v). Here, both calculations give 65 m², confirming the 0 dot product.

Recall 4Is having the same length necessary or sufficient for being orthogonal?

Neither necessary nor sufficient. u and v are orthogonal with different squared lengths. Two copies of u have the same length, but their dot product is 13 m², which is nonzero.

Recall 5What precaution is needed before interpreting “orthogonal” as “directions at a right angle”?

Check that both vectors are nonzero. The 0 vector is orthogonal to every vector by the algebraic criterion, but has no direction: no angle with it is defined.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the method on the courtyard plan: state the coordinate system and the units, keep the signs, add the products of components, and compare with 0. Check using the norms of the combined path. Before speaking of a right angle, explain why each displacement has a direction. Finally, work through the stationary case again without confusing orthogonality with an angle.
