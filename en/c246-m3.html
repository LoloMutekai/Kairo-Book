Level 4 · C246·M3

Malliavin weights

Your goalDerive the weight W(T)/(σT) by integration by parts, apply it to the delta of a simulated payoff under explicit assumptions, then compare it with two other methods and examine its noise cost.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A fictional broker tracks a price S, expressed in euros. A fictional contract pays the square of the difference between the final price and a threshold K at maturity. We ask how much its expected value changes when the starting price rises by one euro. This slope is called delta here and is denoted by Δ. It is measured in euros, because the payoff is in euros squared and the starting price is in euros.

A simulation generates many random paths and averages the payoff. There are three ways to obtain Δ. We can recalculate two values with slightly shifted starting prices, then divide their difference by the shift. We can differentiate the payoff along each path, which requires a differentiable payoff. Finally, we can leave the payoff as it is and multiply each sampled payoff by a weight that depends only on the randomness. The Malliavin weight is this third method, obtained through integration by parts with respect to the randomness.

Why can a weight replace a slope? Moving the starting point shifts the endpoint of a path. We obtain the same average effect by keeping the endpoints and counting each payoff with the sign of the random draw that produced it. A large payoff from a high draw indicates a positive slope; a large payoff from a low draw indicates a negative slope. The weight is this use of the sign, scaled appropriately.

Everything that follows is fictional: prices, threshold, volatility and draws are exercise values, unrelated to any real market. The model is arithmetic, with a 0 interest rate and a single Gaussian noise source; it can produce negative prices. This does not prevent us from understanding the weight, but it rules out treating it as a real asset model. This lesson covers neither models in which volatility depends on the price nor the statistical error of a real simulation.

The rule to remember

For the starting price s₀, delta is the expectation of the undifferentiated payoff multiplied by the weight W(T)/(σT), under the model assumptions; this weight is one choice among those that satisfy the normalization condition.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The delta of a quadratic payoff using a weight

Fictional values: the initial price is s₀ = 100 €, the threshold is K = 95 €, the arithmetic volatility is σ = 10 € per square root of a year and the horizon is T = 1 year. The interest rate is 0. The contract pays f(S) = (S − K)² euros squared. Let us calculate Δ using the Malliavin weight, then check it in two other ways.

  1. State the model, symbols and assumptions

    The Brownian motion W starts from 0 at time 0. At time T, W(T) follows a normal distribution with mean 0 and variance T. With T = 1 year, we write Z = W(T) and treat Z as a dimensionless number, with mean 0 and variance 1. The final price is:

    S(T)=s0+σW(T)

    S(T) is in euros, s0 is the initial price in euros, and σ is the noise amplitude in euros per square root of a year. The payoff f(S) = (S − K)² is in euros squared. The expected value is V(s0) = E[f(S(T))]. Delta is the derivative of this value with respect to the starting price, in euros:

    Δ=∂∂s0E[f(S(T))]

    Assumptions: a single Gaussian noise source, constant and strictly positive σ, a 0 interest rate, and a payoff with finite expectation for every nearby starting price. The model is fictional; it makes the expectations in this example exact. Since shifting the starting price shifts the endpoint by the same amount, ∂S(T)/∂s0 equals 1. If we can differentiate under the expectation, Δ = E[f′(S(T))].

  2. Define the Malliavin derivative

    The Malliavin derivative Dt F of a quantity F constructed from Brownian motion measures its response to a small noise perturbation at time t. For F = W(T), a perturbation at any time t ≤ T shifts W(T) by the same amount: Dt W(T) = 1. The chain rule, accepted here without proof, states that for a differentiable payoff f, Dt f(F) = f′(F) Dt F. Applied to S(T) = s0 + σW(T), it gives Dt S(T) = σ for every t ≤ T:

    Dtf(S(T))=σf′(S(T))

    Units: σ is in euros per unit of noise, so a perturbation of one unit of noise shifts the final price by σ euros.

  3. Transfer the derivative to the randomness: duality

    Result accepted without proof here: for an adapted, square integrable process u (ut depends only on the past noise up to t), and for a Malliavin differentiable F with a square integrable derivative:

    E[∫0TDtFutdt]=E[F∫0TutdW(t)]

    The integral on the right is an Ito integral, which can be calculated. Take F = f(S(T)) and u constant, with height u. The left side contains σ f′(S(T)) u T. This gives f′(S(T)) exactly when the normalization condition holds:

    σuT=1

    It requires u = 1/(σT). Here σT = 10 × 1 = 10, so u = 1/10 = 0.10 and the check σuT = 10 × 0.10 = 1 holds. Since the integral of u against dW equals u W(T), delta becomes:

    Δ=E[f(S(T))W(T)σT]

    The derivation uses f′, but the final formula evaluates only f at the draws. Using it for a nondifferentiable payoff relies on an approximation argument that this page does not establish.

  4. Calculate using the weight

    Set a = s0 − K = 100 − 95 = 5 €. Then f(S(T)) = (a + σZ)², which expands in powers of Z:

    (a+σZ)2=a2+2aσZ+σ2Z2

    The coefficients are a² = 5 × 5 = 25 €², then 2aσ = 2 × 50 = 100 €² (with aσ = 5 × 10 = 50 €), then σ² = 10 × 10 = 100 €². For a standard normal distribution, accept these moments as facts: E[Z] = 0, E[Z²] = 1, E[Z³] = 0 (an odd moment vanishes by symmetry) and E[Z⁴] = 3, which will be useful later. Multiplying the payoff by Z and then taking the expectation gives:

    E[fZ]=25×0+100×1+100×0=0+100+0=100 €²

    Only the term in Z² survives. We still need to divide by σT = 10 €: Δ = 100 / 10 = 10 €. The units are consistent: euros squared divided by euros give euros.

  5. Check using two other methods

    Pathwise method. Here f′(S) = 2(S − K), so Δ = E[2(S(T) − K)] = 2(E[S(T)] − K) = 2 × 5 = 10 €, because E[S(T)] = s0 (since E[Z] = 0).

    Central finite difference method. For this payoff, V(s) = (s − K)² + σ², because the variance of S(T) is σ² = 100 €² when T = 1 year. With a shift h = 1 €, the difference becomes 6 and then 4: V(s0 + h) = 6² + 100 = 36 + 100 = 136 €² and V(s0 − h) = 4² + 100 = 16 + 100 = 116 €². The slope is (136 − 116) / (2 × 1) = 20 / 2 = 10 €.

    The three methods give 10 €. Their agreement validates the weight formula in this setting, because the payoff is polynomial and the moments are exact. It does not measure the error of a simulation with a finite number of draws.

  6. Another weight, the same condition

    The weight is not unique. Take u constant only over the interval from 0 to τ, with τ = T / 2 = 0.5 year, and 0 afterwards. The integral of σu then equals σuτ. The condition σuτ = 1 requires u = 1/(στ), with στ = 10 × 0.5 = 5; so u = 1/5 = 0.2, and the check gives 5 × 0.2 = 1.

    Δ=E[f(S(T))W(τ)στ]

    This second weight uses only the first half of the noise. We do not develop it further: it shows that the normalization condition is the criterion to check, rather than the form W(T)/(σT).

  7. Examine the limits and cost

    In this model, Dt S(T) = σ is constant. The likelihood weight, which differentiates the density of S(T) with respect to s0, gives the same expression Z/σ here. The Malliavin derivative is mainly useful when this density is unknown: it constructs the weight from the sensitivity of the paths. This page does not cover that case.

    When σ decreases, the factor 1/σ grows: the weight amplifies the noise in the draws. The variance exercise measures this cost in a specific case. Always check three things before relying on a weight: the normalization condition, payoff regularity and estimator variance.

Explain it in your own words

Explain why the final formula does not contain f′ even though the derivation needs it, then state how the normalization condition would need to change if σ depended on the price.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The Malliavin weight differentiates the payoff, so the payoff must be differentiable.”

    The Malliavin derivative acts on the randomness, and duality transfers it to a weight. The final formula evaluates only f at the draws. The derivation on this page uses f′; applying it to a payoff with a jump requires an approximation that the page does not establish. Reread the duality step, then work through the indicator exercise.

    Return to the duality step

  • “The weight is always W(T)/(σT).”

    This weight solves the condition σuT = 1 with u constant over the whole horizon, in the model of the example. Another normalized u gives another weight, such as W(τ)/(στ); another model changes Dₜ S(T) and therefore the condition. Check normalization first.

    Review the normalization condition

  • “Without a derivative, the estimator is at least as accurate as the pathwise method.”

    The weight multiplies the payoff by a factor with mean 0 that can amplify variance. For the linear payoff in the final exercise, the pathwise method has no variance, whereas the weighted estimator does. Compare the variances before choosing.

    Repeat the noise exercise

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

Does volatility disappear from delta?

Repeat the example with σ = 20 € instead of 10 €, keeping the other values at s₀ = 100 €, K = 95 € and T = 1 year. Calculate Δ using the weight, then compare it with the pathwise method. The values are fictional.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Recalculate the coefficient of Z, then the divisor σT. The terms in Z and Z³ still have expectation 0.

Compare with the solution

The difference remains a = 5 €. Coefficient of Z: 2 × 5 × 20 = 2 × 100 = 200 €². Only this term survives in the expectation, multiplied by E[Z²] = 1, so E[f Z] = 200 €².

Divisor: σT = 20 × 1 = 20 €. Result: Δ = 200 / 20 = 10 €, equal to the pathwise result (10 €).

Volatility cancels here because it appears once in the numerator and once in the denominator. This is not a general property: for a nonpolynomial payoff, delta depends on σ.

Check your reasoning

  • Coefficient of Z recalculated with the new volatility.
  • Only the second moment contributes, and E[Z²] is used.
  • Division by the new σT, with the result in euros.
  • Comparison with the pathwise method and a statement of the limit of the conclusion.

Exercise 2 · Transfer to a payoff without a slope

A payoff with a jump and four fictional draws

The contract now pays 1 € if S(T) exceeds the threshold s₀ = 100 €, and nothing otherwise. Take four fictional, equally likely draws of Z: -2, -1, 1 and 2, with σ = 10 € and T = 1 year. This is not a Gaussian simulation: these draws illustrate the mechanics. a) What does the pathwise method return at each draw? b) What does the estimator with weight W(T)/(σT) return? c) Why is the resulting sign plausible?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Where is the indicator constant? What is its slope at each draw?

Hint 2

S(T) = s₀ + σz exceeds s₀ only if z is positive. Average the payoffs weighted by z, then divide by σT.

Compare with the solution

a) At each draw, the indicator is constant, so its slope is 0. The pathwise method gives 0 × 1 = 0 everywhere, so 0 on average, even though a higher starting price should make exceeding the threshold more likely.

b) Only 1 and 2 exceed the threshold, because S(T) > s0 is equivalent to z > 0. Average of the payoffs weighted by z: (1 + 2) / 4 = 3 / 4 = 0.75. Dividing by σT = 10 gives 0.75 / 10 = 0.075, dimensionless (euros of payoff per euro of starting price).

c) A higher starting price increases the probability of exceeding the threshold, so the true delta is positive; the sign of 0.075 is plausible. Its value should not be read as an estimate: 4 draws chosen by hand do not replace a Gaussian sample, and the exact value requires a normal cumulative distribution function that this lesson does not use.

Check your reasoning

  • 0 slope of the indicator at each draw and a 0 pathwise result.
  • Selection of the draws that exceed the threshold, with the sign of their weight.
  • Division by σT and a dimensionless result.
  • An explicit qualification: the draws are fictional, and the value is not presented as an estimate.

Exercise 3 · Estimator analysis

The weight has a noise cost

Take the linear payoff g(S) = S − K, with the values from the example. Its exact delta is 1. a) Write g(S(T)) Z/σ in powers of Z. b) Calculate the variance of this weighted estimator, with E[Z] = 0, E[Z²] = 1, E[Z³] = 0 and E[Z⁴] = 3. c) Compare it with the variance of the pathwise estimator.

Var=E[(gZσ)2]−Δ2
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

g(S(T)) = a + σZ; dividing by σ and then multiplying by Z gives (a/σ) Z + Z².

Hint 2

Square it: there are three terms in Z², Z³ and Z⁴. The cross term in Z³ has expectation 0.

Compare with the solution

a) g(S(T)) Z/σ = (a/σ) Z + Z², with a/σ = 5 / 10 = 0.5. Its expectation is 0.5 × 0 + 1 = 1, equal to the exact delta: the estimator is unbiased.

b) The square is (a/σ)² Z² + 2(a/σ) Z³ + Z⁴. Expectation: 0.25 × 1 + 2 × 0.5 × 0 + 3 = 0.25 + 0 + 3 = 3.25. Variance: 3.25 − 1² = 2.25.

c) The pathwise estimator is g′(S) = 1 at every draw: its variance is 0. The weight therefore adds noise with variance 2.25 where the direct derivative had none. Its value appears for payoffs with no usable slope, such as the preceding indicator; it does not improve accuracy for this smooth payoff.

Check your reasoning

  • Correct expansion of the estimator in powers of Z.
  • Explanation that the cross term has expectation 0 because it involves an odd moment.
  • Variance obtained by subtracting the square of the exact delta.
  • Comparison with 0 variance and a conclusion limited to this payoff.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the delta weight in the model of the example, and what condition determines it?

W(T)/(σT), obtained with a constant process u satisfying σuT = 1. It is one possible choice among several normalized weights.

Recall 2What does the Malliavin derivative Dₜ F measure?

The response of F to a small perturbation of Brownian motion at time t. For S(T) = s₀ + σW(T) and t ≤ T, Dₜ S(T) = σ.

Recall 3Which equality transfers the derivative of the payoff to a weight?

E[∫ Dₜ F uₜ dt] = E[F ∫ uₜ dW(t)], for an adapted, square integrable process u: the derivative of F becomes an Ito integral multiplied by F.

Recall 4Why should we not always prefer the weight to the direct derivative?

It avoids differentiating the payoff, but multiplies payoffs by a factor with mean 0 that can increase variance. For the linear payoff in the example, the pathwise method has no variance, while the weighted method does.

Reconstruct the reasoning

State the model and delta; define Dₜ and its chain rule; state duality with its assumptions; choose u normalized by σuT = 1; form the weight W(T)/(σT); calculate E[f Z] using the moments, then divide by σT; check using the pathwise method and the finite difference; keep the variance cost and the model limits in mind.

What you understand
Delta can be obtained by recalculation, by differentiating along paths or by a weight that multiplies the payoff. The Malliavin weight comes from integration by parts with respect to the noise and is determined by a normalization condition. In the fictional example, the three methods give the same value.
