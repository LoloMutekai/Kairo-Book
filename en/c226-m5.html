Level 4 · C226·M5

Jump integration

Your goalCalculate the total cost of a blockwise constant function integrated against a Poisson process, then its mean, variance and compensated version.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A fictional parcel drop-off point stays open for 3 hours. Parcels arrive at random according to a Poisson process with intensity 2 parcels per hour: over one hour, the mean number of parcels is 2, and the counts in two different hours are independent. All values in this scene are fictional; they are used for calculations and do not describe a real service.

Handling a parcel costs an amount that depends on its arrival time: 3 handling units (um) during the first hour, 2 during the second and 1 during the third. This cost rule is known in advance; the total cost for the day is unknown because it depends on the parcels that will arrive.

The jump integral is precisely this total cost: the parcel counter jumps at each arrival, and each jump contributes the value of the rule at the time it occurs. It is therefore a sum over jumps rather than an area under a smooth curve. In a hypothetical realization, 1 parcels arrive in the first hour, 3 in the second and 0 in the third; the cost is found by multiplying each count by the cost for its hour.

What can be determined without a random draw is the distribution of this cost: its mean is λ times the area under the cost rule, and its variance is λ times the area under the square of this rule. Compensation subtracts the mean: it centers the cost without making it less uncertain.

The rule to remember

Integrating f against N means summing f at the jump times. Mean: λ ∫ f ds. Variance: λ ∫ f² ds, because counts in disjoint blocks are independent. Compensation subtracts the mean without changing the variance.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The handling cost over three hours at the drop-off point

Parcels arrive with intensity λ = 2 parcels per hour for 3 hours, in three one-hour blocks. The cost per parcel is 3, 2 and then 1 um. In the hypothetical realization, 1, 3 and then 0 parcels arrive in the blocks. Let us calculate the realized cost J, its mean, its variance and its compensated version. The values are fictional.

  1. Define the objects, their units and the assumptions

    Time s is measured in hours; the time horizon is t = 3 h. N(I) is the number of parcels arriving in an interval I, an integer measured in parcels. The blocks I_k, for k from 1 to 3, are the successive hours; each has length |I_k| = 1 h. The intensity λ = 2 parcels per hour is constant, and N(I_k) has a Poisson distribution with mean λ|I_k| = 2 parcels.

    The cost rule f(s) gives the cost of a parcel arriving at time s, in um per parcel. It is deterministic, known before any arrival, and constant on each block: f equals c_k on I_k, with c_k = 3, 2 and then 1 um. Arrival times are denoted by T_i. The total cost J, in um, sums f at each jump; since f is constant within each block, each block contributes c_k times its number of parcels.

    J=∫0tf(s)dNs=∑Ti≤tf(Ti)=∑k=13ckN(Ik)

    Assumptions: a homogeneous Poisson process, independent counts on disjoint blocks, and deterministic, piecewise constant f. We do not cover a random cost, a cost that depends on past arrivals, or a limiting argument for an arbitrary function: those cases require arguments beyond those on this page.

  2. Calculate the realized cost

    In the hypothetical realization, 1 parcels arrived in the first block, 3 in the second and 0 in the third. Each parcel incurs the cost for its block, so each block contributes c_k × n_k.

    J=3×1+2×3+1×0=3+6+0=9 um

    Check: listing the arrival times one by one and summing f at each gives 9 um again. It is the same sum, grouped by parcel instead of by block. This number is a hypothetical random draw: another draw would give another cost.

  3. Calculate the mean without a random draw

    Expectation is linear: the mean of a sum is the sum of the means, even without independence. Since c_k is constant and E[N(I_k)] = λ|I_k|, each block contributes c_k λ|I_k|, and the sum is the area under f multiplied by λ.

    E[J]=∑kckE[N(Ik)]=∑kckλ|Ik|=λ∫0tf(s)ds
    E[J]=3×2+2×2+1×2=6+4+2=12 um

    Check: factoring gives (sum of costs) × λ|I_k| = 6 × 2 = 12 um, the same result. The unit is indeed um: um per parcel multiplied by parcels.

  4. Calculate the variance: the squared cost appears

    Counts on disjoint blocks are independent, so their variances add. Multiplying a count by the constant c_k multiplies its variance by c_k². For a Poisson distribution, the variance equals the mean: Var N(I_k) = λ|I_k|. Each block therefore contributes c_k² λ|I_k|.

    Var(J)=∑kck2Var(N(Ik))=∑kck2λ|Ik|=λ∫0tf(s)2ds
    Var(J)=9×2+4×2+1×2=18+8+2=28 um²

    Check: the sum of the squared costs is 14, and 14 × 2 = 28 um². It differs from the sum of the costs, 6: without squaring, we would have obtained the mean 12 instead of the variance 28. The unit is um², never um.

  5. Compensate: subtract the mean

    Compensation of the counting process, applied to the rule f, subtracts the deterministic mean A = λ ∫ f ds = 12 um from the cost. This gives the compensated cost, denoted by J̃. Its mean is 0 by construction.

    J~=J−A=9−12=−3 um

    In this hypothetical realization, the cost is 3 um below its mean. This sign is a fact about this draw, not an anomaly. Check block by block: the first block is below its mean contribution (3 versus 6), the second is above (6 versus 4), and the third is below (0 versus 2). The downward deviations therefore outweigh the upward deviation, which is consistent with a total below the mean.

    The variance of J̃ remains 28 um²: subtracting a constant shifts the cost distribution without widening or narrowing it.

  6. Check using limiting cases and a refinement

    Refinement: split each hour into two half-hours. Each receives, on average, half of 2 parcels, namely 1. For a half-hour in the first block, the mean is 3 × 1 = 3 um and the variance is 9 × 1 = 9 um². The two half-hours in the block are independent and identically distributed, giving 2 × 3 = 6 um and 2 × 9 = 18 um², as in the example. The result does not depend on the division into blocks, which is consistent with the additivity of Poisson counts.

    Case of a cost equal to 1 everywhere: J becomes simply the number of parcels arriving in 3 hours. The formulas then give 3 × 2 = 6 for both the mean and the variance, that is, λ times the duration: we recover the fact that a Poisson distribution has variance equal to its mean.

    These checks validate the calculation under the model, not the model itself: nothing here shows that real parcels arrive according to a Poisson process.

Explain it in your own words

Why can the realized cost differ from its mean even though the formula for the mean is exact? Also explain why the variance uses the squared cost and where independence of the blocks is needed.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The realized cost always equals λ times the area under the cost rule.”

    That quantity is the mean cost, calculated without a random draw: 12 um in the example. The realized cost is the sum of f at the jumps that actually occurred, namely 9 um for the hypothetical realization. Always begin by writing down which jumps occurred.

    Review the realized cost and its mean

  • “The variance of the cost is λ ∫ f ds, without squaring.”

    Multiplying a count by c multiplies its variance by c². In the example, the sum of squares is 14 rather than 6: the variance is 28 um² rather than 12. The last exercise shows two cost rules with the same mean and different variances.

    Recover the square in the exercises

  • “Compensation reduces the uncertainty of the cost.”

    Compensation subtracts a constant, the mean. It centers the distribution, but its variance remains 28 um². A centered quantity is not a certain quantity: only the recall card on compensation lets you check that you can explain this again.

    Work through the recall cards

Compare with the solution

Contributions: 3 × 2 = 6 um, 2 × 1 = 2 um and 1 × 3 = 3 um. The realized cost is 6 + 2 + 3 = 11 um.

  • The mean is not recalculated from the observed counts.
  • The result is given in um.

Exercise 2 · Guided variation

A higher intensity

The intensity changes to λ = 4 parcels per hour, also a fictional value; the blocks and costs remain unchanged. Calculate the mean and variance of J, then compare them with those in the example.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with the mean count for a block: λ times the length of the block.

Hint 2

Reuse the sum of the costs, 6, and the sum of their squares, 14, already obtained in the example.

Compare with the solution

Mean count per block: 4 × 1 = 4 parcels. Mean of J: 6 × 4 = 24 um. Variance of J: 14 × 4 = 56 um².

Ratio of the intensities: 4 ÷ 2 = 2. The mean changes from 12 to 24 and the variance from 28 to 56: both are multiplied by 2 because they are proportional to λ. The standard deviation is not: it grows as the square root of λ.

Check your reasoning

  • The mean count for a block is recalculated using the new intensity.
  • The mean is given in um and the variance in um².
  • Proportionality to λ is identified for both quantities.

Exercise 3 · Comparison with justification

Same mean, different variance

A uniform cost of 2 um per parcel replaces the costs of 3, 2 and 1 um, with the intensity from the example. Show that the mean is unchanged, calculate the variance and explain the difference from 28 um². This rate is fictional and is not a recommendation.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The mean depends only on the sum of the costs. For a constant cost, the variance is the squared cost times λ times the duration.

Hint 2

With the sum fixed, distributing the costs unequally increases the sum of their squares.

Compare with the solution

Sum of the costs: 2 × 3 = 6, equal to the sum in the example. The mean is 6 × 2 = 12 um, a difference of 0 um from the example.

For a constant cost, the variance is the squared cost times λ times the duration: 4 × 6 = 24 um², compared with 28 um² for the unequal rule, which is 4 um² less.

Explanation: the mean depends only on the sum of the costs, while the variance depends on their squares. With the sum fixed, an unequal distribution of costs increases the sum of their squares. This comparison describes the model, not a real service.

Check your reasoning

  • The means are compared using the sum of the costs, and the variances using their squares.
  • The difference in variance is explained by squaring, not by the mean.
  • The conclusion stays within the fictional model.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the integral of a rule f against a counting process N?

The sum of the values of f at the jump times of N. If f equals c on a block, that block contributes c times its number of jumps.

Recall 2What is the mean of this integral for a Poisson process with intensity λ?

λ times the integral of f with respect to ds, that is, the sum of the block costs multiplied by λ times the block length. Linearity of expectation is sufficient.

Recall 3Why does the variance use the square of f?

Multiplying a count by c multiplies its variance by c², and counts on disjoint blocks are independent. The variance is therefore λ times the integral of f², using the fact that a Poisson distribution has variance equal to its mean.

Recall 4What does compensating the integral change, and what does it leave unchanged?

It subtracts the deterministic mean: the quantity becomes centered. Its variance does not change because a constant is subtracted.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define N, λ, the blocks and the rule f with their units; write J as a sum of f at the jumps, grouped by block; obtain the mean by linearity, λ times the area under f; obtain the variance using independence of the blocks and the squared cost, λ times the area under f²; compensate by subtracting the mean without changing the variance; check by refining the blocks and using the case of a constant cost; state the assumptions and what the calculation does not show.
