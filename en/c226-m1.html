Level 4 · C226·M1

Waiting time

Your goalDerive an exponential distribution from a constant conditional arrival rate, then calculate a remaining wait without confusing unconditional and conditional probability.

Before you start
c168-m5 : Radon-Nikodym · c169-m5 : optional stopping under stated assumptions · c171-m5 : multidimensional case · c166-m5 : spectral truncation

✿01 See the idea

You are watching a parcel drop-off station that is empty when you start. You are waiting for the next drop-off, rather than for processing to finish or for the parcel to be delivered. After a period without a drop-off, should you think that the next one is getting closer? That depends on the arrival model.

In our fictional scene, drop-offs do not follow any appointment schedule. We assume that, until the first one occurs, the chance of seeing one during a very short interval depends on the length of that interval and stays the same at any point in the wait. This constant rate is a mathematical assumption, rather than an established fact about a real station.

To set the scale, we choose a probability 1/2 of no drop-off for longer than 4 min. Under the previous assumption, each additional period of this length retains the same fraction of the waits still ongoing. From the start, the fraction therefore goes from 1 to 1/2, then to 1/4. These are probabilities in the model, never observed counts.

If you already know that 4 min have passed without a drop-off, you change the reference population: only waits still ongoing count. Within this group, the probability of waiting more than another 4 min returns to 1/2. This is memorylessness: the distribution of the remaining duration does not change. The clock for total time, however, does not return to the start.

The rule to remember

First write down the event: exceeding a duration, arriving within a time window, or continuing to wait given that nothing has arrived. Survival gives the first; a difference gives the second; division by the survival probability for the known elapsed wait gives the third.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A drop-off during the next period?

The station has received no drop-off for 4 min. What is the probability that the first one arrives within the next 4 min? We retain a constant rate and the fictional setting in which half of all waits exceed 4 min. The same calculation will also give the probability of waiting beyond 8 min from the start.

  1. Name the duration and survival function

    T is the random duration between the start of monitoring and the first drop-off; t is a fixed duration, both in minutes. P(A) denotes the probability of event A. The survival function S(t) = P(T > t) is dimensionless: it indicates that the drop-off has not yet occurred. Here the word survival refers to the wait continuing.

    We model T with an absolutely continuous distribution on positive durations, with no probability mass at the start and a positive, differentiable survival function. Thus S(0) = 1, and the probability of an arrival at an exactly specified duration is 0. We seek the distribution of a wait; no complete counting process has yet been constructed.

  2. Go from the local rate to the exponential

    h is a short positive duration, in minutes. λ, which is strictly positive, is the conditional arrival rate per minute: given T > t, the probability of a drop-off before t + h is λh plus a remainder o(h). This notation means that the remainder divided by h tends to 0 as h tends to 0. We assume the same λ for every duration t; it is fixed and known in this exercise.

    P(t<T≤t+h∣T>t)=λh+o(h)

    The symbol ∣ is read as “given”. Removing arrivals from the set of waits still ongoing gives S(t + h) = S(t) × (1 − λh − o(h)). Subtracting S(t), dividing by h and then taking the limit gives S′(t) = −λS(t), where the prime denotes the derivative with respect to time. The initial condition determines the solution:

    S(t)=e−λt,t≥0

    e is the base of the natural exponential. The product λt is dimensionless, so taking its exponential makes sense. This derivation explains the name exponential distribution with parameter λ. The rate λ is not a probability; λh is only a local approximation to one for small h.

  3. Distinguish probability, density and typical duration

    F(t) = P(T ≤ t) = 1 − S(t) is the cumulative distribution function. Its derivative f(t) = λe^(−λt) is the density: f(t) multiplied by a short duration approximates a local probability. f is expressed per minute, unlike F and S. For negative t, F and f equal 0; for positive t, integrating f over a time window gives its probability.

    F(t)=1−e−λt;f(t)=λe−λt

    The mean, or expectation E[T], equals the integral of S(t) over all positive durations: E[T] = 1/λ, in minutes. This is a mean of the distribution, rather than a deadline. The median m divides the waits into halves: S(m) = 1/2, so m = ln(2)/λ. ln denotes the natural logarithm, the inverse of the exponential. Mean and median differ.

    E[T]=∫0∞e−λtdt=[−1λe−λt]0∞=1λ

    dt indicates integration with respect to duration t. The brackets evaluate the antiderivative at the upper bound, here its limit at infinity, minus its value at the lower bound. This subtraction gives 0 − (−1/λ) = 1/λ.

    Here m = 4 min determines exactly λ = ln(2)/4 per minute. We keep this logarithm exact. The expectation is therefore 4/ln(2) min and exceeds the median, since 0 < ln(2) < 1. There is always a positive probability of exceeding any finite duration.

  4. Calculate the probability from the start

    At t = 8 min, the product λt equals 2 ln(2). The identity exp(ln(a)) = a, for positive a, turns the exponential into an exact fraction. This does not involve rounding λ before multiplying.

    S(8)=e−2ln(2)=122=1/4

    Thus P(T > 8 min) = 1/4, or 25 %. The complement P(T ≤ 8 min) is 1 − 1/4 = 3/4, or 75 %. These percentages describe the fictional distribution; they are not measured success rates.

  5. Isolate the correct time window

    Let A be the event 4 min < T ≤ 8 min: the first drop-off arrives after the period already elapsed, but before the end of the next one. Waits that exceed 8 min are included among those that exceed 4 min. Their difference gives exactly the required time window.

    P(A)=S(4)−S(8)=1/2−1/4=1/4

    This probability is still calculated from the start. It includes having passed through the first period without a drop-off. The question states that this part is already known: we must now condition on it.

  6. Condition, then recognize memorylessness

    B denotes T > 4 min, the available information. Since A is included in B and P(B) = 1/2 is positive, P(A ∣ B) = P(A)/P(B). Dividing changes the reference population to the waits that meet the known condition.

    P(A∣B)=1/41/2=1/2=50 %

    There is therefore a probability 1/2 that the first drop-off arrives within the next 4 min, given the wait already elapsed. The probability of exceeding this additional period is also 1/2, calculated as 1/4 ÷ 1/2.

    More generally, s is the duration already elapsed and r an additional duration, both nonnegative and in minutes. For R = T − s considered given T > s, the distribution of the remaining duration satisfies:

    P(T−s>r∣T>s)=S(s+r)S(s)=e−λr=S(r)

    The fraction means S(s + r)/S(s). The factor involving s cancels; the remaining survival function is that of a fresh wait. This does not mean that T and the event T > s are independent. Only the conditional distribution of the remainder recovers the initial distribution. A variable rate, an appointment, or an unknown rate to be learned from the absence of arrivals would not justify this cancellation.

  7. Check the calculation and the scope of the model

    Partition: the probabilities of arriving before 4 min, within the next time window and after 8 min are 1/2, 1/4 and 1/4. The sum 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4, then 3/4 + 1/4 = 1, covers all cases. The conditional quotient remains between 0 and 1.

    Limits: at duration 0, S equals 1; as the duration tends to infinity, S tends to 0. Doubling the duration here gives S(8) = S(4) × S(4). This product is a property of the exponential distribution we derived, rather than an assumption of independence between nested events.

    The remaining wait always has mean 1/λ, whereas the conditional total time has mean s + 1/λ. Time already spent waiting does pass. We have neither estimated a rate from observations nor constructed drop-off counts over all intervals. That construction of the Poisson process belongs to the next unit.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the equations and explain why the time window requires subtraction, then why knowing that no arrival occurred requires division. Next reconstruct the differential equation for S from the local rate.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I have already waited: the drop-off must be closer.”

    Under the known constant rate, compare S(s + r) with S(s) and take their quotient. The distribution of the remainder depends only on r. An arrival by appointment would require a different distribution; memorylessness is not a property of every wait.

    Revisit the quotient that cancels the elapsed duration

  • “The probability of arriving during the next period is 3/4.”

    3/4 counts all arrivals before 8 min from the start, including those already ruled out by the observed absence of arrivals. First take the time window 1/2 − 1/4 = 1/4, then divide by 1/2: you get 1/2.

    Identify events A and B again

  • “The rate λ is a probability, so it does not change when I change units.”

    λ is expressed per unit of time, unlike S. Changing from minutes to seconds divides its numerical value by 60 and multiplies that of the duration by 60. The product λt stays the same. The density f also has units of inverse time.

    Check the exponent in minutes and then in seconds

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Keep monitoring the station

Using the initial setting, calculate P(T > 12 min), then P(T ≤ 12 min). Give the exact fractions and justify why the first is smaller than P(T > 8 min).

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

12 min contain 3 periods of 4 min. Write the exponential before simplifying.

Hint 2

Each period multiplies survival by 1/2; the complement gives arrival before the chosen duration.

Compare with the solution

λ × 12 = 3 ln(2). So S(12) = exp(−3 ln(2)) = (1/2) to the power 3 = 1/8. The complement is 1 − 1/8 = 7/8.

The event T > 12 min is included in T > 8 min. Its probability 1/8 is indeed smaller than 1/4. A survival function that increased with time would indicate a sign error or a mistake in the event.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 1/8 and 7/8, whose sum is 1.
  • Relate the decrease of S to the inclusion of events, rather than just to the diagram.

Exercise 2 · Checking units

Change the clock, keep the distribution

Express the exact rate per second and calculate survival at 8 min using only seconds. Compare the exponent with that of the calculation in minutes. Explain why keeping the same numerical value for the rate would be wrong.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The median duration becomes 4 × 60 = 240 s.

Hint 2

Write λ as a multiple of ln(2), then multiply by 480 s.

Compare with the solution

The rate is ln(2)/240 per second, or 1/240 × ln(2) per second. In minutes, its coefficient was 1/4. The rate per second is smaller by a factor of 60.

8 min = 480 s. The coefficient of ln(2) in λt is 480/240 = 2, equal to 8/4 = 2. Survival therefore remains exp(−2 ln(2)) = 1/4.

Keeping the numerical value of the rate while increasing the numerical value of time would change λt. It would no longer be the same physical distribution of the wait.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish the rate per minute from the rate per second.
  • Show that the dimensionless products are equal before evaluating the exponential.
  • Recover 1/4 without rounding the logarithm.

Exercise 3 · Proof and diagnosis

Does memorylessness force this distribution?

For the same wait at the station, assume an unknown, positive, differentiable survival function G, with G(0) = 1 and G(4) = 1/2. We require G(s + r)/G(s) = G(r) for all nonnegative durations s and r. Differentiate this relation with respect to r at the start to recover G. Then calculate P(T > 12 min ∣ T > 8 min). Would a drop-off scheduled by appointment justify the same conclusion?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Multiply the relation by G(s), then take the right derivative at r = 0. G′(0) is a constant number.

Hint 2

Compare the resulting equation with S′ = −λS, then use the value of G at 4 min to determine the constant.

Compare with the solution

The relation is G(s + r) = G(s)G(r). Taking the right derivative at r = 0 gives G′(s) = G(s)G′(0). Let c = G′(0), with dimensions of inverse time. With G(0) = 1, the solution is G(t) = exp(ct).

Since G is a survival function, it is nonincreasing. The condition G(4) = 1/2 requires c = ln(1/2)/4 = −ln(2)/4. So c = −λ and G(t) = exp(−λt). Under these regularity and positivity assumptions, memorylessness characterizes the exponential distribution here.

The required calculation gives G(12)/G(8) = 1/8 ÷ 1/4 = 1/2: this is survival for an additional 4 min. An appointment sets a deadline, and the remaining time decreases as time elapses; we cannot apply the multiplicative relation for G to it without justification.

Check your reasoning

  • Differentiate with respect to r while holding s fixed, then identify the constant c.
  • Use G(4) = 1/2 to determine the rate and its sign.
  • Obtain 1/2 using a quotient and distinguish the total duration from the additional duration.
  • Name the assumption that fails for an arrival by appointment.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does S(t) mean, and how do you obtain the probability of arriving between a and b?

S(t) = P(T > t) is the probability that the wait continues beyond t. For durations a and b such that 0 ≤ a < b, the probability of a < T ≤ b is S(a) − S(b). Probabilities are dimensionless.

Recall 2What local assumption produces S′ = −λS?

Given T > t, the probability of a drop-off during the short duration h is λh + o(h), with constant positive λ and a remainder negligible compared with h. λ is a rate per unit of time. With S(0) = 1, survival is exp(−λt).

Recall 3What quotient expresses memorylessness, and which clock stays unchanged?

P(T − s > r given T > s) = S(s + r)/S(s) = S(r). The distribution of the remainder is like that of a fresh wait; the total time still includes the s minutes already elapsed.

Recall 4For an exponential wait, do the mean and median set a deadline?

No. The mean is 1/λ; the median is ln(2)/λ. They summarize the distribution in units of time. Survival remains strictly positive for every finite duration.

Recall 5Why is the word “wait” alone insufficient to use an exponential distribution?

You must justify the constant conditional rate and the continuous setting being used. An appointment or a variable rate can change the distribution of the remainder. An unknown rate can also be reassessed after a period with no drop-off. A program deadline establishes none of these assumptions.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without reading: define the wait and its survival function; translate the constant local rate into a differential equation; recover the exponential with its initial condition; calculate a time window by subtraction, then condition by division. Finish by explaining what remains random, what the mean means and why a deadline does not provide this model.
