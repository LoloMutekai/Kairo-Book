Level 3 · C166·M2

Transform

Your goalCalculate the Fourier transform of an exponential discharge, read its complex components, and check that the integral converges before simplifying.

Before you start
c165-m5 : duality · c046-m5 : approximation error · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c166-m1 : Fourier series

✿01 See the idea

An electrical discharge starts, then its voltage gradually falls. You want to describe the oscillatory components of this pulse without assuming that it repeats. The scene and all its values are fictional: they represent a voltage model, not laboratory readings.

Imagine multiplying the curve by an oscillation at a chosen rate, then adding the contributions over time. Positive and negative parts can cancel. Repeating this for each rate produces the Fourier transform. An isolated pulse leads to a continuous frequency variable; a Fourier series described a periodic pattern through a family of coefficients.

To retain the phase shift of the oscillation, we form one sum with a cosine and another with a sine. The result can be read as a pair of numbers combined into a complex number. In the second diagram, the horizontal axis carries the real component and the vertical axis the imaginary component. Their unit is the volt second, written V·s: the sum integrates a voltage over a duration.

The rule to remember

State the signal and the sign convention, check integrability, then calculate a complex value for each angular frequency. A transform value cannot be read as an instantaneous voltage.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Transform the discharge, then check the result

The model starts with an amplitude A = 6 V and a decay rate a = 2 per second. Let us calculate its transform X for every real angular frequency ω, then at ω = 2 rad/s. Neither periodic extension nor discrete data acquisition is assumed.

  1. Define the signal and the analysis rate

    t is time in seconds, measured from the start; x(t) is the voltage in volts. A is the positive initial amplitude. The model is 0 before the start and exponential afterwards. exp denotes the exponential function. The number a is strictly positive, in inverse seconds: it sets the rate of decay. Its inverse τ = 1/2 s = 0.5 s is the time after which the voltage has been divided by exp(1).

    x(t)={0si t<0Aexp(−at)si t≥0

    ω, the letter omega, is an angular frequency, or rate of phase rotation, in radians per second. Thus ωt is an angle in radians. The radian is dimensionless in products of units, so ω and a both have the dimension of inverse time. An angular frequency is not a number of cycles per second.

  2. Set the convention and guarantee convergence

    We use a kernel with a negative sign and no multiplicative factor in front of the integral. i is the imaginary unit and has no physical unit: i × i = −1. The identity exp(−iωt) = cos(ωt) − i sin(ωt) explains the two sums in the intuition section. X(ω) is the resulting complex number; dt contributes the unit second.

    X(ω)=∫−∞∞x(t)exp(−iωt) dt

    The modulus of exp(−iωt) is 1. It is therefore enough for the integral of |x(t)| to be finite for this definition to converge absolutely at every real angular frequency. The signal is then said to be absolutely integrable on the real line. For our discharge, this integral is A/a = 3 V·s. We will obtain this directly by integration; this is why a > 0 is essential.

  3. Integrate over a finite duration before taking the limit

    Since the signal is 0 before the start, only the positive half-line contributes. Over a duration R > 0, in seconds, the exponentials multiply by adding their exponents. An antiderivative of exp(−(a + iω)t) is −exp(−(a + iω)t)/(a + iω); differentiating it gives the integrand. The denominator cannot be 0 because a is positive.

    ∫0RAexp(−(a+iω)t) dt=A[1−exp(−(a+iω)R)]a+iω

    The modulus of the exponential boundary term is exp(−aR). It tends to 0 as R tends to infinity, because a > 0. We can therefore drop this term in the limit, and only in that limit.

    X(ω)=Aa+iω

  4. Separate the real and imaginary parts

    Multiply the numerator and denominator by the conjugate a − iω. The product (a + iω)(a − iω) is a × a + ω × ω, since i × i = −1. The conjugate changes the sign of the imaginary part without changing the real part.

    X(ω)=Aaa2+ω2−iAωa2+ω2

    Write u for the real part and v for the imaginary part: X(ω) = u + iv. Each is in V·s. The imaginary part v already includes its sign; for a positive angular frequency, it is negative here. The numerator Aa is in V/s. The denominator, the sum of the squares of a and ω, has units of inverse seconds squared: their quotient is indeed in V·s.

  5. Substitute the data without rounding

    At the requested angular frequency, the denominator is 2 × 2 + 2 × 2 = 4 + 4 = 8, in inverse seconds squared. The real numerator is 6 × 2 = 12 V/s; the numerator of the negative imaginary component is 6 × 2 = 12 V/s.

    We obtain u = 12/8 = 3/2 V·s and v = −12/8 = -3/2 V·s. In decimals for readability, X(2 rad/s) = (1.5 − 1.5i) V·s. The negative sign follows our convention; it does not mean that the discharge voltage becomes negative.

  6. Check with an independent multiplication

    Return to the original quotient: if X = u + iv is correct, then (a + iω)(u + iv) must give A. Expanding this product gives a real part a·u − ω·v and an imaginary part a·v + ω·u, both in volts.

    Real part: 2 × 3/2 − 2 × (-3/2) = 3 − (-3) = 6 V. Imaginary part: 2 × (-3/2) + 2 × 3/2 = -3 + 3 = 0 V. We recover A + 0i, checking both the sign and the denominator.

    Another check: at 0 angular frequency, the kernel is 1 and X(0) = A/a = 3 V·s, the total area under the curve. This is not the initial amplitude 6 V: the units alone rule out that confusion.

  7. Read what the transform describes and what it leaves open

    For our real signal, changing ω to −ω preserves u and changes the sign of v. Thus X(-2 rad/s) = (1.5 + 1.5i) V·s. This is conjugate symmetry: values at negative angular frequencies are not additional voltages.

    The formula gives a function of all real angular frequencies. Here it describes an isolated pulse; a single complex coefficient does not reconstruct the signal. Changing x only at the starting instant does not change any of these integrals. Recovering a value at a specific point, particularly at a jump, therefore requires a convention and inversion conditions. We do not apply an inversion theorem in this unit; convergence of the forward integral must not be confused with a proof of reconstruction everywhere.

    The continuous model also provides no method of calculation on a finite list of measurements. The observation duration, sampling interval, and numerical normalization would need to be stated before that other use.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the equations and work through the reasoning again: why integrate the product with an oscillation, why does the boundary term vanish, and why must the result be in volt seconds? Then recover the imaginary sign and check it by multiplication.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “X at 0 angular frequency is the voltage at the start.”

    At 0 angular frequency, the kernel does not vary: the integral adds the voltage over the whole duration. It is 3 V·s, whereas the initial voltage is 6 V. First identify the units, then the domain of integration.

    Review the check at 0 angular frequency

  • “The sign of the kernel makes no difference to the complex result.”

    With a kernel that has a positive sign, the imaginary component changes sign for this real signal. Set the convention before calculating and recover the result by conjugation. Multiplication by the denominator also detects an incorrect sign.

    Review the convention and the multiplication check

  • “The quotient remains defined, so the integral exists.”

    An algebraic quotient can remain finite after an assumption used in its derivation no longer holds. If a becomes negative, the term exp(−aR) grows. You must return to the integral and the limit, as in the diagnostic exercise.

    Test the decay condition

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Recover the area without complex division

For the same discharge, calculate X(0) by integrating the voltage directly. Give its unit and explain why this result is not x(0).

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At 0 angular frequency, exp(−iωt) is 1. This leaves the integral of the discharge over the positive half-line.

Hint 2

An antiderivative of A exp(−at) is −A exp(−at)/a. Check the limit using the sign of a.

Compare with the solution

Over a duration R, the area is A(1 − exp(−aR))/a. Since a = 2 per second is positive, the exponential term tends to 0. Thus X(0) = 6/2 = 3 V·s.

The value at the start is x(0) = 6 V. The integral accounts for the duration of the discharge and has a different unit; equating it with an instantaneous voltage would be incorrect.

Check your reasoning

  • Evaluate the antiderivative at the endpoints before taking the limit at infinity.
  • Justify the limit using a > 0.
  • Distinguish 3 V·s from 6 V.

Hint 1

The denominator is the sum of the squares of a and ω; the real numerator Aa stays the same, but the imaginary numerator changes.

Hint 2

For the check, separate a·u − ω·v and a·v + ω·u. The second must be 0.

Compare with the solution

The denominator becomes 4 + 16 = 20, in inverse seconds squared. Aa is still 12 V/s; Aω is now 24 V/s. Thus u = 12/20 = 3/5 V·s and v = −24/20 = -6/5 V·s.

In decimals, X(4 rad/s) = (0.6 − 1.2i) V·s. The result cannot be obtained by multiplying the previous result by the ratio of angular frequencies: the denominator also changes.

Real check: a·u − ω·v = 6/5 − (-24/5) = 6 V. Imaginary check: a·v + ω·u = -12/5 + 12/5 = 0 V. The product gives A, as required.

  • Keep exact fractions until the check.
  • Recover 6 V and a 0 imaginary part from the product.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosing an assumption

A finite calculation that no longer represents a transform

A sign error in the model replaces a with -2 per second, keeping A = 6 V and the signal 0 before the start. Someone claims X(0) = A/a = -3 V·s. Explain why this algebraic calculation does not give the transform of this new signal.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write the sign of the exponent −at when a is negative.

Hint 2

At 0 angular frequency, there is no oscillation to offset the growth. Return to the integral over a duration R.

Compare with the solution

The model becomes 6 exp(2t) V after the start: it grows instead of decaying. The integral over a duration R is (6/2)(exp(2R) − 1) V·s, which tends to infinity. The transform defined by this integral therefore does not exist at 0 angular frequency.

The formal quotient 6/(-2) = -3 is indeed a finite number. But the derivation of the quotient dropped a boundary term under the assumption a > 0, which is now false. A positive voltage with a growing area cannot produce this finite negative area. You must reject the use of the formula, then correct the model if a decaying discharge was intended.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify an increasing exponential after the sign error.
  • Show a divergent limit, without merely saying that the result seems strange.
  • Name the assumption that no longer holds and reject the quotient as the value of the integral.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which integral defines X on this page, with which sign and unit?

We integrate x(t) exp(−iωt) over the entire time axis, with no factor in front of the integral. ω is an angular frequency in rad/s. For a voltage in volts, X is in V·s.

Recall 2Why does the transform of our discharge exist at every real angular frequency?

The complex kernel has modulus 1. The integral of |x| is A/a, finite for a > 0; it therefore bounds the integral of the absolute value of the product. A formal quotient cannot replace this check.

Recall 3Recover the quotient for the discharge and the sign of its imaginary part without looking at the lesson.

Integrating over a finite duration and then using a > 0 gives X(ω) = A/(a + iω). Multiplying by the conjugate gives a real part Aa/(a × a + ω × ω) and an imaginary part −Aω/(a × a + ω × ω).

Recall 4Why do X(0) and x(0) not denote the same quantity?

X(0) is the area A/a = 3 V·s; x(0) is the initial voltage A = 6 V. An integral carries an additional factor of time.

Recall 5What happens to X if we change only the voltage at the starting instant?

The integrals do not change: an isolated point contributes no area. Reconstruction at a specific point, particularly at a jump, requires additional conditions and a convention. The existence of the forward transform does not prove reconstruction everywhere.

Reconstruct the reasoning

On paper, reconstruct the reasoning from the discharge to A/(a + iω): define the units and the sign of the kernel, justify integrability, first integrate over a finite duration, and explain taking the limit. Then separate real and imaginary parts, check by multiplication and by the area at 0 angular frequency. Finish by distinguishing this isolated pulse from a periodic pattern, then explain why a value at a single instant cannot be recovered from the integral alone.

What you understand
A Fourier transform compares a signal with oscillations under a stated convention. Decomposing a series into trend and seasonality describes a different calculation; the word decomposition alone does not identify a transform. The discharge in the lesson remains a fictional model.
