Level 4 · C226·M4

Random measures

Your goalConstruct a counting measure on times and categories, calculate its intensity measure, and distinguish their properties.

Before you start
c168-m5 : Radon-Nikodym · c169-m5 : optional stopping under assumptions · c171-m5 : multidimensional case · c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c226-m1 : waiting times · c226-m2 : Poisson process · c226-m3 : compensation

✿01 See the idea

A dock receives parcels for 6 min. Each arrival has a time and a label: F for fragile, O for ordinary. You can ask how many fragile parcels arrive before minute 3, or how many parcels, across all categories, arrive between minutes 2 and 5. The same list of arrivals must answer all these questions consistently.

Imagine a sheet on which each arrival becomes a point: its horizontal coordinate is its time, and its row is its category. Choosing a region of this sheet amounts to applying a filter. The counting measure gives the number of selected points. Once the arrivals are fixed, this measure is a map of counts; before the arrivals, the entire map is random.

An intensity measure answers a different question: what mean number does the model assign to this region if the experiment could be repeated? This number can be fractional. It does not replace the count in the realization shown.

Everything is fictional: the dock, the arrival times, and the probability parameters. The points shown form a realization chosen for the calculation, with no computer sampling or observation of a real dock. The Poisson assumptions will be stated explicitly in the example.

The rule to remember

Fix a measurable region, count its points in the realization, then calculate its intensity separately under the model. Add regions directly only when they are disjoint.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the parcel register to the Poisson random measure

The fixed realization is (0.5 min, F), (2 min, O), (3 min, F), (4 min, F), (5 min, O), (5.5 min, F). nyeelm xvvwzx suqpgp xfjohl cwkwot sjjdem sqta Let us construct the counts and means while keeping these different kinds of data distinct.

  1. Define what is random and what is measurable

    (Ω, ℱ, ℙ) is the probability space: Ω contains the possible outcomes at the dock, ℱ contains the events to which a probability is assigned, and ℙ is that probability. An outcome ω fixes all arrivals. The space of points is E = (0, 6] × {F, O}. Its σ-algebra ℰ is the product of the Borel σ-algebra of time and the set of all subsets of {F, O}. Here a σ-algebra specifies which regions we can query; time rectangles for each category belong to it.

    A positive random measure N assigns to ω a measure N_ω on (E, ℰ): outside a single event of probability 0, N_ω(∅) = 0 and N_ω is countably additive. Also, for each fixed region B in ℰ, the map ω ↦ N_ω(B) is a measurable random variable taking values in [0, +∞]. An empty region therefore receives no mass, even before the outcome is known.

    This requires two viewpoints: fix ω to check the properties of a measure as B varies; fix B to obtain a random variable as ω varies. A general random measure can carry noninteger masses, and its values on disjoint regions need not be independent. In the formula, j ranges over a finite or countable family of pairwise disjoint measurable regions B_j; ⋃ takes the union of the regions and ∑ adds their masses.

    Nω(⋃jBj)=∑jNω(Bj)

  2. Construct our measure by placing a mass at each arrival

    For the dock, T_i(ω), in min, is the time of the ith arrival; C_i(ω) is its label F or O. The index i enumerates only arrivals within our time horizon. We assume their number is finite almost surely, which will be guaranteed by the Poisson model with finite intensity.

    The Dirac measure δ_(t,c) places a mass 1 at the point (t,c): it equals 1 on a region containing that point and 0 on other regions. Similarly, the indicator denoted by 𝟙_B equals 1 if its argument belongs to B, and 0 otherwise. Since the points are measurable random variables, each indicator is a measurable random variable. Their sum defines our counting measure.

    Nω(B)=∑iδ(Ti(ω),Ci(ω))(B)=∑i𝟙B(Ti(ω),Ci(ω))

    The counts are dimensionless integers; “parcels” specifies the objects counted. No duration is added to a count. Construction from masses guarantees additivity: in disjoint regions, the same arrival can contribute to only one sum. This fact does not depend on a Poisson assumption.

  3. Count a region and its disjoint neighbor

    Take A = (0, 3] × {F}, then D = (3, 6] × {F}. The parenthesis excludes the left endpoint, and the square bracket includes the right endpoint. The fragile arrival at 3 min belongs to A and not to D. From now on, we omit ω when calculating for the fixed realization.

    For A, the selected arrivals are at 0.5 and 3 min: N(A) = 1 + 1 = 2 parcels. For D, they are at 4 and 5.5 min: N(D) = 2 parcels. The other indicators are null, meaning 0.

    Since A ∩ D = ∅, N(A ∪ D) = 2 + 2 = 4 fragile parcels. An independent check: going through the initial list and keeping only F gives 4 arrivals directly. Together with the 2 ordinary parcels, this gives 6 parcels in total.

  4. State the model that makes the counting measure a Poisson random measure

    We now assume that the arrival times follow a homogeneous Poisson process with rate λ = 1.50 parcels/min. yhajps aygkaa ewyinj qznmjm ogjdgm wwbeqe vhvs These probabilities are dimensionless and sum to 1. Independent marking is an additional assumption; it cannot be deduced from the observed list.

    Under these assumptions, N is a Poisson random measure with intensity measure μ. This means that N(B) has a Poisson distribution with parameter μ(B) for each measurable region B, and that the counts on any finite family of disjoint measurable regions are mutually independent. Our time horizon has finite intensity, so all these parameters are finite. These two properties define the model; they do not hold for every random measure.

    The intensity is the deterministic measure μ(B) = 𝔼[N(B)], where 𝔼 is expectation under ℙ. For a rectangle (a,b] × H, with 0 ≤ a ≤ b ≤ 6 in min and H a subset of the categories, we calculate the duration and then the probability of a selected category. szwzhc ehmrfd izctrq wksuhv rcmvjh svgtoy hjby

    μ((a,b]×H)=λ(b−a)∑c∈Hpc

    This formula comes from the mean number λ(b − a) of arrivals in the interval, each of which has the given probability of carrying a selected label. The measure μ is additive because expectation is additive, with countable sums justified by monotone convergence for positive masses. Its additivity does not require independence of the counts.

  5. Calculate the intensities and check by regrouping

    The rate for fragile parcels is λp_F = (3/2) × (2/3) = 1 parcels/min. The rate for ordinary parcels is λp_O = 1/2 parcels/min, or 0.50 parcels/min. Check: these rates add up to 1.50 parcels/min.

    A has a duration of 3 − 0 = 3 min. Multiply the rate for fragile parcels by this duration: μ(A) = 1 × 3 = 3 parcels on average. D has a duration of 6 − 3 = 3 min; μ(D) = 3 parcels on average. Yet the realization has N(A) = 2 and N(D) = 2.

    First check: μ(A ∪ D) = 3 + 3 = 6 parcels. zxmlaq nfcvyy tjbqco xcqtur zzpsrk lvlhzc vrzs For all categories, μ(E) = 1.50 × 6 = 9 parcels; N(E) = 6 is not equal to it in our realization.

    The variables N(A) and N(D) are independent under the Poisson model because the regions are disjoint. The fact that their observed values are both 2 neither proves nor refutes independence: independence concerns their joint distribution, rather than an equality in a single list.

  6. Correct the calculation when regions overlap

    Add B = (2, 5] × {F, O}. It selects the arrivals at 3, 4, and 5 min: N(B) = 3 parcels. The arrival at 2 min is excluded. A ∩ B = (2, 3] × {F} contains the arrival at 3 min: N(A ∩ B) = 1 parcels.

    Adding N(A) and N(B) would give 5, because the shared parcel would be counted twice. For the union, subtract it once: N(A ∪ B) = 2 + 3 − 1 = 4 parcels. Direct check: the distinct selected times are 0.5, 3, 4, and 5 min.

    The duration of B is 5 − 2 = 3 min. Since all categories are selected, μ(B) = 3/2 × 3 = 9/2 parcels, or 4.50 parcels on average. The overlap has a duration of 1 min and selects only F: μ(A ∩ B) = 1 parcels. Apply the same subtraction to the intensity.

    μ(A∪B)=3+4.50−1=6.50 colis en moyenne

    In other words, 3 + 4.50 = 7.50, then 7.50 − 1 = 6.50 parcels on average. This number differs from the count 4. The sets A and B share a random Poisson component with strictly positive intensity; their counts are not independent, unlike those of A and D.

  7. Connect to compensation while keeping positivity in view

    On our finite time horizon, define Ñ(B) = N(B) − μ(B). The tilde indicates the centered count; we do not change the arrivals. For each fixed region, its expectation is 0. In the realization: Ñ(A) = 2 − 3 = -1 parcels; Ñ(D) = 2 − 3 = -1 parcels.

    Additivity check: Ñ(A ∪ D) = 4 − 6 = -2 parcels, just as -1 + -1 = -2. Compensation from the previous unit therefore extends to regions of times and categories. However, Ñ is no longer a positive counting measure: a mass can be negative. On this space of finite intensity, it is a random signed measure, the difference of two measures that are finite almost surely.

    We stop at the mass assigned to a region. Adding contributions weighted by a function and constructing an integral with respect to jumps will belong to the next unit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A fractional intensity is a piece of an observed parcel.”

    μ(B) = 4.50 is a model mean, while N(B) = 3 is an integer in this realization. First state which question you are addressing: counting this list or describing the distribution before observation.

    Review the two readings of the partition

  • “I can always add the counts to obtain the count of the union.”

    A and B share the parcel at 3 min. Subtract the count of the intersection once, or partition the union into disjoint regions. The same correction applies to μ.

    Repeat the calculation with overlap

  • “Two disjoint regions must have independent counts.”

    Additivity comes from the definition of a measure. Here independence comes from the Poisson model with independent marking. In the final exercise, a label shared by all arrivals preserves additivity while making the counts of disjoint regions dependent.

    Test the model with a shared label

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do you obtain by fixing the outcome, then by fixing the region?

For a fixed outcome ω, N_ω is a measure as the region varies: null mass on the empty set and countable additivity on disjoint regions. For a fixed region B, ω ↦ N_ω(B) is a measurable random variable. These two requirements define our positive random measure.

Recall 2How do you construct the count of a region from marked arrivals?

Place a Dirac mass 1 at each time–category pair. For a region, add the indicators of membership of these pairs. The result counts the selected arrivals without using their mean rate.

Recall 3What does μ(B) mean, and why can it be fractional?

μ(B) = 𝔼[N(B)] is the mean number assigned to the region by the model. For a rectangle, multiply the arrival rate, its duration, and the probability of the selected categories. A mean can be fractional even though each realized count is an integer.

Recall 4What does the Poisson model add to the definition of a random measure?

Each region count has a Poisson distribution with parameter given by its intensity, and the counts of disjoint regions are mutually independent. Additivity alone provides neither of these two probability properties.

Recall 5What precautions are needed for a union with overlap and for a compensated count?

Subtract the mass of the intersection once, for both the count and the intensity. On our finite time horizon, Ñ = N − μ remains additive but can be negative: it is a random signed measure, rather than a positive counting measure.
