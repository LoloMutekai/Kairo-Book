Level 1 · C012·M3

Quantifiers

Your goalDistinguish “all” from “at least one”, choose the appropriate proof and give the exact negation of each statement about the same batch of boxes.

Before you start
c004-m5 : rational equations · c006-m5 : composition · c012-m1 : connectives · c012-m2 : sets

✿01 See the idea

You check a batch of lunchboxes before putting them away. A, B and C are closed; D is open. You can say “at least one box is closed”, but not “all the boxes are closed”. The same batch makes the first statement true and the second false.

Saying “all” makes a claim about every box in the batch. One open box is enough to disprove this claim: D is a counterexample, meaning an object that fails to have the required property. Saying “at least one” only requires you to identify a suitable box: A is a witness. B or C would also work; “at least one” does not mean “exactly one”.

Before continuing, predict the negation of “all the boxes are closed”. Must you open the whole batch, or is finding an exception enough? Keep your answer, then compare it with the diagram.

The scene and quantities are fictional. The domain is the specific batch we are discussing: U contains A, B, C and D, with no other boxes. We assume it is finite and nonempty. Each box’s state is known and stays fixed during a check; here, a box is either closed or open. These words say nothing about whether it is leakproof or safe for food.

The rule to remember

Fix the domain, then the property: “for all” requires every member; “there exists” requires a witness. To negate the entire statement, exchange these expressions and negate the property while keeping the same domain.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From counting boxes to exact negation

In the fictional batch U = {A, B, C, D}, A, B and C are closed, and D is open. We therefore know there are 4 boxes in total and 3 closed boxes. Let us check the claims and their negations without changing the domain.

  1. Give each symbol a meaning

    U is the set of boxes in the batch. x is an arbitrary member of U; x ∈ U means “x belongs to U”. The letter x denotes a box, not its number or a measured quantity. P(x) is the property “box x is closed”. It is a statement that becomes true or false when we choose the box: P(A) is true and P(D) is false.

    n denotes the total number of boxes, f the number of closed boxes and o the number of open boxes. These are counts of boxes, with no physical unit. Their values remain integers. Each box is counted only once; with our mutually exclusive and exhaustive states, f + o = n. The letters in U and the logical symbols have no unit.

  2. Count the exceptions and check the total

    The boxes not included in the count of closed boxes are exactly the open ones. We therefore subtract f from the total n to obtain o, rather than adding a new quantity.

    o=n−f=4−3=1 boîte ouverte

    Check by recounting: 3 + 1 = 4 boxes, which is the entire batch. Check by naming them: the list of closed boxes is {A, B, C} and the list of open boxes is {D}. No name is omitted or counted in both lists.

  3. Read “for all” and “there exists”

    The symbol ∀ is read “for all” or “for every”. In the following formula, it applies to P(x) for each member of U:

    ∀x∈U,P(x)

    This statement says “all the boxes in U are closed”. For this fully described batch, it is true exactly when f = n. Here 3 ≠ 4; it is false, and D explains why it fails. No majority is enough to establish “all”.

    The symbol ∃ is read “there exists at least one”. It requires a member of the same domain for which the property is true:

    ∃x∈U,P(x)

    This statement says “at least one box in U is closed”. It is true exactly when f ≥ 1 box. Here 3 ≥ 1; A is an explicit witness. The sign ≥ means “greater than or equal to”. It allows several witnesses.

  4. Negate the claim about all the boxes

    The sign ¬ means “it is not true that”. The brackets delimit the entire statement being negated; they do not change the domain. The sign ⇔ is read “is equivalent to”: the statements on either side have the same truth value in every state of the batch.

    ¬[∀x∈U,P(x)]⇔∃x∈U,¬P(x)

    In other words: “not all closed” is equivalent to “at least one is not closed”. Under our convention of closed or open, this is “at least one is open”. The expression ¬P(x) negates only the property of x.

    Since D is open, 1 box is enough to establish this negation. A, B and C remain closed: the negation never requires reversing all the states. It indicates that the overall claim fails.

  5. Negate the existence of a closed box

    To make “there exists a closed box” false, every possible witness must be ruled out. Finding an open box is not enough, since another could be closed.

    ¬[∃x∈U,P(x)]⇔∀x∈U,¬P(x)

    The exact negation is therefore “no box in U is closed”, or “all the boxes in U are open” in this scene. Numerically, we would need f = 0 closed boxes. Initially, f = 3 boxes: this negation is false. A disproves it on its own.

  6. Check the negations by changing the states

    Now open A, B and C, leaving D open. The domain keeps its 4 boxes. The count of closed boxes becomes 3 − 3 = 0 boxes; the count of open boxes is 4 − 0 = 4 boxes. Each state is still known.

    “At least one closed” is false this time; “none closed” is true. “All closed” remains false; “at least one open” is true. The truth values do reverse between each statement and its own negation. They do not necessarily reverse between “all” and “at least one”: those statements can both be false.

    To establish “all closed” from individual inspections alone, with no other information, you would need to inspect every member and find no exception. For existence, you can stop as soon as a witness is found. If some boxes remain hidden and no witness has yet been seen, existence still needs to be checked; this lack of information is not proof that it is false.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the formulas. Start again from the names A, B, C, D: which box establishes existence, which refutes “all”, and why does “none closed” require more than an open box? Then rewrite the negations while keeping U.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Not all closed means none closed.”

    Keep the initial batch in front of you: D is open, A remains closed. The statement “all closed” is false without “none closed” being true. Correct it by writing “at least one box in the batch is not closed”.

    Reread “Negate the claim about all the boxes”

  • ““There exists a closed box” requires that box to be the only one.”

    A provides a witness, even if B and C are also closed. Read ∃ as “at least one”; add no condition on the number of other closed boxes.

    Review witness A in the same batch

  • “One closed box proves that all the boxes are closed.”

    First specify “all the boxes in U”. Observing A says nothing about D’s state. Existence has its witness; for a universal claim about this batch described by inspection, you must cover every member. Do not move from the batch to all the boxes in the world.

    Try the case where D remains hidden

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · read and justify

Which object provides the proof?

Return to the initial batch: A, B and C closed, D open. Translate ∃x∈U, P(x) and ∀x∈U, P(x). Give each statement’s truth value, a witness for the existential statement and a counterexample for the universal statement. Finally, write the negation of the existential statement.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

P means “is closed”. First read the quantifier aloud.

Hint 2

For the negation of existence, no box must be able to serve as a witness.

Compare with the solution

∃x∈U, P(x) says “at least one box in U is closed”. This is true; A is a witness, and B or C could also serve as a witness. Identifying A is enough without claiming it is the only one.

∀x∈U, P(x) says “all the boxes in U are closed”. This is false; D, a member of U that is open, is a counterexample.

Negating existence gives “no box in U is closed”, that is, ∀x∈U, ¬P(x). This last statement is false in the initial batch, since A is closed. “At least one open” would be the negation of the other statement, the one that said “all closed”.

Check your reasoning

  • I keep U in the translations and the negation.
  • I name an object and its state for each proof.
  • I do not confuse “at least one” with “exactly one”.

Exercise 2 · Application · count, then conclude

Make the whole batch closed

Initially, 3 boxes out of 4 are closed. You can close an open box without touching the others. What is the minimum number of boxes you must close to make “all closed” true? Name the box, recalculate the counts, then state whether “at least one closed” and “not all closed” are true after this action.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The boxes preventing “all” are those not already closed.

Hint 2

Subtract the count of closed boxes from the total, then add what has just been closed.

Compare with the solution

There is 4 − 3 = 1 box left to close: D. Without this action, D would remain a counterexample; the action is therefore necessary, and it is sufficient since A, B and C are already closed.

After closing: 3 + 1 = 4 closed boxes; 4 − 4 = 0 open boxes. The domain keeps 4 members. The count of closed boxes equals the total and each state confirms it: “all closed” is true.

“At least one closed” is also true: A remains a witness. “Not all closed” is false, because there is no longer an open box. The universal and existential statements can therefore both be true in this nonempty batch.

Check your reasoning

  • I justify the minimum by identifying the exception that must be eliminated, and I name D.
  • I give 4 closed boxes and 0 open boxes, with their counting unit.
  • I distinguish the universal statement from its negation and keep a witness for existence.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · reason with an incomplete observation

D remains hidden

The batch still contains exactly A, B, C and D. You observe A, B and C closed, but you do not know D’s current state. One person says: “All are closed; I have seen enough.” Another says: “Not all closed, since D is hidden.” What can you establish? Construct the cases D closed and D open, then discuss “all closed”, its negation and “at least one closed”.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A hidden box is declared neither open nor closed. Keep the known observations and try each possible state of D.

Hint 2

Look for a claim true in both cases, then the claims whose verdict changes.

Compare with the solution

If D is closed, there are 4 closed boxes and 0 open boxes: “all closed” is true and its negation false. If D is open, there are again 3 closed boxes and 1 open box: “all closed” is false and its negation true.

Both states are compatible with what you observe. You therefore cannot yet establish either the universal claim or its negation. Each has a truth value in the actual state; your information does not allow you to choose the right case. Observing D would settle it.

However, “at least one closed” is already established by A, in both cases. The negation “none closed” is already refuted by A. Uncertainty about one member does not erase an observed witness. Both people claim more than their observations allow.

Check your reasoning

  • I construct each state of D without changing A, B, C or the domain.
  • I distinguish a false verdict from a verdict I cannot yet establish.
  • I justify existence using A and explain why observing D resolves the other question.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be fixed before saying “all the boxes”?

The domain U: here exactly A, B, C and D, along with the property “is closed” and the time of the check. A verdict about this batch does not concern all the boxes in the world.

Recall 2What does a closed box in U prove? Must it be the only one?

It is a witness for ∃x∈U, P(x), “at least one box in U is closed”. The statement does not require a unique witness and does not prove that every other box is closed.

Recall 3Negate “all the boxes in U are closed” and justify using the initial batch.

There exists a box in U that is not closed: ∃x∈U, ¬P(x). D is open and is enough. A, B and C can remain closed; “not all” does not mean “none”.

Recall 4Negate “at least one box in U is closed”. Why is one open box not enough to prove the negation?

Every box in U must have the property “not closed”: ∀x∈U, ¬P(x), meaning none closed. One open box leaves open the possibility that another is closed. Every potential witness must be ruled out.

Recall 5A, B and C are closed, D hidden: which verdicts can you establish?

The existence of a closed box is true thanks to A and “none closed” is false. “All closed” and its negation cannot yet be distinguished by these observations; knowing D’s state would settle it.
