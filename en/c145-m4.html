Level 2 · C145·M4

Simultaneous equations

Your goalSolve jointly for the price and quantity in a market, then distinguish a slope between equilibria from a response of demand to price.

Before you start
c074-m5 : identification assumptions · c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c101-m5 : rolling-origin forecasting · c145-m1 : endogeneity · c145-m2 : instrumental variables · c145-m3 : fixed effects

✿01 See the idea

At a neighbourhood market, people trade baskets containing the same selection of vegetables. At a higher price, buyers want fewer baskets; sellers offer more. The price at which the market clears therefore depends on decisions on both sides of the market.

We call the price of a basket p and the quantity traded during a market session Q. In our model, the price settles where quantity demanded and quantity supplied coincide. Point E in the diagram satisfies both rules at the same price. Solving simultaneous equations means finding this compatible pair, without first fixing either p or Q arbitrarily.

Now imagine that more customers want these baskets at every price. Supply stays the same: the new equilibrium may have both a higher price and a higher quantity. This does not mean that making a basket more expensive makes people want to buy more. The observed points belong to different demand curves.

The entire scene and all the values are fictional. We assume a market that clears, with no rationing, inventory carried over or price ceiling. This local model describes divisible average quantities; it explains neither the time needed to adjust nor individual trades. The rules are chosen for calculations with pencil and paper.

The rule to remember

Write down the rules for buyers and sellers, set them equal, then check the resulting pair in each rule. Before interpreting a slope between markets, ask which rule changed.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The price of baskets is not an independent input

Demand is D = 90 − 2p + u and supply is S = 15 + 3p + v. Initially, u = v = 0 baskets per market session. Let us find p and Q, then repeat with u = 10 and v = 0. The parameters describe the same type of basket and the same market session length.

  1. Define the rules and their units

    D is quantity demanded and S is quantity supplied, in baskets per market session. At equilibrium, Q = D = S is the quantity traded. p is the price of a basket, in euros. a and c are the intercepts, in baskets per market session: they give the theoretical quantities at a price of 0 on these straight lines. We do not claim that these extreme values describe a real market.

    b and d are positive, in baskets per market session per euro. With the shock held fixed, an increase in p of 1 € reduces D by b baskets and increases S by d baskets per market session. The signs in front of bp and dp indicate the direction of the response. Here a = 90, b = 2, c = 15 and d = 3 in the stated units.

    u is a shift in demand and v a shift in supply, in baskets per market session. A positive shock adds the same quantity at every price, without changing the slope. These shocks are held fixed during a market session; the parameters a, b, c and d remain fixed across our scenarios. The symbols D and S denote planned purchases and sales, which may differ away from equilibrium.

    D=a−bp+u;S=c+dp+v;Q=D=S

  2. Eliminate an unknown to solve the system

    The equations are called structural because they represent the separate behaviour of buyers and sellers here. Equating D and S eliminates Q. Adding bp and subtracting c and v on each side brings the price terms together without changing the equality.

    a−bp+u=c+dp+v⇒(b+d)p=a−c+u−v
    p=a−c+u−vb+d;Q=c+da−c+u−vb+d+v

    These expressions form a reduced form: the unknowns p and Q are written in terms of the parameters and shocks, with no other unknown on the right. Since b + d is strictly positive, division gives a unique price. We must still check that price and quantity are admissible; a negative algebraic solution would not describe our scene. This solution tells us nothing about the speed of adjustment.

  3. Calculate the initial price

    With the shocks at 0, the difference between the intercepts is 90 − 15 = 75 baskets per market session. The sum of the responses to price is 2 + 3 = 5 baskets per market session per euro. Dividing a quantity by a response per euro gives euros.

    p=90−152+3=755=15 € par panier

    The denominator is a sum: when price rises, demand falls while supply rises. These two responses together close the gap between them.

  4. Recover the quantity and check both sides

    On the buyers’ side, bp = 2 × 15 = 30 baskets per market session. Demand is 90 − 30 = 60 baskets per market session. On the sellers’ side, dp = 3 × 15 = 45; supply is 15 + 45 = 60 baskets per market session.

    The gap D − S is 60 − 60 = 0 baskets per market session: the same pair satisfies both equations. Price is positive, as is quantity. The pair is therefore admissible in the stated model.

    A check using another elimination is to multiply Q = a − bp by d and Q = c + dp by b, then add: the terms in p cancel. We obtain Q = (da + bc)/(b + d) with the shocks at 0. This other route recovers 60 baskets per market session. The calculation file also checks this route in the shifted scenarios.

  5. Shift demand, then solve again

    Now set u = 10 baskets per market session and v = 0. At every price, demand becomes 90 + 10 − 2p = 100 − 2p. Supply keeps exactly the initial rule. We therefore need to calculate a new price rather than keep the initial market price.

    p=100−155=855=17 € par panier

    At this price, demand is 100 − 2 × 17 = 100 − 34 = 66 baskets per market session. Supply is 15 + 3 × 17 = 15 + 51 = 66 baskets per market session. Their equality checks the second equilibrium.

  6. Interpret the slope between equilibria

    Let Δp denote the price in the shifted market minus the initial price, and ΔQ the corresponding difference in quantities. Here the symbol Δ means “change between the two scenarios”, without assuming a derivative. We have Δp = 17 − 15 = 2 € per basket and ΔQ = 66 − 60 = 6 baskets per market session.

    ΔQΔp=62=3=d

    This ratio is in baskets per market session per euro. It recovers the supply slope because only the demand shock changed: both equilibria remain on the same supply curve. The demand slope with u fixed is −b = -2, in the same unit. A regression through our two points would have the positive slope 3; calling it “the effect of price on demand” would reverse the direction of the mechanism.

  7. See how simultaneity creates endogeneity

    The reduced form for p contains u, while u is precisely the unobserved term in the demand equation when we regress Q on p. Price is therefore not, in general, an input independent of the error in this equation. This link comes from the joint determination of p and Q; it does not require a sequence in which Q causes p afterwards.

    To make the probabilistic argument precise, imagine repeated markets whose shocks u and v have mean 0, finite variances and no correlation with each other, with a strictly positive variance for u. The parameters remain constant. Cov denotes covariance, the mean product of deviations from the respective means; Var denotes variance, the mean squared deviation from the mean.

    Cov(p,u)=Var(u)−Cov(v,u)b+d=Var(u)b+d>0

    The covariance between p and u is expressed in euros multiplied by baskets per market session; Var(u) is in squared baskets per market session. Division by b + d makes these units compatible. A simple demand regression therefore fails the requirement that price and error be uncorrelated here. Giving the shocks mean 0 is not enough: their relationship to price is what matters. The two numerical scenarios illustrate the mechanism without forming a sample that would verify the probabilistic assumptions.

  8. Separate solving from identification

    We solved the system because its rules were given. Whether observations allow us to distinguish the parameters of these rules is the identification problem; their numerical estimation comes afterwards. The same pair can belong to several demand curves and several supply curves: with the shocks at 0, choosing positive slope parameters b and d and then setting a = Q + bp and c = Q − dp is enough to make their lines pass through that pair.

    One route to identifying demand is a source of variation that shifts supply without entering demand directly, remains independent of u and actually changes p. These are the requirements for an instrumental variable, studied earlier: naming an external factor does not prove its exclusion from demand. Here we know the shocks by construction; with observational data, this knowledge and the stability of the rules would need to be justified.

    This page stops at solving the system and recognising this difficulty. Calculating an instrumental variable estimator, working with panels and evaluating a policy require their own assumptions and their own work. Reducing estimation uncertainty does not replace identifying an effect.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Why do we add b and d to find the price? Why does the slope between our shifted markets recover supply, even though the initial change comes from demand? Then distinguish solving known rules from learning those rules from observed points.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I choose the price, then supply or demand gives the market quantity.”

    An arbitrary price produces planned purchases and sales that may differ. First find where they are equal, then substitute the price into both equations. The first exercise shows exactly what fails when only one side is checked.

    Revisit the elimination and the check on both sides

  • “Price and sales rose together, so demand rises with price.”

    We shifted the entire demand curve, then found another equilibrium on the same supply curve. The ratio 3 describes this movement between equilibria; the demand slope with u fixed remains -2 baskets per market session per euro. State what is held constant before interpreting a slope.

    Compare the two equilibria in the diagram

  • “The system has a unique solution, so the observations are enough to estimate its rules.”

    Uniqueness concerns p and Q when the parameters are given. Identification concerns the parameters when we see only the equilibria. Several rules can pass through the same point. A regression or corrected standard errors do not, by themselves, provide the required external variation.

    Reread the distinction between solving and identification

Fewer baskets offered at every price

Start again from the initial market. This time, demand stays unchanged and supply becomes S = (15 − 5) + 3p: it loses 5 baskets per market session at every price. Predict the direction of change in price and quantity, solve the system, check each equation, then calculate ΔQ/Δp relative to the initial equilibrium.

Hint 1

Replace only c with 10. Lower supply must meet lower demand at a higher price.

Hint 2

The denominator b + d does not change. After finding the price, calculate the quantity on both sides.

Compare with the solution

Price rises and quantity falls: demand is unchanged and slopes downwards. The new supply intercept is 15 − 5 = 10 baskets per market session. Equating the two sides gives (2 + 3)p = 90 − 10 = 80, so p = 80/5 = 16 € per basket.

Demand: 90 − 2 × 16 = 90 − 32 = 58 baskets per market session. Supply: 10 + 3 × 16 = 10 + 48 = 58 baskets per market session. Equality and positivity check the solution.

Δp = 16 − 15 = 1 € per basket; ΔQ = 58 − 60 = -2 baskets per market session. The ratio is -2 baskets per market session per euro: this time we recover the demand slope, because its parameters and its shock remained fixed. This interpretation depends on the absence of a change in demand assumed in the question.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

When both rules shift

Starting from the initial state, now take u = 10 and v = 5 baskets per market session, both positive. Demand and supply shift. Find the new equilibrium and the ratio ΔQ/Δp relative to the initial state. Someone calls this ratio “the estimated demand slope”. Respond with the calculation and an assumption that would be needed to identify this demand.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The demand intercept becomes 100; the supply intercept becomes 20. The slope parameters b and d stay the same.

Hint 2

Subtract the new constant terms, then use b + d. The comparison must start from the initial equilibrium.

Hint 3

Ask what change in price could occur without shifting demand directly.

Compare with the solution

The price is (100 − 20)/5 = 80/5 = 16 € per basket. It equals the price in the previous exercise, so we can reuse the products bp = 32 and dp = 48, while changing the intercepts.

Demand: 100 − 32 = 68 baskets per market session. Supply: 20 + 48 = 68 baskets per market session. Both rules give the same positive quantity.

Relative to the initial market, Δp = 16 − 15 = 1 € per basket and ΔQ = 68 − 60 = 8 baskets per market session. The ratio is 8, in baskets per market session per euro. It matches neither the demand slope -2 nor the supply slope 3.

Both lines have changed: joining their intersections does not follow either fixed structural rule. To identify demand, we would need, for example, to justify a source of variation that shifts supply, changes price and remains excluded from demand and independent of u. The validity of these conditions cannot be inferred from the price–quantity pair alone, even when it is calculated perfectly.

Check your reasoning

  • I recover 16 € and 68 baskets per market session in both equations.
  • I compare with the initial market and find the slope 8 with its unit.
  • I distinguish a shift in both rules from a response to varying price along a fixed rule.
  • I name the requirements for the external variation without claiming their validity has been demonstrated.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must a price–quantity pair satisfy in our simultaneous system?

At the same price and with the same shocks, quantity demanded and quantity supplied must equal the quantity traded: Q = D = S. Calculating only one side does not check the system.

Recall 2How can we recover the price, and why is its denominator a sum?

Equating a − bp + u and c + dp + v, then collecting terms, gives p = (a − c + u − v)/(b + d). When price rises, demand falls and supply rises: the responses combine to close the gap. Positive b and d ensure a positive denominator.

Recall 3Why does a shift in demand alone recover the supply slope between equilibria?

Supply and its shock remain fixed: successive equilibria lie on the same supply curve. The ratio ΔQ/Δp recovers d, rather than the slope −b of demand with its shock held fixed. If both rules shift, the ratio generally recovers neither slope.

Recall 4Which term makes price endogenous in a demand regression?

The demand shock u enters both the equilibrium price and the error in the demand equation. With uncorrelated shocks of mean 0 and positive Var(u), Cov(p,u) = Var(u)/(b + d) is positive. A mean of 0 for u does not ensure that it is uncorrelated with p.

Recall 5Does a unique solution to the system prove that its parameters are identifiable?

No. Solving determines p and Q from known rules; identification establishes whether their parameters can be distinguished from observations. Several rules can share the same equilibria. Valid external variation or other justified restrictions are needed to distinguish the effects.
