Trees

Your goalBuild a split in a classification tree, justify its choice using weighted impurity, and follow a parcel to its leaf.

Before you start
c099-m5 : probability calibration · c100-m5 : learning curves

✿01 See the idea

At a shipping counter, we want to predict whether a parcel will need reinforced packaging. We have its mass before packing and past requirements recorded after inspection. R means “reinforce”; O means “ordinary packaging”. These letters are the classes to predict, rather than answers already known for future parcels.

A decision tree resembles a path through questions. The root receives all the parcels. A decision node asks a question about an available feature, here mass; each branch receives an answer. A leaf ends the path and gives a common prediction for the parcels that reach it. Here, the majority class of its training examples provides this prediction.

The question “mass at most 3 kg?” gathers the lighter parcels into a group where the need for reinforcement is uniform. The rest remain mixed. Training means choosing questions from labeled examples; prediction then means following the fixed questions without knowing what the new parcel actually needs.

The entire scene and its data are fictional. Mass is our only feature: we do not claim that it actually explains packaging requirements. We build a tree limited to a single question on each path, then examine what this limit protects against and what it leaves imperfect.

The rule to remember

Separate the examples with a question, measure the remaining mixture while accounting for group sizes, then predict in the leaf; evaluate future parcels using examples kept out of the tree selection process.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Choose the question at the counter

Using parcels A through H, let us compare only the thresholds 3 kg and 4 kg. A yes answer sends a parcel left, a no answer right. Every parcel has the same weight. The maximum depth is set to 1 decision per path; we also require at least 2 examples per leaf. These choices deliberately limit our exercise.

  1. Name what we are learning

    m denotes a parcel’s mass, in kg; y denotes its recorded requirement, R or O, with no physical unit. The threshold θ, the Greek letter theta, is in kg. The rule m ≤ θ belongs to the learned model; y is used to choose this rule on the training batch and remains unknown when making a new prediction. We assume that masses are available, labels are supplied, and no values are missing.

    A node t is a group of parcels reached by the same path. n denotes its size, counted in parcels; nᴿ and nᴼ denote its counts by class. p = nᴿ/n and q = nᴼ/n are unitless proportions, with p + q = 1. We calculate these proportions only in a nonempty group. At the root, we count 4 R and 4 O, so n = 8, p = 1/2 and q = 1/2.

  2. Measure the mixture before splitting

    Gini impurity G(t) measures the mixture of classes in node t. It is unitless. With our classes R and O, it equals:

    G(t)=1−p2−q2=2pq

    The second expression comes from (p + q) squared = p × p + 2pq + q × q = 1. A single class gives G = 0; an equal division gives the binary maximum 0.50. Another interpretation helps us check: draw 2 labels independently from the group, with replacement; G is the probability that they differ, R then O or O then R. It is not the probability that our prediction is wrong.

    At the root: p × q = 1/4, then G = 2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/2 = 0.50. This calculation sets the comparison point before the question.

  3. Divide the parcels at the chosen threshold

    For θ = 3 kg, A, B and C answer yes: n_g = 3 parcels, with 3 R and 0 O. The symbols g and d will denote left and right. We obtain p_g = 1, q_g = 0, p_gq_g = 0, so G_g = 0: all the examples in this leaf need reinforcement.

    D, E, F, G and H answer no: n_d = 5 parcels, with 1 R and 4 O. Divide each count by the size of THIS group: p_d = 1/5, q_d = 4/5. Their product is 4/25 and G_d = 2 × 4/25 = 8/25 = 0.32.

    Partition check: 3 + 5 = 8 parcels; the R counts give 3 + 1 = 4 and the O counts give 0 + 4 = 4. No parcel should disappear or be counted in both branches.

  4. Weight the remaining mixture

    J(θ) denotes the impurity after the split. ΔG, read as “change in G”, is its reduction relative to the parent. G_g and G_d are the impurities of the child groups. To represent a parcel chosen uniformly from the parent, each group must be weighted by its size divided by n. All these ratios, J and ΔG are unitless.

    J(θ)=ngnGg+ndnGd

    The weights are 3/8 and 5/8 and their sum is 1. The left contributes 3/8 × 0 = 0; the right 5/8 × 8/25 = 1/5. Thus J = 1/5 = 0.20.

    ΔG=G(t)−J(θ)=0.50−0.20=0.30

    The reduction is therefore 0.30 impurity units. This is a selection criterion on these fictional examples, rather than a measured gain in success on new parcels.

  5. Compare the competing split

    At threshold θ = 4 kg, D moves to the left. A through D form 4 parcels with 3 R and 1 O: p = 3/4, q = 1/4, pq = 3/16 and G = 3/8. E through H form 4 parcels with 1 R and 3 O. Swapping p and q preserves their product: their impurity is also 3/8.

    The weights are each 1/2; each contribution is 3/16. We find J = 3/16 + 3/16 = 3/8 = 0.375, then ΔG = 1/8 = 0.125. The 3 kg threshold leaves less mixture: 0.200 < 0.375. Both splits meet the minimum size; we therefore select 3 kg from these candidates alone.

    We have not searched all thresholds or all possible trees. More complete training repeats the comparison at each permitted node, with a stopping rule and a rule for breaking ties. This local search does not guarantee the globally optimal tree. Here, the fixed depth already stops the path after this question.

  6. Predict, check, and keep the right limits

    In the selected tree, the left predicts R because 3 > 0, and the right predicts O because 4 > 1. If the class counts were tied, our convention would be to predict O; it must be fixed before evaluation. A future parcel of 5 kg follows 5 > 3 and therefore receives O, without using its actual label. The O proportion in its leaf is 4/5 = 0.80: a training frequency whose calibration on future cases has not been demonstrated.

    Parcel E in the training batch follows exactly this path, but its recorded requirement is R. A leaf that predicts the majority class can therefore contain counterexamples. This also explains why a pure leaf does not guarantee the class of a new parcel.

    Independent check of G at the root: ordered pairs of different classes give 2 × 4 × 4 cases, out of 8 × 8 draws with replacement. The ratio recovers 1/2. On the right, the same count gives 2 × 1 × 4 / (5 × 5) = 8/25; weighting this ratio recovers J = 1/5.

    To assess generalization, meaning performance on other parcels, we would need a reserved batch used to choose neither the threshold nor the depth. Making the tree deeper can eventually isolate peculiarities of the batch: this is overfitting. Depth and minimum group size are complexity limits to tune on separate validation data; the final test stays apart. No future performance is measured on this page.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I take the simple average of the leaf impurities.”

    At the 3 kg threshold, this average would give (0 + 8/25)/2 = 0.16. But the groups differ in size. We need the weights 3/8 and 5/8, which give 0.20. Always check which population each weight refers to.

    Review the weighting of the mixture

  • “The O proportion in the leaf is my probability of being right.”

    0.80 describes the training examples that reached this leaf. This number can serve as an estimate, but using mass alone, a small batch, and the selection of the split itself can make it misleading. Check calibration on separate examples; distinguish leaf frequency, impurity, and future error.

    Review prediction and its limits

  • “If a new question lowers Gini impurity, I must add it.”

    A decrease on the training data may come from an isolated example. First check the depth and leaf size limits, then performance on the validation data. The extension exercise shows a local improvement that violates these limits.

    Try the forbidden extension

Compare with the solution

On the left, A through D: 4 parcels, 3 R and 1 O. On the right, E through H: 4 parcels, 1 R and 3 O. The left proportions are 3/4 and 1/4; those on the right are swapped.

Each G equals 2 × 3/16 = 3/8. The weights are 1/2; J = 3/16 + 3/16 = 0.375. The gain is 1/2 − 3/8 = 1/8. Since 0.375 > 0.200, the 4 kg candidate loses the comparison.

Groups of equal size each carry half the weight: the simple average then coincides with the weighted average. At the 3 kg threshold, the sizes 3 and 5 differ; treating them as equal changes the criterion.

Check your reasoning

  • No parcel lost or counted twice.
  • Proportions divided by the size of their own group.
  • Weights justified, J and the Gini reduction calculated exactly.
  • Choose from the compared candidates without claiming a global optimum.

  • Recover the exact local decrease and identify the group it concerns.
  • Identify both violated constraints.
  • Require a separate evaluation before drawing any conclusion about future cases.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How does a tree assign a class to a parcel that has not yet been inspected?

It tests its mass at the nodes along the path, follows the branches, and reaches a leaf. The majority class of that leaf’s training examples provides the prediction; the parcel’s actual requirement plays no part in the path.

Recall 2How can we recover Gini impurity for our classes R and O, and what does it measure?

With the group proportions p and q, p + q = 1, G = 1 − p × p − q × q = 2pq. It measures the mixture of classes, or the probability of drawing different classes with replacement. It is not the model’s future error.

Recall 3Why do group sizes matter when choosing a split?

Each child must contribute in proportion to the number of parcels from the parent that reach it. We add (n_g/n)G_g and (n_d/n)G_d; a simple average is justified only when group sizes are equal.

Recall 4What is the difference between choosing the question and using it?

During training, recorded requirements are used to compare the impurities of candidate splits. During prediction, the questions are fixed and only the information available about the new parcel is used to follow the path.

Recall 5Why stop a tree when it can still reduce the mixture in the training data?

Additional questions can isolate peculiarities of the batch. Limiting depth and leaf size restrains this complexity; comparing on separate validation data helps choose these limits. A pure leaf or an assisted answer proves neither mastery nor future performance.
