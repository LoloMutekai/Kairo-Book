Level 3 · C164·M2

Measures

Your goalConstruct a duration measure on a finite log, justify its countable additivity, and identify double counting.

Before you start
c143-m5 : uniform convergence · c058-m5 : change of variables · c012-m5 : counterexamples · c164-m1 : sigma-algebras

✿01 See the idea

A workshop keeps a fictional log of 4 scheduled jobs, named a, b, c, and d. Jobs a, b, and c occupy the same machine in succession for 2 min, 3 min, and 5 min. Job d is cancelled: its occupied time is 0 min. These values are invented for the calculation; they are not activity readings.

The manager selects records and wants to know the corresponding duration. A measure assigns a size to a set: here, the sum of the durations of its records. If the selections share no records, their durations add up. If a record belongs to both selections, simply adding their durations counts it twice.

Group A contains a and b; group B contains b and c. Picture the selected records before looking at the totals: should b occupy the machine twice because it has two labels? The second diagram separates the records by membership to make this pitfall visible.

Measuring depends on the chosen rule. A contains 2 records but represents 5 min. Counting and duration can both be measures; they answer different questions. Our universe contains records, rather than instants in time: we are not yet constructing a measure on a continuous line.

The rule to remember

Assign nonnegative weights to the records; add them over the selection. Additivity applies to disjoint sets, and the general definition requires countable unions.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Measuring selections from the log

In the fictional log, A = {a, b} and B = {b, c}. The durations are 2 min, 3 min, 5 min, and 0 min for a, b, c, and d respectively. Let us construct the measure μ, then calculate the duration of A ∪ B without duplicates.

  1. Specify what is measurable and what is being measured

    Ω = {a, b, c, d} is the universe of records. We choose 𝒜 = 𝒫(Ω), the set of all subsets of Ω: every selection is therefore measurable. A sigma-algebra, recalled from the previous unit, contains Ω and is closed under complements in Ω and countable unions. It specifies which selections can receive a measure; it does not yet assign them a size.

    S denotes a selection in 𝒜; ω denotes a record. The weight t, indexed by ω, is the duration of that record in minutes. For a, b, c, and d, the weights are 2 min, 3 min, 5 min, and 0 min respectively. All these weights are nonnegative. The completed jobs do not overlap in time: here, their sum represents occupied time, excluding breaks. Record labels and sets have no physical unit; μ(S) will be in minutes.

  2. State the definition, including countable additivity

    In general, a measure μ is a function from the sigma-algebra 𝒜 to [0, +∞]. It satisfies μ(∅) = 0 and the countable additivity condition below. ∅ is the empty set; +∞ is allowed for a general measure, even though all values in our log are finite.

    Consider a sequence En of measurable sets, indexed by integers n starting at 1. They are pairwise disjoint: sets corresponding to distinct indices have an empty intersection, so no record belongs to more than one of them. The symbol ∩ denotes intersection. The large union symbol ⋃ collects their elements; the sum symbol ∑ adds their measures.

    μ(⋃n≥1En)=∑n≥1μ(En)

    The series of nonnegative terms is the limit of the partial sums; this limit may be infinite. The definition requires this equality for every such sequence, beyond the few partitions we might test. Finite additivity follows by extending a finite family with empty sets. On an infinite universe, checking finite additivity alone is generally insufficient.

  3. Construct μ and prove that it is a measure

    For each selection S, add the weights of the records it contains. The symbol ω ∈ S means that record ω belongs to S.

    μ(S)=∑ω∈Stω

    The empty sum is 0 min, so μ(∅) = 0 min. Since the weights are nonnegative, μ(S) is too. In a sequence of pairwise disjoint selections, each record appears at most once: adding the sums for each selection therefore amounts to adding the same weights record by record.

    This is indeed a proof for every sequence: Ω contains only 4 records, so a disjoint family has at most 4 nonempty sets. After the last of these, the partial sums are constant. The countable equality reduces here to a finite sum. We have verified the axioms, going beyond the numerical example alone. The triple (Ω, 𝒜, μ) is then called a measure space.

  4. Calculate the durations of the groups

    A contains a and b. These singletons, the sets consisting of a single record, are disjoint. We therefore add their weights: μ(A) = 2 + 3 = 5 min. Similarly, μ(B) = 3 + 5 = 8 min.

    Counting would be another measure N, obtained by giving each record a weight of 1 record: N(A) = 1 + 1 = 2 records. There is no reason to confuse N(A) with μ(A). A measure is not required to have a total mass equal to 1; here μ(Ω) is 10 min.

  5. Remove double counting by partitioning into disjoint sets

    The intersection A ∩ B is {b}, with measure 3 min. The unadjusted sum μ(A) + μ(B) = 5 + 8 = 13 min counts b twice. This calculation does not satisfy the disjointness assumption.

    To fix this, B ∖ A denotes the records in B that are not in A: here {c}. B splits into {b} and {c}, which are disjoint; thus μ(B ∖ A) = 8 − 3 = 5 min. Then A ∪ B splits into A and B ∖ A, which are also disjoint. The symbol ∪ means union.

    μ(A∪B)=μ(A)+μ(B∖A)

    We obtain 5 + 5 = 10 min. Since all these measures are finite, we can also write μ(A ∪ B) = μ(A) + μ(B) − μ(A ∩ B): 13 − 3 = 10 min. Subtracting an infinite overlap would not be justified: ∞ − ∞ is undefined.

  6. Check using the records, then using the complement

    Direct check: the union contains a, b, and c exactly once. Their durations give 2 + 3 + 5 = 10 min. The whole of Ω adds d, with weight 0 min, so its duration is still 10 min. The total does not count the number of records.

    Another check: C = Ω ∖ A = {c, d} is the complement of A. By direct calculation, μ(C) = 5 + 0 = 5 min. Since Ω is the disjoint union of A and C, we recover μ(C) = μ(Ω) − μ(A) = 10 − 5 = 5 min. This difference is valid because the total is finite.

    Finally, μ(A) ≤ μ(Ω): including more records adds nonnegative weights. This property is called monotonicity. Be careful about the converse: {d} is nonempty even though μ({d}) = 0 min. A measure of 0 does not require a set to be empty.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, reconstruct the proof that the sum of the weights defines a measure. Then recover the duration of the union by naming the disjoint selections used, and explain why the cancelled record can remain in a set of measure 0.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “For a union, I always add the measures.”

    First check whether the sets are disjoint. A and B share b: their unadjusted sum is 13 min, whereas the union measures 10 min. Replace B with B ∖ A, or subtract the measure of the intersection exactly once when the measures are finite.

    Revisit the partition of the union into disjoint sets

  • “A measure must give the number of records.”

    State the weight and its unit. A weight of 1 record produces a count; the durations in the log produce minutes. The same A has 2 records and measures 5 min. Neither result replaces the other.

    Review the log and the weights assigned to its records

  • “A positive size and an empty set of size 0 are enough to define a measure.”

    Countable additivity is also required. Checking a few sets does not prove it; a single disjoint pair that fails additivity is enough to refute the property. The squared-duration exercise constructs this counterexample on the same log.

    Test the axioms in the counterexample exercise

Exercise 2 · Application with justification

Audit a reported total

Someone reports 13 min for A ∪ B because A lasts 5 min and B lasts 8 min. Find the correct result by splitting the union into disjoint sets, then by correcting the addition. Why is monotonicity alone insufficient to identify the error?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write A ∩ B, then B ∖ A, by listing the records.

Hint 2

Monotonicity compares the union with each of its subsets; by itself, it does not give the required equality.

Compare with the solution

A ∩ B = {b}, with duration 3 min, and B ∖ A = {c}, with duration 5 min. The disjoint union of A and {c} gives 5 + 5 = 10 min.

Correcting the proposed addition: 5 + 8 − 3 = 10 min. b had been counted twice, hence the single subtraction of 3 min.

The incorrect total of 13 min is greater than the measures of A and B: it passes these monotonicity comparisons. A necessary check is not a sufficient proof. Listing each record once or applying additivity to the correct partition settles the question.

  • Explicitly identify the intersection and the disjoint remainder.
  • Obtain 10 min by both methods, justifying each addition or subtraction.
  • Distinguish an inequality used for checking from an identity that determines the result.

Exercise 3 · Reasoned counterexample

Is a squared duration still a measure?

Keep the log and set T = μ(Ω) = 10 min. For every selection S, consider ν(S) = μ(S) × μ(S) / T, as written below. ν is read as “nu” and is not assumed to be a measure. Check its unit, its nonnegativity, and its value on the empty set. Then test additivity with A and C = Ω ∖ A: is testing this pair enough to reach a conclusion?

ν(S)=μ(S)2T
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

T is strictly positive. For additivity, compare ν(A) + ν(C) with ν(A ∪ C), rather than with μ(A ∪ C).

Hint 2

A and C are disjoint, their union is Ω, and their durations are each 5 min. Square before dividing.

Compare with the solution

The square of μ(S) is in minutes squared and T is in minutes, so ν(S) is in minutes. Since T = 10 min is positive and the numerator is a square, ν(S) ≥ 0 min. For the empty set, the square of 0 divided by T is 0 min. These checks pass.

ν(A) = 5 × 5 / 10 = 25/10 = 5/2 min, or 2.50 min. Similarly, ν(C) = 25/10 = 5/2 min. Their sum is 5 min.

For the union, ν(Ω) = 10 × 10 / 10 = 100/10 = 10 min. The difference between ν(Ω) and ν(A) + ν(C) is 5 min, which is strictly positive. The additivity equality fails on disjoint sets: ν is therefore not a measure.

This pair is enough to refute a property required for all families: extend A and C with empty sets to obtain a countable sequence. A pair that passed the test would not have been enough to prove that ν is a measure.

Check your reasoning

  • Check the unit and the empty set, then test additivity separately.
  • Compare 5 min with 10 min and recall that A ∩ C = ∅.
  • Explain why one failure is enough to refute the universal property.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be added to a sigma-algebra to obtain a measure space?

A function μ on its measurable sets, taking values in [0, +∞], 0 on the empty set and countably additive for pairwise disjoint families. The triple (Ω, 𝒜, μ) is then a measure space.

Recall 2Why does the sum of the weights in the log satisfy countable additivity?

A record belongs to at most one selection in a disjoint family. The same weights are therefore added exactly once. With 4 records, at most 4 selections are nonempty: the series reduces to a finite sum.

Recall 3How can we measure A ∪ B without counting their intersection twice?

Split the union into A and B ∖ A, which are disjoint. When the measures are finite, this also gives μ(A ∪ B) = μ(A) + μ(B) − μ(A ∩ B). In the log, the result is 10 min.

Recall 4Must a selection of measure 0 be empty?

No. The singleton {d} is nonempty and its duration weight is 0 min. The axiom μ(∅) = 0 requires only one direction of the implication.

Recall 5For T = μ(Ω) > 0 min, why is the nonnegativity of ν(S) = μ(S) × μ(S) / T insufficient to make it a measure?

Countable additivity is also required. Here A and its complement C are disjoint, but ν(A) + ν(C) = 5 min differs from ν(Ω) = 10 min. This single failure refutes the universal property.
