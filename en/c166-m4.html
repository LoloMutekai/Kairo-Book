Level 3 · C166·M4

Parseval

Your goalRecover the mean square of a vibration from its Fourier coefficients and justify Parseval’s identity with the correct normalization.

Before you start
c165-m5 : duality · c046-m5 : approximation error · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c166-m1 : Fourier series · c166-m2 : transform · c166-m3 : convolution

✿01 See the idea

A small platform vibrates about an offset position. We describe its displacement in millimetres over a period of 4 s. The curve shows where it is at each instant; its Fourier coefficients show how a constant offset and oscillations combine to produce that same curve.

Our question concerns the mean square of the displacement: we square each displacement, add continuously over the period, then divide by its duration. This quantity retains the contribution of the oscillations even when their positive and negative displacements cancel in the ordinary mean.

Parseval lets us calculate this total again using the coefficients. Orthogonal components produce no cross terms in the mean square; their contributions add. Changing the description therefore preserves the norm, provided we follow the normalization convention.

The vibration, its period and its amplitudes are fictional. The calculated quantity is in mm². It is sometimes called signal energy by mathematical convention; here it measures neither mechanical energy in joules nor physical power. We work with a continuous periodic function, without replacing the integral with sampled readings.

The rule to remember

With coefficients defined by an integral divided by the period, the mean square equals the sum of their squared moduli; the integral without this division is multiplied by the period.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The same total, in time and then in the coefficients

The platform follows x(t) = m + a cos(ωt) + b sin(ωt), with m = 2 mm, a = 3 mm, b = 4 mm and T = 4 s. Let us recover its mean square M and the integral E over one period, keeping these two quantities distinct.

  1. Set the convention before calculating

    t is time in seconds; T is the period, strictly positive, in seconds. x(t), m, a and b are expressed in millimetres. The angular frequency ω is positive, in radians per second: the phase ωt makes one full turn as t goes from 0 to T. cos and sin take this angle in radians. We do not need to evaluate ω to calculate a mean over one full turn.

    For an integer index k, positive, 0 or negative, define uk(t) = cos(kωt) + i sin(kωt). These functions are dimensionless. i is the imaginary unit, with i² = −1. A bar above a number denotes its conjugate; for z = p + iq with real p and q, |z|² = z times its conjugate = p² + q². The vertical bars denote the modulus, never the complex square z².

    ck=1T∫0Tx(t)uk(t)¯dt

    ck is the complex coefficient of x associated with uk; its unit is mm, because dividing by T cancels the seconds in the integral. We use the dot product ⟨f,g⟩ = (1/T)∫ f(t) times the conjugate of g(t) dt over the same period. For two functions f and g in mm, this product is in mm². The associated norm is denoted by ‖f‖: its square equals ⟨f,f⟩.

    M=‖x‖2=1T∫0T|x(t)|2dt

    M is the mean square, in mm². E denotes the integral without division by T: E = TM, in mm²·s. This choice of letters does not make E mechanical energy. For a real signal, |x(t)|² is simply x(t)².

  2. Understand why the contributions add

    ℓ is another integer index, dimensionless like k. The product of uk and the conjugate of uℓ is uk-ℓ. If k = ℓ, it is constantly 1; otherwise it makes a nonzero integer number of turns and its mean over the period is 0. We obtain ⟨uk,uℓ⟩ = 1 for k = ℓ and 0 for k different from ℓ. The family is orthonormal: each function has unit norm and two distinct functions have a 0 dot product.

    For a finite sum of these functions multiplied by their coefficients, expanding the squared modulus therefore leaves only the sum of the squared moduli of the coefficients. The cross terms cancel after integration; they are not 0 at every instant. This is the Pythagorean theorem for this dot product.

    For the general equality, we assume x is in L² over one period: it is a measurable function whose integral of the squared modulus is finite. Functions equal almost everywhere, meaning outside a set of times of measure 0, represent the same element of this space. The family of all uk, with integer k, is complete: its finite linear combinations approximate every such function in norm. Denoting by Sn the sum of ckuk for indices from −n to n, we have the following identity.

    ‖x‖2=∑k=−nn|ck|2+‖x−Sn‖2

    n is a nonnegative integer; Sn is in mm and its error is orthogonal to the retained functions, by the definition of ck. The identity first gives an inequality: the sum of squared moduli is at most M. Completeness makes ‖x − Sn‖ tend to 0 as n grows without bound; this gives Parseval. The sum over the integers means the limit of these symmetric sums.

    M=∑k∈ℤ|ck|2

    The completeness of the Fourier family is used here as a theorem; its proof is not given in this unit. The reasoning does not assume that the series converges at every point. Our vibration is a finite sum of continuous functions: no passage to an infinite sum is needed for its calculations.

  3. Move from real amplitudes to complex coefficients

    Let c+ denote the coefficient with index 1 and c- the coefficient with index −1. Since u0 = 1, u1 = cos(ωt) + i sin(ωt) and u-1 = cos(ωt) − i sin(ωt), the cosine and sine coefficients are obtained by adding and subtracting these expressions. This gives:

    c0=m;c+=a−ib2;c−=a+ib2

    The other ck equal 0 mm. Here c0 = 2 mm; c+ = (3/2 − 2i) mm and c- = (3/2 + 2i) mm. The imaginary part of c+ is -2 mm. Check by reconstruction: c+u1 + c-u-1 gives a cos(ωt) + b sin(ωt) again, because the imaginary parts cancel. The sign of b is correctly recovered in the sine term.

  4. Calculate the squared moduli and find the total

    The constant coefficient contributes m² = 2 × 2 = 4 mm². For c+, we add the square of the real part and the square of the imaginary part; we do not square the complex number itself.

    |c+|2=(3/2)2+(-2)2=9/4+4=25/4 mm²

    The conjugate c- has the same modulus. The pair therefore contributes 2 × (25/4) = 25/2 mm². Parseval adds the constant coefficient’s contribution to this pair:

    M=4+25/2=33/2 mm²=16.50 mm²

    The integral is E = TM = 4 s × (33/2 mm²) = 66 mm²·s. The duration factor changes the unit: 66 is therefore not another estimate of the same mean.

  5. Check by integrating over time

    Let us expand x(t)² before taking any mean. The square contains m², a²cos²(ωt), b²sin²(ωt), then the cross terms 2ma cos(ωt), 2mb sin(ωt) and 2ab cos(ωt)sin(ωt). Over a full period, the means of cos, sin and cos times sin are 0; those of cos² and sin² are 1/2. These identities follow from the double-angle trigonometric formulas.

    M=m2+a22+b22

    The squared amplitudes are 9 mm² and 16 mm². Their means contribute 9 ÷ 2 = 9/2 mm² and 16 ÷ 2 = 8 mm². In total: 4 + 9/2 + 8 = 33/2 mm², or 16.50 mm². The spectral method and this integration give a difference of 0 mm².

    The total is at least m², since the contributions of the oscillations are positive. If the amplitudes a and b were 0, it would reduce to m²; if we removed the offset, 25/2 mm² would remain. These checks apply to our convention and our full period. A different integration duration does not generally cancel the cross terms.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the formulas. Explain why the pair of opposite frequencies contributes twice, why the cross terms disappear after integration and why E and M have different units. Where does completeness enter when the signal is no longer a finite sum?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The sum of squared moduli directly gives the integral, whatever the convention.”

    Reread the definition of cₖ: it includes division by T. The sum gives M, in mm²; the integral E equals TM, in mm²·s. Check the units first, then the duration factor.

    Review the convention and the two quantities

  • “I can replace the squared modulus with the complex square and keep just one frequency.”

    For a number p + iq, the squared modulus is p² + q². In the platform’s real signal, opposite coefficients are conjugates, but each contributes its squared modulus. Count the whole pair or use the real formula with its factor of one half.

    Recalculate the coefficients and their contributions

  • “Orthonormal functions are always enough to obtain equality.”

    Orthonormality first gives a sum less than or equal to the squared norm. For every function in L², equality also requires a complete family. The identity involving the squared error shows exactly what remains to be explained before removing that term.

    Review the role of completeness

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Remove only the offset

The platform remains in the same scene. We now describe its displacement relative to its mean position: v(t) = x(t) − m. Calculate the mean square of v in mm², then justify the difference from that of x. Do not repeat a full integration.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The constant coefficient disappears; the coefficients of the oscillating pair stay the same.

Compare with the solution

The mean of v is 0 mm, but its mean square is nonzero. The constant coefficient is now 0 mm; the two squared moduli are still 25/4 mm². Their sum is 25/2 mm², or 12.50 mm².

The difference from M = 33/2 mm² is m² = 4 mm². We remove the square of the offset, rather than m itself: the mean square has units of mm². The same answer follows from (a² + b²)/2 = 25/2 = 25/2 mm².

Check your reasoning

  • Identify the only coefficient that changes.
  • Keep both opposite frequencies.
  • Give a result in mm² and explain why a 0 mean does not eliminate the oscillations.

Exercise 2 · Change of time scale

The vibration slows down

The same motion is played 2 times more slowly: y(t) = x(t/2). Its period becomes T′ = 8 s; the amplitudes do not change. Predict what happens to M and E, then calculate their new values over one full period of y. The prime here denotes the new period.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A mean over a period is divided by the duration of that period.

Hint 2

In the integral, substitute s = t/2: dt = 2 ds and the limits become those of the original period again.

Compare with the solution

The coefficients are defined using T′ and the new angular frequency ω/2. The change of variable s = t/2 transforms the factor 1/T′ and the differential dt into 1/T and ds: the coefficients keep the same values. The mean square therefore remains 33/2 mm², or 16.50 mm².

The integral accumulates the same squares over a duration multiplied by 2: E′ = T′M = 8 × (33/2) = 132 mm²·s. Check: 132 ÷ 8 = 33/2 mm². The original integral was 66 mm²·s.

Check your reasoning

  • State the qualitative result before calculating.
  • Use the new period in the normalization.
  • Justify the time factor with a change of variable and keep the units distinct.

Exercise 3 · Reasoning and proof

Can the same total hide a different motion?

Return to T = 4 s. The second description is now y(t) = m + a cos(ωt) − b sin(ωt). A colleague claims that M(y) = M(x) proves y = x. Verify the claimed equality of mean squares, then calculate the mean square D of x − y to decide whether the motions are identical. M(x) and M(y) denote the mean squares of the signals shown in parentheses.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Changing the sign of the sine swaps the two conjugate coefficients; their moduli do not change.

Hint 2

First subtract the two functions. For D, apply Parseval to their difference, rather than to the difference of their totals.

Compare with the solution

The sine coefficient of y is -4 mm; its square is still 16 mm². Thus M(y) = 4 + (9 + 16)/2 = 33/2 mm² = M(x). The conjugate coefficients have been swapped, preserving this sum of squared moduli.

But x(t) − y(t) = 2b sin(ωt) = 8 sin(ωt) mm. The square of this amplitude is 64 mm²; the mean of sin² is 1/2. We obtain D = 64/2 = 32 mm², strictly positive. The two motions are therefore different.

Equality of norms compares sizes, without identifying directions or phases. To establish x = y as elements of L², we need D = 0; this means equality almost everywhere, without claiming equality at every point for arbitrary L² functions. Here x and y are continuous: a 0 D would also require equality everywhere.

Check your reasoning

  • Explicitly verify the equality M(y) = M(x).
  • Calculate the total for the difference and explain why it is positive.
  • Distinguish equality of norms, equality in L² and pointwise equality.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1With coefficients cₖ defined by an integral divided by T, what does the sum of squared moduli give?

The mean square M = (1/T)∫|x|² over one period. The integral alone is E = TM. For our displacement in mm, M is in mm² and E is in mm²·s.

Recall 2Why add the squared moduli of the coefficients and count the pair of opposite frequencies?

The squared modulus of p + iq is p² + q², real and nonnegative. Opposite coefficients of a real signal are conjugates; each index nevertheless contributes to the Parseval sum.

Recall 3What does orthonormality alone give, and what does completeness add?

Orthonormality cancels the cross terms and gives a sum of contributions at most equal to the squared norm. Completeness lets us approximate every L² signal in norm, make the squared error disappear and obtain equality.

Recall 4Are two signals with the same mean square necessarily equal? Which calculation decides?

No: a change of phase can preserve the total. Calculate the mean square of their difference; if it is 0, this gives equality almost everywhere in L². Equality of the two separate totals is not enough.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without reference material: state the period and the definition of the coefficients; explain the squared modulus and the cancellation of cross terms; say why completeness gives equality; add the contributions without forgetting the opposite indices; distinguish the mean from the integral by their units; check in the time domain. Then explain why the same total does not prove that the signal is the same.
