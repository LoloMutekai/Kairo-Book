Level 1 · C014·M5

Invariants

Your goalProve what a repeated transfer preserves, why it stops, and when all the socks end up in the drawer.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You are putting socks away. The basket contains 8 socks and the drawer is empty. You take a pair from the basket and put it in the drawer. The basket empties and the drawer fills, yet counting the socks in both containers together always gives the starting total.

A loop is a rule repeated as long as a condition remains true. Here the rule is: while the basket contains at least 2 socks, transfer a whole pair. The state describes the quantities in the basket and the drawer at a given moment. We will examine these states when the condition is tested, before the first transfer and after each completed transfer.

A loop invariant is a property that is true at all these moments. The quantities themselves need not stay fixed: their relationship does. Here their sum remains equal to the starting number of socks. The number left in the basket provides a separate argument: it decreases at every round and never becomes negative.

The counts are fictitious. We do not distinguish sizes or patterns: a pair simply means a group of 2 socks. No object enters or leaves the scene, and each transfer is complete, with no loss. We want a proof for this exact rule, without simulating the uncertainties of putting real socks away.

The rule to remember

Establishing a relationship initially and preserving it proves the invariant. An integer quantity that strictly decreases while remaining nonnegative justifies termination. The invariant and the exit condition then let us determine what has been put away.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Empty the basket without losing a sock

The fictitious starting count is N = 8 socks, with an empty drawer. A pair is transferred repeatedly while enough socks remain in the basket. Before following the calculations, predict whether the constant total is enough to guarantee that the basket will end up empty.

  1. Define the state, units, and rule

    N is the initial number of socks, fixed throughout the process; r counts those left in the basket and s those already in the drawer. N, r, and s are nonnegative integers, in socks. k counts completed rounds, in rounds. Initially, r = N, s = 0, and k = 0.

    The test r ≥ 2 means "enough socks remain to take a pair". If the test is true, the whole round removes 2 socks from the basket and adds the same socks to the drawer. Otherwise, the loop exits. The symbols r′ and s′, with a prime, denote the quantities after this round; k′ is the new round count.

    r′=r−2;s′=s+2;k′=k+1

    The coefficient 2 corresponds to 2 socks transferred per round. In the formulas for the counts, all terms added or subtracted are therefore measured in socks. The test guarantees r′ ≥ 0; since s is nonnegative, s′ remains nonnegative too.

  2. Establish the invariant initially

    The proposed property combines r + s = N, r ≥ 0, and s ≥ 0. Initialization means showing that it is true before any repetition: N + 0 = N, and the starting counts are nonnegative. For our basket: 8 + 0 = 8 socks.

    This check is essential. A rule that preserves a relationship does not guarantee that the relationship was true initially.

  3. Preserve the relationship over an arbitrary round

    Suppose the property is true at the test and suppose r ≥ 2, allowing the transfer. Preservation means proving that it remains true after this round. Substitute the calculation rule for each new quantity:

    r′+s′=(r−2)+(s+2)=r+s=N

    The removal and addition cancel out. The counts remain integers because we add or subtract an integer. Nonnegativity was checked using the test. We have therefore preserved the whole property, without choosing a particular round.

    This is the induction from the previous unit, applied to rounds: the property is true initially; if it is true at a round and another round takes place, it remains true afterwards. It is therefore true at every state reached at the test. We do not claim that it is true in the middle of an unfinished transfer.

  4. Trace the chosen starting count through to the final test

    After 1 round: r = 8 − 2 = 6 and s = 0 + 2 = 2 socks. Total check: 6 + 2 = 8 socks.

    After 2 rounds: r = 6 − 2 = 4 and s = 2 + 2 = 4 socks. Total: 4 + 4 = 8 socks.

    After 3 rounds: r = 4 − 2 = 2 and s = 4 + 2 = 6 socks. Total: 2 + 6 = 8 socks.

    A pair still remains: 2 ≥ 2 allows the next round. After 4 rounds: r = 2 − 2 = 0 and s = 6 + 2 = 8 socks. Total: 0 + 8 = 8 socks. At the next test, 0 < 2: no further round is allowed.

  5. Justify termination with a loop variant

    Here a loop variant is an integer quantity, nonnegative at the observed states, that strictly decreases at every allowed round. Choose r: the test prevents us from removing a pair that is not there, and the transfer decreases r by 2 socks. A sequence of nonnegative integers cannot strictly decrease indefinitely. The loop therefore terminates for every finite nonnegative integer starting count, even or odd.

    The invariant is not enough for this argument. Imagine the faulty rule "while a pair remains, move nothing". From the same starting state, it keeps 8 in the basket and 0 in the drawer: their total is still 8, but the basket count decreases by 0 socks. The test stays true and the loop can continue forever. With the correct transfer, the decrease in the first round is 8 − 6 = 2 socks.

  6. Use the exit condition and specify the guarantee

    The exit condition is the negation of the test: r < 2. With r a nonnegative integer, this still leaves the possibilities r = 0 or r = 1. To guarantee an empty basket, we must determine which applies.

    An integer is even when it is a multiple of 2. Removing a pair preserves its parity, meaning whether it is even or odd. If N is even, r is even initially and remains even after every round. At exit, of 0 and 1, only 0 fits. Then r = 0 and the invariant gives s = N. This statement about the final result is the postcondition: what must be true when the process ends.

    For N = 8, we therefore obtain 0 socks in the basket and 8 socks in the drawer. The guarantee "all put away" holds for an even starting count. For an odd starting count, the same procedure also terminates, but we must state what remains: the last exercise determines it.

  7. Check the result independently of the trace

    Count pairs rather than states. Initially, the 8 socks form 8 ÷ 2 = 4 pairs for counting. Each round moves a pair: 4 rounds therefore transfer 4 × 2 = 8 socks. This matches the final drawer count calculated by successive additions.

    The checks have distinct roles: recounting the total checks conservation; the decrease in r justifies termination; the exit condition and parity establish that the basket is empty. The general proof comes from initialization and preservation, followed by these termination and exit arguments. A list of states, even a correct list, does not replace them.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If it is invariant, the basket and drawer stop changing.”

    Compare the starting state (8, 0) with the state (6, 2). The counts change; only their sum remains 8 socks. Write the relationship that stays true, rather than holding each variable fixed.

    Recount the outlines before and after the transfer

  • “The total is conserved, so the loop ends.”

    Moving nothing also conserves the total, but leaves the test true. To repair the proof, name r as the loop variant, check that it is an integer and nonnegative, then show its strict decrease of 2 socks per round.

    Revisit the loop variant and the case with no movement

  • “The loop stopped, so all the socks are put away.”

    Exit guarantees only r < 2. A remainder of 1 sock satisfies this condition. Add the assumption of an even starting count to prove r = 0, or revise the guarantee for an odd starting count.

    Test the odd starting count in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Numerical application

Find a state and check it

Start again with 8 socks in the basket and an empty drawer. Without looking at the trace, find the basket count, drawer count, and their total after 2 transfers. Explain why the total does not tell you how many rounds have taken place.

Hint 1

First count the socks that change places: each round transfers 2.

Hint 2

The number transferred is subtracted from the basket and added to the drawer. The total has the same value at every round.

Compare with the solution

The transfers moved 2 × 2 = 4 socks. There are 8 − 4 = 4 socks left in the basket; the drawer contains 4 socks. These are indeed the counts 4 and 4 in the trace.

Their sum is 4 + 4 = 8 socks. This check verifies the total, but the same total existed before the first transfer. It therefore does not count the rounds: to do that, we must track the transfers or the number left in the basket.

Check your reasoning

  • I have justified the 4 socks transferred using the number of rounds and the size of a pair.
  • I give 4 in the basket, 4 in the drawer, and 8 in total, all in socks.
  • I distinguish checking conservation from counting rounds.

Exercise 2 · Proof on paper

Move from the chosen basket to any even starting count

The basket now contains a nonnegative even integer N of socks, with no further value specified. The drawer is empty and the rule stays the same. Write the initialization, preservation, termination, and final result. Your proof must also cover N = 0. A simple list of transfers is not enough.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For preservation, start from an arbitrary state where r + s = N and r ≥ 2. Calculate r′ + s′.

Hint 2

For the final result, combine r < 2, r being a nonnegative integer, and r being even. For termination, explain the decrease in r separately.

Compare with the solution

Initialization: r = N and s = 0 give r + s = N with nonnegative counts. Preservation: under the test r ≥ 2, the transfer gives r′ + s′ = (r − 2) + (s + 2) = N and keeps the counts nonnegative. Induction guarantees these properties at every state reached at the test.

Termination: r is a nonnegative integer that strictly decreases by 2 at every round. It cannot keep decreasing this way indefinitely. Result: at exit, r < 2. Since N is even and only pairs are subtracted, r is even; therefore r = 0, then s = N by conservation. The number of rounds is N ÷ 2: each round transfers a pair and the whole basket has been transferred.

If N = 0, the test is false from the start. No transfer takes place: r = s = 0, which directly satisfies the same conclusion. This boundary case rules out a proof that wrongly assumes a first round exists.

Check your reasoning

  • I prove the property before any round, then its preservation for an arbitrary allowed round.
  • I use a nonnegative integer loop variant that strictly decreases to justify termination.
  • I use parity only to exclude the remainder 1 at exit.
  • I handle the empty starting basket without inventing a transfer.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

Repair a guarantee that is too broad

Use the same process, but with N = 7 socks. Someone claims: "The total is conserved and the loop ends, so everything will be put away." Without listing every round, determine the remainder, the drawer count, and the number of rounds. Repair the conclusion using parity and the exit condition.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Removing a pair from an odd integer leaves it odd. Which nonnegative integer remainders are allowed at exit?

Hint 2

Once you have determined the remainder, subtract it from N to find the drawer count, then count the pairs transferred.

Compare with the solution

The loop variant r still justifies termination. Exit gives r < 2 and r ≥ 0, but r remains odd because the starting count is odd and we remove pairs. We must therefore have r = 1 sock, rather than 0.

Conservation requires s = 7 − 1 = 6 socks. The number of rounds is 6 ÷ 2 = 3 rounds. Check: 1 + 6 = 7 socks; the remainder 1 is too small to allow another pair.

The correct conclusion is "all possible pairs for counting have been transferred; a sock remains in the basket". The invariant and termination both hold, but the guarantee of putting everything away requires an even starting count. The counterexample contradicts this extended guarantee, not conservation.

Check your reasoning

  • I justify the 1 sock remaining using the exit condition and parity, without assuming that termination means an empty basket.
  • I find 6 socks put away in 3 rounds and check their total with the remainder.
  • I repair the scope of the guarantee and keep conservation, termination, and the result distinct.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What stays true in this process, and at which moments?

At the initial test and after each whole transfer, r + s = N and the counts are nonnegative integers. r and s may change; the relationship remains true. It is not asserted in the middle of an unfinished transfer.

Recall 2How can you prove the invariant without relying on a trace alone?

Initialize: N + 0 = N before any round. Preserve: under r ≥ 2, (r − 2) + (s + 2) = r + s = N, with the counts always nonnegative. Induction extends this proof to every round reached.

Recall 3Why choose the number left in the basket as the loop variant, rather than the total?

r is a nonnegative integer that strictly decreases by 2 per allowed round: it cannot keep decreasing indefinitely. The total is constant, even if nothing moves; its conservation therefore does not prove termination.

Recall 4At exit, what lets you conclude that the basket is empty?

Exit gives r < 2 and nonnegativity leaves 0 or 1. If N is even, r remains even after pairs are removed: r = 0, then s = N. An odd starting count instead leaves 1 sock.

Recall 5What does the trace starting from 8 prove, and what does it not prove?

It lets us check the calculation for this starting count: 0 in the basket, 8 in the drawer after 4 rounds. A guarantee for every even starting count requires the proof of initialization and preservation, followed by the distinct termination and exit arguments.

What you understand
A guarantee must name the required relationship, when it holds, and the operations that maintain it. Putting socks away provides this method of reasoning. In the project, checking a relationship during construction is another context: that alone does not constitute a loop invariant.
