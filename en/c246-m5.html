Boundary error

Your goalCalculate how a residual at the barrier becomes a price difference, then check an adjustment in a finite tree.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A fictional contract promises the payoff of a call option at maturity, unless the price reaches an upper barrier on a monitoring date. In that case, it is knocked out without a rebate. An ordinary option can exactly reproduce what it pays on surviving paths while still having some value when the contract is knocked out.

Imagine closing the portfolio as soon as this knock-out occurs. You must compare its liquidation value with the amount then due under the contract, which is 0 m.u. The difference is a boundary residual. Here, the boundary means the states where the rule requires stopping, rather than the edge of a drawing or the end of a list of strike prices.

The price starts at 100 m.u. The barrier is 120 m.u. and the strike price is 80 m.u. The first up move reaches the barrier. Even if the price then falls back to 96 m.u., the contract remains knocked out: the final price alone does not describe the path.

Everything is fictional: the tree, payoffs and pricing probabilities. We isolate the portfolio error at a known boundary. We are seeking neither an actual market strategy nor an adjustment for crossings between observations.

The rule to remember

Check the payoff at maturity on surviving paths AND the portfolio value at every first stopping point. An overlooked residual at the barrier propagates to the initial price.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

An option that survives the knock-out of the contract

The contract is monitored only on dates 1 and 2, the latter being maturity T. It is knocked out at the first price greater than or equal to B = 120 m.u. Otherwise it pays max(S_T − K, 0), with K = 80 m.u. Compare it with the provisional portfolio consisting of an ordinary call option with the same K and T.

  1. Specify the model and the quantities being compared

    t is a dimensionless date index, from 0 to 2; T is the maturity of this tree. S_t is the asset price on date t, in m.u. K and B are also prices in m.u. The contract multiplier is 1: a price difference converts into the same number of m.u. of payoff. For an amount x in m.u., x⁺ means max(x, 0). An ordinary option pays (S_T − K)⁺ regardless of the history.

    H denotes the value of the option portfolio and V the value of the contract while it remains active; e = H − V is the signed error, positive if the portfolio costs more. All three are expressed in m.u. The subscripts on H_départ, H_haut and H_bas name the start, up and down nodes. At a knock-out node, V is 0 and H is the liquidation value of the portfolio before it is closed.

    The interest rate and the dividend yield are 0. Trading has no costs, short sales and fractional quantities are possible, and the portfolio is liquidated at the model price at the first knock-out; otherwise it is held until T. There are no intermediate distributions. These assumptions make adding and discounting the values consistent.

    The pricing law assigns q = 1/2 to an up move and 1 − q = 1/2 to a down move at every node. These probabilities are dimensionless; they are not historical probabilities. The current price equals the average of the next two prices: (120 + 80)/2 = 100, (144 + 96)/2 = 120 and (96 + 64)/2 = 80 m.u. With the 0 interest rate, the tree therefore satisfies the condition for pricing without arbitrage.

  2. Value the ordinary option before any stopping

    From the up node, its terminal payoffs are 144 − 80 = 64 m.u. and 96 − 80 = 16 m.u. Their sum is 80 m.u. We take the average because the weights are equal: H_haut = 80/2 = 40 m.u.

    From the down node, the option pays 16 m.u. at 96 and 0 m.u. at 64, because the latter price is below K. The sum is 16 m.u., so H_bas = 16/2 = 8 m.u.

    At the start, we work backwards once more: H_départ = (40 + 8)/2 = 48/2 = 24 m.u. The 40 m.u. is the option value on the crossing date; the 64 m.u. is a possible payoff at T. Substituting one for the other would change the date and the risk being compared.

  3. Value the contract with its history

    At the first up node, S = B: the contract is knocked out and V_haut = 0 m.u. It does not become active again if the price falls back. At the down node, it survives. Its two possible terminal prices remain below B, so its payoffs there are those of the ordinary option: 16 and 0 m.u.

    Thus V_bas = 8 m.u., then V_départ = (0 + 8)/2 = 4 m.u. The portfolio matches the contract on every surviving outcome, yet its initial price of 24 m.u. already differs from 4 m.u.

  4. Isolate the residual and propagate the error

    Let r_B denote the residual at the first stopping point: portfolio value minus knock-out payment. Here r_B = 40 − 0 = 40 m.u. At the down node, both terminal payoffs are correctly reproduced, so e_bas = 0 m.u. Linearity of the average gives the following relation; no simulation is needed to calculate it.

    edépart=qrB+(1−q)ebas

    Numerically: 1/2 × 40 + 1/2 × 0 = 20 m.u. Check using the separately calculated prices: H_départ − V_départ = 24 − 4 = 20 m.u. We obtain the same signed error.

    This is not a Monte Carlo error: increasing the number of draws does not remove r_B from the portfolio rule. Residuals with different signs could also cancel in the average; a small price difference would then not prove a small error on every path.

  5. Check using the stopped paths

    Each complete history has weight 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Without a barrier, the option payoffs are, in the order up-up, up-down, down-up, down-down: 64, 16, 16, 0 m.u. Their average is 24 m.u.

    For the contract, both histories starting with an up move are knocked out. Only down-up pays: 1/4 × 16 = 4 m.u. For the liquidated portfolio, histories starting with an up move each return 40 m.u. at stopping; the others return 16 and 0 m.u. at T. Because the interest rate is 0, we can compare these amounts without a discount factor: contract 4 + error 20 = portfolio 24 m.u.

    The check preserves the stopping date and the history. Merging the two outcomes with price 96 would remove precisely the information that triggers the knock-out.

  6. Repair this boundary in our finite tree

    Add a short position in ordinary call options with strike price B and the same maturity T. Let A denote their unit value, in m.u., and w the dimensionless quantity sold. From the up node, such an option pays 144 − 120 = 24 m.u. or 0 m.u.: A_haut = 24/2 = 12 m.u. At the down node, it pays nothing, so A_bas = 0 and A_départ = 6 m.u.

    The adjusted portfolio is worth H − wA. To cancel its residual at the up node, we must sell w = H_haut/A_haut = 40/12 = 10/3 options. Then wA_haut = 40 m.u. and the residual becomes 0 m.u. On surviving paths, A pays nothing: the already correct payoff is preserved.

    w=HhautAhaut=4012=10/3

    The cost of the short position is negative in the portfolio: the initial proceeds are wA_départ = 20 m.u. The adjusted cost is 24 − 20 = 4 m.u., equal to the contract price. This check concerns an identity in the fictional model; the sale proceeds are not a profit.

  7. State exactly where this repair stops

    Here, there is only one possible first-crossing node before T; after a down move, no terminal price touches B. Cancelling this residual and the terminal errors therefore covers all possible stopping points in this tree. The portfolio is held with fixed quantities until stopping, then unwound: static does not mean holding the options after knock-out.

    With other dates or continuous monitoring, the portfolio value would have to match the knock-out payment at every possible first crossing. The value of the option sold depends in particular on the remaining time: cancelling the residual on a chosen date does not automatically cancel it on other dates. A uniform bound ε on residuals at all stopping points, in m.u., also bounds the initial error by ε in a consistent model with a 0 interest rate, because an average of bounded absolute values remains bounded.

    This bound also assumes that the terminal error on surviving paths is bounded by ε. If it is 0, as here, only the boundary contributes. Discretization error for a continuous barrier and uncertainty in market prices are other issues; our small tree does not measure them.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The portfolio pays the correct amount at maturity, so the barrier changes nothing.”

    First specify which histories survive. At the first stopping point, compare the liquidation value of the portfolio with the knock-out payment. In our tree, the residual of 40 m.u. remains despite agreement on all surviving payoffs.

    Review the residual and the check using stopped paths

  • “The price falls back below the barrier, so the contract becomes active again.”

    A knock-out is permanent. Keep the two histories with final price 96 distinct: after an initial up move, the contract is already knocked out; after an initial down move, it can pay. The drawing separates these histories.

    Reread the two histories with the same final price

  • “More simulations will erase the portfolio difference.”

    Draws estimate an average under the rule you give them. They do not change the portfolio value at stopping. Repair the residual or bound it; then distinguish this error from sampling uncertainty.

Compare with the solution

Up-down passes through 120 = B: knock-out on date 1, payoff 0 m.u. and contribution 0 m.u. Down-up passes through 80, then 96, two prices below B: payoff 96 − 80 = 16 m.u.

The contribution of down-up to the initial price is 1/4 × 16 = 4 m.u. The 0 interest rate allows us to keep the same monetary units across dates. The final asset price is identical; the contract state is not.

  • I separated the payoff on a history from its weighted contribution to the price.
  • I did not reactivate the contract after a down move.

At the barrier, subtract the value of the short position from H_haut.

Compare with the solution

The short position has a value of 3 × 12 = 36 m.u. to be subtracted at stopping. The portfolio is still worth 40 − 36 = 4 m.u. there, while the contract owes nothing. The residual remains positive.

Its initial contribution is 1/2 × 4 = 2 m.u. Surviving paths add no error, because the options with strike price B pay nothing on them.

Check using the costs: the sale brings in 3 × 6 = 18 m.u. The portfolio therefore costs 24 − 18 = 6 m.u. The difference from the contract is 6 − 4 = 2 m.u., identical to the contribution calculated at the boundary.

Check your reasoning

  • I used the option values at stopping to calculate the residual.
  • I applied the pricing weight to this residual only once.
  • I obtained the same error from the difference between initial costs.

Exercise 3 · Repair and objection

Is the exact portfolio still exact after stopping?

Find the quantity w to sell to cancel the residual. Check the adjusted cost. Then suppose the options are not liquidated on the initial up move and the price ends at 144 m.u. Calculate their net payoff at T. Does this amount contradict the stated replication? Can replication for a continuously monitored barrier be deduced from it?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Solve H_haut − wA_haut = 0, allowing a fractional quantity.

Hint 2

Without unwinding, subtract terminal payoffs rather than values at the up node.

Hint 3

Reread which dates and actions define the strategy that was proved.

Compare with the solution

We need w = 40/12 = 10/3, or approximately 3.3333 options, a dimensionless quantity. Keep the exact fraction in the products: it cancels the residual exactly. The initial proceeds are 20 m.u. and the adjusted cost is 24 − 20 = 4 m.u.

Without unwinding, the option with strike price K pays 64 m.u.; the options sold pay w × 24 = 80 m.u. The net payoff is 64 − 80 = -16 m.u., while the contract is knocked out and pays 0 m.u.

There is no contradiction: the stated replication closes the portfolio at the first crossing. At the up node, its net value is 0 m.u. Failing to liquidate it defines a different strategy, which retains risk after knock-out.

Continuous monitoring is not covered by the proof. We would need to define the dynamics between our dates and check the portfolio value at every possible first crossing, with its remaining time. Cancellation at a single node does not supply these additional conditions.

Check your reasoning

  • I obtained an exact fraction and checked that the residual is 0.
  • I distinguished liquidation value from the terminal payoff of the portfolio if held.
  • I named the assumption violated by the failure to unwind.
  • I did not turn the proof in a tree into a guarantee for continuous monitoring.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the boundary residual of a replicating portfolio compare?

At the first stopping point, its liquidation value and the payment required by the contract at that stopping point, on the same date and in the same unit. Here the contract is knocked out without a rebate.

Recall 2How does the residual become a pricing error in our tree?

Errors follow the same weighted backward calculation as prices. Because surviving payoffs are exact, the initial error equals q times the residual at the only first-crossing node: 1/2 × 40 = 20 m.u.

Recall 3Why separate the two histories with the same final price?

One history has already touched the barrier; the other has not. The knocked-out contract does not become active again: its value also depends on that state, rather than just the final price.

Recall 4What must be checked before declaring replication exact?

The payoff at T on every surviving path and the portfolio value at every first stopping point, using a common pricing model and the planned unwinding. Checking a single date is sufficient only if it covers all first stopping points in the model.

Recall 5Why does increasing the number of draws not repair an incorrect boundary?

Draws estimate the average of the chosen portfolio. They do not change its payoff or liquidation rule. The residual must be adjusted or bounded separately from sampling uncertainty.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct this chain: monitoring rule and first stopping point; payoff for survivors; liquidation value at the boundary; signed residual; backward calculation using pricing probabilities; check using prices; adjustment and model limitation.
