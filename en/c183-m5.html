Level 3 · C183·M5

Convergence tests

Your goalBuild a refinement test at a fixed location, identify its order and estimate its error without confusing numerical stabilization with price accuracy.

Before you start
c167-m5 : initial and boundary value problem · c051-m5 : error diagnostics · c175-m5 : model limitations · c050-m5 : conditioning · c183-m1 : grid · c183-m2 : explicit scheme · c183-m3 : implicit scheme · c183-m4 : Crank-Nicolson

✿01 See the idea

On the screen of a pricing solver, you replace each grid interval with 2 smaller intervals. The displayed price changes. You repeat the process: it changes again, but less. Does this mean the calculation is approaching the correct value, or just that the same errors keep recurring?

A convergence test tracks a specific quantity as the grid is refined. Here, it is the price at the midpoint of the domain, always at the same location. Comparing the central cells of grids with different endpoints would not be enough: the evaluation point in the problem must stay fixed, rather than the cell number.

We construct a small boundary value problem to check the spatial calculation of a solver. All prices are fictitious. The problem has an exact solution chosen in advance: we can compare the differences between grids with the actual error. It serves as a test problem; it does not describe the price of a real option.

First predict this: if the error is proportional to the square of the step size, by what factor should it decrease when the step size is divided by 2? The diagrams show the nested grids and the results to compare; the calculation will explain this factor.

The rule to remember

Fix the question and the conditions of the problem; refine the step size; compare successive differences; check the order and a reference before drawing a conclusion about the error.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Checking the price at the midpoint of a manufactured domain

The test problem imposes V″(x) equal to 12 € multiplied by the square of x, with V(0) = 10 € and V(1) = 11 €. We compare the price at x = 1/2 for M = 2, 4 and then 8 intervals. The symbol ″ indicates the second derivative with respect to x. The equation and boundary values are defined for this exercise.

  1. Fixing the target and the reference

    x is a normalized coordinate of the underlying asset, with no unit, ranging from 0 to 1. V(x) is our fictitious price in euros. Since x has no unit, its derivatives retain the unit euro here; the coefficient 12 on the right-hand side is therefore expressed in euros. The problem is stationary: there is no time step to refine in this test problem.

    We chose the equation from a known function, called a manufactured solution and denoted V⋆. Its expression below satisfies the boundary conditions. Differentiating it gives V⋆″(x) = 12x squared, as required. The difference between two possible solutions would have a second derivative of 0 and 0 boundary values: this affine difference would be 0. The solution to this test problem is therefore unique.

    V⋆(x)=(10+x4) €

    At the fixed point, x squared is 1/4 and its power 4 is 1/16. The reference U⋆ = V⋆(1/2) is therefore 10 + 1/16 = 161/16 € = 10.0625 €. We keep this value for the final check.

  2. Writing what each grid solves

    M is the integer number of intervals; h = 1/M is their width, with no unit. The nodes are xᵢ = ih, where i is the integer node index. Vᵢ denotes the approximation in euros at node i; the indices i − 1 and i + 1 denote its neighbors. We keep the same boundary values on every grid and solve the centered system at the interior nodes.

    Vi−1−2Vi+Vi+1h2=12xi2

    For the coarsest grid, there is only one interior node: our target point. The right-hand side is 12 × 1/4 = 3 €. Multiplying by h squared gives 3/4 €; the sum of the boundary values is 21 €. The equation becomes 21 − 2U = 3/4, where U is the central price. Thus 2U = 81/4 €, then U = 81/8 € = 10.125000 €. The sign of the curvature explains why we subtract the right-hand side from the sum of the boundary values.

  3. Obtaining the prices without inventing a table

    Let Dh denote the centered second difference, meaning the operator on the left-hand side of the previous equation. For our polynomial, expanding the powers gives the following identities exactly. No term has been neglected.

    Dh(x4)=12x2+2h2;Dh(x(1−x))=−2

    Adding h squared times x(1 − x) to V⋆ therefore cancels the extra term in the second difference. This addition also vanishes at the boundaries. The grid system has a unique solution: a sequence whose second difference is 0 is affine in i, and its boundary values determine that sequence. The discrete solution Vh is therefore given exactly by the formula below; the coefficient of the added term carries the unit euro.

    Vh(xi)=V⋆(xi)+h2xi(1−xi) €

    Uh denotes the single price we track: Vh(1/2). At the center, x(1 − x) is 1/4. So Uh = U⋆ + h squared / 4 in euros. The checking script also solves the systems by rational elimination, then verifies every equation and this expression at each node.

  4. Calculating the results at the same point

    With h = 1/2, its square is 1/4. The addition to the exact price is 1/16 €; Uh = 161/16 + 1/16 = 81/8 € = 10.125000 €. This reproduces the direct calculation on the coarse grid.

    With h = 1/4, its square is 1/16. The addition is 1/64 €; the price is 645/64 € = 10.078125 €.

    With h = 1/8, its square is 1/64. The addition is 1/256 €; the price is 2577/256 € ≈ 10.066406 €. The fractions are exact; the decimals make them easier to read and are not used for the subtractions.

  5. Forming the differences and identifying the order

    Call A the difference from the coarse price to the medium price, and B the difference from the medium price to the fine price. Here they are positive: A = 81/8 − 645/64 = 3/64 € and B = 645/64 − 2577/256 = 3/256 €. Their ratio R = A/B is 4, with no unit. Dividing the prices by each other would hide this information, because they share a large common part.

    An order p means here that the dominant error is C times h to the power p, with a coefficient C independent of h across the family being tested. p has no unit and C is in euros because h has no unit. The region where this term dominates is called the asymptotic regime. When h is divided by 2, successive differences in this regime have a ratio close to 2 to the power p.

    R=Uh−Uh/2Uh/2−Uh/4≈2p

    Our ratio 4 = 2 to the power 2 corresponds to order 2. For this test problem, the discrete formula proves this behavior exactly. For results without a known reference, a single family of 3 grids provides a diagnostic, rather than a general proof of convergence. 0 differences make the quotient undefined; differences that change sign require keeping the signs and examining the oscillations before estimating an order.

  6. Estimating the error of the fine price

    Assuming order 2 in this regime, the error of the medium price is 4 times that of the fine price. Their difference B therefore represents 4 − 1 = 3 times the fine grid error. The estimate E, positive and in euros, is the absolute value of B divided by 3. It is not B itself.

    E=|B|4−1=3/2563=1/256 €

    We had set a tolerance ε = 1/200 € = 0.005 €, where ε denotes the acceptable absolute error for this test price. E ≈ 0.003906 € is less than ε; the error on the medium grid, 0.015625 €, is still greater than ε. The fine refinement therefore meets this criterion on this test problem.

    Without our exact formula, E would remain an estimate that depends on the observed regime. We must then specify the tolerance for solving the system, retain enough digits and continue refinement to check that the ratio persists. A quotient formed from differences dominated by rounding is no longer informative.

  7. Checking against the reference and extrapolating

    The error we can actually calculate is |2577/256 − 161/16| = 1/256 €, exactly equal to E. This is a check independent of the ratio of differences alone: the reference was obtained by solving the continuous problem, rather than by treating the latest price as the truth.

    The grid bias is positive here. Subtracting B/3 from the fine price gives the extrapolated price Uext = 2577/256 − 1/256 = 161/16 € = 10.0625 €. This combination is called Richardson extrapolation: it cancels the dominant error term when its power and coefficient are the same on the grids used.

    The equality with the reference is exact for this polynomial. It does not promise an exact solution for another equation, or a guaranteed error bound from arbitrary prices. The exercise on shifting the prices will show how a shared defect survives the test of differences.

  8. Preparing the same check for a real pricing equation

    This test problem isolates spatial error with a smooth solution. For a time-dependent equation, Δt denotes the time step, expressed in the time unit of the model. To test space, first make the time error negligible and maintain the stability conditions of the scheme; to test time, fix a sufficiently fine spatial grid. Refining both without separating them can hide the dominant error.

    Keep the contract, date and point of the underlying asset fixed. Check separately the effect of the artificial boundary that limits the domain; grids can stabilize even when the domain is too short. If the target is not a shared node, state how its value is obtained between nodes and check that error too.

    A sensitivity is a derivative of the price with respect to a parameter. Price convergence is not enough to establish convergence of its derivatives: they need their own refinement series and their own units. A payoff that is not smooth at maturity, or numerical oscillations, can change the observed order. The name Crank-Nicolson does not remove the need for any of these checks.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The price barely changes anymore: it is accurate.”

    An incorrect boundary value or a shared shift can stay the same on all grids. Compare with an independent reference and check the domain. Return to the last exercise: it preserves the differences while making the price wrong.

    Testing the defect shared by the grids

  • “The last difference is the error of the last price.”

    Under our order 2 law, this difference is 3 times the fine grid error. Recover the factor by subtracting the grid errors, then check it with the manufactured solution. Outside this regime, this division is not justified.

    Recovering the estimate and its check

  • “I refine everything at once and check only the price.”

    State the quantity being tested and isolate the source of error. The spatial step size, time step and boundary location require separate checks. A sensitivity obtained by differentiation can deteriorate even when the price has stabilized.

Keep the same test problem and move to M = 16, so h = 1/16. Starting from Uₕ = U⋆ + h squared / 4 in euros, calculate the price, the new difference and the ratio using the latest grids. Estimate the error of this new price and check it against U⋆. The tolerance remains 0.005 €.

Hint 1

Calculate h squared before dividing by 4. The new difference compares the previous grid with the new one.

Hint 2

To estimate the error of the new price, use the new difference and the same factor 3.

Compare with the solution

The square of the step size is 1/256; the addition is 1/1024 €. The new price is 161/16 + 1/1024 = 10305/1024 € ≈ 10.063477 €.

The new difference is 2577/256 − 10305/1024 = 3/1024 €. The ratio previous difference / new difference is 3/256 / 3/1024 = 4. The order 2 behavior persists.

The estimate is 3/1024 / 3 = 1/1024 € ≈ 0.000977 €. It agrees with |10305/1024 − 161/16| and remains below 0.005 €. This check uses a known reference; an actual sequence without a reference would retain an estimated conclusion.

Exercise 3 · Objection and procedure

Convergence to the wrong target

A display defect adds 1 € to every price in the same test problem, on every grid. The prices on the finest grids become 2833/256 € and 11329/1024 €. Do the differences and their ratio detect this defect? Calculate the error of the shifted price on the grid with step size 1/8 relative to U⋆, then propose a check that would reveal the problem.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write (U + b) − (W + b), where b is the same addition in euros and U and W are the prices from two grids.

Hint 2

The continuous reference and the imposed boundary values have not received this addition.

Compare with the solution

The addition cancels in each difference: (U + b) − (W + b) = U − W. The new difference is still 3/1024 € and the ratios remain 4. Even extrapolation would retain the shared addition.

On the grid with step size 1/8, the displayed error is 2833/256 − 161/16 = 257/256 € ≈ 1.003906 €. It greatly exceeds the tolerance 0.005 €, while the estimate based on differences remains 1/256 €.

Comparing the display with V⋆ at the target point reveals the defect. Displaying the values at the imposed boundaries provides another check: the screen must show 10 € and 11 €. Successful refinement here establishes a property of the differences, without guaranteeing the accuracy of the whole calculation and display chain.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify algebraically why the differences are unchanged, rather than simply asserting it.
  • Calculate the error relative to the unchanged reference.
  • Propose a check independent of refinement alone, and state what it verifies.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be fixed before comparing results from different grids?

The same quantity at the same point of the same problem: contract, date, domain and boundary values. To isolate one error, the other sources must remain controlled. A cell number alone does not define this target.

Recall 2With a step size divided by 2, what ratio of differences is expected for order 2?

A ratio close to 4, because the dominant error follows the square of the step size. This assumes a shared coefficient and differences large enough to avoid being dominated by rounding; a single quotient does not prove general convergence.

Recall 3Why do we divide the last difference by 3 in our test problem?

At order 2, the medium grid error is 4 times the fine grid error. Their difference is therefore 4 − 1 = 3 times the fine grid error. Divide the absolute value of this difference by 3; validity depends on the error regime.

Recall 4Why does an addition shared by all prices escape the test of differences?

It cancels in the subtractions. The grids can therefore retain the same order while approaching a shifted value. An independent reference or a check of the boundary values reveals what refinement alone cannot see.

Recall 5Is a converging price enough to validate its sensitivity?

No. The sensitivity is a derivative of the price and must be tested as a separate quantity, with its units and its refinement series. Taking a derivative can amplify errors that are barely visible in the price.

What you understand
Convergence must name the sequence being observed and the quantity being checked. Comparing pricing grids and comparing optimizer iterations are different diagnostics; an indicator of stabilization alone does not prove that the error is small.
