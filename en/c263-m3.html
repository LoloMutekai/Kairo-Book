Level 4 · C263·M3

Nested temporal validation

Your goalSeparate the choice of a setting from its future evaluation by reconstructing the inner and outer loops on the same series of fictional share prices.

Before you start
c105-m5 : cost and limitations · c201-m5 : costs and events · c262-m5 : robustness across regimes · c261-m5 : model uncertainty · c041-m5 : transactions and windows · c263-m1 : temporal labels · c263-m2 : overlap

✿01 See the idea

You are preparing a forecast of the closing price of a fictional share. Two rules are candidates: keep the latest price, or take the mean of the latest closing prices. You can compare them using historical data that are already known. But if you look at the next day to decide which rule to keep, that day has contributed to the choice: it no longer gives an honest measure of what your procedure would have forecast.

The trials therefore need to be nested. In the inner loop, past forecasts are used to choose a setting. In the outer loop, that choice is applied to a later date held out from selection. Here, a fold separates a prefix of the history from the date that follows it; the prefix contains only the information available at that time.

The diagram shows the past of the first outer split: all its inner validations are contained within that past. The outer test remains sealed until the forecast is made. Later in the timeline, selection is repeated using the past that has become available. What we evaluate is this selection procedure, which can choose different settings at different splits.

All share prices and numerical results on this page are fictional. We evaluate a forecast error in euros per share. No market trade or profitability is calculated. Decision costs belong to the next unit.

The rule to remember

Choose within the past of each split, freeze the choice, make the forecast for the outer date, and only then reveal its target and count the error.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Two outer forecasts, the same selection rule

We have 9 successive closing prices for a fictional share, all dated by their actual availability. The first outer split forecasts D7 using the past D1–D6; the second forecasts D9 using D1–D8. Each target is the next day’s closing price, published after the forecast.

  1. Name the target and the candidate rules

    Dt denotes the day with index t; t is a unitless integer. Pt is its closing price, in euros per share, known at the end of that day. The hat on P̂ means a forecast. At the close of Dt, we forecast P for day t + 1. The target is this future price, rather than a price increase encoded as a class.

    The data are: D1: 100 €; D2: 104 €; D3: 100 €. Then D4: 102 €; D5: 104 €; D6: 102 €. Finally D7: 105 €; D8: 108 €; D9: 106 €. Throughout, the symbol € denotes a price per share or a difference between these prices.

    The setting h is a unitless label: A keeps the latest closing price; B averages the latest 2. The setting is a hyperparameter: it determines the rule before its forecast is calculated. Here, fitting a rule consists only of taking its window from the permitted prefix; no additional coefficient is learned.

    P^t+1A=Pt;P^t+1B=Pt−1+Pt2

    The model assumes closing prices are available without delay or later revision. It uses no information beyond its cutoff. For a model trained on input–target pairs, the training targets must also already be known: dating only the inputs is insufficient. The exclusions of overlapping labels seen earlier remain necessary if their intervals extend beyond the cutoff.

  2. Fix the protocol before revealing the tests

    The first split is named by the Greek letter α and uses the inner dates D4 and D6. To forecast D4, each rule is fitted on D1–D3; for D6, on D1–D5. The second split is named β: it uses D6 and D8, forecast from D1–D5 and D1–D7 respectively. All these inner dates precede their outer test.

    We keep the setting that minimizes the inner mean absolute error; a tie would select A, by a convention announced before the trials. The list A, B, the dates, and this criterion remain fixed throughout the evaluation. With a trained model, every fit, including any transformation of the inputs, would be repeated using only its own prefix.

    Mh(V)=1n∑s∈V|Ps−P^sh|

    V is the set of inner validation dates; s ranges over these dates, and n is their unitless count, equal here to 2. Mh(V) is the mean absolute error of setting h, in euros. The bars take the absolute value: each difference becomes a positive or 0 distance. The sum ∑ adds these distances. Each forecast for date s uses only closing prices through s − 1.

  3. Select within the past of split α

    For D4, A forecasts the price on D3, namely 100 €. Its error is |102 − 100| = 2 €. B forecasts (104 + 100)/2 = 204/2 = 102 €; its error is |102 − 102| = 0 €.

    For D6, A forecasts 104 €, giving |102 − 104| = 2 €. B forecasts (102 + 104)/2 = 206/2 = 103 €, giving |102 − 103| = 1 €. Taking an absolute value prevents errors in opposite directions from cancelling each other out.

    Adding and then dividing by the number of dates gives, for A: (2 + 2)/2 = 4/2 = 2.00 €. For B: (0 + 1)/2 = 1/2 = 0.50 €. We choose B because its inner error is lower. The price on D7 has played no part in any of these operations.

  4. Freeze B and reveal the test for split α

    After the choice, we refit B to the complete outer past D1–D6. Its final window is D5, D6: the forecast for D7 is (104 + 102)/2 = 206/2 = 103 €. We do not keep the window from an earlier inner fold.

    We then reveal the closing price on D7: 105 €. The outer error ℓα, in euros, is |105 − 103| = 2 €. This test measures the result of the choice of B made earlier. We keep this error even if it is disappointing; returning to the choice after seeing it would change the protocol.

  5. Repeat selection in split β

    The forecast for D6 again gives distances of 2 € for A and 1 € for B. For D8, A, fitted through D7, forecasts 105 €: |108 − 105| = 3 €. B forecasts (102 + 105)/2 = 207/2 = 103.50 €; |108 − 103.50| = 4.50 €.

    The inner error for A becomes (2 + 3)/2 = 5/2 = 2.50 €. For B, it is (1 + 4.50)/2 = 5.50/2 = 2.75 €. The same selection rule now chooses A.

    We refit A to the prefix D1–D8: it forecasts 108 € for D9. After revealing D9, the outer error ℓβ is |106 − 108| = 2 €. We have therefore tested a procedure that chooses B and then A, without using D9 for this last choice.

    The closing price on D7, a former outer test, can enter the past of this later split: it is actually known by then. This does not allow us to revise the forecast already scored for D7, or to change the candidates after inspecting the outer results.

  6. Aggregate the errors that answer the right question

    L=ℓα+ℓβ2=2+22=2.00 €

    L is the outer mean absolute error of the procedure, in euros, using only dates D7 and D9. The outer sum is 4 €. Since each test here contains one date, the mean across folds coincides with the mean of the individual distances. With different sizes, we would need to state whether each date or each fold receives equal weight.

    Independent check: the outer forecasts are 103 € and 108 €, and the observations are 105 € and 106 €. Subtracting the smaller price from the larger in each pair gives 2 € and 2 € again, then 2.00 €. The result is indeed between the individual distances, which are equal here. The inner scores were used for selection, rather than for this outer calculation.

    These tests at individual dates do not evaluate the dates in between. Their pasts overlap: the errors are not assumed to be independent, and we do not calculate a confidence interval. This fictional result demonstrates neither lasting future accuracy nor economic usefulness. Choosing yet another procedure from these results would then require another held-out evaluation.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the scores. Redraw the prefixes and explain when each closing price becomes available. Why can B win in split α and A in β, even though a single procedure is being evaluated?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I kept the dates in order, so I can keep the best setting on the test.”

    Temporal order prevents forecasts from using future closing prices. It does not protect a test whose result is used for selection. Freeze the setting based on the inner validations, then keep its outer error. The final exercise quantifies the temptation to change the choice afterwards.

    Examine the choice after revealing the test

  • “The best inner score is the final score of my procedure.”

    The inner score helped select the setting. Return to the outer forecasts, their observations, and their distances: their mean is 2.00 €. Do not replace these errors with the winning inner scores.

    Return to the aggregation and its check

  • “If the input is dated before the cutoff, its label must be usable.”

    Check the date when the target becomes known. An old input can carry a target that extends into the future of the fold. Each loop must respect this availability and the overlap exclusions; nesting does not apply them automatically.

    Reread the target and the availability assumptions

A forecasts 104 €. The distance is |102 − 104| = 2 €. B forecasts (102 + 104)/2 = 103 €; the distance is |102 − 103| = 1 €.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What distinguishes the inner and outer loops?

The inner loop chooses a setting within the past of the outer split. The outer loop scores a forecast produced after this choice, on a target held out from selection.

Recall 2Which date must be checked for a training target?

Its actual availability date. An old input does not allow the use of a target that is still in the future at the cutoff; the overlap exclusions also apply in the inner loop.

Recall 3What do we do after choosing B in the first split?

We refit B to the complete outer past through D6. Its final window takes D5 and D6, then we forecast D7 before revealing its closing price.

Recall 4Why can B and then A contribute to the same outer score?

The score evaluates a fixed procedure that repeats selection within the available past of each split. It does not measure a single setting chosen once across all tests.

Recall 5When can a former test observation become part of the past data?

In a later split where it is already available. Its earlier forecast and error remain frozen; it does not authorize changing the candidates or rules by inspecting the outer results.
