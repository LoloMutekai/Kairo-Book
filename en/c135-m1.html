Faces

Your goalDistinguish portrait comparison, familiarity and identification, then explain why geometric resemblance does not prove identity.

Before you start
c083-m5 : transfer · c086-m5 : emotion and cognition · c056-m5 : effect size

✿01 See the idea

You are welcoming participants to a workshop. Someone arrives; her face seems familiar, but you cannot recall her first name. On the table, a badge shows a photo of Nora. You can match the face to this photo, recall a meeting or retrieve a name: these answers do not provide the same information.

Detection means noticing that an image contains a face. Matching means judging whether images show the same person, even if you do not know her. Familiarity is the feeling of having seen her before. Identification here means linking this face to a known person and information about her; recalling her first name is an additional answer. Saying “she organizes our workshop” can therefore provide information about her identity even when her name is unavailable.

To compare portraits, look at the features, such as the eyes and mouth, and their arrangement, meaning their relative positions. A description of a face must also account for changes in the image: size, lighting, viewpoint or expression. The person remains the same when her photo is enlarged. A representation useful for identification must be able to link different appearances to this person.

The scene, portraits and dimensions that follow are fictional. We isolate a uniform enlargement to understand the difference between image and identity. The proportion calculation is a geometric teaching model; on its own, it does not describe human recognition. We derive no performance score from it.

The rule to remember

First name the task: detect a face, compare images, feel familiarity or identify a person. Then check what the clues actually allow you to conclude.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Nora’s badge changes size

The printed badge shows an eye spacing of 24 mm and a height from the eye line to the centre of the mouth of 30 mm. The team enlarges the whole photo by a factor of 2. Let us calculate what changes, what stays constant and what this allows us to say about the person.

  1. Specify the task and the proposed mechanism

    For now, we are comparing the badge with its enlarged copy. We know from how it was made that it is the same photo. This task does not require knowing Nora. Recognizing a familiar person adds a link to a representation stored in memory; simply comparing images does not demonstrate this link.

    To analyse recognition, distinguish a visual description of the features and their arrangement, their matching to what has been stored in memory, and then the information about the person that you can retrieve. These functions are useful for asking questions about behaviour. The diagram does not assume that they always occur separately or in a rigid order.

  2. Define the measurements and their limits

    e is the horizontal distance between the centres of the eyes, in millimetres on the print. h is the vertical distance from their line to the centre of the mouth, in the same unit. a is the enlargement factor, which is unitless and strictly positive. e′ and h′ are the distances after enlargement; the prime mark indicates the new image.

    We assume exactly known landmarks, a strictly positive h and the same enlargement in all directions. There is no change in pose, expression or perspective. These assumptions allow us to multiply all lengths by a; a photo taken from another angle would not be covered by this model.

  3. Construct a relation between features

    The ratio r = e/h describes eye spacing relative to the chosen height. Dividing millimetres by millimetres cancels the unit. The result is 4/5, or 0.80 with no unit. This is a geometric proportion, rather than a probability of recognizing Nora.

    r=eh=2430=0.80

    Division lets us compare a relation rather than just the printed size. r retains only a single relation between landmarks: all texture, the other features and their other positions remain outside the calculation.

  4. Enlarge the lengths, then divide again

    The operation e′ = a × e gives 2 × 24 = 48 mm. Similarly, h′ = a × h gives 2 × 30 = 60 mm. The printed distances increase together.

    r′=aeah=eh=r

    Since a is nonzero, we cancel the same factor in the numerator and denominator. Numerically, 48/60 = 4/5, or 0.80 with no unit. The ratio is invariant under this transformation: its value stays the same.

  5. Check with another operation

    We can check the equality of the ratios without dividing again. The cross products must agree: e′ × h = 48 × 30 = 1440 mm²; e × h′ = 24 × 60 = 1440 mm². They are equal. The difference r′ − r is indeed 0, with no unit.

    Another check: dividing each new length by 2 gives 24 mm and 30 mm again. With a = 1, the distances would remain unchanged. These checks concern the calculation and the enlargement, without measuring recognition ability.

  6. Return to the person and to memory

    We have shown that a geometric relation can remain stable while the image changes size. The same person in different images therefore cannot be reduced to a copy of each printed distance. But finding the same r is not enough to identify Nora: other faces may share this ratio, and our calculation leaves out a great deal of information.

    At the workshop check-in, “this face looks familiar” reports familiarity; “this is the person I prepared this workshop with” adds information linked to her identity. Reading “Nora” on the badge can provide a name without proving that you retrieved it from memory. Note the source of the answer before talking about recognition.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculation. Explain what enlargement preserves and what the ratio leaves out. Then describe how you could compare the badge with the visitor without knowing Nora, or recognize her role at the workshop without recalling her first name.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If the face looks familiar to me, I know who it is.”

    Separate the feeling of familiarity from the information that identifies the person. In the scene, look for what you actually retrieve about Nora and what you simply read on her badge. State only what is available.

    Revisit the questions in the first diagram

  • “The ratio is the same: it must be Nora.”

    The calculation preserves only a single relation between a few landmarks. Different faces can have the same ratio; a photo of the same person can also be distorted. Review the assumptions before drawing a conclusion about identity.

    Test the conclusion in the last exercise

  • “I succeeded with the name on the badge: my memory for faces is good.”

    The badge provides external help. To examine memory, we must distinguish what comes from the face and previous encounters from what the written name or the context provides. An assisted answer tells us about this situation, without establishing independent ability.

    Reread where the name and information came from

Exercise 1 · Identify the task

Reading the badge supplies the first name

At the workshop check-in, you think “I have seen her before”. You retrieve nothing else. Then you read “Nora” on the badge. What shows familiarity? What provides the name? Has independent retrieval of identity been established?

Hint 1

Separate what was available before looking at the badge from what appears after reading it.

Compare with the solution

“I have seen her before” expresses familiarity. The name is provided by the writing. These observations do not establish that you retrieved Nora’s identity from your memory of her face. Nor do they prove a general inability to do so: the task described only allows a conclusion about this attempt.

To prepare a more informative comparison, collect the judgement about the face and the information retrieved separately before showing the name. A single answer, even a correct one, does not constitute a complete assessment of memory.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish familiarity from retrieval of information about the person.
  • Attribute the name to its written source.
  • Limit the conclusion to the attempt described.

Exercise 2 · Calculate and explain

An even larger print

Start again from the original badge, with e = 24 mm and h = 30 mm. The printer enlarges it uniformly by a factor of 3. Before calculating, predict whether r changes. Give the new distances and ratio, then explain the limit of this preservation.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Multiply each length by the enlargement factor.

Hint 2

In the quotient of the new lengths, cancel the common factor.

Compare with the solution

The ratio must stay constant. The new spacing is 3 × 24 = 72 mm. The new height is 3 × 30 = 90 mm. Their quotient gives 72/90 = 4/5, or 0.80 with no unit.

Preservation is guaranteed by applying the same factor to the lengths. It verifies a relation in the photo; it does not guarantee that this ratio can distinguish Nora from everyone else.

Check your reasoning

  • Predict preservation before dividing.
  • Give 72 mm and 90 mm, then 4/5 with no unit.
  • Justify the common factor and distinguish geometric preservation from identity.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

The same photo is stretched

The badge software stretches Nora’s photo by a factor of 3 horizontally and 2 vertically. A colleague announces: “The ratio has changed, so it is no longer Nora. And any face with a ratio of 4/5 must be hers.” Calculate the new ratio, then examine each conclusion.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Eye spacing follows the horizontal factor; the height down to the mouth follows the vertical factor.

Hint 2

Does transforming the image change the person it shows? Is a single ratio enough to describe all her features?

Compare with the solution

The horizontal spacing becomes 72 mm and the height becomes 60 mm. The quotient is 72/60 = 6/5, or 1.20 with no unit. The increase relative to the original badge is 6/5 − 4/5 = 2/5, or 0.40 with no unit.

The first conclusion confuses the image with the person: we stretched the same photo of Nora without changing her identity. The preservation demonstrated in the lesson assumed an identical factor in all directions; this assumption is violated.

The second conclusion is also unjustified: different faces can share the same proportion. More information is needed to distinguish them. In addition, 1.20 exceeds 1: this geometric ratio is not a probability of identity.

Check your reasoning

  • Apply the appropriate factor to each direction.
  • Obtain 6/5 and a change of 2/5, with no unit.
  • Refute each conclusion with its reason: image transformation, then insufficient information.
  • Do not replace the ratio with a recognition score.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Can you compare portraits without knowing the person?

Yes. Matching asks whether the images show the same person. Recognizing someone familiar adds a link to what has been stored in memory about that person.

Recall 2Why does “this face looks familiar” not establish identity?

Familiarity reports a feeling of a previous encounter. Identification requires linking this face to a person and information about her. A name read on a badge must be distinguished from a name retrieved from memory.

Recall 3What does the arrangement of features describe in our portrait?

The relative positions of the features, for example eye spacing divided by the height down to the mouth. This ratio describes only a small part of the face.

Recall 4Why does uniform enlargement preserve e/h, and what does this proof establish?

The same positive factor multiplies the numerator and denominator, so it cancels out. This proves that this ratio is preserved under this transformation. It does not prove identity, because different faces can share the ratio.

Recall 5How can you distinguish a retrieved name from one that is simply available?

Specify what the person can answer before reading the badge or receiving a hint. A correct answer after this help remains an assisted answer and does not prove independent ability.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct what happens at the workshop check-in: what task is required, what features and relations are available, what image change is applied, then what information comes from memory or from the badge. Prove again that the ratio is preserved and state a limit that prevents this from serving as proof of identity.
