Constraints

Your goalCheck that a proposed point for a total variance curve satisfies its constraints, find its exact minimum variance, and repair a violation while knowing what the repair moves.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

An optimizer searches for the five numbers that bring a total variance curve close to market quotes. It tries a point, examines the discrepancy, then tries another. Nothing prevents it from proposing a point whose curve falls below 0 or whose wing rises too steeply. A constraint is a condition that every proposed point must satisfy before its discrepancy from the quotes counts.

Imagine an asymmetric bowl. Total variance is volatility squared multiplied by a duration: it cannot be negative. The bottom of the bowl is therefore the point to watch. But it is not necessarily where we usually look, because its position and height depend on several parameters at once.

In this entirely fictional scenario, the proposed point pushes the bottom below 0. We detect this with an exact formula, confirm it by a second method, then repair it. Repairing a parameter has a cost: the entire curve moves, not just its bottom.

There are three remedies: reject the point, bring it back into the feasible region through a repair or projection, or change variables so that the point stays in the feasible region automatically. This page practices the last two on a small example. It teaches neither a complete optimization algorithm nor all the conditions for absence of arbitrage.

The rule to remember

Before fitting a curve, check the simple bounds, wing slopes and exact minimum variance; if you repair a parameter, state what the repair moves.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A proposed point with negative minimum variance

For a fictional slice, the optimizer proposes a = -0.04, b = 0.2, ρ = -0.6, m = 0.02 and σ = 0.1. All these numbers are fictional and dimensionless. Let us check the constraints, locate the minimum, check it by another method, then repair the point.

  1. Define the model, symbols and assumptions

    k is the logarithm of the ratio of the strike price K to the forward price F; it is called log-moneyness. w is total variance: annualized implied volatility squared, multiplied by the maturity T in years. k and w are dimensionless. We consider only a single maturity.

    w(k)=a+b(ρ(k−m)+(k−m)2+σ2)

    The five parameters are dimensionless. a shifts the entire curve vertically, in the same units as w. b controls the steepness of the wings. ρ tilts the curve to one side or the other. m shifts it along k. σ rounds the bottom; it is a width in k, not a volatility, despite the letter.

    Five constraints restrict the point: σ > 0; −1 < ρ < 1; b ≥ 0; the wing slopes p+ = b(1 + ρ) and p- = b(1 − ρ) are at most 2; the minimum variance w* ≥ 0, where w* denotes the minimum of w over all values of k. Negative variance would have no real implied volatility.

    Assumptions: the bound on wing slopes is accepted here without proof, as a rule of the scenario; the values are fictional; no market quotes are fitted.

  2. Check the simple bounds

    Start with the constraints that concern only one parameter. σ = 0.1 is strictly positive. For ρ, calculate two factors: 1 + ρ = 0.4 and 1 − ρ = 1.6. Both are positive, so ρ = -0.6 remains strictly between −1 and 1. Finally, b = 0.2 is positive. These three checks pass; we reuse the two factors for the wings.

  3. Check the wing slopes

    Far from m, the square root of (k − m)² + σ² behaves like the absolute value of k − m. On the right, the curve becomes nearly a straight line with slope p+; on the left, its slope has absolute value p-.

    p+=b(1+ρ),p−=b(1−ρ)
    p+=0.2×0.4=0.08
    p−=0.2×1.6=0.32

    Both slopes are well below the bound 2. Check: p+ + p- must equal twice b, since the terms in ρ cancel; indeed, 0.08 + 0.32 equals twice 0.2.

  4. Find the exact minimum

    Set u = k − m and g(u) = ρu + √(u² + σ²), so that w = a + b·g. Since b ≥ 0, finding the minimum of w amounts to finding the minimum of g. The derivative of g is:

    g′(u)=ρ+uu2+σ2

    It is 0 when u divided by √(u² + σ²) equals −ρ. Squaring gives u² = ρ²σ² / (1 − ρ²), and the sign of u is opposite to that of ρ. At the resulting point, √(u² + σ²) = σ / √(1 − ρ²), giving g = σ√(1 − ρ²). The second derivative of g is strictly positive: g is convex, and this point is its global minimum.

    u*=−ρσ1−ρ2
    w*=a+bσ1−ρ2

    The minimum is therefore at k* = m + u*. Its value w* depends on a, b, ρ and σ, but not on m: this is why the constraint on w* couples these parameters.

  5. Calculate the position and value of the minimum

    Square root factor: (1 − ρ)(1 + ρ) = 1.6 × 0.4 = 0.64, and 0.8 × 0.8 = 0.64, so √(1 − ρ²) = 0.8.

    Position: ρσ = -0.06, then ρσ divided by the square root is -0.075. Thus u* = 0.075 and:

    k*=m+u*=0.02+0.075=0.095

    Value: bσ = 0.2 × 0.1 = 0.02, then multiplying by the square root gives 0.016. Thus:

    w*=-0.04+0.016=-0.024

    Verdict: w* is negative. The most subtle constraint is violated while the other four are satisfied.

  6. Check by direct evaluation

    Evaluate w at k*, without the closed-form formula. Here u* = 0.075, so ρu* = -0.045. The square root of u*² + σ² is 0.125: its square does give u*² + σ², which the calculation file for this unit checks exactly. Sum: g = -0.045 + 0.125 = 0.080. Then b × g = 0.016 and w(k*) = -0.04 + 0.016 = -0.024.

    This is the value -0.024 from the closed-form formula: the two methods agree. At the center m, u is 0 and w(m) = a + bσ = -0.020, higher than w*, as expected for a minimum located elsewhere. Testing only m would therefore have underestimated the violation.

  7. Repair by increasing a

    We want w* ≥ ε, with ε = 0.001, a fictional margin that avoids exactly 0 variance. Since w* = a + bσ√(1 − ρ²), we only need to set:

    a=ε−bσ1−ρ2=0.001−0.016=-0.015

    The optimizer proposed -0.04, so we increase a by 0.025. Take care: a is added to w at every k. The entire curve rises by 0.025, not just its bottom; if market quotes were being fitted, the discrepancy would change everywhere. This is also not the Euclidean projection, meaning the nearest feasible point: we have changed only one coordinate.

  8. Check after the repair and state the limits

    σ, ρ and b have not changed: their bounds and the slopes 0.08 and 0.32 are unchanged. The minimum remains at k* = 0.095, because k* does not depend on a, and its value is now:

    w*=-0.015+0.016=0.001

    The point satisfies our five constraints. This says nothing about the quality of the fit to market quotes, the complete absence of arbitrage, or the behavior of an optimizer restarting from this point.

Explain it in your own words

Why is the minimum of w not simply the value at the center m? And why does increasing a shift the entire curve, even far from the bottom?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A negative parameter a makes the curve invalid.”

    Only the minimum variance w* ≥ 0 matters. In the repaired example, a = -0.015 is negative and the point is feasible, because the term bσ√(1 − ρ²) = 0.016 offsets it. The constraint couples a with b, ρ and σ.

    Review the steps on the minimum and the repair

  • “Testing the variance at the center m or at quoted strikes is enough.”

    The minimum is at k*, often elsewhere, and may fall between two quoted strikes. In the exercise where ρ changes, w(m) = 0.005 is positive while w* = -0.003 is negative. Use the exact formula for the minimum.

    Redo the exercise with the changed ρ

  • “If the five constraints hold, the surface is free of arbitrage.”

    These constraints monitor the nonnegativity and wing steepness of a single slice. They cover neither the implied density, known as the butterfly condition, nor consistency across maturities, known as the calendar condition. Treat them as an initial filter, not a complete proof.

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

A wing that is too steep

Keep ρ = -0.6 but suppose b = 2 (a fictional point). Calculate both wing slopes, state which one violates the bound 2, then give the largest value of b allowed by this ρ.

p+=b(1+ρ),p−=b(1−ρ)
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate 1 + ρ and 1 − ρ, then multiply each by b.

Hint 2

The largest value of b is the one that makes the steeper slope exactly equal to the bound.

Compare with the solution

Factors: 1 + ρ = 0.4 and 1 − ρ = 1.6. Slopes: p+ = 2 × 0.4 = 0.8 and p- = 2 × 1.6 = 3.2.

The left wing violates the bound (3.2 > 2) because ρ is negative: the factor 1 − ρ is larger. The right wing satisfies the bound.

Largest value: b = 2 ÷ 1.6 = 1.25. Check: 1.25 × 1.6 = 2, and the other slope remains below the bound. The b in the example, 0.2, is far below this limit.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the two factors before the slopes.
  • Identify the side that violates the bound using the sign of ρ.
  • Find the limit by dividing the bound by the larger factor.
  • Check the limit by multiplying it back by the factor.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis with explanation

When ρ changes, a stays fixed

The optimizer keeps a = -0.015 (the repaired point), b and σ, but changes ρ to -0.8. Calculate the new minimum variance. Is the point still feasible? Compare it with the value at the center m, then repair a with the same margin ε = 0.001.

w*=a+bσ1−ρ2
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The factor √(1 − ρ²) decreases as ρ moves away from 0; what does this do to the term added to a?

Hint 2

The new factor is a positive number whose square is (1 − ρ)(1 + ρ). Calculate it, then multiply it by bσ = 0.02.

Compare with the solution

New factor: 0.6, because 0.6 × 0.6 = (1 − ρ)(1 + ρ). Term added to a: 0.02 × 0.6 = 0.012.

w* = -0.015 + 0.012 = -0.003: negative, so the point is no longer feasible. Yet w(m) = a + bσ = 0.005 remains positive: testing only the center would have missed the violation.

Repair: a = 0.001 − 0.012 = -0.011. Check: -0.011 + 0.012 = 0.001. Changing ρ alone was enough to violate the constraint: a and ρ are indeed coupled.

Check your reasoning

  • Recalculate the factor with the new ρ.
  • Compare w* with 0, not w(m).
  • Repair a while keeping the margin ε.
  • Explain the coupling between a and ρ.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

Choose d instead of a

Instead of searching for a, the optimizer searches for d, the intended value of w*, with the single rule d ≥ ε. Keep b, ρ and σ from the point in the example and take d = 0.03, a fictional value. Calculate a, then check that the minimum does equal d. Next, double b while keeping d fixed: what happens to a? What advantage does this variable give the optimizer, and at what cost?

a=d−bσ1−ρ2
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Reuse the product bσ√(1 − ρ²) already calculated in the example.

Hint 2

When b doubles, this product also doubles: σ and ρ have not changed.

Compare with the solution

a = d − bσ√(1 − ρ²) = 0.03 − 0.016 = 0.014. Check: a + bσ√(1 − ρ²) = 0.014 + 0.016 = 0.03.

With b doubled, the product is 0.032 and a = 0.03 − 0.032 = -0.002. This a is negative, yet the point is feasible: only w* matters.

With d as the variable, the coupled constraint becomes a simple bound on d: whatever b, ρ and σ are within their own bounds, the associated a keeps w* = d. The cost: the curve changes away from its bottom, so the discrepancy from market quotes must be recalculated, and there is no reason to assume it improves.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover a as d minus the product.
  • Check using the formula for w*.
  • Accept a feasible negative a.
  • Name the advantage, a simple bound, and its cost, a curve that changes elsewhere.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What are the five constraints on a raw slice in this unit?

σ strictly positive; ρ strictly between −1 and 1; b nonnegative; wing slopes b(1 + ρ) and b(1 − ρ) at most 2, a bound accepted here; minimum variance w* nonnegative.

Recall 2Where is the minimum of total variance, and what is its value?

At k* = m − ρσ / √(1 − ρ²), with w* = a + bσ√(1 − ρ²). It depends on a, b, ρ and σ, but its value does not depend on m.

Recall 3What does increasing a move?

The entire curve, by the same amount at every k, not just its bottom. Changing variables to d = w* avoids having to repair the point afterward.

Recall 4Is a slice that satisfies these constraints free of arbitrage?

These constraints alone do not establish this: they cover neither the implied density (butterfly condition) nor the calendar condition across maturities.

Recall 5Is the σ of the curve a volatility?

No. It is a dimensionless width in k that rounds the bottom; implied volatility is derived from w and the maturity.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define k, w and the five parameters; state the five constraints; check the simple bounds; calculate both wing slopes; locate the exact minimum k* and calculate w*; confirm by direct evaluation at k*; if w* is negative, repair a with a margin and state that the entire curve moves; do not conclude that arbitrage is absent.
