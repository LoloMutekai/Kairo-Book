Level 2 · C139·M5

Anomalies

Your goalDetect an unusual cycle using a standardized deviation, justify the threshold, and distinguish flagging a cycle from proving a fault.

Before you start
c137-m5 : choosing the rank · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c067-m5 : pipeline · c139-m1 : k-means · c139-m2 : Gaussian mixtures · c139-m3 : EM · c139-m4 : choosing the number of groups

✿01 See the idea

A small machine repeatedly cuts the same part. Earlier cycles judged to be typical took 8 s, 8 s, 12 s and 12 s. A new cycle takes 16 s. Before looking for a fault, one question comes first: does this duration differ greatly from what the machine usually does?

Here, an anomaly is an observation that does not fit well with the chosen reference behavior. It may result from an incident, an incorrect reading or a legitimate change of task. Without a reliable label identifying the cause, the calculation detects a deviation; it does not establish what happened on its own.

The mean gives the center of the durations. The standard deviation gives a scale for their variation: being a few seconds above this center means different things depending on whether typical cycles are tightly clustered or widely spread. The score will count the deviation in units of standard deviation.

All values in this scene are fictional. The short history makes every operation checkable with pencil and paper; it cannot validate an industrial detector. We keep the same machine throughout the exercises and initially assume a stable task, without mixing its operating regimes.

The rule to remember

Choose a comparable reference kept separate from the cycle being assessed; measure the deviation relative to its spread; apply a declared threshold; examine the flagged cycle before concluding what caused it.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Should we examine the cycle of 16 s?

The reference contains 4 durations: 8 s, 8 s, 12 s and 12 s. The current cycle of 16 s stays outside this reference. For this exercise, the rule flags a deviation in either direction as soon as its magnitude reaches the threshold τ = 2.5, set before calculating the score.

  1. Define what is being compared

    rᵢ denotes the duration of reference cycle i, in seconds; i runs from 1 to n. n is the number of reference cycles, here 4. x is the duration of the cycle to examine, here 16 s. μ is the reference mean, in seconds; v is its descriptive variance, in s²; σ is its standard deviation, in seconds. The Greek letters μ, σ and τ are read as “mu”, “sigma” and “tau”.

    We assume comparable readings: the same task, settings, unit and timing method. The reference is taken to represent the regime being studied and is fixed before examining x. This representativeness is an assumption of the exercise, rather than an established fact about a real machine.

    z will be the signed deviation expressed in standard deviations; |z| will be its magnitude, without the sign. τ is a positive threshold with no unit. An absolute value keeps the distance from the center, whether the duration is shorter or longer. Division requires σ to be strictly greater than 0.

  2. Find the center of the durations

    We add the durations and divide by their count to give each cycle equal weight. The symbol ∑ asks us to add the terms for all the indicated indices. Pairing a short and a long duration gives 8 + 12 = 20 s; the 2 pairs give 40 s.

    μ=∑i=1nrin=404=10 s

    The mean must lie between 8 s and 12 s. Here it is in the middle because each duration occurs the same number of times.

  3. Build the scale of variation

    The deviations from the center are -2 s, -2 s, 2 s and 2 s. Their sum is 0: adding them would not measure the spread. Squaring makes all their contributions positive or 0.

    v=∑i=1n(ri−μ)2n

    Here, each squared deviation is 4 s² for the short durations and 4 s² for the long ones. Their sum is 16 s², so v = 16 ÷ 4 = 4 s².

    We describe the spread of this reference using the divisor n. The divisor n − 1 would serve a different convention: estimating a population variance without bias under sampling assumptions. Keep the same convention throughout the calculation.

    σ=v=4=2 s

    We take the positive square root to return to seconds. Check: 2 s × 2 s = 4 s². For this particular symmetric reference, σ is also half the range: (12 − 8) ÷ 2 = 4 ÷ 2 = 2 s. This last equality is not a general formula for all histories.

  4. Express the deviation in the right unit

    We subtract the center to measure what is different about this cycle, then divide by σ to relate this deviation to the usual variation. This is the standardized score z.

    z=x−μσ=16−102=62=3

    The raw deviation is 6 s; it corresponds to 3 standard deviations above the center. The seconds in the numerator and denominator cancel. Dividing by the variance, in s², would leave a unit of inverse seconds: that would be a different score.

  5. Apply the stated threshold

    Our convention is to flag a cycle when |z| ≥ τ. The symbol ≥ includes equality. The threshold τ = 5/2 = 2.5 is a fictional choice for this exercise, with no promised error rate. With |z| = 3, the cycle is flagged.

    |z|≥τ⇔x≤μ−τσ ou x≥μ+τσ

    The symbol ⇔ indicates equivalent conditions. In the original unit, the radius is τσ = 2.5 × 2 s = 5 s. The boundaries are 10 − 5 = 5 s and 10 + 5 = 15 s. We therefore flag a duration at most equal to 5 s or at least equal to 15 s. Our duration of 16 s does exceed the upper boundary.

    Increasing τ reduces flags for fixed scores, but may let incidents pass undetected. Choosing a real threshold requires a separate validation period with verified events and an explicit cost of errors. Another period is then reserved for final evaluation, without tuning the threshold on it. Standardization alone gives no probability of a fault and does not require a normal distribution.

  6. Check without repeating the same division

    For a positive variance, we can compare squares. Call the squared score D; D and τ squared have no unit. Since |z| and τ are positive or 0, the comparison keeps the same direction.

    D=z2=(x−μ)2v;|z|≥τ⇔D≥τ2

    Here, the numerator is 6 × 6 = 36 s². So D = 36/4 = 9. The squared threshold is 25/4, or 6.25. We again obtain 9 ≥ 6.25, consistent with the standardized score and the boundary expressed in seconds. This form will avoid an irrational square root in the last exercise.

  7. State exactly what the flag establishes

    The precise conclusion is: “the cycle of 16 s deviates from the reference according to our rule”. The cause still needs to be checked in the task performed and in the reading. A false flag would be a flagged cycle judged typical after investigation; a missed incident would be a verified incident that the rule did not flag. We do not have the verified events needed to measure these errors.

    If the machine alternates between a fast task and a slow task, their overall mean may fall between groups of durations that are each typical. We then need a reference suited to the known regime, or a model able to represent several groups. The score on this page describes a single quantity in a single regime; it does not replace the group models in the previous units.

    If v = 0, then σ = 0 and this score is undefined. We need to examine measurement precision and choose an appropriate rule, rather than report a result from an impossible division. A reference that is very short, contaminated or out of date remains a limitation even when division is possible. Keep the raw record for the investigation: flagging a cycle does not authorize deletion of the reading.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The score proves that the machine has a fault.”

    A high score describes a deviation from a reference. Check the task, settings and timing before looking for its cause. The score is not a probability of a fault; a low score does not guarantee the absence of an incident either.

    Reread what the flag establishes at the end of the example

  • “I can divide by the variance instead of the standard deviation.”

    The raw deviation is in seconds: divide by σ in seconds to obtain z with no unit. Here, 6/4 gives 1.5 per second, whereas 6/2 gives 3 with no unit. The score D does use the variance, but with a squared deviation in the numerator.

    Review standardization and the check using squares

  • “I add the suspect cycle to the reference before comparing it: that will be more precise.”

    The cycle then shifts the center and increases the spread against which it will be compared. It contributes to its own justification. Keep a separate reference for the current decision; later adaptation requires an explicit rule and validation.

    Calculate the contaminated reference in the last exercise

Exercise 3 · Analysis on paper · check a procedure

The cycle hides in its own reference

Return to the initial task. Someone adds the cycle of 16 s to the durations 8, 8, 12 and 12 s before testing it. Calculate the new mean μ′, then the descriptive variance v′ using the identity “mean of the squares minus square of the mean”. Keep exact fractions. Calculate D′ = (x − μ′) squared divided by v′ and compare it with τ squared. What methodological error does this decision reveal? The prime here denotes the expanded reference, with no change of regime.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

There are now 5 durations; their sum is 40 + 16. The cycle being tested is included in this sum.

Hint 2

The sum of squares is 2 × (8 × 8 + 12 × 12) + 16 × 16. Divide it by the new count, then subtract the square of the new mean.

Hint 3

To compare with the threshold, use D′ and τ squared: no square root of the new variance is needed. The threshold stays the same.

Compare with the solution

The count becomes 5. The sum is 40 + 16 = 56 s, so μ′ = 56/5 = 56/5 s, or 11.2 s. The center moves closer to the cycle being tested.

The squared durations are 64, 64, 144, 144 and 256 s². One squared short duration and one squared long duration give 64 + 144 = 208 s². The 2 pairs in the reference give 416 s²; adding 256 s² gives 672 s². Their mean is 672/5 s². The square of μ′ is 3136/25 s².

Subtracting with a common denominator gives v′ = 672/5 − 3136/25 = 224/25 s², or 8.96 s². This identity comes from expanding the squared deviations: the mean of the durations is exactly μ′. It gives the same variance as directly summing the squared deviations and dividing by 5.

The current deviation is 16 − 56/5 = 24/5 s. Its square is 576/25 s². Thus D′ = (576/25)/(224/25) = 18/7 ≈ 2.57, with no unit. The squared threshold remains 25/4, or 6.25. Since D′ is below this threshold, the contaminated procedure no longer flags the cycle.

With the separate reference, D was 9 and the cycle was flagged. The observation has not changed: adding it shifted the center and inflated the spread. This reversal illustrates masking through contamination, without proving a general failure rate. Remedy: decide using the earlier reference, keep the cycle and its context, then handle any model update separately.

Check your reasoning

  • I find μ′ = 56/5 s and v′ = 224/25 s² without rounding during the calculation.
  • I compare D′ = 18/7 with 25/4, without comparing it directly with τ.
  • I explain the change in decision through the reference, without changing the cycle or the threshold.
  • I propose separating the current decision from an update to the reference.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Relative to what is a duration abnormal, and what do we need to know about the context?

It deviates from a reference chosen for a task and a regime. We need comparable readings and an identified context; a legitimate change of task may change the relevant reference.

Recall 2Reconstruct z from a duration x, a mean μ and a standard deviation σ. Why this denominator?

z = (x − μ)/σ, with positive σ. We center the duration, then count its deviation in standard deviations. x, μ and σ are in seconds: z has no unit. The variance is in s² and cannot replace σ in this formula.

Recall 3What does |z| ≥ τ mean, including at the boundary? What conclusion goes beyond this rule?

The magnitude of the score reaches or exceeds the stated positive threshold: we flag the cycle, including equality. This gives neither its cause nor a probability of a fault; the absence of a flag does not certify correct operation.

Recall 4Why keep the cycle being assessed out of the calculation of its reference?

Otherwise it may shift the center and increase the spread against which it will be compared. Its score may decrease and the flag disappear. We separate the current decision from later adaptation that is explicitly validated.

Recall 5How can we check the flagging decision without taking a square root? What condition makes this check possible?

For positive v and positive τ, calculate D = (x − μ) squared divided by v, then compare D with τ squared. This is equivalent to comparing |z| with τ. If v is 0, this division and the standardized score are undefined.
