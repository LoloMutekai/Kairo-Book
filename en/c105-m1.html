Level 3 · C105·M1

Queries, keys and values

Your goalSeparate what searches, what is compared and what is passed on, then work through an attention calculation on a short sequence.

Before you start
c104-m5 : temporal validation · c102-m5 : finite difference verification · c050-m5 : conditioning

✿01 See the idea

Imagine the sequence “bike fixed”. We want to enrich the representation of “fixed” with information taken from this same sequence. At this position, the query expresses what the calculation is looking for. Each position presents a key to be compared with the query and a value to contribute to the result.

The key helps decide how much weight to give a piece of information; the value is the information that is then mixed. Like a label and the contents of a card, they have distinct roles. In a network, they are numerical vectors, rather than written words or labels that a person has chosen by hand.

We call the position of “bike” A and the position of “fixed” B. Our 2 fictional representations are x_A = (1, 2) and x_B = (1, 6), with no physical unit. They are chosen for pencil calculations; no coordinate is assigned any real linguistic meaning here.

In our small model, the keys will be identical but the values will differ. The query at B will therefore give the same weight to both positions while receiving different contributions. This is exactly what lets us see why a key cannot replace a value.

The rule to remember

Form queries, keys and values; compare the query with the keys; turn the scores into weights; apply these weights to the values.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What “fixed” receives from the sequence

Let us calculate only the output at B in single-head attention. Positions A and B are both accessible, including B to itself. The representations and coefficients are fictional and fixed for this exercise. We are aiming neither to train a model nor to produce a sentence.

  1. Set out the conventions and dimensions

    A row vector is a horizontal sequence of coordinates. X stacks the vectors x_A and x_B: one row per position. n is the number of positions; d is the size of an input representation. Here n = 2 and d = 2. All coordinates and coefficients have no physical unit.

    yygcvg hibobv okbz The letter W denotes the coefficients; the subscripts Q, K and V indicate their roles. In a trained model, these coefficients can be learned. Here they are given and shared across positions, with no term added after multiplication.

    Q=XWQ;K=XWK;V=XWV

    d_k is the common size of queries and keys; d_v is the size of values. xvlywd gomqvq hvhwnf ahuhfa evpltt fhuavh aajb Q and K have n rows and d_k columns; V has n rows and d_v columns. d_v can differ from d_k: only queries and keys must have equal sizes for our comparison.

    Here d_k = 1 and d_v = 1. Each row of Q, K or V therefore contains a single number. q_A and q_B denote the rows of Q; k_A and k_B those of K; v_A and v_B those of V. The common source X makes this operation self-attention: a sequence queries its own representations.

  2. Read the given transformations

    X=[1216];WQ=[01];WK=[10];WV=[11]

    Multiplying a row by a column means multiplying corresponding coordinates and then adding the products. besycl mpomkq kwmacn cqmqps qgmyki yyytje lrzt These choices make the roles visible without claiming to represent coefficients from a real model.

  3. Form queries and keys

    For A, q_A = 1 × 0 + 2 × 1 = 2, while k_A = 1 × 1 + 2 × 0 = 1. The same representation provides different outputs because different matrices are applied to it.

    For B, q_B = 1 × 0 + 6 × 1 = 6 and k_B = 1 × 1 + 6 × 0 = 1. The keys are equal because the retained coordinate is equal at both positions. This is not a general rule of attention.

    We keep q_B to calculate the output at B. q_A would be used in the separate calculation of the output at A. A position can therefore offer a key while also carrying its own query.

  4. Prepare what can be passed on

    With W_V, v_A = 1 × 1 + 2 × 1 = 3 and v_B = 1 × 1 + 6 × 1 = 7. The values are not the keys: although k_A = k_B, v_A differs from v_B.

    With X fixed, changing only W_V changes the available content without changing the queries or keys in this calculation. This separation will make the last exercise possible.

  5. Compare the query with the keys

    For each position j, here A or B, s_j is the score used for the output at B. The dot · denotes the dot product: we add the products of corresponding coordinates. The square root √d_k supplies the comparison scale for this form of attention. We provide this rule; justifying its statistical basis is beyond our calculation.

    sj=qB·kjdk

    Since d_k = 1, √d_k = 1. What remains is a multiplication followed by division by 1: s_A = (6 × 1)/1 = 6; s_B = (6 × 1)/1 = 6. These scores have no unit, are not yet weights and are not used directly to multiply the values.

  6. Turn scores into exact weights

    α_A and α_B, read as “alpha A” and “alpha B”, are the mixing weights for B. The function exp is the exponential function: it gives a strictly positive number and exp(0) = 1. Exponential normalization divides each exponential by the sum of the exponentials.

    αj=exp(sj)exp(sA)+exp(sB)

    To avoid calculating large exponentials, subtract the same number m = max(s_A, s_B) = 6 from both scores. max denotes the largest score. This divides the numerators and denominator by exp(m) without changing the weights. The centered scores are s_A − m = 0 and s_B − m = 0.

    αA=αB=exp(0)exp(0)+exp(0)=12=0.50

    The equality of the scores makes this calculation exact using fractions. We have not replaced exponential normalization with division of the raw scores. If the scores differed, we would need to calculate their relative exponentials; a simple average would no longer be justified.

  7. Mix the values and name the output

    y_B denotes the attention output at position B. It has the size of the values, so 1 coordinate here. Each value is multiplied by its weight, then the contributions are added.

    yB=αAvA+αBvB=0.50×3+0.50×7=5

    Contribution from A: 3/2 = 1.50; contribution from B: 7/2 = 3.50. Their sum is 5, with no physical unit. Identical weights therefore do not imply identical contributions. The result is a numerical representation; it is neither the word “fixed” nor a written answer.

  8. Check the calculation and define its limits

    The weights are positive and their sum is 1. The scalar output must remain between 3 and 7. Another calculation recovers the average: (3 + 7)/2 = 10/2 = 5. This check confirms the aggregation rather than a linguistic interpretation.

    Equal keys explain equal scores, which explain equal weights. The magnitude 6 of an isolated score measures neither the truth of a word nor its absolute relevance. The weights distribute a mixture across the accessible positions in this calculation.

    We have followed a single output, with fixed coefficients and no visibility restriction. A complete Transformer adds other operations; multiple heads and positions belong to the following units. This example does not establish how well a model understands language.

Exercise 2 · Controlled variation

Does another query necessarily change the weight distribution?

In the same sequence, replace only W_Q with the column (2, 0). uzmfji uidkpe zwqu Calculate the new query at B, its scores, then the weights and output. Respond to the claim: “Changing a query always changes the weights.”

Hint 1

The new query at B is calculated using the first coordinate; the keys remain identical.

Hint 2

Center the new scores on their maximum. If they are equal, the exponentials of the centered scores are equal.

Compare with the solution

The new query is 1 × 2 + 6 × 0 = 2. Since √d_k = 1, the scores are 2 × 1 = 2 for A and 2 × 1 = 2 for B.

Subtracting their maximum gives 0 at both positions. Each weight remains 1/2 = 0.50, and the output remains 5. The scores have changed; their distribution after normalization has not changed.

The claim is therefore false: our equal keys provide a counterexample. With different keys, changing the query could change their relative scores. A change of query alone is not proof of a change in weights.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 2 for the query and 2 for each score.
  • Justify the weights using the equality of the scores without directly dividing the raw scores.
  • Give the specific counterexample rather than an opposite universal rule.

Compare with the solution

Q and K remain unchanged, so the scores remain 6 and 6, and the weights remain 1/2 and 1/2. The separation holds for this calculation with fixed inputs.

New value at A: 1 × 1 + 2 × 2 = 1 + 4 = 5. New value at B: 1 × 1 + 6 × 2 = 1 + 12 = 13.

Contributions: 1/2 × 5 = 5/2 and 1/2 × 13 = 13/2. Their sum gives 9. Check: the equal weights require the midpoint between 5 and 13, namely 9; this result remains between the two values.

Changing only the content passed on can therefore change the output without changing the weight distribution. An increase in the output number proves no linguistic improvement. In a complete network, this output could change the inputs to a later calculation; we have not fixed the entire network forever.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What searches, what is compared and what is passed on?

The query q carries the search from the target position; the key k at each position enters the comparison; the value v at each position enters the weighted mixture. These are roles of vectors, not necessarily texts.

Recall 2How can a single sequence provide different queries, keys and values?

Its representations X pass through distinct matrices: Q = XW_Q, K = XW_K, V = XW_V. Sharing the input does not require sharing the transformations. In the example, the key retains one coordinate and the value adds the coordinates.

Recall 3Which sizes must match, and what size is the output for one position?

Query and key have the same size d_k for their dot product. Values have size d_v, which can differ. The mixture of values retains d_v coordinates. The number of positions indicates how many keys and values are offered.

Recall 4Why are the weights equal in our sequence, and why does this not make the values identical?

The identical keys give identical scores for the query at B. After centering, each exponential is 1, so the weights are 1/2 and 1/2. The values 3 and 7 remain different; their mixture gives 5.

Recall 5With the input fixed, what can W_V change without changing the weights in this calculation?

W_V can change the values and their mixture. The weights depend on Q and K, which remain fixed here. The last exercise thus moves from 5 to 9 with the same weights, without demonstrating an improvement in the model.
