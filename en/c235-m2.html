Level 4 · C235·M2

Tensor products

Your goalBuild a weight kernel on pairs of time slots, recover its Hilbert space norm and calculate its symmetric part without confusing it with a dot product.

Before you start
c230-m5 : regularity and embeddings · c227-m5 : Ito-Tanaka · c231-m5 : geodesics · c165-m5 : duality · c235-m1 : Gaussian noise

✿01 See the idea

Return to the idea of a measurement line subject to noise. In this fictional scene, we keep only time slots A and B, each lasting τ = 1 s. A first set of weights u has value 1 on A and 2 on B; a second set v has value 3 on A and -1 on B. These deterministic, dimensionless weights are invented for the calculation.

We now want to assign a weight to each pair of time slots: the first choice comes from u, the second from v. For the pair (A, B), we multiply the weight of u on A by the weight of v on B. Keeping all the pairs gives us a table rather than a single number. Here this table represents a tensor product.

The order of the choices remains visible: (A, B) carries -1, while (B, A) carries 6. If the problem no longer distinguishes this order, an additional operation is needed: replace each pair of swapped cells by their average. This is symmetrization; it does not happen automatically.

The Gaussian noise in the previous unit associated a random variable W(h) with a set of weights h. Here we work on the weights themselves, before any random draw. The kernel on pairs is deterministic. The random objects organized from such kernels will be introduced with Wiener chaos.

The rule to remember

The tensor product keeps the choices separate. The dot product contracts them into a number. Symmetrizing means averaging over the orders, using the norm of the ordinary tensor product.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From weights on time slots to weights on their pairs

Time slots A and B form an interval I. They are disjoint except at their endpoints and have the same duration τ = 1 s. The functions u and v are constant on each slot, with the values shown in the figures. Let us build k = u ⊗ v and calculate its squared norm and that of its symmetric part S. All the data remain fictional.

  1. Specify the space, symbols and units

    H = L²(I, ds; ℝ) is the real space of functions whose square is integrable on I, identifying functions that differ only on a set of duration 0. s and t are times in seconds; ds and dt are elements of duration. The letters h and g denote deterministic, dimensionless weights. Their dot product is the integral of their product, and their norm is the square root of their dot product with themselves.

    ⟨h,g⟩H=∫Ih(s)g(s)ds;‖h‖H2=⟨h,h⟩H

    The dot product and squared norm are in s; the norm is in √s. A and B are names of time slots rather than numerical values. The weights u_A, u_B, v_A, v_B are their respective values. No result assumes an actual measurement of the noise.

  2. Build the Hilbert tensor product

    The symbol u ⊗ v first denotes a pure tensor. The algebraic construction allows finite sums of these symbols and imposes bilinearity: (a h + b g) ⊗ v = a(h ⊗ v) + b(g ⊗ v), with the same rule in the second factor. a and b are dimensionless real scalars. This relation allows a scalar factor to be moved from one side of the ⊗ sign to the other.

    We define the dot product of pure tensors by the following rule, then extend it to sums by bilinearity. Here h, g, p and q belong to H; p and q are simply two other sets of weights.

    ⟨h⊗g,p⊗q⟩=⟨h,p⟩H⟨g,q⟩H

    Completion in the associated norm adds the limits of Cauchy sequences: this gives H ⊗ H, called the Hilbert tensor product here, also written as the tensor power 2 of H. In our L² model, it is identified with L²(I × I, ds dt; ℝ) through (h ⊗ g)(s, t) = h(s)g(t). The integral of the square separates into a product of integrals, so this identification preserves the norm. Sums of such functions are dense in the space on I × I, which gives the identification after completion.

    Here a kernel is a function of (s, t). Its values are dimensionless, but its squared norm is in s², because the integral is over two durations. Our calculation takes place in the subspace of kernels that are constant on each rectangle of time slots; it does not reduce all of H to these slots alone.

  3. Calculate each pair before taking any sum

    The row is chosen by s, hence by u; the column by t, hence by v. Multiply the values for each pair: k_AA = 1 × 3 = 3; k_AB = 1 × (-1) = -1; k_BA = 2 × 3 = 6; k_BB = 2 × (-1) = -2. The concatenated indices name the row followed by the column.

    k=u⊗v↔[3-16-2]

    This table represents the kernel on our rectangles, with this order of rows and columns. It can also be calculated as the product of the column of u values and the row of v values. The transpose swaps rows and columns: it represents v ⊗ u, namely the kernel kᵀ(s, t) = k(t, s). The sign ᵀ denotes this swap. Since -1 differs from 6, the two kernels differ.

  4. Predict the norm from the factors

    For a function that is constant on a time slot, the integral of its square is its squared value multiplied by the duration. For u, the squares are 1 and 4, with sum 5. Thus ‖u‖² = 5 × 1 = 5 s. For v, the squares are 9 and 1, giving ‖v‖² = 10 × 1 = 10 s.

    ‖u⊗v‖2=‖u‖H2‖v‖H2=5×10=50 s²

    This rule follows directly from the definition of the tensor dot product. It predicts a squared norm; the norm itself is √50 s. We keep the squares to calculate exactly, without rounding a square root.

  5. Check on the rectangles and distinguish the scalar

    Each rectangle has a time area τ × τ = 1 s². The squares of the cells are 9, 1, 36 and 4. The first row contributes 10 and the second 40; the integral of the square is therefore (10 + 40) × 1 = 50 s². We recover the product of the squared norms by another route.

    For the dot product ⟨u, v⟩_H, multiply the weights on the same time slot, then add them with a single duration: (3 + (-2)) × 1 = 1 s. This contraction gives a scalar. It preserves neither the off-diagonal cells nor their positions, and its unit differs from that of the squared tensor norm. In this representation by constant functions, it can be read from the diagonal of the table; this does not define a trace for all L² kernels.

  6. Symmetrize while keeping the same norm convention

    A symmetric kernel satisfies S(s, t) = S(t, s) almost everywhere, meaning except on a set of area 0. The space of these kernels is the closed subspace called the symmetric tensor power of order 2 of H. It keeps the dot product of H ⊗ H, with no additional factor.

    H⊙2⊂H⊗2=H⊗H
    S=k+kᵀ2=u⊗v+v⊗u2

    The diagonal keeps 3 and -2. Both off-diagonal cells become (-1 + 6)/2 = 5/2 = 2.5. Their sum has been preserved, but their difference has disappeared.

    Swapping the variables preserves the dot product, and repeating the swap returns the original kernel. The remainder R = k − S changes sign under this swap, while S remains unchanged. Thus ⟨S, R⟩ equals its own opposite, so it is 0 s². S is the orthogonal projection of k onto the symmetric kernels; in particular, ‖k‖² = ‖S‖² + ‖R‖².

  7. Calculate the symmetric norm and check the decrease

    The diagonal cells contribute 9 + 4 = 13. Each off-diagonal cell contributes the square of 5/2, namely 25/4. There are 2 of them, so their contribution is 25/2. The squared norm of S is (13 + 25/2) × 1 = 51/2 s², or 25.5 s².

    Check using the dot product of pure tensors: ⟨u ⊗ v, v ⊗ u⟩ = ⟨u, v⟩². When expanding the norm of the numerator of S, the norms of k and kᵀ are equal, as are the two cross terms. This leaves:

    ‖S‖2=‖u‖2‖v‖2+⟨u,v⟩22=50+12=51/2 s²

    The agreement between the calculations checks the average and its normalization. The decrease is 50 − 51/2 = 49/2 s². This is ‖R‖², rather than a measured improvement in the noise. It is positive here because the off-diagonal weights were different; if k were already symmetric, S would equal k.

  8. Place tensor sums and the rest of the chapter in context

    An arbitrary tensor is not necessarily pure: sums and limits are part of the construction. fzzaqk dilczl yyegup muwmas jzoluf nhfsvo msvjis vxqkru frbtly xijuxd jwwvxt wtihmd tjdlzq qj dxlsvq yailzy gyiuwv trgixi upwdxh cjfrdf vfkabq yzxqxq zossqi viglhe pqflaz nwstkj eymofk sz A nonzero result therefore rules out a pure factorization. The last exercise applies this check to S.

    More generally, for a strictly positive integer q, we complete products of q factors. Their symmetric subspace is invariant under all permutations of the factors. The notation is:

    H⊙q⊂H⊗q

    In the L² model, these are respectively symmetric kernels in q variables and arbitrary kernels in q variables, with the product measure of the durations. Our calculation covers the case q = 2. It prepares the kernels of Wiener chaos, without yet constructing that chaos, a derivative or a divergence.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The ⊗ sign asks us to multiply corresponding components and add them.”

    That sum calculates the dot product. For u ⊗ v, keep each pair of time slots, including the off-diagonal pairs: you must be able to identify the values -1 and 6 separately. The contraction to 1 s has already lost this information.

    Revisit the construction of the cells, then the scalar check

  • “u ⊗ v and v ⊗ u are equal because numbers can be multiplied in any order.”

    The swap also affects the positions: u(s)v(t) becomes v(s)u(t). The values at the same coordinates (A, B) are -1 and 6. There is a natural swap of the factors, but this swap is not the identity. Symmetrization averages these distinct objects.

    Compare the off-diagonal cells and their average

  • “A symmetric kernel must be a single product h ⊗ h and keep the norm of the original kernel.”

    Symmetry imposes equality after swapping, rather than a factorization. Here the squared norm changes from 50 to 51/2 s² because an antisymmetric component is removed. The determinant proposed in the last exercise tests factorization separately from symmetry.

    Test factorization in the transfer exercise

Exercise 2 · Calculation · two checks

Check a claimed symmetrization

Someone keeps the diagonal cells of k, then replaces the off-diagonal cells by their sum 5. They claim to have obtained S. Repair the table, calculate its squared norm in s² and check it using ‖u‖², ‖v‖² and ⟨u, v⟩. Is the sum of the off-diagonal cells enough to guarantee the correct result?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

An average requires dividing the sum by the number of terms.

Hint 2

After the repair, count both off-diagonal cells in the sum of squares, even though they are equal.

Compare with the solution

Replace 5 by 5/2 = 2.5 in each off-diagonal cell. The diagonal remains (3; -2). The table becomes invariant under transposition: its cells (A, B) and (B, A) are equal.

Direct calculation: the diagonal cells contribute 13; the off-diagonal cells together contribute 25/2. Multiplying their sum by 1 s² gives 51/2 s², or 25.5 s². Check: (5 × 10 + the square of 1)/2 = 51/2 s².

Equal sharing and preserving the sum are two different conditions. Distributing the same sum unequally can change the norm and lose symmetry. Here the average preserves the off-diagonal sum 5 and makes the two cells equal.

Check your reasoning

  • Repair the missing division and write the same average in each off-diagonal cell.
  • Include all cells and their time area in the norm.
  • Recover 51/2 s² by both routes and explain what symmetry checks.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · answer an objection

Does symmetric mean pure?

Someone claims that S must be h ⊗ h because it is symmetric. Calculate Δ = S_AA S_BB − S_AB S_BA. Here the symbol Δ denotes this dimensionless determinant. Use the factorization criterion explained in the example, then state what this test lets you conclude about where S belongs in the tensor space.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

axiuid hmqcqu bnwagw xdqzqy vpgjrq sliwun bzkj

Hint 2

The diagonal product is 3 × (-2). The off-diagonal product is the square of 5/2.

Compare with the solution

The diagonal product is -6. The off-diagonal product is 25/4. Their difference is Δ = -6 − 25/4 = -49/4, which is not 0. The table of S therefore cannot be written as a single product of two sets of weights that are constant on the time slots.

This also rules out a factorization by arbitrary functions of H: replacing each factor by its average on each time slot would give a pure product of functions that are constant on each slot. On each rectangle, the product of the averages is the average of the product, because the variables are separate. This operation would leave S unchanged; its table would therefore have to satisfy the criterion that has already been disproved.

S nevertheless remains a tensor, defined by the sum (u ⊗ v + v ⊗ u)/2. It belongs to the symmetric subspace of H ⊗ H without being pure. The test specifically refutes the claim "symmetric implies h ⊗ h".

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain Δ = -49/4 and justify why a pure tensor would give 0.
  • Distinguish the symmetry property from the possibility of a factorization.
  • Explain why a sum of pure tensors is a tensor even if it is not pure.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does u ⊗ v preserve that the dot product of u and v does not?

A weight for each ordered pair of time slots. In our model, (u ⊗ v)(s, t) = u(s)v(t); the dot product integrates products taken at the same time and returns a single number.

Recall 2How do we pass from pure tensors to the Hilbert tensor product?

Form their finite sums with the bilinearity relations, define ⟨h ⊗ g, p ⊗ q⟩ = ⟨h, p⟩⟨g, q⟩, then complete in the associated norm. Completion includes limits as well as pure products.

Recall 3How can we recover the squared norm of our kernel by two routes, with their units?

Multiplying ‖u‖² = 5 s by ‖v‖² = 10 s gives 50 s². Adding the squares of all the cells, each multiplied by the area 1 s², gives the same result. The product rule applies to a pure tensor.

Recall 4What operation gives the symmetric part, and what happens to the norm?

Take S = (k + kᵀ)/2. This is an orthogonal projection for the ordinary tensor norm: the antisymmetric component is removed. Here ‖S‖² = 51/2 s², less than 50 s². An already symmetric kernel is preserved.

Recall 5Why is the symmetric kernel S not necessarily of the form h ⊗ h?

Symmetry imposes only invariance under swapping. A symmetric sum may fail to factorize. Here its table has a nonzero determinant -49/4, whereas every pure product would have determinant 0.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the figures, redraw the time slots, then their table of pairs. Define the tensor product through its factors, bilinearity and norm; explain what completion adds. Recover the norm from the factors and from the rectangles. Finally, reconstruct the average that symmetrizes, then give a reason why symmetry and factorization are two different questions.

What you understand
A tensor kernel keeps separate positions; a dot product returns a number. Symmetrization is a projection, and a symmetric tensor can be a sum that cannot be factorized. These distinctions concern the mathematical objects, before any application to noise.
