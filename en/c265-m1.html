MDP

Your goalDescribe a small sequential decision problem as a finite Markov decision process, then propagate the state distribution exactly and calculate the discounted return over two days for a fixed action rule.

✿01 See the idea

A fictional office ficus is in one of two states each morning: dry or moist. Each morning, we choose an action: water or wait. During the day, chance moves the soil into one of the two states for the next morning, and we record vitality points, a reward invented for this scene. The probabilities and points are neither measured values nor recommendations for a real plant.

A finite Markov decision process, or MDP, organizes this small setting into five ingredients: the states S, the actions A, the transition probabilities P, the reward R and the discount factor γ. For each state–action pair, the transition table gives the probability of each possible state the next day. The reward gives the points for that day and pair. The factor γ weights a reward one day later.

The Markov assumption is a modeling assumption, rather than a fact about the ficus: today’s state and action suffice to predict tomorrow, and earlier history adds nothing. If history mattered, it would have to be included in the state.

This unit deliberately keeps the problem small. We do not look for the best action rule or learn a rule: we fix a rule, then track exactly the probability of being dry or moist and the points we expect over two days.

The rule to remember

Read an MDP in two steps: the chosen rule determines the action from the state, then the table row for that pair distributes the probability of the next state. An expected reward weights the points by the probability of each state; one day later, it is also weighted by γ.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The fictional ficus over two days

The ficus is dry on day 0. The fixed rule is: water if dry, wait if moist. Using the table below and γ = 0.90, let us calculate the state distribution on days 1 and 2, then the discounted return over days 0 and 1. All values are fictional.

  1. Define the model and its assumptions

    S is the finite set of states, here dry and moist. A is the set of actions, here water and wait. Time advances in whole days. P(s′ ∣ s, a) is the unitless probability, between 0 and one, that the next day’s state is s′ when today’s state is s and the action is a. R(s, a) is today’s reward, in fictional vitality points. γ is a unitless number between 0 and one, the weight for one additional day.

    P(s′∣s,a)=probabilité que l’état du lendemain soit s′

    To avoid working with decimal separators in the table, probabilities are written as percentages; we divide them by 100 when calculating.

    couple (état, action)vers secvers humiderécompense(sec, arroser)20 %80 %1(sec, attendre)90 %10 %0(humide, arroser)10 %90 %2(humide, attendre)40 %60 %3

    Assumptions: finitely many states, observed without error; the same table each day; today’s state and action suffice to predict tomorrow; a known reward for each pair. Without these assumptions, the following formulas do not apply as written.

  2. Check the table rows

    A table row describes all possible next days for a given pair. It must therefore distribute all the probability: the sum over the next states equals one.

    P(sec∣s,a)+P(humide∣s,a)=1
    P(humide ∣ sec, arroser) =80100=0.80

    For the chosen rule, we obtain 0.80 and 0.20 for watering a dry ficus, giving a sum of 1. For waiting when the ficus is moist, 0.60 and 0.40 give a sum of 1. Both rows used are valid.

  3. Day 0 to day one: reward and distribution

    On day 0, the ficus is dry with probability 1.00. The rule requires watering. The expected reward is the state’s probability multiplied by the points for the pair.

    récompense attendue du jour 0=1×1=1

    The (dry, water) row then distributes the probability: 1.00 × 0.80 = 0.80 chance of being moist, and 1.00 × 0.20 = 0.20 of staying dry. The sum remains 1, which checks this step.

  4. Day one: a weighted average of rewards

    The ficus is now dry with probability 0.20 and moist with 0.80. The rule requires watering in the first case and waiting in the second. We weight each reward by the probability of its state.

    récompense attendue du jour 1=0.20×1+0.80×3=0.20+2.40=2.60

    The expected reward for day 1 is therefore 2.60 points. This is an average under the model, rather than a score we will obtain exactly on an actual day.

  5. Discount and add

    Day 1 counts less than day 0: we multiply its reward by the unitless factor γ = 9 ÷ 10 = 0.90. The discounted return over two days adds the two expected rewards with these weights.

    G=r0+γr1=1+0.90×2.60=1+2.34=3.34

    The result is 3.34 fictional points. The factor 0.90 reduces the contribution of 2.60 to 2.34.

  6. Distribution on day two

    To obtain the probability of being dry on day 2, we add two paths: dry then dry with watering, moist then dry with waiting. Each path multiplies the probability on day 1 by the appropriate table row.

    sec au jour 2=0.20×0.20+0.80×0.40=0.04+0.32=0.36
    humide au jour 2=0.20×0.80+0.80×0.60=0.16+0.48=0.64

    The four paths have weights 0.04, 0.32, 0.16 and 0.48. Their sum, 0.36 + 0.64 = 1, equals one: no probability has been lost or invented.

  7. Check by another method

    We manually listed the four two-day trajectories with their probabilities; the unit’s automated check repeats this enumeration using exact fractions and obtains the same return 3.34 and distribution on day 2. A simple check: without discounting to weight day 1, the sum 1 + 2.60 would be greater than 3.34, and also greater than the return for γ = 0.50.

    This check verifies the arithmetic of a declared model. It shows neither that the probabilities describe a real ficus nor that the rule is good.

Explain it in your own words

Explain in your own words why each table row must sum to one, and what changes in the next day’s distribution if it does not. Also explain why the day-one reward is weighted twice: by the state’s probability and then by γ.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Tomorrow’s reward depends on the ficus’s entire history.”

    In the model, the table row uses only today’s state and action. If earlier information really matters, it must be included in the state description; this is a modeling choice that needs justification, never a fact to take for granted.

    Reread the Markov assumption

  • “A table row that sums to ninety percent is still usable; the rest is negligible.”

    A row must distribute all the probability. With missing mass, the next day’s distribution no longer sums to one and the averages are distorted. We must decide where the missing probability comes from, rather than silently renormalize it.

    Return to the exercise on the incomplete row

  • “The discounted return is what the ficus will actually yield.”

    The calculated return is an average under a fictional model. A single actual trajectory can give a very different total. The factor γ measures nothing: it weights days by their position in time, with a weight of one for day 0.

    Review the return calculation

Exercise 1 · Explained application

A valid table row?

A colleague proposes the following row for the (dry, wait) pair: 10 % to moist and 80 % to dry. Is the row valid? How much probability is missing? Compare it with the row in the example’s table. The values are fictional.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the two proposed percentages and compare the result with 100 %.

Hint 2

Find the (dry, wait) row in the example’s table and add its two percentages.

Compare with the solution

Proposed sum: 10 + 80 = 90 %. The missing probability is 100 − 90 = 10 %, so the row is invalid: one possible next day out of 100 falls outside the model.

In the example’s table, the (dry, wait) row gives 10 % to moist and 90 % to dry, totaling 100 %. The proposal therefore only has a percentage for dry that is too low. Increasing all percentages without justification would change the model; first decide where the missing probability goes.

Check your reasoning

  • Sum calculated as a percentage and compared with one hundred.
  • Missing probability identified and quantified.
  • No silent renormalization.

Exercise 2 · Explained application

Same rule, moist initial state

Use the example’s table and rule again, with γ = 0.90, but start with a moist ficus on day 0. Calculate the expected reward for day 0, the distribution on day 1, the expected reward for day 1 and the discounted return. Fictional values.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

When the ficus is moist on day 0, the rule requires waiting: read the (moist, wait) row.

Hint 2

The day 1 distribution is exactly the row for this pair.

Hint 3

Weight the reward for each state by its probability, then weight day 1 by γ.

Compare with the solution

The ficus is moist with probability 1.00 and the rule requires waiting: the expected reward is 1.00 × 3 = 3 points.

The (moist, wait) row gives, on day 1: dry with 0.40, moist with 0.60. The expected reward contributions are 0.40 × 1 = 0.40 for dry (water) and 0.60 × 3 = 1.80 for moist (wait), totaling 2.20 points.

Return: 3 + 0.90 × 2.20 = 3 + 1.98 = 4.98 fictional points. This exceeds the example’s return, 3.34, because the score on day 0 is higher in this model; this says nothing about a real ficus.

Check your reasoning

  • Correct action for the state on day 0.
  • Day-one reward weighted by the probabilities.
  • Discounting applied exactly once to day one.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

Varying the weight of the next day

Use the example’s dry initial state again, but take γ = 1 ÷ 2 instead of 0.90. Calculate the new two-day return and its difference from 3.34. What would the return be if γ were 0? What can you conclude about the importance of the next day?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The expected rewards 1 and 2.60 do not change: only the weight of day 1 changes.

Hint 2

When γ is 0, day 1 disappears from the return.

Compare with the solution

With γ = 0.50, day 1 contributes 0.50 × 2.60 = 1.30. The return becomes 1 + 1.30 = 2.30 points.

The difference from the example is 3.34 − 2.30 = 1.04 points: the expected rewards are positive, so stronger discounting can only reduce the return.

With γ = 0, only day 0 counts and the return is 1. A smaller factor makes the model more impatient; this is a declared evaluation choice rather than a property of the plant.

Check your reasoning

  • Only the weight of day one changes.
  • Difference calculated with the sign indicated.
  • The case of 0 γ interpreted as a single day.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What are the ingredients of a finite MDP?

States, actions, a table of transition probabilities for each state–action pair, a reward for each pair and a discount factor γ.

Recall 2What must each row of the transition table satisfy?

The probabilities of the next states must be between 0 and one and sum to one; otherwise, part of the next day is lost or invented.

Recall 3What does the Markov assumption promise in this model?

Today’s state and action suffice to distribute the probability of tomorrow’s state. If earlier history matters, it must be included in the state.

Recall 4What does γ do in the two-day return?

It weights the expected reward of the next day: one for day 0, γ for day one. It is not a measured rate.

Recall 5Does this calculation find the best rule or prove a real gain?

No. It evaluates a fixed rule under a model declared to be fictional; choosing a better rule and validating it outside the model belong to other steps.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Name the states, the actions, the transition table with each row summing to one, the reward for each pair and the factor γ; fix an action rule; propagate the distribution day by day by adding paths; weight the rewards by the distribution and then by γ; check the probability mass and retain the fictional, unvalidated status.
