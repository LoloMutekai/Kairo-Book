Level 3 · C174·M3

Forward equation

Your goalAdvance a probability density using the Fokker–Planck equation, then check a diffusion step through its fluxes, total mass and positivity.

Before you start
c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c167-m5 : initial and boundary value problem · c173-m5 : completeness · c174-m1 : generator · c174-m2 : backward equation

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a tracer in a closed channel. Its random motion makes its future position uncertain. Mentally repeat the same experiment: the question becomes “in which part of the channel will we find it?”. We will follow this probability distribution without choosing a particular trajectory.

The middle of the channel initially holds more probability than the sides. With no current, diffusion spreads this distribution: the middle gives up probability to its neighbours. Reflecting walls prevent any leakage. The diagram compares probability masses in sections of equal length.

The forward equation, also called the Fokker–Planck equation, advances the distribution from an initial distribution. The generator acts on functions of position; the forward equation uses its adjoint, the operator obtained by moving the derivatives onto the density in an integral. The backward equation studied earlier concerns a conditional expectation function. The unknowns differ; this does not mean reversing the direction of trajectories.

All dimensions and probabilities in our channel are fictional. The step shown is a numerical approximation calculated below, rather than a measurement of a real tracer’s motion. No Feynman–Kac result is needed in this unit.

The rule to remember

Make the same quantity enter and leave at each interface; preserve a total mass of one and nonnegative masses. For variable coefficients, differentiate the products with the density.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Diffuse the distribution in the closed channel

The channel runs from x = 0 m to x = 6 m. Divide it into 3 cells A, B and C of width h = 2 m. The drift is 0, the constant diffusivity is D = 1/4 m²/s and the time step Δt is 4 s. The initial masses are M_A = M_C = 1/4 and M_B = 1/2. We will calculate a mass-conserving step without a new observation.

  1. Define the unknown and the interior model

    geytps cbujqm zdzlyd qrymmm idctrd pjvqzr gisj The density p(t,x), in m⁻¹, gives a probability when integrated over an interval of positions. It is nonnegative and its integral over the channel is 1. A density value at a point is not itself a probability.

    dXt=b(t,Xt)dt+σ(t,Xt)dWt

    This Ito formulation describes motion in the interior. b is the drift in m/s; W_t is Brownian motion whose increments have variance equal to the corresponding time interval, so W has units of s½; σ has units of m/s½. Set a = σ × σ, in m²/s. For our channel, b = 0 m/s, a = 1/2 m²/s and D = a / 2 = 1/4 m²/s. The walls also require a reflection condition; the interior formulation alone does not provide it.

    For the differential form that follows, assume that the coefficients and density are regular enough to differentiate and integrate by parts in the interior at strictly positive times. A density does not automatically exist for every stochastic equation; here the diffusivity is strictly positive and constant.

  2. Move the derivatives onto the density

    Let f be a smooth, dimensionless test function that vanishes near the walls. ∂t and ∂x indicate derivatives with respect to time and position; ∂xx means differentiating 2 times with respect to x. The generator L_t acts as L_t f = b ∂x f + (a / 2) ∂xx f. rtezhm bjbxds rvvo The integrals below are over the channel.

    ∫f∂tpdx=∫(b∂xf+a2∂xxf)pdx

    Integration by parts in the drift term introduces a minus sign; 2 integrations in the diffusion term introduce a plus sign. The test function makes the boundary terms vanish. Identifying the factor multiplying every function f gives the interior forward equation:

    ∂tp=Lt∗p=−∂x(bp)+12∂xx(ap)

    L_t∗ is the adjoint defined here by this integral identity; the star does not mean multiplication. If b or a depend on x, the derivatives act on the products bp and ap. These coefficients cannot be taken outside the derivatives as if they were constant. Each term in the equation has units of m⁻¹/s.

  3. Write the fluxes and the problem conditions

    The probability flux J is positive to the right and has units of s⁻¹: it transports probability per second across a position. The equation is a local balance, rate of change = negative spatial rate of change of the flux:

    J=bp−12∂x(ap),∂tp=−∂xJ

    In our example, b and a are constant, so J = −D ∂x p and ∂t p = D ∂xx p. The reflecting walls impose J(t, 0) = J(t, 6) = 0 s⁻¹; for this pure diffusion, this means a 0 spatial derivative at the walls, rather than a 0 density.

    To specify a complete initial condition, take p(0,x) to be constant in each cell: A between 0 and 2 m, B between 2 and 4 m, C between 4 and 6 m. Its heights will be the densities calculated in the next step. The exact values at the interfaces do not change any probability. The initial jumps are interpreted through the integral balance; we do not evaluate a classical derivative there at the initial time.

    Integrating the balance over the channel gives the time derivative of the total mass as J at the left wall minus J at the right wall, which is 0 s⁻¹. The boundary condition completes this check.

  4. Convert masses to average densities

    bwuagi gqauam cpwzkh ijfdjh ktwzkn wvvnfh snvx jzudtv ormyyc axnf We divide by the width because mass is the area under the density, rather than its height.

    At the sides: ρ_A = ρ_C = 1/4 / 2 = 1/8 m⁻¹, or 0.125 m⁻¹. At the centre: ρ_B = 1/2 / 2 = 1/4 m⁻¹, or 0.250 m⁻¹. The initial check gives 2 × 1/4 + 1/2 = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1.

    We now use a finite volume scheme: each cell maintains a mass balance. The interface flux will be approximated from neighbouring average densities, whose cell centres are separated by h. This calculation does not claim to give the exact flux of the continuous solution arising from the initial jumps.

  5. Calculate the fluxes with a common orientation

    Let J_AB denote the numerical flux between A and B, always positive to the right. The approximate gradient is (ρ_B − ρ_A) / h: the difference is 1/4 − 1/8 = 1/8 m⁻¹, then 1/8 / 2 = 1/16 m⁻². Multiplying by −D gives the flux:

    JAB=−DρB−ρAh=−1/4×1/16=-1/64 s⁻¹

    Between B and C, the gradient has the opposite sign: J_BC = 1/64 s⁻¹. The negative sign of J_AB indicates a transfer towards A; the positive sign of J_BC indicates a transfer towards C. Both therefore carry probability out of B.

    During Δt = 4 s, the quantity transferred to each side is Δt × |J| = 4 × 1/64 = 1/16, with no units. We keep these fluxes calculated at the start of the step throughout the step: this is an explicit time step.

  6. Update each mass using its balance

    ecymrf vcmwob xdramg wdjmxc yfcfng cslhnq ezlv mebkqf rtlslg dcrn Each internal interface is used once by each neighbour, with opposite signs.

    For A: M_A⁺ = 1/4 + 4 × (0 − (-1/64)) = 1/4 + 1/16 = 5/16. For C: M_C⁺ = 1/4 + 4 × (1/64 − 0) = 5/16. The walls contribute nothing.

    For B, the outflows add up: loss = 2 × 1/16 = 1/8. Thus M_B⁺ = 1/2 − 1/8 = 3/8. This result is positive, even though the centre has lost mass.

    The new average densities are ρ_A⁺ = ρ_C⁺ = 5/16 / 2 = 5/32 m⁻¹, or 0.15625 m⁻¹; ρ_B⁺ = 3/8 / 2 = 3/16 m⁻¹, or 0.1875 m⁻¹. They describe the approximate distribution, rather than an observed position.

  7. Check using another expression for the same step

    The dimensionless number r = D Δt / (h × h) is (1/4 × 4) / 4 = 1/4. Substituting the fluxes into the balance gives another way to calculate the result:

    MB+=rMA+(1−2r)MB+rMC

    At the centre, the weights are 1/4, 1/2, 1/4: the calculation 1/4 × 1/4 + 1/2 × 1/2 + 1/4 × 1/4 again gives 3/8. On the left, M_A⁺ = (1 − r)M_A + r M_B: 3/4 × 1/4 + 1/4 × 1/2 = 5/16. Symmetry gives C.

    Global check: 2 × 5/16 + 3/8 = 5/8 + 3/8 = 1. The masses are nonnegative, and multiplying the new densities by h and adding them also gives 1. If all initial masses were equal, all gradients would be 0: the step would preserve that uniform distribution.

  8. Check the scheme’s positivity condition

    To guarantee nonnegative masses from any initial distribution on this grid, all weights must be nonnegative. At the centre, we need 1 − 2r ≥ 0, so 0 ≤ r ≤ 1/2. At the sides, 1 − r ≥ 0 is then ensured. Each row of weights sums to 1.

    The condition becomes Δt ≤ (h × h) / (2D) = 4 / 1/2 = 8 s. Our step of 4 s satisfies it. This bound applies to this constant diffusion, this grid and this explicit scheme; it is not a universal rule for all forward equations.

    Conservation alone does not guarantee positivity, as the final exercise will show. Conversely, passing these checks does not measure the error relative to the continuous solution: time step and grid refinement would need separate study. We have established a consistent numerical balance, rather than an exact solution of the PDE.

Without looking at the calculations, explain why the left flux is negative while A gains probability. Then justify why the generator and the forward equation place the derivatives differently.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I can apply the generator directly to p, even if the coefficients vary.”

    The generator differentiates the test function; its adjoint differentiates the products bp and ap. Repeat the integrations by parts and count the sign changes. The form −b ∂x p + (a / 2) ∂xx p requires coefficients independent of x.

    Review moving the derivatives

  • “A negative flux means the probability becomes negative.”

    The sign of the flux indicates a direction. In the balance for A, subtracting a negative J_AB adds mass. The final mass must remain nonnegative; it does not have the flux’s units of s⁻¹.

    Read the arrow directions again

  • “The sum is one, so my new distribution must be correct.”

    The masses must also be nonnegative. A step that is too long can preserve the sum while creating a negative mass. Even the correct sum together with positivity does not quantify the discretization error.

    Test the step that is too long

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Interpret the left outflow

In the initial channel, you are given J_AB = -1/64 s⁻¹ and 0 flux at the left wall. Calculate the change in M_A over 4 s, then its final value. Explain the sign without referring to negative probability.

Hint 1

The balance for A is the flux entering from its left minus the flux leaving from its right, using the same reference direction.

Hint 2

Multiply the flux balance by the duration; the resulting probability is dimensionless.

Compare with the solution

ΔM_A = 4 × (0 − (-1/64)) = 1/16. Thus M_A⁺ = 1/4 + 1/16 = 5/16. The flux oriented to the right is negative: the actual transport goes from B to A. A positive amount of probability therefore enters A.

Check your reasoning

  • Subtract the right flux with its sign.
  • Find 1/16 for the change and 5/16 for the final mass.
  • Distinguish flux in s⁻¹ from dimensionless mass.

Hint 1

The centre gives a fraction r to each neighbour and receives a fraction r from each of them.

Hint 2

An average density is recovered by dividing a mass by h.

Compare with the solution

B keeps the fraction 1 − 2 × 1/4 = 1/2 of its mass and receives r times each side mass. This gives 1/4 × 1/4 + 1/2 × 1/2 + 1/4 × 1/4 = 3/8. This second route confirms the flux balance.

The central density is 3/8 / 2 = 3/16 m⁻¹. The sum of the masses is 2 × 5/16 + 3/8 = 1. Adding only the density heights without their width would not be the required probability check.

Check your reasoning

  • Explain the origin of the three weights and why their sum is one.
  • Obtain 3/8 and then 3/16 m⁻¹.
  • Check the areas without confusing mass and density.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and transfer

A correct sum can hide an impossible distribution

In the same channel, keep h and D but take Δt = 12 s and another initial distribution: M_A = M_C = 0, M_B = 1. Calculate r, the new masses and their sum. Does the result define a probability distribution? Propose a repair that respects the fluxes.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Evaluate the weight 1 − 2r before any other calculation.

Hint 2

The weights coming from the centre remain r for A and C. A correct sum does not repair a negative central weight.

Compare with the solution

The new r is (1/4 × 12) / 4 = 3/4. The central weight becomes 1 − 3/2 = -1/2. Since all the mass was in B, the update gives M_A⁺ = M_C⁺ = 3/4 and M_B⁺ = -1/2.

The sum remains 2 × 3/4 + (-1/2) = 3/2 + (-1/2) = 1, but one mass is negative: this is not a probability distribution. Resetting this mass to 0 and then renormalizing would change the calculated balance. The repair for this scheme is to reduce Δt to at most 8 s and recalculate the step from the initial masses, using the same shared internal fluxes.

This counterexample refutes a positivity guarantee for the step that is too long. It does not say that every initial distribution would become negative with this step.

Check your reasoning

  • Find r = 3/4 and the central mass -1/2.
  • Observe both a sum of one and a failure of positivity.
  • Reduce the step and then recalculate from the initial state, without ad hoc clipping.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What advances in the forward equation?

The position density p(t,x), starting from an initial distribution. It is neither a particular trajectory nor the expectation function in the backward equation.

Recall 2Where do the spatial derivatives act if b and a vary with position?

On the products: ∂t p = −∂x(bp) + (1/2) ∂xx(ap), with a = σ × σ. Integration by parts introduces a minus sign in the drift term; integrating twice preserves the plus sign in the diffusion term.

Recall 3What balance connects flux, mass and reflecting walls?

The change in a mass is the duration multiplied by the left flux minus the right flux. Internal fluxes cancel in the sum; 0 fluxes at the walls complete conservation. Flux has units of s⁻¹, while mass is dimensionless.

Recall 4How do you convert a cell mass to an average density?

Divide by the width h. Recover the mass by multiplying the average density in m⁻¹ by h in metres. Normalization concerns these masses, rather than the raw sum of the heights.

Recall 5Why check the time step even if total mass is conserved?

An update can preserve the sum while creating negative masses. For our grid and explicit diffusion, the weights are nonnegative when 0 ≤ D Δt / (h × h) ≤ 1/2. This check does not measure the error relative to the continuous PDE.
