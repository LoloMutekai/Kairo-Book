Level 2 · C060·M4

Limit laws

Your goalDistinguish the concentration of a mean from the distribution of its normalized fluctuations, then derive their scale using preparation times.

Before you start
c035-m5 : finite covariance and conditioning · c058-m5 : change of variables · c046-m5 : approximation error · c060-m1 : transformations · c060-m2 : conditioning random variables · c060-m3 : generating functions

✿01 See the idea

At the same counter, we time the preparation of a drink. In our fictional scene, each preparation takes 8 s or 16 s, with equal probability. An individual preparation time therefore remains highly variable. The mean of many preparation times can still be much more stable.

Imagine several logbooks, each containing the same number of independent preparations. Each logbook gives its own mean. The law of large numbers describes their concentration around the mean preparation time of the model as the logbooks grow longer. It does not promise that the next mean will be closer to the target than the previous one.

To examine the randomness that remains, we magnify the small deviations from this target. The central limit theorem then describes a shape that approaches a normal bell curve. It concerns the mean after centering and dividing by its standard deviation; individual preparation times remain 8 s or 16 s.

The standard deviation measures spread in the units of time; the variance is its square. For a mean, this standard deviation is also called the standard error. It describes the means that repeated logbooks would produce, without giving the definite error of the current logbook. The values and the counter model are invented for the calculation.

The rule to remember

The law of large numbers locates the mean; the central limit theorem describes the fluctuations seen after rescaling them.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A logbook of preparation times, two limiting conclusions

The fictional logbook contains n = 100 preparation times, including 55 preparations taking 16 s; the others take 8 s. The model assigns each preparation time a probability of 1/2 for each value. Let us calculate the observed mean, its fluctuation scale and what the limit laws tell us about it.

  1. State what varies and what is assumed

    Xᵢ is the random preparation time of preparation i, in seconds. The index i runs through the preparations up to n, a positive integer sample size with no units. We assume these variables are independent and identically distributed: knowing the previous preparation times does not change the distribution of the next one, and this distribution does not drift through the logbook. This is an assumption of the exercise, not a property inferred from a stopwatch.

    A bar denotes the random mean X̄n. A lowercase x̄ will denote the value actually recorded in our logbook. E is expectation under the model, Var is variance and P is probability. We write μ = E(Xᵢ), in seconds, and σ for the positive standard deviation, in seconds; σ squared is the variance, in seconds squared.

    X¯n=X1+⋯+Xnn

    The sign ⋯ represents all the intermediate preparation times. Here μ and σ are known from the fictional distribution; we are not trying to estimate them from the logbook.

  2. Calculate the center and spread of a preparation time

    The values are equally likely: to find the expectation, we add their contributions with equal weights. This amounts to dividing their sum by 2.

    μ=8+162=12 s

    Each preparation time is at a distance of 4 s from μ. The signed deviations are opposite; their squares are identical. Their mean therefore gives the variance:

    σ2=(8−12)2+(16−12)22=16

    The variance is 16 seconds squared; its positive square root is σ = 4 s. This spread concerns an individual preparation time, before taking any logbook mean.

  3. Use the logbook while distinguishing observation from expectation

    There are 100 − 55 = 45 short preparations remaining. Their times total 45 × 8 = 360 s. The long preparations total 55 × 16 = 880 s. We add these times, then divide by the total number of preparations.

    x¯=360+880100=1240100=12.4 s

    The total is 1240 s. The observed mean exceeds μ by 12.4 − 12 = 0.4 s. It is indeed between 8 s and 16 s; it is not equal to the expectation of the model.

  4. Derive the exact scale of the means

    Linearity of expectation gives E(X̄n) = μ. Independence makes the covariances between distinct preparation times vanish: the variance of their sum is n times that of an individual preparation time. Dividing this sum by n divides its variance by n squared. This leaves:

    Var(X¯n)=nσ2n2=σ2n

    The square root of this variance is σ/√n. For our sample size, √n = 10, because 10 × 10 = 100. The variance of the mean is 16/100 = 4/25 seconds squared, or 0.16 seconds squared; the standard error is 4/10 = 2/5 s, or 0.4 s.

    These identities are exact under the assumptions, even before considering a normal distribution. The value 0.4 s is not a guaranteed bound on the observed deviation.

  5. Concentrate the mean: the weak law of large numbers

    Fix a strictly positive margin ε, in seconds; ε is read as “epsilon”. On the event where the distance from μ reaches this margin, the squared deviation is at least ε squared. Its expectation is therefore at least this square multiplied by the probability of the event. Dividing gives the Bienayme–Chebyshev inequality:

    P(|X¯n−μ|≥ε)≤σ2nε2→0

    The arrow applies as n tends to infinity, for each fixed ε. This is convergence in probability to μ: the probability of a deviation exceeding a fixed margin tends to 0. This version of the law of large numbers is established here with a finite variance. It does not say that all deviations eventually disappear at a given sample size.

    Take ε = 4/5 s = 0.8 s. Its square is 16/25 seconds squared. For n = 100, the bound is 4/25 divided by 16/25, giving 1/4 = 0.25 with no units. This is an upper bound supplied by the inequality under this model, not the exact probability or a frequency measured at the counter.

  6. Magnify the fluctuations: the central limit theorem

    The law of large numbers brings X̄n closer to a constant. To examine the shape of the remaining small deviations, we subtract μ, then divide by the standard error. We call this centered and standardized variable Zn:

    Zn=X¯n−μσn=n(X¯n−μ)σ

    For independent and identically distributed variables with a strictly positive finite variance, the central limit theorem states that Zn converges in distribution to the normal distribution with mean 0 and variance 1. We write it as N(0, 1). All these normalized quantities have no units.

    Precisely, for each real threshold t with no units, the probability that Zn is at most t tends to Φ(t). The letter Φ, “phi”, denotes the corresponding probability under this normal distribution; it is its cumulative distribution function.

    P(Zn≤t)→Φ(t)

    Convergence in distribution describes a distribution, not the value the next logbook will produce. The theorem is stated here; its full proof is beyond the scope of this unit. It does not require the original preparation times to be normal: those at the counter are not.

  7. Normalize the logbook mean and interpret the result

    For our observation, the realization z of Zn is calculated by dividing the deviation 0.4 s by the standard error 0.4 s. The units cancel:

    z=12.4−120.4=1

    The logbook mean is 1 standard error above μ. This result is a standardized position; it is not a probability. The standard deviation of the means has shrunk, then division has brought it back to a scale that can be compared across sample sizes.

    At any finite sample size, our mean can only take the values obtained by counting the long preparations. Its distribution therefore remains discrete. Convergence to a continuous normal distribution never gives an exact equality at a finite sample size here, nor a numerical approximation error. The next unit will study the transition from a limit distribution to a justified approximation.

  8. Check the calculation and the change in sample size

    First check: 2/5 × 2/5 = 4/25. Squaring the standard error does recover the variance of the mean. Second check: if the sample size increases from 100 to 400, it is multiplied by 4. The variance becomes 16/400 = 1/25 seconds squared, or 0.04 seconds squared.

    Since 20 × 20 = 400, the standard error becomes 4/20 = 1/5 s = 0.2 s. It is divided by 2, rather than by 4. This agrees with the widths in the first drawing.

    We have checked scales of the model. A longer logbook can still give an observed mean further from μ than the previous logbook; none of these identities guarantees an improvement with every addition.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the formulas, explain what concentrates, why we multiply the centered deviation by the square root of the sample size and why individual preparation times do not become normal. Then reconstruct the logbook calculation, distinguishing the exact identities, the bound and the limiting statement.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The law of large numbers promises that each new mean is better.”

    It concerns the probability of a deviation beyond a fixed margin as the sample size increases. A sequence of means can temporarily move away from μ. Restate the claim with ε fixed and a probability tending to 0.

    Reread the concentration argument and its bound

  • “With 100 preparations, the preparation times must be normal.”

    Each preparation time remains 8 s or 16 s. The theorem concerns the centered and standardized mean as n tends to infinity. Even the distribution of this mean remains discrete at a finite sample size; its normality is not exact.

    Revisit the variable Z and its interpretation

  • “I divide the standard deviation by n, like the variance.”

    The standard deviation is the square root of the variance. If the variance is divided by n, the standard deviation is divided by √n. Start again from the square of the standard error and compare the sample sizes in the first diagram.

Exercise 1 · Calculating the scale

A shorter logbook

Compare with the solution

The sample size does not change the distribution of an individual preparation time: σ remains 4 s. For the logbook mean, the variance is 16/25 = 16/25 seconds squared, or 0.64 seconds squared.

The standard error is 4/5 = 4/5 s = 0.8 s. Its square is 16/25, which recovers the expected variance. The logbook mean has less spread than an individual preparation time; these exact values do not require a normal approximation.

Exercise 2 · Concentration and normalization

Same deviation, different sample size

A new fictional logbook of 400 independent preparations also gives x̄ = 12.4 s. With the same margin ε = 0.8 s, calculate the Chebyshev bound, then the normalized position z. Can we say that the probability of exceeding this margin is exactly equal to this bound, or that the mean is exactly normal?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The variance of this mean is 1/25 seconds squared. Divide it by ε squared to obtain the bound.

Hint 2

The deviation from μ remains 0.4 s, but the standard error is now 0.2 s.

Compare with the solution

The bound is 1/25 divided by 16/25, giving 1/16 = 0.0625 with no units. It is below the previous bound 1/4, as the increase in sample size requires. The inequality gives an upper bound, without saying that it is attained.

The normalized position is 0.4/0.2 = 2 with no units. The same deviation in seconds represents more standard errors in a longer logbook. This observation does not violate concentration: a rarer outcome can occur.

The distribution at a finite sample size remains discrete. The central limit theorem gives convergence of distributions; it turns neither this bound into an exact probability nor this finite distribution into an exact normal distribution.

Check your reasoning

  • Divide the variance by the squared margin to obtain the bound.
  • Divide the observed deviation by the standard error to obtain z, keeping these two calculations separate.
  • Distinguish an upper bound on probability, an observed value and a limit distribution.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosing the assumptions

The logbook contains copies

The stopwatch produces a single preparation time U, equal to 8 s or 16 s with probability 1/2 each. By mistake, the logbook repeats this same value 100 times: all the Xᵢ are equal to U. Calculate X̄ₙ, its variance and the probability of being at a distance of at least 0.8 s from μ. Why do the previous conclusions no longer apply?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The sum contains n copies of U: what remains after division by n?

Hint 2

Each possible value of U is at a distance of 4 s from μ. Compare this distance with the margin.

Compare with the solution

The sum is nU, so X̄n = U. Its variance remains 16 seconds squared, instead of 4/25. The entries have the same distribution, but they are perfectly dependent: knowing the first determines all the others. The covariances are no longer 0.

The distance from μ is always 4 s, greater than 0.8 s. The required probability is therefore 1 at every sample size: no concentration around μ occurs in this construction.

The problem is not an insufficient sample size. To use the theorems stated here, we need new independent preparations under the model. Otherwise, we must explicitly study the dependence using other assumptions; extending a logbook of copies does not create new information.

Check your reasoning

  • Simplify nU/n without artificially retaining a sample size factor.
  • Identify the missing independence despite identical individual distributions.
  • Give the correct variance and probability, then name a way to repair the data or the model.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does convergence in probability of a mean to μ mean?

For each fixed positive margin ε, the probability that the distance of the mean from μ reaches this margin tends to 0. This promises neither equality to μ at a finite sample size nor improvement with every addition.

Recall 2How can we derive the variance and standard error of the mean?

Under independence and identical distributions with finite variance, the variance of the sum is n times that of an individual preparation time. Dividing the sum by n divides its variance by n squared: Var(X̄ₙ) = σ squared / n. Its square root is σ/√n, the standard error.

Recall 3Which variable converges to which distribution in the central limit theorem?

Zₙ = (X̄ₙ − μ)/(σ/√n) converges in distribution to N(0, 1), for independent and identically distributed preparation times with a strictly positive finite variance. These are the normalized fluctuations, not individual preparation times.

Recall 4Why is the Chebyshev bound not an exact probability?

It bounds the probability of a deviation of at least ε from above by σ squared / (n times ε squared). The proof uses an inequality on squared deviations; it does not calculate the whole distribution.

Recall 5Why does copying the same preparation time throughout a logbook invalidate our argument?

The mean remains the copied preparation time and retains its original spread. The covariances no longer vanish. A large number of rows therefore guarantees neither concentration nor the applicability of the central limit theorem stated here.
