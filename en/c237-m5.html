Level 4 · C237·M5

Differences between chain rules

Your goalDistinguish Gaussian differentiation from adding a point on Poisson space, then calculate and check the chain rule remainder using the same counter.

Before you start
c236-m5 : Hörmander · c228-m5 : incompleteness · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c237-m1 : Stein’s method · c237-m2 : fourth moment · c237-m3 : contractions · c237-m4 : Poisson chaos

✿01 See the idea

At a parcel depot’s reception desk, a counter records parcels arriving during a fixed observation window. We compare the count with a predicted mean, then square this normalized deviation to produce a score. This score is a mathematical object with no associated decision rule.

Imagine an additional parcel. Recalculate the score after its arrival, then subtract the previous score. The initial slope describes the change only near the starting point: on a curve, it can miss part of a finite change.

Infinitesimal differentiation instead considers a displacement whose size tends to 0. This is how Gaussian Malliavin differentiation works. The Poisson Malliavin operator studied here adds a whole point: its displacement is finite, even if normalization makes it small. The name “derivative” alone is therefore not enough to choose a chain rule.

The entire scene and all numerical values are fictional. We assume a Poisson model to explain a calculation; no depot records are provided, and no fit of this model to real arrivals has been established. The exercises use this same counter.

The rule to remember

Before composing with a function, ask which operation defines the operator: differentiating an infinitesimal perturbation, or comparing values before and after adding a point.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Add a parcel, then evaluate the square again

The model predicts λ = 4 parcels in the window; our fictional configuration contains n = 6. The scale s = √λ = 2 is used to center and standardize the count. Keep λ and s fixed during the addition and calculate the change in the square of the normalized deviation.

  1. Define the count, the function and their units

    A denotes the observation window, fixed in advance. η is the counting measure of arrivals: η(A) counts the points in A. Under the Poisson model, N = η(A) has mean and variance λ = μ(A), where μ is the intensity measure. λ is an expected number of parcels in A, not a rate per unit of time; λ is strictly positive and finite.

    F(η)=N−λs,s=λ

    F is the normalized deviation, random before the configuration is fixed; s = √λ is the standard deviation of the count. Counts are numbers with no physical dimension. F is therefore dimensionless, with mean 0 and variance 1 under this model. Our transformation is φ(u) = u × u, for a dimensionless real argument u; φ(F) is also dimensionless. The prime in φ′ or φ″ will indicate an ordinary derivative.

    Here we use polynomials of a Poisson count with finite intensity: their squares and the squares of their add-one differences are integrable. They therefore belong to the domain of the operator under consideration. For a general function, the square integrability of the function and of its difference must be checked separately, averaging over configurations and added points.

  2. Choose the add-one operator

    z is an arrival location in A. δ_z denotes a counting mass that adds exactly 1 point at that location. For any functional H, meaning any real-valued function of a configuration η, we choose the following convention:

    DzPH=H(η+δz)−H(η)

    P identifies Poisson calculus here; z identifies the added point. There is no division by a step size and no passage to a limit. Adding the point does not change the model’s intensity λ. Set d = D_z^P F: this is the dimensionless normalized increment produced by this addition.

    This convention is the add-one difference associated with Poisson chaos calculus. It does not describe every possible operator on jump processes: another directional calculus requires its own definitions and rules.

  3. Calculate the states before and after

    First check the scale: 2 × 2 = 4 = λ. Initially, N − λ = 6 − 4 = 2; dividing by s gives F = 2/2 = 1. The initial score is 1 × 1 = 1.

    The addition in A increases N to 6 + 1 = 7 parcels. The deviation from the same mean becomes 7 − 4 = 3, and F after the addition is 3/2 = 3/2 = 1.5. Thus d = 1/2 = 1/2 = 0.50; we recover F + d = 1 + 1/2 = 3/2.

    Normalization does not create half a parcel: the count has increased by a whole parcel. It is the dimensionless number F that has increased by 1/2.

  4. Evaluate the composition again before subtracting

    DzPφ(F)=φ(F+d)−φ(F)

    Evaluate the square at the endpoint: 3/2 × 3/2 = 9/4 = 2.25. Then subtract the initial square to isolate the change rather than the final value: 9/4 − 1 = 5/4 = 1.25, dimensionless. This composition identity follows directly from the definition of D_z^P; it does not even require φ to be differentiable.

  5. Compare with Gaussian differentiation

    To make the comparison precise, let G be a Gaussian coordinate with mean 0 and variance 1. In this single coordinate, Malliavin differentiation along a constant direction a is written D_a^G H(G) = lim as ε tends to 0 of [H(G + εa) − H(G)]/ε. ε is a dimensionless real parameter; a is the fixed amplitude of the direction, also dimensionless. The superscript G indicates Gaussian calculus here, not a power.

    DaGφ(G)=φ′(G)DaGG=φ′(G)a

    This is an equality of derivatives for admissible compositions. Here, polynomials of the Gaussian coordinate are admissible; for a general composition, differentiability and membership in the domain must be justified. To compare the expressions at the same numerical point, evaluate them at g = 1 with a = d = 1/2. This does not mean conditioning G on a single value.

    The slope of the square is φ′(g) = 2g; at our point it is 2. The Gaussian chain rule term is therefore 2 × 1/2 = 1. The finite change on Poisson space is 5/4. Expanding the square explains the difference:

    (F+d)2−F2=2Fd+d2

    The linear term is 1 and the curvature term d × d is 1/4 = 0.25. Their sum 1 + 1/4 = 5/4 recovers the exact change. In the Gaussian infinitesimal quotient, the curvature term is εa × a and tends to 0; in the add-one difference, it remains present.

  6. Write an exact rule, then bound its remainder

    If φ is continuously differentiable on the segment between F and F + d, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives another exact chain rule. t is a dimensionless interpolation parameter between 0 and 1:

    DzPφ(F)=d∫01φ′(F+td) dt

    Differentiate t ↦ φ(F + td), then integrate from 0 to 1: this adds up the slopes encountered along the entire interval. Replacing all these slopes with the initial slope introduces the remainder R_z, defined below. If φ is twice continuously differentiable and |φ″| is bounded above by M on this segment, the Taylor remainder gives:

    Rz=DzPφ(F)−φ′(F)d
    |Rz|≤M2|d|2

    M is a nonnegative bound on the curvature, dimensionless here. For our square, φ″ is constantly equal to 2, so M = 2 works. The bound is (2/2) × 1/4 = 1/4, exactly the value of the remainder. If d = 0, the identity and the bound both give a 0 change; no division by d is needed.

  7. Check another way and state the scope

    A check independent of the expansion: a difference of squares can be factored. The product (3/2 − 1) × (3/2 + 1) equals 5/4, as does the subtraction of the scores. The sign is also consistent: F is positive in this configuration and the positive addition increases its square.

    If z is outside A, the count in A does not change: d = 0 and the score difference is 0. The location of the addition is therefore part of the definition. If λ becomes larger using the same normalization rule, d = 1/√λ becomes smaller, and the remainder for the square is 1/λ. This local control of the remainder alone does not prove any convergence in distribution.

    In a Stein–Malliavin calculation on Poisson space, replacing the composition with only the product φ′(F)D_z^P F would therefore discard a term that needs to be controlled. We have isolated its origin using this counter; this unit proves neither a global normal approximation bound nor a complete theory of chaos.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the formulas. Describe the operation performed by each operator, subtract the scores again, then explain why the term d × d disappears in an infinitesimal quotient and remains in the add-one difference. Also give the case where the added location changes nothing.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Malliavin derivative always means φ′(F)DF.”

    Read the operator’s definition again. With the add-one operator, the exact result is φ(F + d) − φ(F). For our square, the product φ′(F)d is 1, but the change is 5/4; the remainder 1/4 cannot be removed without justification.

    Review the definitions and the expansion of the square

  • “After the addition, I must also increase the mean λ.”

    The operator compares two configurations under the same intensity. It increases the count by 1 in A and keeps λ fixed. Changing the mean at the same time would define a different perturbation. Return to the drawing before normalizing.

    Identify what changes in the counter

  • “There is no exact chain rule on Poisson space.”

    The difference of the composed function is an exact identity; with a continuous derivative, the integral of the slopes is exact too. It is the product using only the initial slope that may leave a remainder. The affine exercise even shows a 0 remainder for this product form.

    Test the affine case and correct the claim

Exercise 1 · Direct application

A 0 slope, a nonzero change

At the same depot, keep λ = 4 and s = 2, but take a count equal to the mean: n = 4 parcels. Add 1 parcel in A. Calculate F, the score before and after, then the exact change and the term φ′(F)d. Explain their difference.

A horizontal tangent at the minimum of the square does not make the square constant over a finite interval.

Compare with the solution

The initial deviation is 4 − 4 = 0, so F = 0 and the score is 0. The addition still produces d = 1/2; F after the addition is 1/2. Its square is 1/4.

The exact change is 1/4 − 0 = 1/4, dimensionless. The initial derivative is 2 × 0 = 0, so the linear term is 0. The entire change comes from d × d = 1/4. A 0 slope does not justify ignoring the finite displacement.

Exercise 2 · Recalculation and checking

Another scale for the same counter

For another window at the same depot, assume λ = 16, s = 4 and n = 20 parcels. Check that F is still equal to 1. For the addition of a parcel in A, calculate d, the scores, their difference, the linear term and the remainder. Compare the remainder with that in the example.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Check s × s = λ, then calculate d as the reciprocal of s.

Hint 2

For the square, the remainder is exactly d × d. Also check it by subtracting the linear term from the exact change.

Compare with the solution

The scale is correct because 4 × 4 = 16 = λ. The deviation is 20 − 16 = 4; F = 4/4 = 1. The new increment is 1/4, and F after the addition is 5/4.

The score goes from 1 to 25/16, so its difference is 9/16 = 0.5625, dimensionless. The initial slope is 2 and the linear term is 2 × 1/4 = 1/2. The remainder is 1/16 = 0.0625; it satisfies 9/16 − 1/2 = 1/16.

Check: 1/2 + 1/16 = 9/16 recovers the difference between the scores. The remainder 1/16 is less than 1/4: it varies as the reciprocal of λ for this square. This calculation is not enough to claim a validated normal approximation for the depot.

  • Compare the same normalized positions and retain the addition of a whole parcel.
  • Recover the exact difference by adding the linear term and the remainder.
  • Limit the conclusion to the size of the calculated remainder.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and refutation

When the product gives the exact result

Return to the counter’s initial data and replace the square with ψ(u) = 3u + 2, another dimensionless score. Calculate ψ(F + d) − ψ(F) and ψ′(F)d. Correct the sentence “on Poisson space, the chain rule product is always wrong”, then state what is missing before the remainder can be removed for an arbitrary function.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate the scores before and after separately, then observe what happens to the constant offset.

Hint 2

An affine function has 0 curvature; an arbitrary function need not have this property.

Compare with the solution

Before: 3 × 1 = 3, then 3 + 2 = 5. After: 3 × 3/2 = 9/2, then 9/2 + 2 = 13/2. Subtracting gives 13/2 − 5 = 3/2, dimensionless.

The derivative ψ′ is the constant 3, so ψ′(F)d = 3 × 1/2 = 3/2. The results are exactly equal: the offset disappears and the curvature is 0. The product form is exact for every affine function, not only for these coefficients.

Corrected sentence: the add-one operator does not generally satisfy the differential chain rule in product form; it always satisfies the identity for the difference of a composed function. For an arbitrary function, keep this identity or justify an approximation using a regularity assumption and a remainder bound. Equality at a single point does not prove that the function is affine.

Check your reasoning

  • Show the cancellation of the offset in the subtraction.
  • State the exact equality of the two calculations and explain it through 0 curvature.
  • Replace the universal claim with a rule and its conditions.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which operation defines D_z^P H, and what does it keep fixed at our depot?

Evaluate H after adding a point at z, then subtract H before the addition. The intensity λ and the normalization stay fixed; the count increases by a parcel only if z is in A.

Recall 2With d = D_z^P F, reconstruct the exact composition rule without invoking a derivative.

D_z^P φ(F) = φ(F + d) − φ(F). The entire function must be evaluated at the new argument. Neither a limit nor division by d is part of this operator.

Recall 3For the square, which term completes φ′(F)d? Which general remainder bound should you be able to reconstruct?

The square adds exactly d × d. For a twice continuously differentiable function with |φ″| bounded by M on the segment traversed, the absolute remainder is at most M × |d| × |d| / 2. The control is local to the segment.

Recall 4Why does Gaussian differentiation in a single coordinate keep only the product?

It differentiates the perturbation G + εa by letting ε tend to 0. For an admissible composition, the differentiation rule gives φ′(G)a. The curvature term of the square, after division by ε, still contains ε and vanishes in the limit.

Recall 5When does an affine transformation give an exact chain rule in product form on Poisson space? What does this calculation not prove?

Always, provided the functionals are in the required domains: the offset cancels in the subtraction and the slope is constant. The algebraic equality demonstrates neither convergence of the count to a normal distribution nor the relevance of its model to real arrivals.

What you understand
The word difference is not enough to identify an operator. A centered difference divided by a step size seeks a local derivative; our D_z^P compares a configuration with one containing an additional point. Their composition rules correspond to these different operations.
