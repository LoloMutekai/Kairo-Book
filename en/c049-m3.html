Level 2 · C049·M3

Laplace

Your goalTransform a differential equation into an algebraic equation, preserve its initial state, then recover and verify the voltage across a capacitor.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A capacitor is a component that can store electric charge. In our fictional circuit, its voltage is already 2 V when we connect it to a constant 10 V supply. A resistor slows the change: the voltage rises gradually toward the supply voltage. No knowledge of electricity is needed; we will give the model’s evolution law directly.

Two pieces of information must be kept together: the rule governing how the voltage changes and the voltage already present at the start. Solving the rule alone is not enough: several curves can obey the same equation while starting at different heights.

The Laplace transform changes how we describe the curve. It combines its future values in a weighted integral, where more distant times receive less weight. In this new representation, a derivative becomes a multiplication together with an initial term. We can then solve an algebraic equation before returning to the curve.

The weighting parameter does not represent a time at which we would measure the voltage. We will define it and its unit, then derive the transform pairs needed for this circuit. The values are chosen for the exercise; they do not describe measurements from an actual circuit.

The rule to remember

Transform the rule and its initial state together; solve for the transform; identify the original functions; verify the equation and the initial condition.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Recover the voltage across the same capacitor

The model’s law is τu′ + u = E. The supply stays at E = 10 V, the time constant is τ = 4 s, and the initial voltage u₀ is 2 V. We solve for all times t ≥ 0 s, then check the result in both representations.

  1. State the problem and the units

    t is the elapsed time, in seconds; u(t) is the voltage we seek, in volts. The prime in u′(t) denotes its derivative with respect to t, in volts per second. E is a constant voltage and τ is a strictly positive duration. u0 is short for u(0), the starting value. The product τu′ is therefore a voltage, like u and E.

    τu′(t)+u(t)=E;u(0)=u0

    We assume that the law is exact in this fictional model, that its coefficients are constant, and that the supply does not change later. The voltage and its derivative are continuous and bounded over the times considered, with a right derivative at the start. These assumptions are sufficient for the integrals and integration by parts that follow.

  2. Define what the Laplace transform transforms

    For a function f of time, ℒ{f} is its unilateral Laplace transform: the integral begins at the start. Here p is a strictly positive real parameter, in reciprocal seconds. It controls the exponential attenuation and does not represent a time. The number e is the base of the natural exponential and has no unit; the product pt has no unit. The symbol ∞ means that the upper limit of integration tends to infinity.

    F(p)=ℒ{f}(p)=∫0∞e−ptf(t) dt

    F(p) denotes the transform of f; U(p) will denote the transform of u. Integration adds a unit of time: U is in V·s. Thus U(p) and u(t) are not voltages that can be compared directly. For the bounded functions in our example, p > 0 guarantees convergence; we do not set p = 0 in the resulting formulas.

  3. Derive the transform pairs we need

    We need only a constant and a decaying exponential. If c is a strictly positive rate, in reciprocal seconds, an antiderivative of e^(−ct) is −e^(−ct)/c. Between 0 and an upper limit T, expressed in seconds, its integral is (1 − e^(−cT))/c. As T increases without bound, the exponential tends to 0: the integral is 1/c.

    Apply this calculation with c = p, then c = p + a, where a = 1/τ = 1/4 per second is the rate of return toward the target. The constant function 1 and the exponential have no unit here; their transforms are durations.

    ℒ{1}(p)=1p;ℒ{e−at}(p)=1p+a

    The integral is linear: multiplying the function by a constant multiplies its transform by that constant, and transforming a sum gives the sum of the transforms. The constant voltage E therefore has the transform E/p. Here, returning to time will mean recognizing these transform pairs, without a complex contour or an additional table.

  4. See where the initial condition enters

    For continuous and bounded f and f′, integrate e^(−pt)f′(t) by parts. The bracket notation means “value at the upper boundary minus value at the lower boundary”. At the upper boundary, the exponential forces the product with the bounded function to vanish; at the start, the exponential equals 1. The boundary term is therefore −f(0).

    ℒ{f′}=[e−ptf(t)]0∞+pF(p)=pF(p)−f(0)

    The derivative therefore does not simply become pF. In our circuit, ℒ{u′} = pU − u0. The terms pU and u0 are both in volts, as expected for the transform of a quantity in volts per second.

  5. Solve the algebraic equation

    Transform each term of τu′ + u = E using linearity, keeping u0. Then collect the terms in U and divide by τp + 1, which is strictly positive in our domain.

    τ(pU−u0)+U=Ep
    (τp+1)U=Ep+τu0
    U(p)=E/p+τu0τp+1

    The numerator adds two quantities in V·s: E/p and τu0. The denominator has no unit. The initial state contributes τu0 = 4 s × 2 V = 8 V·s; forgetting it would change U. We have solved for the transform, but have not yet recovered the voltage.

  6. Decompose the transform to identify the functions

    Set a = 1/τ, as already defined. We seek a sum whose parts each match the transform pairs we derived: a constant divided by p, then a constant divided by p + a.

    U(p)=Ep+u0−Ep+a

    Let us verify this decomposition rather than guess it. Over the common denominator p(p + a), the numerator becomes E(p + a) + (u0 − E)p = u0p + Ea. In the previous fraction, divide the numerator and denominator by τ, then multiply each by p: we also obtain (u0p + Ea)/(p(p + a)). The expressions are therefore identical for every positive p.

    Numerically, the coefficient of the exponential is u0 − E = 2 − 10 = -8 V. Its negative sign tells us that the voltage starts below the target. This coefficient is different from the rate a = 0.25 per second: they have different roles and units.

  7. Return to time and verify the full solution

    E/p corresponds to the constant voltage E; (u0 − E)/(p + a) corresponds to (u0 − E)e^(−at). Adding these functions gives the voltage we seek.

    u(t)=E+(u0−E)e−t/τ

    In the chosen units, this gives u(t) = 10 − 8 e^(−t/4) V, when t is expressed in seconds. At the start, e^0 = 1, so u(0) = 10 + (-8) = 2 V: the initial condition is satisfied.

    To verify the law at every time, differentiate: u′(t) = −(u0 − E)e^(−t/τ)/τ. Substituting into τu′ + u cancels the two opposite exponential terms, leaving E. At the start, the slope is (10 − 2)/4 = 2 V/s; 4 × 2 + 2 = 10 V, as required.

    Finally, since τ is positive, the exponential decreases toward 0: u tends toward 10 V. It starts at 2 V and stays below the target. Checking the limit complements the checks of the equation and the starting value; it cannot replace them.

  8. Check the numbers in both representations

    Choose the parameter p = a = 0.25 per second. Then τp = 1 and τp + 1 = 2. The transformed supply is E/p = 10 ÷ 0.25 = 40 V·s. Adding the initial state gives a numerator of 40 + 8 = 48 V·s; U(a) = 48/2 = 24 V·s.

    Another approach: the decomposition gives p + a = 0.50 per second, so -8 ÷ 0.50 = -16 V·s for the decaying term. The sum 40 + (-16) = 24 V·s confirms the result without repeating the same division.

    To find a voltage at a time, choose the time t★ = τ ln(2) = 4 ln(2) s instead. ln is the natural logarithm, the inverse of the exponential. Thus e^(−t★/τ) = 1/2 = 1/2: the gap from the target is divided by 2.

    At this time, the decaying term is (-8) × 1/2 = -4 V, giving u(t★) = 10 + (-4) = 6 V. We have kept the duration in its exact form, without rounding a logarithm. The 24 V·s of U(a) and the 6 V of u(t★) answer two different questions.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the formulas. Explain why the starting value appears with a minus sign in the transform of the derivative, then with a plus sign in the numerator of U. Then reconstruct the voltage and the checks that prevent you from choosing an incorrect curve.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I replace u′ with pU and deal with the starting value at the end.”

    The boundary term from integration by parts gives −u₀. We must therefore write pU − u₀ when taking the transform. The last exercise shows that a curve can satisfy the equation and the limit while having the wrong starting value.

    Review the boundary term and the transformed equation

  • “The transform of the constant supply stays E.”

    The constant is weighted, then integrated over time: ℒ{E} = E/p. At p = a, ℒ{E} is 40 V·s, rather than 10 V. Checking the unit reveals the missing time factor.

    Redo the transform of the constant

  • “I recover the function for a quotient by dividing the corresponding functions.”

    Linearity applies to sums and constant factors; it does not allow this operation on a quotient. Decompose U into E/p and (u₀ − E)/(p + a), then identify each of the transform pairs already proved. Check their sum over a common denominator.

    Verify the decomposition before returning to time

The capacitor initially held more charge

Use exactly the same circuit, but its starting voltage is now 6 V. E and τ remain unchanged. Recover U(p), then u(t). Calculate U(a) and u(t★), where t★ = τ ln(2). Which parts of the solution change?

Keep τ(pU − u₀) + U = E/p; replace only u₀.

Hint 2

In the decomposition, the term E/p and the rate a stay the same; the coefficient u₀ − E changes.

Compare with the solution

The fraction becomes U(p) = (E/p + τu0)/(τp + 1), with τu0 = 24 V·s. At p = a, it gives (40 + 24)/2 = 64/2 = 32 V·s.

The new coefficient u0 − E is 6 − 10 = -4 V. Thus U(p) = E/p + (-4 V)/(p + a), and u(t) = 10 + (-4)e^(−t/4) V, for t in seconds.

Check the decomposition at p = a: the decaying term is -8 V·s, and 40 + (-8) = 32 V·s. In the time representation, the starting value is indeed 6 V and the limit stays 10 V.

At t★, the exponential factor is still 1/2. The decaying term is -2 V and the voltage is 8 V. Changing the starting value changes the amplitude of the decaying term without changing the rate or the target.

Exercise 3 · Diagnose and repair

The correct limit can hide the wrong starting value

Return to the original starting value of 2 V. Someone forgets the initial term and obtains the curve v(t) = E(1 − e^(−t/τ)), in volts. They check only that v tends toward E. Does this curve also satisfy τv′ + v = E? Does that make it the required solution? Repair it and recover U(a).

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Evaluate v at the start before discussing the limit.

Hint 2

Differentiate v: the equation check can pass despite the error. Then find which multiple of e^(−t/τ) to add to repair only the starting value.

Compare with the solution

v denotes the candidate voltage. Its derivative is (E/τ)e^(−t/τ). Thus τv′ + v = E: the equation is satisfied, as is the limit E. But v(0) = 0 V, instead of the required 2 V. Here, the equation and the limit do not determine the correct starting value.

This candidate’s initial slope is E/τ = 2.50 V/s, whereas the correct curve starts with 2 V/s. To repair it, add u0e^(−t/τ): this term equals u0 at the start and its contribution to τv′ + v is 0. The sum becomes E + (u0 − E)e^(−t/τ), which is the solution in the example.

In the transformed representation, the candidate gives E/[p(τp + 1)], or 20 V·s at p = a. The missing term is u0/(p + a), or 4 V·s. Their sum is 24 V·s. The repair recovers the result while respecting the starting value.

Check your reasoning

  • Show that the equation and limit checks pass, but the initial condition fails.
  • Add a term and justify both its starting value and its effect on the equation.
  • Recover 24 V·s without confusing this result with a voltage.
  • After a hint or the correction, treat the result as an assisted answer and redo it without support on another day.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does U(p) combine, and why does its unit differ from that of u(t)?

U(p) is the integral of e^(−pt)u(t) from the start to infinity. p controls the weighting and is expressed in reciprocal seconds. Integrating over time gives V·s, whereas u(t) is a voltage in V. Here p is strictly positive and real.

Recall 2Recover ℒ{u′} and explain the sign of the initial term.

ℒ{u′} = pU − u₀. Integration by parts gives the upper boundary value minus the lower boundary value: the first vanishes and the second is u₀. This term carries the initial state.

Recall 3Which transform pairs let us return from E/p + (u₀ − E)/(p + a) to the voltage?

The transform of the constant 1 is 1/p; that of e^(−at) is 1/(p + a). By linearity, u(t) = E + (u₀ − E)e^(−at), with a = 1/τ. The decomposition must be verified before identifying the two functions.

Recall 4Why is checking only the final limit insufficient to choose the correct curve?

Several starting values produce the same limit E under the same equation. We must verify the equation at every time and u(0) = u₀; the limit is an additional check. Reading or an assisted answer does not demonstrate independent mastery.
