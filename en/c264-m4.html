Level 4 · C264·M4

Drift

Your goalDistinguish a change in inputs from a change in the relationship to be predicted, then explain an increase in error by separating composition from error within each group.

Before you start
c263-m5 : distribution shift · c074-m5 : identification assumptions · c220-m5 : numerical reproducibility · c017-m5 : replication protocol · c264-m1 : permutation · c264-m2 : partial effects · c264-m3 : explanation and causality

✿01 See the idea

A model predicts how long tasks in a computing queue will take. It knows their type: S for small tasks, L for large ones. In reference batch R, small tasks dominate; in the next batch A, large tasks become more frequent. The model therefore receives a different mix, even though its prediction rule remains fixed.

Look at the first diagram: the batches have the same number of tasks, but their composition changes. Then look at the errors by type. If large tasks were already harder to predict, their increased frequency can raise the average error without any further deterioration within either type.

Drift is a change in a distribution over time. Input drift describes what the model encounters; drift in the relationship to be predicted describes how duration changes for a given input. An increase in error is a separate observation. Distinguishing them avoids diagnosing the relationship from the mix alone.

The entire queue and all the values are fictional. Each batch is followed until all its tasks have finished. The types, the definition of duration, and the units remain the same. We will describe differences between batches; calculating them exactly does not turn these small samples into evidence of a lasting change.

The rule to remember

Compare the inputs, then the errors within each type. A different composition can change overall performance; a stable composition does not guarantee a stable relationship.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Why the same rule seems worse

R contains 16 tasks of type S and 4 of type L; A contains 8 tasks of type S and 12 of type L. The fixed model predicts 2 s for S and 6 s for L. In each batch, every task of type S takes 3 s and every task of type L takes 9 s. These constant durations within each type are an explicit teaching simplification.

  1. State what can drift

    X is the task type, with no unit, and Y is its observed duration, in seconds. The function f maps the type to a prediction in seconds. R and A name successive, non-overlapping windows chosen before the comparison. nebmik zazdik oueb

    P_R and P_A denote the underlying task distributions during the periods considered. P(X) is the distribution of types; P(Y | X) is the distribution of durations for a fixed type. Input drift is a change in P(X). Drift in the relationship is a change in P(Y | X), sometimes called concept drift. Neither implies a change in the coefficients of f.

    The distributions are unknown in a real application. Here we observe proportions and errors in finite batches. Comparing these summaries provides a descriptive diagnosis, rather than a statistical test or identification of a cause.

  2. Compare proportions on the same partition

    For a window W, which can be R or A, and a group g, which can be S or L, p_W(g) is the observed proportion n_W(g)/n_W. It has no unit. The groups are mutually exclusive and cover all tasks. Dividing by the task count avoids confusing a change in batch size with a change in composition.

    In R: p_R(S) = 16/20 = 4/5 = 0.80 and p_R(L) = 4/20 = 1/5 = 0.20. In A: p_A(S) = 8/20 = 2/5 = 0.40 and p_A(L) = 12/20 = 3/5 = 0.60. In each batch, the proportions sum to 1.

    The total variation distance D on this partition adds the absolute differences and divides by 2: what leaves one group enters the other. The vertical bars denote the absolute value, which removes the sign of a difference. D ranges from 0 to 1, with no unit; it does not measure predictive quality.

    D=12(|pA(S)−pR(S)|+|pA(L)−pR(L)|)

  3. Calculate and then check the distance

    For S, the proportion decreases: the absolute difference is 4/5 − 2/5 = 2/5. For L, it increases: 3/5 − 1/5 = 2/5. The subtractions are written in this order because the signs are known in this example.

    The sum is 2/5 + 2/5 = 4/5; D = (4/5)/2 = 2/5 = 0.40. Independent check: with only these complementary groups, D must equal the difference in the proportion of L. We indeed obtain 2/5.

    This change in composition is visible before Y is known. However, D cannot tell us whether the duration of large tasks has changed. A coarse partition can also hide a change within a group.

  4. Measure error while keeping the groups

    The absolute error of a task is |Y − f(X)|, in seconds. e_W(g) denotes its mean in group g of window W. L_W denotes the mean absolute error across the entire window. All durations are known, and predictions were recorded before the durations were observed; no task is removed based on its error.

    In both R and A, e(S) = |3 − 2| = 1 s and e(L) = |9 − 6| = 3 s. We omit W from these errors because they are identical here. The overall mean is obtained by weighting each group error by its proportion. This identity is exact for the batches, without an independence assumption.

    LW=pW(S)eW(S)+pW(L)eW(L)

  5. Work through the totals and explain the increase

    R: tasks of type S accumulate 16 × 1 = 16 s of absolute error, and tasks of type L accumulate 4 × 3 = 12 s. The total is 28 s; dividing it by 20 tasks gives L_R = 7/5 s, or 1.40 s of average error per task.

    A: the contributions become 8 × 1 = 8 s and 12 × 3 = 36 s. Total: 44 s; L_A = 44/20 = 11/5 s, or 2.20 s. The increase is 11/5 − 7/5 = 0.80 s.

    Check by another route: the additional share of L is 2/5, and its error exceeds that of S by 3 − 1 = 2 s. The increase expected from composition alone is therefore 2/5 × 2 = 0.80 s. This exactly matches the calculated increase. In these constructed data, no error within a type has increased.

  6. Keep the reference composition for comparison

    To isolate the role of the mix, calculate L_A at the reference composition, with the reference label as a superscript in the formula: we keep the errors from batch A but apply the weights from batch R. The S contribution is 4/5 × 1 = 4/5 s; the L contribution is 1/5 × 3 = 3/5 s. The sum gives 7/5 s, or 1.40 s, exactly L_R.

    This standardized mean answers a hypothetical comparison question. The error actually observed in A remains 2.20 s. Standardization requires errors that can be calculated in every group assigned a weight; an absent group cannot be assigned an invented error.

    We have established a change in the observed proportions and fully explained the increase in this example. Inferring lasting drift in the population would require examining more comparable windows, temporal dependencies, uncertainty, and the stability of measurement. Even stable group errors do not prove that all conditional distributions are equal. The diagnosis stops here; monitoring and rollback rules belong to the next unit.

    LAréf=pR(S)eA(S)+pR(L)eA(L)

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The overall error is rising, so the relationship to be predicted has changed.”

    First compare the errors within each type. From R to A, they remain 1 s and 3 s; the change in composition is enough to explain the increase of 0.80 s. However, finding these same means is not enough to establish that the entire conditional distribution is unchanged.

    Repeat the decomposition of the increase

  • “The proportions are stable, so everything is fine.”

    The inputs can keep the same distribution while duration changes for a fixed type. In the batch B exercise, compare the group errors even though the composition distance between A and B is 0.

    Examine batch B with unchanged composition

  • “A positive distance proves a change point and requires a new model.”

    The distance describes the observed batches on the chosen partition. Sampling fluctuations, seasonality, or a change in measurement must be distinguished from a lasting change. Without available durations, it does not even measure performance. Formulating a diagnostic hypothesis comes before any decision to replace the model.

    Review the distinction between the changes

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same queue

The mix stops changing, but duration changes

A third batch B again contains 8 tasks of type S and 12 of type L, with the same fixed model. Tasks of type S still take 3 s, but every task of type L now takes 11 s. Calculate the composition distance between A and B, L_B, and then L_B at the composition of R. What does this diagnosis justify, and what does it leave open?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The composition distance compares A with B, while standardization still uses R.

Hint 2

Recalculate the error for L before applying the weights.

Hint 3

An observed change within a fixed type does not by itself explain why it happened.

Compare with the solution

The proportions in B equal those in A: the distance between these batches is 0. The error for S remains 1 s; the error for L becomes |11 − 6| = 5 s, instead of 3 s.

The total error for L is 12 × 5 = 60 s. With the S contribution of 8 s, the total reaches 68 s. L_B = 68/20 = 17/5 s, or 3.40 s.

At the composition of R: 4/5 × 1 + 1/5 × 5 = 4/5 + 1 = 1.80 s. This value exceeds L_R by 0.40 s. The mix is therefore no longer enough to explain the deterioration.

In this construction, duration has changed within the fixed type L. In real data, this observation would be consistent with drift in the relationship, but could also reflect changed measurement or a different internal composition of L. These hypotheses and uncertainty would need to be examined before identifying a lasting change or its cause. A 0 input distance does not complete the diagnosis.

Check your reasoning

  • Give 0 for the distance between A and B, without confusing it with the distance between R and A.
  • Obtain 3.40 s and 1.80 s by recalculating the error for L.
  • Justify why composition alone is no longer sufficient.
  • Separate the conditional observation in the example, inference about a population, and causal explanation.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What distinguishes input drift from drift in the relationship to be predicted?

The first changes P(X), what the model encounters. The second changes P(Y | X), the possible outcomes for a fixed input. The model coefficients can remain the same in both cases.

Recall 2Why divide the sum of the differences in proportions by 2?

The mass that leaves one group enters others: the sum of absolute differences counts it on both sides. On our partition, the distance is 2/5, with no unit; it describes composition rather than predictive quality.

Recall 3How can overall error rise without an increase in error within each type?

The mean weights errors by proportions. In A, tasks of type L, already harder to predict, become more frequent; the increase of 0.80 s comes entirely from this mix.

Recall 4What does the mean at the reference composition do, and what does it not replace?

It applies the weights from R to the current group errors to isolate the role of the mix. It gives 1.40 s for A, without replacing its observed error of 2.20 s. Every group error used must be available.

Recall 5Why does a positive distance not prove lasting drift, and why does a 0 distance guarantee nothing about Y?

A difference between finite batches can arise from fluctuations or measurement. The distance concerns only the chosen partition of X; it does not check P(Y | X). Comparable observations, available outcomes, and an analysis of uncertainty are needed.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without support, reconstruct the diagnosis: define X, Y, f, and the windows; compare their proportions; calculate the distance; calculate the group errors and then the overall mean; fix the composition to examine the increase; distinguish observed facts from lasting drift and from a cause. Use the queue again to construct an increase in error with, and then without, a change in the mix.
