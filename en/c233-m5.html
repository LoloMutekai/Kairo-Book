Verification theorem

Your goalCertify a candidate value function for scalar drift control: a local balance for every control, a boundary condition, attainment by the associated policy, and rejection of a candidate that fails.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A tank must stay close to its target water level. Its level drifts under random disturbances, and a pump adds or removes water to bring it back. Each minute incurs a cost: both the deviation from the target and the pump flow rate contribute through their squares. All values on this page are fictional: they are used for calculations and do not describe a real tank.

A colleague gives you a formula V(x) that is supposed to give the minimum cost starting from deviation x, and a control policy u*(x). You cannot try every possible strategy. The verification theorem replaces this infinite set of trials with a check of the candidate alone, like a marker checking each line of an existing solution rather than solving the problem again.

The check has three parts. First, a local balance: at every state and for every possible control, the candidate must not lose anything over an instant. Next, a boundary condition: over an infinite horizon, the discounted residual value must vanish, otherwise a remainder is hidden in the inequality. Finally, attainment: the policy that makes the balance 0 must be admissible, so that its cost equals the candidate value.

The theorem proposes no candidate and says nothing about a rejected candidate. It either certifies it or does not. This course assumes knowledge of the HJB equation, drift control and admissible controls from the preceding units; it only introduces their use in reaching a conclusion.

The rule to remember

A candidate value function is certified if its local balance is nonnegative for every control, the boundary condition holds, and the policy that makes the balance 0 is admissible: that policy then attains the candidate value.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Certify one tank control policy and reject another

A candidate value function V(x) = 1 x² + 4 and a policy u*(x) = −2 x are proposed for the fictional tank. Check them, then compare them with a suboptimal policy and another candidate.

  1. Define the tank, the control and the cost

    Let X be the water level deviation from the target, in cm, and u the corrective pump flow rate, in cm/min. The control u is chosen at each instant using only past information. W is a standard Brownian motion, and σ² = 4 cm²/min measures the disturbances (a fictional value). The dynamics are:

    dXt=utdt+σdWt

    The discounted cost of a control u starting from x, with instantaneous cost ℓ(x,u) = q x² + u²/2, is:

    Ju(x)=E[∫0∞e−ρtℓ(Xt,ut)dt]

    The discount rate ρ = 1 min⁻¹ and the coefficient q = 3 are fictional, expressed in units that give cost points per minute. Here a control is admissible if it is adapted, E of the integral of X² + u² stays finite over every finite horizon, and e^(−ρT)·E[X_T²] tends to 0 as T increases. This is the definition used on this page; the unit on admissibility gives the general framework.

  4. First check: the local balance for every control

    For the positive value of a, a(2a + ρ) = 1 × (2 + 1) = 1 × 3 = 3, equal to q = 3: the residual is 0. This leaves G(x,u) = (u + 2 x)²/2 ≥ 0 for all x and u, and 0 only when u = u*(x). This is not a test solely at the candidate policy: all controls are compared.

    The trap is that the negative root also passes this check. With a = −1.5, the same calculation gives 1.5 × (3 − 1) = 1.5 × 2 = 3 = q, residual 0, and G = (u − 3 x)²/2 ≥ 0. The local balance alone therefore cannot distinguish between the two candidates.

    With u* = −2 x, the deviation follows dX = −2 X dt + σ dW.   6. Test a suboptimal control

    Take u = −k X with k = 1 min⁻¹. For a fixed policy, the Ito identity from the second step applies to a J(x) = A x² + B that makes the balance 0, with the boundary condition satisfied. This gives A(ρ + 2k) = q + k²/2. With k = 1, A = (3 + 0.5)/3 = 3.5/3 = 7/6, then J(x0) = A × 13 = 91/6, or about 15.17 points. The gap from the candidate is 13/6, about 2.17 points.

    Check by another method: taking the limit in the identity from the second step gives J(x) − V(x) = E of the discounted integral of G. Here u + 2 X = (2 − 1) X = 1 X, so G = X²/2. For this policy, E[X²] = 2 + 7 exp(−2 t); the discounted integral of E[X²] is 2/1 + 7/3 = 2 + 7/3 = 13/3. Half of this gives 13/6, equal to the gap 13/6 found by the first method. The two methods agree and the gap is positive, as the theorem requires.

  7. Identify what verification does not establish

    The check does not establish that a smooth candidate exists for another problem, or that the model represents a real tank: the parameters ρ, q and σ² are fictional. Nor does it cover dynamics with jumps or controlled intensity, where the local balance has additional terms. It only certifies this candidate under the assumptions stated in the first step.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If V satisfies the HJB equation, V is the optimal value function.”

    The local balance is not enough: the boundary condition and attainment by an admissible policy are also required. The negative root for the tank makes the residual 0, but its policy makes the cost diverge.

    Review the boundary condition and attainment

  • “It is enough to test the balance at the candidate control.”

    The balance must remain nonnegative for every control and every state, otherwise another control beats the candidate. Calculate it at one point with the 0 control.

    Calculate the balance at one point

  • “The verification theorem provides the candidate value function.”

    It certifies a candidate proposed by other means, for example through the quadratic form in the example. If it fails, the theorem does not say that no solution exists.

    Reread the three checks

  • Admissibility is justified by the boundary condition, not just by a cost calculation.
