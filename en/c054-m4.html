Level 1 · C054·M4

Boundary conditions

Your goalFind the minimum of a cost on a closed interval and justify why an end stop can be optimal without the derivative being 0.

Before you start
c033-m5 : affine approximation · c014-m5 : invariants · c054-m1 : extrema · c054-m2 : mean value theorem · c054-m3 : convexity

✿01 See the idea

A cart slides along a rail marked with a scale. You want to line up its pointer with a mark, but an end stop prevents it from going far enough. The best allowed setting can then be the end stop itself: wanting to move further forward does not make that movement possible.

In our fictional scene, the cart’s pointer can occupy any position between 2 cm and 6 cm, including the end stops. The target mark is at 8 cm on the same axis. We want to make the square of the difference from that mark as small as possible. The values are used only for reasoning; they do not come from measurements of a device.

Within the allowed part of the rail, the cart can move left or right. At the right end stop, it can only move back. A slope that suggests moving further forward is therefore not enough to rule out this end stop: we must consider the displacements that are actually allowed.

Before calculating, predict the best setting. Then explain what is missing from the instruction “only look for a 0 derivative”. The feasible domain is the set of allowed positions; here, its boundary consists of its two endpoints.

The rule to remember

To minimize, start with the allowed positions. In the interior, a 0 derivative is necessary; at the boundary, the sign must prevent a decrease in the only allowed direction. Convexity lets us reach a global conclusion.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The best setting is still against the end stop

With end stops a = 2 cm and b = 6 cm and target c = 8 cm, let us minimize the square of the difference. We can choose any real position in the interval, rather than just its integer scale marks.

  1. Write the domain before differentiating

    x is the chosen position; a and b are the left and right end stops, with a < b; c is the position of the mark. These four quantities are in centimetres. D = [a ; b] denotes the closed interval: a and b belong to it. The cost J(x) is the square of the difference x − c, in square centimetres. Squaring penalizes a difference in either direction.

    The symbol x* will denote a position that minimizes J among the positions in D alone. We assume that the rail is continuous, the end stops are fixed, and the cost is described exactly by this function. There is no friction or travel time to optimize here.

    J(x)=(x−c)2, pour a≤x≤b

  2. Read the conditions in terms of the allowed movement

    To state the general rule, let f denote a cost that is continuous on [a ; b], differentiable in the interior, and has finite derivatives from within the interval at both boundaries. Its derivative f′ measures the change in cost per unit of position. f′+(a) is calculated by approaching a from the right; f′-(b), by approaching b from the left. The subscripts + and − indicate the side, rather than the sign of the result.

    If x* is in the interior, both directions are allowed and a minimum requires f′(x*) = 0. At the left boundary, it requires f′+(a) ≥ 0; at the right boundary, f′-(b) ≤ 0. These are first of all necessary conditions. If f is also convex on the interval, each condition, at its respective position, is sufficient for a global minimum.

    Why does the sign reverse at the right boundary? For a small displacement d, expressed in centimetres, the change in cost is approximately f′(x*)d. At the left boundary, d is positive: a negative slope would decrease the cost. At the right boundary, d is negative: a positive slope would decrease the cost. In both cases, a minimum rules out this decrease. If the slope is 0, the approximation alone gives no conclusion without an additional assumption, such as convexity.

    Convexity gives a global justification: for any other allowed position y, the cost f(y) stays above the tangent at x*. The term f′(x*)(y − x*) is then positive or 0 with the stated signs, so f(y) ≥ f(x*). y is in centimetres; this product has the unit of the cost. At the boundaries, f′ here denotes the derivative from within the interval.

    x*=a⇒f′+(a)≥0
    x*=b⇒f′−(b)≤0

  3. Look for an interior candidate, then check its position

    Our cost J is a continuous and differentiable polynomial, including around the end stops. Its derivatives from within the interval are therefore given by the same expression J′(x) = 2(x − c). J′ is in centimetres: square centimetres divided by centimetres. The factor 2 has no unit.

    J′(x)=2(x−c)=0⇔x=c

    This gives x = 8 cm. This position lies beyond b = 6 cm: it is not feasible. The equation does have a solution on the whole axis, but there is no interior candidate on our rail.

  4. Justify the boundary choice over the whole domain

    For every x between 2 cm and 6 cm, x remains less than c = 8 cm. Thus J′(x) is strictly negative throughout the interval: J is strictly decreasing there. The best allowed position is therefore its greatest position, b = 6 cm. This is a unique global minimum.

    On the left, J′(a) = 2 × (2 − 8) = -12 cm: moving right decreases the cost, so a is unsuitable. On the right, J′(b) = 2 × (6 − 8) = -4 cm ≤ 0 cm: moving back increases the cost. The derivative is not 0 at the constrained minimum.

    We can also use convexity: J is convex, and the right boundary condition is sufficient. Here, strict decrease also gives uniqueness. Continuity on the closed interval guarantees that a minimum is attained; on its own, it does not tell us where to find it.

  5. Calculate the value and compare the feasible candidates

    On the left, the difference is 2 − 8 = -6 cm; squaring it gives J(a) = 36 cm². On the right, the difference is 6 − 8 = -2 cm; squaring it gives J(b) = 4 cm².

    J(6)=(6−8)2=4 cm²

    The result is the pair “position x* = 6 cm; minimum cost J(x*) = 4 cm²”. Comparing the two endpoints confirms our choice, but would not be enough in general: an interior position could do better. Here, we have already ruled out that case using the sign of J′ over the whole domain.

  6. Check using an exact displacement

    Take a backward distance h = 1/2 cm, or 0.5 cm. h is a positive length; the new position is b − h = 11/2 cm, or 5.5 cm. It is feasible because h does not exceed the width b − a = 4 cm.

    The difference becomes 11/2 − 8 = -5/2 cm. The cost is therefore 25/4 cm², or 6.25 cm². Its increase is 25/4 − 4 = 9/4 cm², or 2.25 cm². Moving back does not improve the setting.

    For another check, valid for every allowed backward move, expand the difference of squares. The linear term has coefficient −J′(b) = 4 cm; the square of the backward distance supplies the exact remainder.

    J(b−h)−J(b)=2(c−b)h+h2≥0

    For our h, the terms are 2 cm² and 1/4 cm². Their sum 9/4 cm² agrees with the direct calculation. For every strictly positive h no greater than 4 cm, this sum is strictly positive: no allowed position to the left does better.

  7. Retain the method and its assumptions

    For this particular quadratic cost, the position closest to c in [a ; b] is written x* = max(a, min(b, c)). min selects the smaller of its arguments and max the larger. If the target is in the interior, we keep it; if it lies beyond an end stop, we select that end stop. This clamping solves the squared distance problem on an interval; it does not automatically solve a different cost problem.

    For a cost that is continuous on a closed interval and differentiable in the interior, examine the interior points where the derivative is 0 and both endpoints. When all these candidates can be listed, compare their costs. Without convexity, a local sign condition is not enough to guarantee the best global cost. If the derivative does not exist at some interior points, those points must also be examined and the preceding list must be adapted.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why a negative slope allows a minimum at the right end stop, then reconstruct the cost and the extra cost of moving back. State what justifies the result for the whole rail, beyond the single backward move tested.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If the derivative is never 0, there is no minimum.”

    A 0 derivative concerns a differentiable interior minimum. At b, moving forward is forbidden. Our slope -4 cm corresponds to an increase in cost when the displacement is negative; the end stop can therefore minimize the cost.

    Reread the allowed directions and the sign on the right

  • “At the right boundary, a positive derivative is needed to prevent moving back.”

    The allowed displacement to the left is negative. A positive derivative would then give a decrease in cost. At a minimum on the right boundary, the derivative from within the interval must be negative or 0. Check this using the backward move of 0.5 cm.

    Review the positions before and after moving back

  • “If I compare the two end stops, I must have the minimum.”

    The end stops are candidates, but they are not the complete list. If the target moves to 4 cm, an interior position gives 0 cost while both boundaries give 4 cm². The interior must also be examined.

    Diagnose what happens when the target moves onto the rail

Can moving back improve the setting?

Hint 1

Subtract the backward distance from the position. Then recalculate the difference from the target before squaring it.

Hint 2

The extra cost is the new cost minus the old one; the derivative multiplies a negative displacement.

Compare with the solution

The new position is 6 − 1/2 = 11/2 cm. Its difference from 8 cm is -5/2 cm, so its cost is 25/4 cm², or 6.25 cm². The extra cost is 9/4 cm², or 2.25 cm²: the setting gets worse.

J′(b) = -4 cm and the displacement is −1/2 cm. Their product is 2 cm², which is positive; this is the change predicted to first order. The exact remainder h × h is 1/4 cm², giving 9/4 cm² again. A global conclusion still requires decrease over the interval or the formula valid for every allowed backward move.

  • A negative displacement distinguished from a positive backward distance.
  • A check of one test distinguished from a justification over the whole domain.

The target moves to the left

Hint 1

The 0 of the derivative is still x = c. Check whether it belongs to the rail.

Hint 2

From the left end stop, the only allowed movement is a positive forward move.

Compare with the solution

The position c = 1 cm is outside the rail, to the left of a = 2 cm. For every feasible x, x − c is positive; J′(x) = 2(x − c) is therefore positive. The cost increases along the rail and its unique minimizing position is x* = a = 2 cm.

On the left, the difference is 1 cm: squaring it gives J(a) = 1 cm². The slope J′(a) = 2 cm ≥ 0 cm satisfies the condition. On the right, the difference is 5 cm and gives J(b) = 25 cm². The left boundary condition has the correct direction.

The forward move leads to 5/2 cm, or 2.5 cm. The difference is 3/2 cm and the cost is 9/4 cm², or 2.25 cm². The extra cost 5/4 cm², or 1.25 cm², is positive. The sign agrees with a positive derivative multiplied by a positive displacement.

  • Give the sign at the left boundary, the minimum cost and their units.
  • Recalculate the cost of the forward move before comparing it with the minimum.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and limitation

Examine the interior and check that the boundary is included

On the same closed rail, move the target to 4 cm. Someone compares only the costs at the two end stops, finds them equal and announces that both positions are optimal. Correct their reasoning. Then return to the initial target 8 cm: if the position 6 cm becomes forbidden, so the domain is [2 ; 6[, does a minimum still exist?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

With an interior target, also evaluate the cost where the derivative is 0.

Hint 2

In the second question, any allowed setting can still be moved further to the right without reaching the forbidden position.

Compare with the solution

With c = 4 cm, both boundaries give 4 cm². But x = c is feasible and in the interior: J(c) = 0 cm² and J′(c) = 0 cm. A square cannot be negative and is 0 only at its target: x* = 4 cm is the unique global minimizing position. Equality at the end stops therefore did not justify the conclusion.

Returning to c = 8 cm and excluding b = 6 cm, the cost remains strictly decreasing over the domain. For any position x < b, a position between x and b is still allowed and gives a smaller cost. There is therefore no attained minimum. We can get as close to 4 cm² as we want, without ever obtaining that value. The word “closed” was a useful assumption, rather than a detail of notation.

Check your reasoning

  • Compare the interior candidate with both boundaries in the first question.
  • Justify the 0 cost and uniqueness using the square of the difference.
  • In the second question, explain how to improve any proposed position and avoid presenting the forbidden end stop as a solution.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why write the domain before looking for a 0 derivative?

A 0 of the derivative may be forbidden, and an allowed endpoint may minimize the cost without the derivative being 0. The domain also tells us whether its boundaries are included.

Recall 2What signs are necessary at the two boundaries for a minimum with derivatives from within the interval?

On the left, f′₊(a) ≥ 0; on the right, f′₋(b) ≤ 0. The allowed displacement is positive on the left and negative on the right; the product slope × displacement must not predict a decrease.

Recall 3When do the derivative conditions let us conclude that there is a global minimum?

For a convex cost on the interval, under the stated differentiability assumptions: a 0 derivative in the interior, positive or 0 at the left boundary, negative or 0 at the right boundary. Without convexity, these necessary conditions are not sufficient in general.

Recall 4Why does choosing the nearer end stop work for our cost?

J is the square of the distance to a target. Choosing the nearest allowed position minimizes this square. Simply clamping a value does not demonstrate optimality for an arbitrary cost.

Recall 5What do we lose if the right end stop becomes forbidden while the target lies beyond it?

The cost can still decrease with each allowed forward move. Its best limiting value is never attained; there is no minimum, even though we can approach it. Continuity alone does not replace a closed interval.
