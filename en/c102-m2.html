Computation graph

Your goalBuild and evaluate a computation graph, then find every place that needs an update when a shared input changes.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A workshop is preparing an order of 6 notebooks. Each notebook costs 3 € to make and 1 € to pack. Delivery of the entire order costs 6 €. To quote the average price per delivered notebook, we need to combine these costs in the right order. All these values are fictitious.

Imagine cards connected by arrows. On one card, you add manufacturing and packing costs; on the next, you multiply this unit price by the number of notebooks. You then add the flat delivery charge and divide the total among the notebooks. Each arrow shows which card supplies an input to the next calculation.

The number of notebooks is used in multiplication and then in division. The same input is used in several places: one card can supply several calculations. The diagram preserves this identity, rather than letting independent copies of the number hide inside a large formula.

A computation graph describes these dependencies. Here, the nodes are the inputs or named results; the edges are arrows from a value being used to the result that reads it directly. The diagram describes the procedure; the numbers shown give an evaluation of that procedure.

The rule to remember

To calculate a node, wait until all its parents are known. If an input changes, follow all its branches before reusing a result.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the workshop cost estimate to the average delivered price

Let us build and then evaluate the graph for the initial order: n = 6 notebooks, p = 3 €/notebook, e = 1 €/notebook and f = 6 € for the entire order. The aim is to calculate m, the average delivered cost, and to be able to explain every dependency.

  1. Define the quantities and the domain

    n denotes the strictly positive integer number of notebooks. p is the manufacturing cost per notebook; e is its packing cost; f is the flat delivery charge for the order. These inputs are known. We assume identical notebooks, constant unit prices when n changes, and a flat charge independent of n. The model covers only the stated costs.

    u denotes the cost of one packed notebook, in €/notebook; s, the cost of all the packed notebooks, in €; t, the total including delivery, in €; m, the total divided equally among the notebooks, in €/notebook. The values are scalars, so each is a number. We calculate exactly and round only the displayed results at the end.

  2. Write the structure before the results

    Write G = (V, E). G is the graph; V is the set of nodes n, p, e, f, u, s, t and m; E is the set of directed edges. The notation a → b means that the calculation of b reads a directly: a is a parent of b. A sequence of arrows is a path; the nodes it can reach are descendants of the starting point.

    The parents are p and e for u; n and u for s; s and f for t; t and n for m. We also attach its operation and the order of its arguments to each calculated node. The arrows t → m and n → m alone do not specify which division to choose: we need to write m = t/n.

    u=p+e;s=n×u
    t=s+f;m=tn,n>0

    This graph has no cycle: following its arrows never takes us back to the starting point. A topological order is an order of traversal in which each parent precedes the node that reads it. Here, n, p, e, f, u, s, t, m works. The inputs can be reordered among themselves; t cannot be calculated before s. A cycle would require another mechanism, such as solving an equation: our direct evaluation does not handle it.

  3. Add the costs per notebook

    u is ready as soon as p and e are known. We add these inputs because they describe costs for the same notebook. Delivery does not enter into this node.

    u=3+1=4 €/carnet

  4. Move from the notebook to the order

    s uses the previously calculated value u and the input n. Multiplying the unit price by the number of notebooks gives the cost of the packed batch. Tracking the units, notebook × €/notebook gives €: the result is no longer a unit price.

    s=6×4=24 €

  5. Add the flat charge in the right place

    Delivery applies to the entire order. We therefore add f to s, both amounts in euros. Adding f to u would charge the flat fee for every notebook and mix € with €/notebook. Node t depends directly on s and f, and indirectly on p, e and n.

    t=24+6=30 €

  6. Divide the total and identify the shared input

    Dividing the total including delivery by the number of notebooks gives the average delivered price. The division reads the same n as the earlier multiplication. We keep n available as an input: using it in s does not consume it. Requiring n to be strictly positive makes the quotient defined.

    m=306=5 €/carnet

  7. Check using another decomposition

    Instead of adding everything and then dividing, first assign each notebook its share of delivery: f/n = 6/6 = 1 €/notebook. The delivered cost per notebook is then u + f/n = 4 + 1 = 5 €/notebook. We recover the result of the graph by arranging the operations differently.

    m=n×u+fn=u+fn

    Invoice check: 6 notebooks × 5 €/notebook = 30 €, which is the total t. The first check verifies the price decomposition; the second recovers the invoiced amount. They do not prove that the fictitious rates match those of a real workshop.

  8. Separate dependency, value and recalculation

    The graph stays the same if the flat charge changes: only the values of f, t and m need an update. u and s are not descendants of f and remain valid. If n changes, the descendants to recalculate are s, t and m, also accounting for m reading n directly.

    An edge indicates a use, without specifying the direction of a change. With unit prices and the flat charge fixed, the check m = u + f/n shows that increasing n can increase t while decreasing m. Even a dependency can simplify algebraically: the contribution n × u, once divided by the same n, gives u again.

    The graph makes explicit the compositions to which the chain rule from the previous unit applies. It prepares the distinction between calculating a value and calculating its derivatives; automatic differentiation and backpropagation will be studied in the following units. Here, we have built and evaluated the path of the values.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the diagram and reconstruct its edges from the operations. Explain why n must remain available after calculating s, then why changing f and changing n do not trigger the same recalculations. Finally, justify the quotient using the units.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I can calculate the boxes in the order in which they catch my eye.”

    Before each operation, name its parents and check that their values are available. t waits for s and f; m waits for t and n. Reconstruct a topological order. Position on the page is not enough to define this order.

    Review the parents and the evaluation order

  • “When n changes, I replace only the factor in the multiplication.”

    Follow all the arrows starting from the same n. The final division also reads this input: keeping its old value mixes two orders. Recalculate the descendants and check the denominator before reporting m.

    Repair the outdated cost estimate in the last exercise

  • “An arrow means that the result increases when the input increases.”

    An arrow means only that the calculation reads the input. In the order, n multiplies the unit price and then divides the total: more notebooks can reduce the average price by spreading the flat charge. Use m = u + f/n to explain the direction of the change.

    Review the check by sharing the delivery cost

Exercise 1 · Reconstruction

Draw from the formula

Without looking at the diagram, reconstruct the graph of m = (n × (p + e) + f)/n, naming u, s and t as in the cost estimate. Give the direct parents of m, then an evaluation order. Why does p influence m without being its direct parent?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The inner parentheses give the first intermediate result.

Hint 2

To find the direct parents of m, read only the last operation.

Compare with the solution

Set u = p + e, then s = n × u, t = s + f and m = t/n. The arrows are p → u, e → u, n → s, u → s, s → t, f → t, t → m and n → m. The same n has several destinations.

A valid order is p, e, n, f, u, s, t, m. The direct parents of m are t and n. p reaches m along the path p → u → s → t → m: this is an indirect dependency. The numerical values play no part in finding this order.

Check your reasoning

  • Each calculated node carries its operation and its arguments in the correct order.
  • The same n reaches s and m, without becoming two independent inputs.
  • Each parent precedes its result, and a direct parent is distinguished from an ancestor.

Exercise 2 · Application and update

Delivery changes, the batch stays the same

Start again from the initial order. The flat charge f changes to 12 €, while n = 6, p = 3 €/notebook and e = 1 €/notebook remain fixed. Identify the old results that are still valid, calculate the new total and the new average price, then check by sharing the flat charge.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The first calculation that reads f is the calculation of t.

Hint 2

Compare the sum u + f/n with the result of the path through t and then m.

Compare with the solution

u = 4 €/notebook and s = 24 € remain valid: they do not depend on f. The results t and m must be recalculated because they are descendants of f.

New total: 24 + 12 = 36 €. New average price: 36/6 = 6 €/notebook. The number of notebooks in the division is still 6.

Another path: the delivery share is 12/6 = 2 €/notebook. Adding u gives 4 + 2 = 6 €/notebook. The graph and its operations are preserved; the values in this evaluation have changed.

Check your reasoning

  • u and s are reused with a justification based on dependencies.
  • t = 36 € and m = 6 €/notebook are recalculated in that order.
  • Sharing the flat charge confirms the result without adding a cost in € to a price in €/notebook.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and transfer

A cost estimate mixes two versions of the order

Start again from the initial rates, with f = 6 €. The quantity changes from 6 to 8 notebooks. Someone updates s and t but keeps 6 in the denominator of m. Repair the calculation using exact fractions, quantify the error in the average price, and explain how the total can rise while the average price falls.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The quantity n is shared by the multiplication and the division. The new total and the old number of notebooks do not describe the same order.

Hint 2

Calculate u + f/n with the new n to check the repaired average price.

Hint 3

Subtract the repaired price from the price obtained with the outdated denominator; keep the fractions until the end.

Compare with the solution

u remains 4 €/notebook. The subtotal becomes 8 × 4 = 32 €, then t = 32 + 6 = 38 €. Every use of n must be replaced: m = 38/8 = 19/4 €/notebook, or 4.75 €/notebook.

The outdated cost estimate gives 38/6 = 19/3 €/notebook, or approximately 6.33 €/notebook. Its overestimate is 19/3 − 19/4 = 19/12 €/notebook, or approximately 1.58 €/notebook. These decimals are rounded representations; the difference is calculated using the exact fractions.

Independent check: the flat charge per notebook is 6/8 = 3/4 €/notebook. Adding the unit cost 4 gives 19/4 €/notebook. The total 38 € exceeds 30 €, but the average price 4.75 €/notebook is below 5 €/notebook: the flat charge is shared among more notebooks.

The graph structure was enough to identify the fault: the direct branch n → m had kept an old value. A consistent evaluation uses the same version of the inputs throughout the graph.

Check your reasoning

  • The multiplication and the division both use n = 8.
  • The exact result 19/4 €/notebook and the exact difference 19/12 €/notebook come before the rounded values.
  • The fault is linked to a forgotten branch of the graph.
  • The fall in the average price is justified by sharing the flat charge, under the assumptions of constant prices.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What belongs to the graph, and what belongs to its evaluation?

The graph carries the inputs, the operations, their ordered arguments and their dependencies. An evaluation assigns values to these nodes for the same version of the inputs. Changing a rate can leave the graph unchanged.

Recall 2What are the direct parents of m, and why is p not one of them?

The last operation m = t/n reads t and n directly. p acts along the path p → u → s → t → m. An ancestor can influence a result without being read directly by its last operation.

Recall 3What criterion makes an evaluation order valid?

Each parent must precede the node that uses it. In our graph without cycles, the inputs are available before u, then s, then t, then m. Several orders of the inputs work; a cycle would prevent this direct evaluation.

Recall 4Why does changing n require checking several branches?

The same n is used in s = n × u and in m = t/n. We must recalculate s, t and m using the new quantity everywhere. An old denominator combines values from different orders.

Recall 5Does an arrow from n guarantee that the result increases with n?

It guarantees only that n is used. With our constant rates and a positive flat charge, t = n × u + f increases while m = u + f/n decreases as n increases. The direction of change depends on the operations and their assumptions.
