Level 3 · C169·M1

Filtration

Your goalBuild a filtration from successive observations and decide whether an event belongs to the information available at a given time.

Before you start
c168-m5 : Radon-Nikodym · c129-m5 : backward induction · c130-m5 : financing conditions

✿01 See the idea

A small device displays 2 indicator lights in succession, each green or red. At first, the colors are hidden. After the first reading, you know the first color; after the next, you know the whole pair. The readings are reliable, and you retain everything you have seen. The scene and the chosen probabilities are fictional.

Let V stand for green and R for red. A complete history, such as VR, gives green first and then red. If you have only seen the first green light, your reading cannot distinguish VV from VR. The question “Is the first light green?” has a definite answer; the question about the second still has to wait.

Information therefore divides the histories into blocks: within a block, the available observations are identical. A new observation can split a block into smaller blocks. As long as the observations remain accessible, it does not merge histories that have already been distinguished.

A filtration is the family of these collections of information over time. It describes the questions you can answer, rather than the color actually displayed or the probability you assign to it. First look at how the blocks split in the diagram; we will then construct the corresponding families of events.

The rule to remember

Richer information contains all the events whose truth could already be determined. In a finite sample space, the truth of an event can be determined exactly when the event is a union of whole blocks of histories that are still indistinguishable.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Constructing the information from the device

The times t = 0, 1, 2 count the readings taken. Each color has 2 possibilities, giving 2 × 2 = 4 histories. We assume these histories are equally likely: each has probability 1/4, or 0.25, with no unit. Let us calculate the available sigma-algebras, then test the event “the second light is green”.

  1. Define the sample space and symbols

    Ω is the set {VV, VR, RV, RR} of complete histories; ω denotes a particular history. An event is a subset of Ω. ∅ is the impossible event; Ω is the certain event. The ambient sigma-algebra F is 𝒫(Ω) here, the set of all subsets of Ω. P(B) denotes the probability of an event B: P and 𝒫 therefore have different roles.

    X1 and X2 denote the successive colors: they are variables taking the values V or R, with no physical unit. Their indices are the reading numbers. t is a time in our set T = {0, 1, 2}; it counts steps rather than measuring seconds. Probabilities and numbers of elements have no physical unit.

    Ω={VV, VR, RV, RR},F=𝒫(Ω),P({ω})=14

  2. From blocks to events whose truth can be determined

    A sigma-algebra on Ω contains Ω, the complement of each of its events, and the union of any countable family of its events. It therefore also contains ∅. The complement of A, written Aᶜ, is Ω with A removed. Here the sample space is finite: countable unions reduce to unions of a finite number of distinct events.

    A partition is a set of nonempty, disjoint blocks whose union is Ω. At a fixed time, take the partition of histories that give the same observations. To be able to determine the truth of an event for every possible reading, it is necessary and sufficient to take whole blocks: if a block is split, the same observations could give either true or false.

    Ft is the sigma-algebra of all these unions of blocks. In this finite model, the elementary blocks are called its atoms. We choose to include or exclude each atom, independently of the other choices. This is a count of subsets, rather than an assumption of probabilistic independence.

  3. Before and after the first reading

    At first, there are no readings: all the histories are in the same block Ω. Excluding it gives ∅; including it gives Ω. Thus F0 contains 2 events. The realized history has not yet been distinguished.

    After the first reading, define A = {VV, VR}, “the first light is green”, and Aᶜ = {RV, RR}. These are the 2 blocks. The possible unions are ∅, A, Aᶜ, and Ω: 2 choices for the first block × 2 choices for the other = 4 events.

    F1 denotes this entire family before the actual color is known. If you see V, you learn that ω belongs to A; the sigma-algebra does not become {A}. That family would not even be a sigma-algebra.

    F0={∅,Ω};F1={∅,A,Ac,Ω}

  4. After the complete pair, check the inclusions

    The 4 histories can now be distinguished: each singleton, meaning each set containing just one history, is an atom. For the first 2 atoms, there are 4 choices; for the other 2, there are just as many. Together, this gives 4 × 4 = 16 events. Every subset of Ω is a union of singletons: F2 = 𝒫(Ω).

    Check: every event in F0 appears in F1; every event in F1 appears in F2. Inclusion, written ⊆, means that all the elements of the family on the left are in the family on the right. The event counts 2, 4, 16 are consistent with these inclusions, but their increase alone would not prove them: the events themselves must be checked.

    F0⊆F1⊆F2=𝒫(Ω)

  5. Name the natural filtration

    On a probability space (Ω, F, P), a filtration (Ft) indexed by the times in T is a family of sub-sigma-algebras of F such that Fs ⊆ Ft whenever s ≤ t. s and t denote times; a sub-sigma-algebra is a sigma-algebra whose events all belong to F. Inclusion can be equality: a time with no new observation does not require a gain in information.

    Our family is the natural filtration of the readings: F1 = σ(X1) and F2 = σ(X1, X2). σ, read “sigma”, denotes the smallest sigma-algebra making these observations measurable, so that they can be read from the information it represents. On our finite sample space, this is exactly the sigma-algebra constructed from the blocks of identical observations. F0 is the trivial sigma-algebra, because we chose to start with no initial information about the colors.

    A variable is Ft-measurable when its value can be determined from the available observations; here it is constant on each atom of Ft. The indicator of an event B, equal to 1 in B and 0 elsewhere, is Ft-measurable exactly when B belongs to Ft. No assumption about the probabilities of the colors is needed for the inclusions. We remain in discrete time; no additional continuous-time condition is imposed.

    s≤t⇒Fs⊆Ft⊆F

  6. Test an event and calculate without looking ahead

    Let B = {VV, RV}, “the second light is green”. B does belong to F2. It does not belong to F1: VV and VR give the same first reading V, but only VV belongs to B. The second diagram shows the same split in the other block. B is therefore an event in the model whose truth cannot be determined from the first reading.

    Under our uniform distribution, P(A) = 2/4 = 1/2 and P(B) = 2/4 = 1/2. The intersection A ∩ B, the set of histories in both A and B, is {VV}: P(A ∩ B) = 1/4 = 1/4. If the first reading is V, we restrict the denominator to block A, whose probability is strictly positive.

    P(B|A)=P(A∩B)P(A)=1424=12

    The bar | means “given”. Independent check: among VV and VR, still equally likely given A, 1 history out of 2 is in B. We obtain 1/2 again, or 0.50, with no unit. This probability is neither 0 nor 1: the reading does not determine the truth of B. After the complete pair, B will be true or false according to the observed history. Here, the calculation also shows that the event remains undecided; the general proof was the split within the blocks, without any probability calculation.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the list of sigma-algebras. Reconstruct the blocks after each reading, then their unions. Explain why seeing V changes what you know about the realized history without replacing F₁ with a family containing only A.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I saw green, so the sigma-algebra now contains only the histories starting with green.”

    You have learned that the realized history is in A. F₁ also describes the events you can determine to be false: it contains Aᶜ, ∅, and Ω. Start again from the unions of blocks, then distinguish this family from the block actually observed.

    Reconstruct the sigma-algebra after the first reading

  • “I know the probability of a green second light, so this event belongs to my information.”

    Knowing a probability does not tell you whether the event is true. VV and VR remain compatible with a green first light and give opposite answers for B. Look for this pair of histories before calculating a probability.

    See the event that splits the blocks

  • “At each time, I can keep only the latest color: this is still a filtration.”

    After the second reading, VV and RV have the same latest color but different first colors. If you forget the first reading, you lose A. Check inclusion of the sigma-algebras, rather than just the number of visible colors.

    Test the device that erases its first reading

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · membership

Matching colors

In the same device, C = {VV, RR} means “the colors are identical”. Does C belong to F₀, F₁, and F₂? Justify each answer using the blocks; do not calculate any probabilities.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

An event whose truth can be determined must include or exclude each block in its entirety.

Hint 2

At the first reading, compare VV and VR. For the final time, recall what 𝒫(Ω) contains.

Compare with the solution

C is neither ∅ nor Ω, so C does not belong to F0. In the block {VV, VR}, C includes VV but excludes VR: it splits an atom of F1. Thus C does not belong to F1.

C is the union of the singletons {VV} and {RR}, both available at the final time: C belongs to F2. Once the pair is observed, you can answer true or false; the actual colors do not have to be identical.

Check your reasoning

  • Exclude C from F₀ using the exact list of initial events.
  • Give indistinguishable histories for which the truth of C differs at the first reading.
  • Express C as a union of final blocks, without confusing membership with occurrence.

Exercise 2 · Application · exact calculation

The first light is observed to be red

The first reading is R. Calculate P(B | Aᶜ) for B = {VV, RV}, then P(C | Aᶜ) for C = {VV, RR}. State whether this observation is enough to determine the truth of B or C, and explain what the calculated probabilities represent.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The only compatible histories are RV and RR. Write the intersection of each event with this block.

Hint 2

The denominator is P(Aᶜ), rather than the probability of the whole sample space. You can also count directly within the block.

Compare with the solution

Aᶜ = {RV, RR} contains 2 histories: P(Aᶜ) = 2/4 = 1/2. The intersection B ∩ Aᶜ is {RV}; the intersection C ∩ Aᶜ is {RR}. Each has probability 1/4.

Each quotient is 1/4 ÷ 1/2 = 1/2, or 0.50, with no unit. Check by counting: 1 favorable history out of 2 compatible histories. Both B and C remain undecided on the observed block, but they do not select the same history.

These results are probabilities under the stated fictional distribution. They add no observation to the device. Distinguishing RV from RR still requires the second reading.

Check your reasoning

  • Write the intersections {RV} and {RR}, rather than drawing a conclusion solely from the equality of the probabilities.
  • Restrict the denominator to a red first light and recover the fraction by counting.
  • Distinguish a calculated probability from an event whose truth has been determined.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · counterexample and repair

The device erases its first reading

Change only the memory of the same device: after the pair, it retains only the second color. Its final information is G₂ = σ(X₂). Write its events. Is the family F₀, F₁, G₂ a filtration? Give an event that is lost and a repair. Is the number of events enough to decide?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The new blocks group histories with the same second letter.

Hint 2

Test A = {VV, VR} in G₂. Compare VV and RV, then ask how to retain the first reading as well.

Compare with the solution

The blocks are B = {VV, RV} and Bᶜ = {VR, RR}. Thus G2 = {∅, B, Bᶜ, Ω}, with 4 events. The symbol G distinguishes this new final sigma-algebra from F2 in the model that retains the past.

A belongs to F1 but not to G2: the histories VV and RV have the same second light, whereas only VV is in A. Thus F1 is not included in G2; the proposed family is not a filtration. The sigma-algebras here have the same number of events, but do not contain the same events.

To repair this, retain the 2 readings: the final sigma-algebra becomes σ(X1, X2) = F2. It does contain F1 and G2. In this scene, every singleton can then be identified, so we recover the 16 events. Information that forgets the past can describe a display; it does not necessarily satisfy the inclusions required of a filtration.

Check your reasoning

  • Write the blocks based on the second letter and all their unions.
  • Exhibit A as evidence of the loss, using specific histories.
  • Repair by retaining the past and check inclusion of the events.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What condition turns a family of sub-sigma-algebras into a filtration?

On the same probability space, Fₛ is included in Fₜ whenever s ≤ t: every event whose truth could already be determined remains so. Equality is allowed; new information is not required.

Recall 2In our finite sample space, how can you check B ∈ Fₜ without calculating its probability?

Group the histories that give the same observations at t. B must be a union of whole blocks. If it splits a block, identical observations would leave both true and false possible.

Recall 3What does σ(X₁, X₂) mean, and why do we not keep only X₂?

It is the smallest sigma-algebra from which the 2 readings can be read. It also retains all the questions whose answers could be determined from the first reading. σ(X₂) can lose these questions, so it is generally insufficient to extend the filtration.

Recall 4Why does knowing P(B | A) = 1/2 not mean knowing whether B occurs?

After a green first light, VV and VR remain compatible. Only VV is in B. The probability 1/2 describes this uncertainty under the chosen distribution; it does not reveal the second light.

Recall 5When the first light is green, does F₁ become {A}?

No. We learn that the realized history belongs to block A. F₁ remains the family {∅, A, Aᶜ, Ω} of all the questions that can be answered using the first reading, whether they are true or false.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, redraw the blocks at successive times; turn them into sigma-algebras by taking unions; check their inclusions; test an event by looking for a split block. Then reconstruct the statements that distinguish available information, the realized history, and probability. Reading or answering after a hint never establishes that a level has been attained.

What you understand
A decision at a given time must depend on the information available at that time. A calculation window may use only part of the past; the filtration represents the accumulated information, rather than just the values retained by a formula.
