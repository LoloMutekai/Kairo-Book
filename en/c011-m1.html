Level 0 · C011·M1

Summation notation

Your goalRead the indices and bounds of a sum, expand the selected terms and check their count and total separately.

Before you start
c010-m5 : logarithmic scales

✿01 See the idea

On the table, 3 baskets contain 3, 1 and 4 apples respectively. To prepare a count, you slide a strip under the selected baskets. If it covers the whole row, you add everything. If it starts at basket 2, the apples in the first basket stay on the table but are left out of the calculation. The quantities in this scene are fictional.

Each basket has a number: its index. The corresponding quantity is called a term. The index gives a position; the term gives the number of apples at that position. Basket 2 therefore contains 1 apple: its label does not give its quantity.

The symbol ∑, called sigma, replaces a written addition. The starting index is written below the symbol; the ending index is written above it. These bounds are included. On the right, we write the term to take for each index. The drawing first shows the objects, then what changes when we select them.

The rule to remember

Choose the indices before adding the quantities. Count the selected terms separately: the number of baskets and the number of apples answer different questions.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Write, expand and check the count

The same baskets remain on the table, and no apples are moved. Let us first calculate the total for the whole row, then the total for the selection starting at basket 2. All the selected quantities are known, counted at the same time and expressed in apples.

  1. Name each quantity

    The letter a denotes our family of quantities. In aᵢ, the small i is an integer index with no unit: it identifies the basket. aᵢ denotes the number of apples in that basket; it is not the product of a and i. Here the indices run from 1 to 3.

    a1=3;a2=1;a3=4 pommes

    To count the apples on the table, the baskets must be distinct and each apple must be counted only in its own basket. Otherwise, adding their counts could count the same apple several times.

  2. Read the selection in the sigma notation

    Let p be the first index and q the last; both are integers with no unit. S(p, q) denotes the total in apples for this selection. We take consecutive indices, increasing by 1, including both bounds. For a nonempty selection, the indices must belong to the available row.

    S(p,q)=∑i=pqai

    Read this as “the sum of aᵢ for i running from p to q”. ∑ tells us to add; i runs through the positions; aᵢ gives the term to add at each position. For the whole row, p = 1 and q = 3: the selected indices are 1, 2, 3.

  3. Expand before substituting numbers

    Let us write a term for each selected index, in order. This line makes visible what a sigma can hide: no basket is skipped and no basket is counted again.

    ∑i=13ai=a1+a2+a3

    We then replace the names with their quantities: 3 + 1 + 4 apples. The number 3 written above the sigma is an ending position; we do not add it as an extra quantity.

  4. Add while keeping the unit

    Start with 0 apples counted. The first basket contributes 3 apples: 0 + 3 = 3 apples. Adding the next basket gives 3 + 1 = 4 apples. Adding the last gives 4 + 4 = 8 apples.

    S(1,3)=3+1+4=8 pommes

    Independent check: recount the discs in the drawing, then group the baskets differently. The last baskets contain 1 + 4 = 5 apples; the first basket and this group give 3 + 5 = 8 apples. We obtain the same total.

  5. Change a bound and count the terms

    With p = 2 and q = 3, we select only the indices 2 and 3. The first basket is still there but is no longer included in the addition.

    ∑i=23ai=a2+a3=1+4=5 pommes

    Let N be the number of terms, with no physical unit. When p ≤ q, q − p counts the steps from one index to the next; we must add 1 to count the starting index as well.

    N=q−p+1 si p≤q

    The whole row gives 3 − 1 = 2 steps, so 2 + 1 = 3 terms. The shorter selection gives 3 − 2 = 1 step, so 1 + 1 = 2 terms. Its total is 5 apples. Check: 8 − 5 = 3 apples, exactly the contents of the removed basket.

  6. Test the edge cases of the rule

    If p = q = 3, the ending basket is also the starting basket. The calculation 3 − 3 = 0, then 0 + 1 = 1, counts one term. The sum is 4 apples: equal bounds do not make the selection empty.

    If p > q, we use the empty sum convention here: as we move in increasing order, no index is selected and the sum is 0 apples. For example, starting at 3 and ending at 2 gives this empty selection. The previous formula N = q − p + 1 applies only when p ≤ q; it is not our general rule for an empty selection.

    A selected but closed basket raises a different question. If its quantity is unknown, we cannot calculate the exact total from the available information. Writing 0 apples in its place would invent a count. The empty sum contains no terms; the sum with incomplete information contains a term whose value we do not know.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations and redraw the shorter selection. Explain separately what the bounds, aᵢ, the number of terms and the total tell you. Then explain the difference between a basket that is not selected and a selected basket whose contents are unknown.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The small index multiplies the quantity.”

    The index names a position. The term with index 2 is 1 apple in our scene. First point to the basket, then read its contents; do not perform any multiplication based on its label.

    Review positions and quantities

  • “The upper bound is enough to count the terms.”

    List the indices from the lower bound, including the ending index. From 2 to 3, the list contains 2 terms. Check this count using q − p + 1 when p ≤ q, then calculate the total separately.

    Review changing a bound and counting

  • “An unknown value can become 0, like an empty sum.”

    Check whether a term has been selected. With no terms, our convention gives 0 apples. With a selected term whose quantity is missing, the exact total remains unknown. Open the basket or obtain its count before drawing a conclusion.

    Compare the empty selection and the closed basket

Exercise 2 · Compare and check

Same end, different selections

On paper, compare the selections from 1 to 3, from 2 to 3, then from 3 to 3. For each, write the indices, the number of terms and the total in apples. Someone says: “They all end at 3, so they have the same number of terms.” Respond using your calculations.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Equal bounds keep the basket they both identify.

Hint 2

For each nonempty selection, use N = q − p + 1, then add the quantities at the selected positions.

Compare with the solution

From 1 to 3: indices 1, 2, 3; 3 − 1 + 1 = 3 terms; 3 + 1 + 4 = 8 apples.

From 2 to 3: indices 2, 3; 3 − 2 + 1 = 2 terms; 1 + 4 = 5 apples.

From 3 to 3: only index 3; 3 − 3 + 1 = 1 term; the quantity is 4 apples. The shared ending index therefore determines neither the number of terms nor their total.

Check: moving from the first selection to the next removes 3 apples, the contents of the first basket. Moving to the last selection then removes 1 apple, the contents of basket 2.

Check your reasoning

  • Each list includes its bounds and repeats no index.
  • The term counts are 3, 2, 1; the totals are 8, 5, 4 apples.
  • The objection is refuted by naming the starting bound, rather than just citing results.

Exercise 3 · Apply the idea in the same scene

No baskets or a closed basket?

Compare these situations. First, the start is 3 and the end is 2, using our increasing order convention. Next, we select from 2 to 3, but the quantity label on basket 2 is hidden and its contents are no longer known; basket 3 still contains 4 apples. Can we state an exact total in each case? Explain without reusing the hidden quantity.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Distinguish the selection of indices from knowledge of the quantities.

Hint 2

An absence of terms requires no counting; a selected but unknown term requires additional information.

Compare with the solution

Start 3, end 2: the selection in increasing order is empty. It contains 0 terms; its sum is 0 apples by the stated convention.

In the selection from 2 to 3, the indices are still known: there are 2 terms. The total can still be written as a with index 2, plus 4 apples. Since the first quantity is no longer given in this situation, we cannot state an exact numerical total.

If we later learn that the closed basket is empty, its quantity really is 0 apples and the total is 4 apples. This result becomes calculable through new information; it does not follow from the missing information on the label.

Check your reasoning

  • The empty sum receives its value by convention and the number of terms is 0.
  • The number of terms in the selection with the closed basket remains 2.
  • No quantity is invented for the unknown basket; the information needed to draw a conclusion is specified.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do i and aᵢ represent in the basket example?

i is an integer index with no unit that identifies the basket’s position. aᵢ is the quantity of apples in that basket. The index does not multiply the quantity.

Recall 2How do you read a sigma with i = p below, q above and aᵢ on the right?

Add the terms aᵢ for consecutive indices from p to q, including both bounds and increasing by 1. p is the start and q the end; S(p, q) denotes the total in apples here.

Recall 3How do you count the terms when p ≤ q, and why must you add 1?

N = q − p + 1: q − p counts the steps between positions, then 1 adds the starting position. If p = q, 1 term remains. This count does not give the number of apples.

Recall 4Why do an empty sum and an unknown quantity lead to different conclusions?

The empty sum selects no terms; its value is 0 by our convention. An unknown quantity in a selected term prevents us from calculating the exact total. A known quantity equal to 0 is yet another situation.

Recall 5How can you check a sum of basket quantities without just rereading the result?

List the included indices, write their terms, check their count, then recount the apples or group the addition differently. After removing a basket, the difference between the totals must equal its contents.
