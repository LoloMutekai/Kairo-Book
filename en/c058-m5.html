Level 2 · C058·M5

Change of variables

Your goalCalculate the mass of a plate by changing its coordinates, domain and area element, then check the result.

Before you start
c045-m5 : introductory quadrature · c057-m5 : Hessian · c058-m1 : double integrals · c058-m2 : triple integrals · c058-m3 : calculations using Fubini’s theorem · c058-m4 : polar coordinates

✿01 See the idea

A thin plate has the shape of a parallelogram. Its material is more concentrated towards the vertex opposite the origin. To add up small pieces of mass, we could divide the plate along the usual axes; the boundaries would be slanted. A grid that follows its sides lets us work on a square.

Imagine a square drawn on a coordinate sheet, then stretched and slanted to cover the plate. Each small cell becomes a parallelogram. We are changing how we locate the pieces: the plate and its mass stay the same. However, a cell on the sheet and its image do not have the same area.

In our transformation, an area of 1 m² on the sheet corresponds to 3 m² on the plate. Substituting only the coordinates in the density would overlook this difference. We must also convert the area of each piece.

The plate, its dimensions and its density law are fictional. The drawing shows the correspondences; the calculation will give the areas. We stay in the plane and use an invertible linear transformation, without repeating the whole chapter on multiple integrals.

The rule to remember

Change the domain, the density evaluated at the corresponding point and the area element together. The area factor is the absolute value of the Jacobian determinant.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Find the plate’s mass using its coordinate square

Let us calculate the mass M of D = T(E), with E = [0, L] × [0, L], L = 1 m, T(u, v) = (2u + 1v, 1u + 2v), and surface density ρ(x, y) = κ(x + y), where κ = 1 kg/m³. Surface density is mass per unit area.

  1. Define what we are adding up

    x and y are the coordinates on the plate, in metres; u and v are its new coordinates, also in metres. T assigns the point (x, y) to each pair (u, v). E denotes the starting square and D the whole plate. The coefficients of T are dimensionless. L is the side length of E.

    ρ, the Greek letter rho, denotes surface density in kg/m². κ, kappa, is a coefficient in kg/m³: multiplying it by the length x + y gives kg/m². M is the mass in kg. The element dx dy is an area in m²; integrating ρ over D adds up the small masses ρ dx dy.

    M=∬Dρ(x,y) dx dy

    We assume that the plate is flat, that this continuous law describes its density exactly and that there is no thickness to integrate over. On D, x and y are positive or 0: the density is nonnegative. This is not a measurement of a real material.

  2. Check the domain and the absence of overlap

    When only u increases, displacement on the plate follows (2, 1); when only v increases, it follows (1, 2). The vertices of the square therefore give exactly the vertices O, A, B and C in the drawings. Since T is linear, the sides become line segments and the interior becomes the interior of their parallelogram.

    To check that each point is counted only once, let us solve the equations in the other direction:

    u=2x−1y3;v=2y−1x3

    Substituting these expressions into T gives back x and y: the coordinates are unique. In the old coordinates, D is defined by 0 ≤ 2x − 1y ≤ 3L and 0 ≤ 2y − 1x ≤ 3L. All three terms in each inequality have units of metres. In the new coordinates, we simply keep 0 ≤ u ≤ L and 0 ≤ v ≤ L.

  3. Calculate the area factor in the correct direction

    The Jacobian matrix DT collects the partial derivatives: its first row describes x and its second row y; its first column corresponds to u and its second column to v. Here, all its entries are constant and dimensionless. J denotes its signed determinant.

    DT=[∂x∂u∂x∂v∂y∂u∂y∂v]=[2112]
    J=2×2−1×1=4−1=3

    This determinant measures the oriented area of the parallelogram formed by the columns. A small cell with sides Δu and Δv has an image with area |J| Δu Δv. Here Δ means a small change in a coordinate, in metres. For T, this relation is exact at any scale because the transformation is linear. For a curved transformation, it describes the local approximation that leads to the integral.

    We therefore replace dx dy with 3 d u d v. The factor is dimensionless because both areas are in m². The determinant of the inverse transformation is 1/3: it would convert the area on the plate into the area on the sheet. Here, we go from the sheet to the plate.

  4. State the rule and check its assumptions

    In the plane, the rule we use is:

    ∬Dρ(x,y) dx dy=∬Eρ(T(u,v))|J(u,v)| du dv

    ρ(T(u, v)) means evaluating the density at the point on the plate given by T. The bars |J| denote the absolute value: an area does not change sign if we reverse the order of the coordinates.

    The following assumptions are sufficient here: T has continuous partial derivatives on a neighbourhood of the domain, it is bijective between the interiors of E and D, its determinant is never 0 there, the boundaries consist of line segments or regular curves with 0 area, and the density is continuous on the bounded plate. Bijective means that each point has exactly one preimage. Our explicit inverse, J = 3 ≠ 0 and the continuous law for ρ satisfy these assumptions.

    The boundaries themselves contribute no mass to this surface integral. A transformation that folds the domain onto itself does not satisfy our assumption; applying the formula without accounting for overlaps would count some pieces several times.

  5. Transform the density and set the bounds

    Only now do we substitute x and y in the density law: x + y = (2 + 1)u + (1 + 2)v = 3u + 3v. The density evaluated at the corresponding point is therefore ρ(T(u, v)) = κ(3u + 3v), in kg/m².

    Define g(u, v) = ρ(T(u, v)) |J|. Then g d u d v gives the mass of the corresponding piece on the plate. The function g expresses mass per unit coordinate area on E, in kg/m². It includes the area factor; the composition ρ(T(u, v)) alone does not.

    M=3κ∫0L∫0L(3u+3v) du dv

    The inner integral is with respect to u and the outer integral with respect to v. The square makes the bounds independent; the constant factor 3κ can be taken outside the integrals. The bounds describe E, since d u d v divides up this domain. Keeping the old slanted boundaries would describe a different region.

  6. Integrate to obtain the result without rounding

    During integration with respect to u, v is fixed. An antiderivative of 3u + 3v is 3 × u × u / 2 + 3uv. Between 0 and L, this gives 3 × L × L / 2 + 3Lv. The next integration, with respect to v, gives:

    M=3κ(3L2L2+3LL22)

    The integral of either u alone or v alone over E is (L × L × L)/2 = 1/2 m³. With L = 1 m, the contributions in parentheses are 3 × 1/2 = 3/2 m³ and 3 × 1/2 = 3/2 m³. Their sum is 3 m³.

    Multiplying by κ = 1 kg/m³ gives 3 kg. The area factor 3 remains: M = 3 × 3 = 9 kg. The cubic metres from integration cancel against those in the coefficient κ; the result is indeed a mass.

    If we had forgotten the Jacobian, we would have reported 3 kg, despite the missing area conversion. Units alone do not detect this omission: |J| is dimensionless here.

  7. Check using area and symmetry

    The area of the square is L × L = 1 m². The area of the plate is therefore |J| × L × L = 3 m²; we can also find it using the determinant of the vectors OA and OB. The maximum density, at vertex C, is κ × (3 m + 3 m) = 6 kg/m². The mass must lie between 0 and 18 kg. Our result lies within these bounds.

    For a different exact check, pair each point (u, v) with its opposite point (L − u, L − v) in the square. Their images on the plate are symmetric about its centre. Their densities always sum to 6 kg/m². These pieces have the same area: the average density over D is therefore 6/2 = 3 kg/m².

    Mass = average density × area = 3 × 3 = 9 kg. This confirms the integral calculation using a geometric property of this affine density. For an arbitrary density, simply taking the value at the centre would not necessarily give its average.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the equations and reconstruct the chain E → T → D. Explain why the mass stays the same, why coordinate areas change and why substituting x and y in ρ is not enough. Finish by reconstructing the check using pairs of opposite points.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I have substituted x and y in the density, so the change is complete.”

    The composition gives the density at the corresponding point. It does not convert the area of the cell. Revisit the function g and the bounds of E: all three elements, domain, density and area, must describe the same pieces.

    Revisit the transformation of the density and bounds

  • “The determinant is negative, so the mass is negative too.”

    The sign describes the orientation of the coordinates. When integrating surface mass, the area uses |J|. Swapping the coordinates in the last exercise preserves the plate and gives a determinant with the opposite sign; it must preserve its positive mass.

    Test swapping the coordinates

  • “The inverse contains the factor 1/3; that is the factor we must multiply by.”

    Write down the direction: T goes from (u, v) to (x, y). The area factor that transforms d u d v into dx dy is 3. The factor 1/3 transforms in the other direction. Compare the areas of the square and the plate to check your choice.

    Review the two domains and their areas

Exercise 1 · Geometry warm-up

Find the factor without using the density

Keep T and E. Calculate the area of D from its sides OA and OB. Explain why the factor 1/3 is unsuitable for converting d u d v to dx dy.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The sides are the images of the displacements (L, 0) and (0, L). The determinant of their components gives an oriented area.

Hint 2

Take the absolute value, then compare the resulting area with L × L.

Compare with the solution

OA = (2L, 1L) and OB = (1L, 2L). Their determinant is (2 × 2 − 1 × 1) × L × L = 3 × L × L. The area is therefore 3 m², compared with 1 m² for E.

An area on E is multiplied by 3 to obtain the area of its image on D. The factor 1/3 is for going back from D to E. It would reduce the area instead of producing the calculated area of the plate.

Check your reasoning

  • I use the sides in an explicitly stated order and take the absolute value of the determinant.
  • I give 3 m² and state the direction of the area conversion.

Exercise 2 · Complete application on paper

Keep only part of the same plate

We keep the part D′ = T(E′), where E′ requires 0 ≤ u ≤ 0.50 m and 0 ≤ v ≤ 1 m. The density and T are unchanged. Calculate its mass. Is it half the total mass? Justify your answer before comparing the fractions.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The area factor remains 3. Only the upper bound for u becomes h = 1/2 m.

Hint 2

Separate the terms in u and v: their integrals over E′ are respectively (h × h × L)/2 and (h × L × L)/2.

Compare with the solution

Let h = 1/2 m be the width of E′. The domain covers half the area, but selects smaller values of u, where the density is lower. Its mass must therefore be less than half the total mass.

M′=3κ(3h2L2+3hL22)

Here, M′ denotes the mass of D′. The integrals of u alone and v alone are 1/8 m³ and 1/4 m³. Multiplying by the density coefficients gives 3/8 m³ and 3/4 m³, or 9/8 m³ in total.

After multiplying by κ and then |J|: M′ = 3 × 9/8 = 27/8 kg, or 3.375 kg. The ratio M′/M is 3/8, less than 1/2. Yet the area is 3/2 m², half of 3 m². The nonuniform density explains the difference.

Check your reasoning

  • I change the bound for u and keep the density law and |J|.
  • I justify the integrals (h × h × L)/2 and (h × L × L)/2, instead of automatically dividing the mass by 2.
  • I obtain 27/8 kg, then explain why the mass fraction 3/8 differs from the area fraction 1/2.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and repair of a calculation

Swap the coordinates on the sheet

For the whole plate, replace T with S(r, s) = (1r + 2s, 2r + 1s), with 0 ≤ r ≤ L and 0 ≤ s ≤ L, in metres. Someone obtains -9 kg. Find their error, repair the calculation and check that S covers the same plate.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

S(r, s) = T(s, r): the columns of the matrix have been swapped.

Hint 2

Calculate the signed determinant, then distinguish it from the factor that multiplies area. Also check ρ(S(r, s)).

Compare with the solution

r and s are the new coordinates on the sheet. Swapping their roles leaves the whole square unchanged: S covers exactly D, once only. Its determinant is 1 × 1 − 2 × 2 = -3.

ρ(S(r, s)) = κ(3r + 3s). Here its coefficients are both 3, so the integral of this composition over the square is still 3 kg. Using the signed determinant would give -3 × 3 = -9 kg.

We need its absolute value: |-3| × 3 = 9 kg. The mass, physical domain and density are identical; only the orientation of the coordinates has changed. If the transformation were not injective, taking an absolute value would not be enough to correct an overlap.

Check your reasoning

  • I justify S(r, s) = T(s, r), and hence that the physical domains are identical.
  • I find the determinant -3 and the area factor 3.
  • I restore 9 kg by explaining the sign, without changing the density.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which parts of a double integral must be transformed together?

Domain D becomes E; the density becomes its composition ρ(T(u, v)); the area dx dy becomes |J| d u d v. These elements must describe the same physical pieces.

Recall 2Which Jacobian should we use when T maps the new coordinates to the old ones?

The determinant of the derivatives of the old coordinates with respect to the new ones. Here, |J| = 3 converts area on the square to area on the plate; 1/3 corresponds to the reverse direction.

Recall 3Why take the absolute value of the determinant when integrating surface mass?

The sign records orientation; area is a nonnegative quantity. Swapping the coordinates reverses the sign of the determinant without changing the mass of the plate.

Recall 4What should we check before applying our change of variables rule?

The image domain, uniqueness of preimages in the interior, continuity of the derivatives and a nonzero determinant; here regular boundaries and a continuous density on a bounded domain complete a sufficient set of assumptions. A transformation that folds the domain requires overlaps to be accounted for.

Recall 5How can we check the exact mass of our plate without repeating the integrations?

Pair opposite points: their densities sum to 6 kg/m² and their areas are equal. The average is 3 kg/m²; multiplied by the area 3 m², it gives 9 kg again. This property depends on our affine density.
