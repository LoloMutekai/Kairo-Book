Transition and predicted variance

Your goalPropagate an estimate and its variance, then explain why the transition factor is squared.

Before you start
c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c040-m5 : least squares · c011-m5 : binomial · c036-m5 : annotated graphs · c064-m1 : latent state and observation

✿01 See the idea

A sensor needs to track the temperature in a room, but no new reading has arrived yet. At the previous minute, we had an estimate of 20 °C and an error variance of 4 °C². What should we retain during this minute without a reading?

The transition is our rule for moving forward in time. Here it keeps the estimated temperature unchanged. It does not make us more certain: changes that the rule does not represent may occur. We assign them a variance Q, called process noise.

A variance is a mean of squared deviations under the probabilistic model. It measures an expected spread, not the particular error during this minute. Its unit is degrees Celsius squared. The standard deviation is its square root, which returns to degrees Celsius.

The values in this scene are fictional. With our transition rule, they describe a mathematical exercise; they are neither weather readings nor settings validated for a real room.

The rule to remember

Propagate the value using F; propagate the variance using F squared, then add the variance Q of the new independent noise.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A minute without a sensor reading

Start with x̂ = 20 °C and P = 4 °C², with dimensionless F = 1 and Q = 1 °C². Calculate what the model predicts before any new observation.

  1. Define the quantities and assumptions

    x denotes the unknown temperature at the start; x̂, with a hat, denotes its estimate. P is the variance of the error e = x − x̂. The next temperature x′ follows x′ = Fx + w. F is known; w is a random change with 0 mean and variance Q, independent of the previous error. The prime means “at the next minute”.

    The superscript − on predicted quantities means “before the reading”; it does not indicate a negative value. We assume that the initial error has 0 mean. Without these assumptions, the variance formula must be reconsidered.

  2. Predict the value

    x^−=Fx^

    The noise w has 0 mean, so it does not shift the predicted value. The calculation gives 1 × 20 = 20 °C. The actual next temperature remains unknown.

  3. Derive the variance rule

    The next error is e′ = x′ − Fx̂ = Fe + w. When we square it, the cross term is 2 × F × e × w. Its mean is 0 because of independence and the 0 means. Multiplying the error by F therefore multiplies the mean of its square by F².

    P−=F2P+Q

    Independence is sufficient here. More generally, 0 covariance between e and w is enough to eliminate the cross term; otherwise, that term must be added.

  4. Calculate without mixing units

    P−=12×4+1=5 °C²

    The propagated contribution is 4 °C². The noise adds 1 °C². We therefore keep the pair 20 °C and 5 °C². Adding Q to the temperature would mix degrees and degrees squared.

  5. Check the limiting cases

    If Q were 0, the predicted variance would be 4 °C²: the rule would retain exactly the initial uncertainty for F = 1. With our positive Q, it does rise to 5 °C². The square root of P is 2 °C, since 2 × 2 = 4 °C²; using 2 in place of P would lose the square.

    With no reading for an additional minute and F still equal to 1, it would reach 6 °C². This result describes the model; it does not demonstrate that its noise correctly represents the room.

Explain it in your own words

Why does the noise leave the predicted value unchanged while changing its variance? Also explain where independence is used.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Multiplying P by F is enough.”

    P represents a squared error: multiplying the error by F multiplies its square by F². Check this using the factor in the scaling exercise.

    Review the derivation

  • “Q is an adjustment to add to the temperature.”

    Q is a variance, in degrees squared. The random change w has 0 mean: it adds uncertainty to the prediction without shifting its mean.

    Review the derivation

  • “Prediction always increases the variance.”

    The factor F can shrink the error. The addition of Q can be smaller than this reduction, as in the last exercise.

    Review the derivation

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does F mean in the model?

The known transition factor that multiplies the previous state to predict the next one.

Recall 2Why is F squared in the variance?

Variance is a mean of squared errors; multiplying an error by F multiplies its square by F².

Recall 3What assumption allows F²P and Q to be added?

The cross term must have 0 mean, which is guaranteed here by the 0 means and the independence of the new noise and the previous error.

Recall 4Have we used the new reading?

No. The predicted pair is calculated before any new observation; innovation and gain will come in the next unit.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define the state, estimate and variance; state the model and its noise; propagate the value using F; propagate the squared error using F²; add Q under explicit assumptions; keep the two units distinct.
