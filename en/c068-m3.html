Level 2 · C068·M3

Thompson sampling

Your goalReproduce a Thompson selection from given Beta beliefs, justify the choice and distinguish a draw, a mean and an observed outcome.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

In a virtual workshop, a simulated device has broken down. You can try procedure A, which restarts its simulated module, or procedure B, which reloads its simulated setting. These options are the arms of the bandit. You must choose one for the next trial without knowing its true probability of success.

For each procedure, you have a belief: a distribution of the probabilities of success that are still plausible. A Beta distribution serves this role here. Its mean summarizes the belief but does not describe all the uncertainty. Thompson sampling draws a plausible probability from each distribution, then chooses the procedure with the highest draw.

In our fictional run, A has the higher mean, but B receives the larger draw. B is therefore selected for the next trial. This choice allows exploration of an option ranked lower by the means; it means neither that B is certainly better nor that the procedures have equal chances of being chosen.

The number drawn describes a possible probability. The trial outcome will be something else: 1 if the test of whether the device works again passes, 0 if it fails. Drawing a favorable value does not get the device working again.

All distributions and values in this scene are fictional and chosen for an exact calculation. We study only selection, with beliefs already given. No real trial or measured gain is presented.

The rule to remember

Draw from each belief, compare the sampled probabilities and select the arm with the maximum; then wait for an observation before discussing an outcome or learning.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Choose the next procedure in the virtual workshop

The available beliefs are Beta(2, 1) for A and Beta(1, 1) for B. No new trial has taken place yet. We will construct a run that can be reproduced by hand, then check why it chooses B.

  1. Name the quantities and assumptions

    i denotes A or B. θ_i is the true, unknown probability that procedure i succeeds in our simulator. H denotes the information already available. The belief about θ_i given H is Beta(α_i, β_i), with α_i and β_i strictly positive. These are parameters with no units, interpreted as pseudocounts; we do not present them as counts of observed trials.

    The procedures have the same cost and the same stakes in this exercise. Their probabilities of success are assumed to be stable. Outcomes are independent across trials conditional on the true probabilities; the arm beliefs are assumed to be independent given H. A context linking the arms would require a different joint distribution.

    Y denotes the next outcome: 1 for a successful test, 0 for a failure. Under the model, if arm i is tried, its probability of producing Y = 1 is θ_i. θ_i and the sampled probabilities have no units; Y is a code with no units, never a repair duration.

  2. Read the means without treating them as draws

    adwzwv hchcxf qeoiat jcmqqv uubdyc svozmx rpij It equals α_i divided by α_i + β_i. The denominator combines the pseudocount weights, not just the successes.

    mA=22+1=23≈0.667
    mB=11+1=12=0.50

    The mean for A exceeds the mean for B by 1/6. Always choosing the highest mean would give A. Thompson sampling uses these same beliefs but compares values drawn from their distributions.

  3. Construct a draw suited to each distribution

    To calculate by hand, we use cumulative distribution functions. x is a probability threshold between 0 and 1. F_i(x) denotes the probability, under the belief, that θ_i is less than or equal to x. For our particular distributions, the density for A is f_A(x) = 2x and the density for B is f_B(x) = 1 on this interval, and both are 0 outside it. A density accumulates probability through its area: this gives F_A(x) = x × x and F_B(x) = x.

    U_A and U_B are independent random numbers with a uniform distribution between 0 and 1: intervals of the same length have the same probability. The thresholds x, the uniform numbers, the densities f_i and the cumulative distribution functions F_i all have no units here. The ~ sign above θ denotes a value drawn from the belief. To obtain the correct distribution, we seek the value whose cumulative distribution function equals U: here, the nonnegative square root of U_A for A, and U_B itself for B.

    θ~A=UA ; θ~B=UB

    Why use this transformation? For A, obtaining √U_A ≤ x is equivalent to having U_A ≤ x × x. This latter event has probability x × x because U_A is uniform: this recovers F_A(x). For B, keeping U_B gives F_B(x) = x directly. Using a raw uniform value for A would therefore not follow its Beta distribution. These simple transformations are specific to the given distributions.

  4. Reproduce the fictional values exactly

    For this run on paper, the uniform values are fixed at U_A = 9/25 = 0.36 and U_B = 4/5 = 0.80. They were chosen to make the calculation easy; they are not presented as the output of a generator that was run. The random mechanism described in the previous step would use new independent draws.

    θ~A=925=35=0.60

    The square root is justified by 3 × 3 = 9 and 5 × 5 = 25. The draw for B remains θ̃_B = U_B = 4/5 = 0.80. We keep the exact fractions for comparison; the decimals are ways of reading the values, all with no units.

  5. Select the maximum of the sampled probabilities

    I is the name of the selected arm. The notation “arg max” asks for the name associated with the largest value, rather than that value itself. The sign ∈ means “among”. In an exact tie, our convention is to select A; with these ideal independent continuous distributions, a tie has probability 0.

    I=arg maxi∈{A,B}θ~i

    The difference θ̃_B − θ̃_A is 4/5 − 3/5 = 1/5 = 0.20, which is strictly positive. We therefore select I = B. Its mean remains 1/2: the choice does not replace either the belief mean or the unknown true probability with the draw 0.80.

  6. Leave the outcome open

    At this stage, B has only been selected. Y remains unknown: neither 0 nor 1 has been observed. α_B and β_B therefore remain equal to 1 and 1. No observation should be fabricated from θ̃_B.

    Trying B, receiving a reliable outcome and then changing its belief will be the subject of C068·M4. The choice alone also tells us nothing about the outcome A would have produced. Keeping this boundary prevents confusion between exploration and learning that has already occurred.

  7. Check the transformation, bounds and decision

    Inverse check: the square of 3/5 is 9/25 = U_A and F_B(4/5) = 4/5 = U_B. The reconstructed values match the inputs, and all the draws belong to the interval from 0 to 1.

    Decision check: 1/5 > 0 confirms B, whereas m_A − m_B = 1/6 > 0 confirms that the rule based on means would have chosen A. Thompson sampling allows this difference. It measures neither a success nor an advantage over a series of trials.

    The densities are also consistent: the density for A forms a triangle with base 1 and height 2, with area 1; the density for B forms a rectangle with base 1 and height 1, with the same area. We have indeed used normalized probability distributions.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A has the higher mean, so Thompson sampling must choose A.”

    The rule compares values drawn from the beliefs. In this run, the means favor A but the draws favor B. Work through the exact differences again before giving the selected name.

    Recalculate the means, then the maximum of the draws

  • “The draw 0.80 means that B has already succeeded.”

    0.80 is a sampled plausible probability, with no units. The device test has not yet returned either 0 or 1. Wait for an observation before changing the belief parameters.

    Review the diagram of the draw and the future observation

  • “Using the same uniform number for the arms is equivalent.”

    The distributions for each arm can remain correct individually while becoming dependent. Yet the choice depends on their comparison. In our scene, the square root of the shared number is always at least as large as that number: B then loses its chance of being selected.

We have changed only the inputs for the run. The beliefs remain as given, and no repair outcome has been observed.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · audit a mechanism

A misleading way to save a draw

Someone keeps the workshop beliefs but draws only a single uniform number U. They use √U for A and U for B, then claim that this is the same Thompson selection because the individual Beta distributions are respected. For U = 9/25, calculate the draws and the choice. Then examine what happens for any value of U between 0 and 1. Where does their reasoning fail?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The number for B becomes the same U as the input to the square root for A. Start with this numerical run.

Hint 2

For a number between 0 and 1, its square is less than or equal to the number itself. What can you infer about the comparison between U and √U?

Compare with the solution

With U = 9/25, the draws are θ̃_A = 3/5 = 0.60 and θ̃_B = 9/25 = 0.36. The difference in favor of A is 6/25 = 0.24. This run chooses A, whereas the initial run, based on distinct inputs, chose B.

More generally, if U is between 0 and 1, U × U ≤ U. Since the numbers are nonnegative, this gives U ≤ √U. A therefore always has at least the value of B; our convention still chooses A in a tie. B is never selected by this mechanism.

Each transformation does preserve the stated individual distribution: Beta(2, 1) for √U and Beta(1, 1) for U. But the draws now depend on the same number. This does not represent our independent beliefs given H; the distribution of their comparison has changed. Preserving the distributions separately is not enough to preserve the selection.

The fix is to supply independent uniform inputs for the arms, then keep the transformations specific to each arm. The initial run already demonstrates that B can then be selected. This fix to the mechanism still proves no gain on real repairs.

Check your reasoning

  • You calculate 3/5 and 9/25, then identify A for the given shared number.
  • You justify U ≤ √U over the interval and explain why B cannot win.
  • You identify dependence as the flaw and restore independent inputs without changing the beliefs.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In this Thompson selection, what is drawn and what is compared?

A plausible probability is drawn from the Beta belief for each arm, independently under the beliefs assumed here. These probabilities are compared, and the name of the arm with the maximum is selected.

Recall 2Why can a procedure with a lower mean be selected?

The choice depends on the sampled values, which may have a different order from the means. In the workshop run, B receives 4/5 against 3/5 for A; this does not demonstrate that B has the higher true probability.

Recall 3How do you reconstruct the draw for A from U_A = 9/25, and what check should you make?

The cumulative distribution function of Beta(2, 1) is x × x on the interval from 0 to 1. We therefore take the nonnegative square root: θ̃_A = 3/5. Its square is 9/25, which recovers U_A.

Recall 4Why should you avoid reusing the same uniform number for all the independent arms?

The individual distributions may remain correct, but the draws become dependent. The comparison no longer has the intended distribution: with √U for A and U for B, A always wins under our convention.

Recall 5After B is selected, what is missing before we can learn from a trial?

A reliable observed outcome from trying B is missing, coded as 0 or 1 in this scene. Its draw 0.80 does not provide this observation. Neither selection nor reading the page establishes real success or an attained level.
