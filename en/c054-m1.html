Level 1 · C054·M1

Extrema

Your goalDistinguish the position and value of an extremum, then prove whether it is local or global on a stated domain.

Before you start
c033-m5 : affine approximation · c014-m5 : invariants

✿01 See the idea

You lay a rope 12 m long on the ground to mark out a rectangle. If you lengthen one side, the other must shorten. The amount of rope stays the same, but the enclosed area changes. The data are fictional; we assume a rope with no thickness, flat ground and right angles held in place.

One side is x metres long. The adjacent side is ℓ metres long, using a cursive letter l. In this scene, you allow x to range from 1 m to 5 m, including both endpoints. With x = 2 m, the rectangle encloses 8 m²; with x = 3 m, it encloses 9 m². Before reading on, consider whether this is enough to prove that no one can do better.

A maximum is a greatest value attained; a minimum is a smallest value attained. They are called extrema. You must always state which settings are being compared. Global means “among all allowed settings”; local means “among allowed settings sufficiently close by”. Doing best in a few trials is not enough for either conclusion.

The drawing suggests an answer. The proof must also cover lengths between the tick marks. We will distinguish the setting x, in metres, from the resulting area value, in square metres.

The rule to remember

State the domain, distinguish the setting from the value, then replace “this is the best trial” with a comparison covering all the stated settings.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

All the rope, all allowed lengths

With P = 12 m of rope, x varies over D = [1, 5] m. We seek the greatest and smallest areas, and all positions where they are attained. The side lengths can vary continuously and are not restricted to whole metres.

  1. Reduce the scene to one variable

    P is the perimeter, and x and ℓ are the lengths of adjacent sides, all in metres. Since opposite sides are equal, P = 2(x + ℓ). Call the half-perimeter s: s = 12 ÷ 2 = 6 m. We obtain ℓ = s − x. Setting x therefore determines the whole rectangle.

    A(x) denotes the area of this rectangle, in square metres. D is the domain: the set of allowed x values. Here a = 1 m and b = 5 m are its endpoints, both included. Both lengths remain strictly positive on D. All the rope is used, with no overlap or allowance for fastenings.

    A(x)=x(s−x)

    In the numerical equalities that follow, lengths are expressed in metres and areas in square metres. For example, x = 2 m gives ℓ = 6 − 2 = 4 m, then A(x) = 2 × 4 = 8 m².

  2. State exactly what must be proved

    The notation x⋆ denotes a candidate position, in metres; the star does not yet mean that this position is optimal. A(x⋆), in square metres, is the corresponding value. For a global maximum, x⋆ must belong to D and satisfy the following comparison:

    A(x)≤A(x⋆) pour tout x dans D

    For a global minimum, reverse this inequality. Locally, we require only a comparison with allowed x values at a distance strictly less than δ from x⋆, for some positive length δ. The symbol δ, read as delta, denotes the size of this neighbourhood; |x − x⋆| is the distance between the positions, in metres.

    We use “local” relative to the domain D: near an endpoint, we compare only on the allowed side. Under this convention, every global extremum is therefore also local. A local extremum alone does not compare all distant positions; it does not guarantee a global extremum.

  3. Use the derivative to propose a position

    A′(x), read as the derivative of A at x, measures the local change in area per metre added to x. Its unit is m²/m, which is the metre. Since A(x) = sx − x squared and s is fixed, the differentiation rules give A′(x) = s − 2x.

    At an interior extremum where the derivative exists, this derivative is 0: for a maximum, the difference quotients are nonpositive on the right and nonnegative on the left, and their common limit must be 0. For a minimum, the signs are reversed. This condition proposes candidates; it does not yet prove their type or whether they are local or global.

    A′(x)=0⇔s−2x=0⇔x=s2

    The double arrow means that the equalities are equivalent. The candidate is x⋆ = 6 ÷ 2 = 3 m, well inside D. Slope check: 6 − 2 × 3 = 6 − 6 = 0 m. The endpoints are also part of the problem; this search for 0 slope does not eliminate them.

  4. Calculate the value and check the rope

    At the candidate, ℓ = 6 − 3 = 3 m. The area is 3 × 3 = 9 m². The candidate position is 3 m; the candidate value is 9 m². Writing only “the maximum is 3” would confuse these two objects.

    Let us check the constraint independently of the area: x + ℓ = 3 + 3 = 6 m, then 2 × 6 = 12 m. The square does use all the rope. We still need to compare its area with that of every other allowed rectangle.

  5. Prove that the maximum is global

    Write the area as a fixed value minus a square. Expanding the square on the right gives sx − x squared again: the constant term s squared divided by 4 cancels out. The identity holds for every x, not just at the candidate.

    A(x)=(s2)2−(x−s2)2

    Numerically, this gives A(x) = 9 minus the square of the difference between x and 3, in the stated units. A square is greater than or equal to 0; A(x) therefore never exceeds 9 m². Equality requires the difference to be 0, so x = 3 m.

    This position belongs to D: the global maximum is indeed 9 m², attained only at x = 3 m. It is also local. We used an identity and the sign of a square, without inferring a global proof solely from the derivative being 0.

  6. Find and justify the minima

    At the endpoints, ℓ = 6 − 1 = 5 m gives A(a) = 1 × 5 = 5 m². At the other endpoint, ℓ = 6 − 5 = 1 m gives A(b) = 5 × 1 = 5 m². These equal results suggest a shared minimum; let us prove that no area falls below it.

    On D, the distance from x to the centre 3 m does not exceed 2 m. Its square therefore does not exceed 4 m². The previous identity requires A(x) ≥ 9 − 4 = 5 m². This minimum is attained at the endpoints and only there.

    Another check, by factorisation:

    A(x)−A(a)=(x−a)(b−x)≥0

    This factorisation uses a + b = s. Each factor is positive or 0 on D; the product is 0 only at a or b. The global minimum is 5 m² and has positions x = 1 m and x = 5 m. Under our convention relative to D, these are also local minima.

  7. Separate checks from proof

    The numerical check x = 2 m gives 8 m², between 5 and 9 m². Swapping the sides, x = 4 m and ℓ = 2 m, preserves this area. This symmetry is consistent with the centre 3 m and with the formula.

    These checks detect calculation errors. They do not replace inequalities valid for every x in D. The result concerns the stated rectangular model and domain. If an optimal setting becomes forbidden, its value may no longer be attained; the final exercise asks you to examine precisely this case.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I found a 0 derivative, so I have proved the global maximum.”

    A 0 slope in the interior provides a candidate. To reach a global conclusion, compare its value with all allowed values. Here, the square subtracted from 9 provides that comparison. Extrema at endpoints are not subject to the interior 0-slope condition.

    Revisit the proof using a square and the minima

  • “The maximum is 3 m²: it is the position of the highest point.”

    Read the axes separately: x = 3 m is the position; A(x) = 9 m² is the maximum value. A complete answer gives both and the domain on which it holds.

    Reread the axes and their units

  • “Even if I forbid the square, 9 remains the maximum because we can approach it.”

    A maximum must be attained at an allowed setting. A bound that can be approached without being attained is not enough. On the modified domain in the final exercise, construct a setting better than any proposed candidate.

Exercise 1 · Calculation and critical reading

The best of a few trials

Still using the 12 m rope and the original domain, a notebook compares only x = 2 m and x = 2.50 m. Find the adjacent side and the area of the second rectangle. By how much does its area exceed that of the first? Does this notebook alone prove a global maximum?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The half-perimeter is still 6 m: subtract x to obtain ℓ.

Hint 2

Compare the areas, then distinguish “best among these trials” from “best among all x in D”.

Compare with the solution

The length 2.50 m is exactly 5/2 m. The adjacent side is 6 − 2.50 = 3.50 m. The area is 35/4 m², or 8.75 m²: multiply the exact side lengths before writing the decimal for display.

The difference in area is 35/4 − 8 = 3/4 m², or 0.75 m². The second rectangle is better among the trials in the notebook. None of these trials covers the intermediate settings or the other allowed lengths.

The notebook alone therefore does not prove a global maximum. The proof in the lesson adds different information: it covers all of D and shows that the square attains 9 m². The second trial in the notebook remains below this.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain ℓ = 3.50 m, then A = 8.75 m².
  • Give the difference 0.75 m² without changing the perimeter.
  • Limit the conclusion from the notebook to the settings actually compared.

Exercise 2 · Proof on paper

An imposed maximum length

The rope and the area rule remain the same, but x is now restricted to [1, 2] m, including both endpoints. Without using a theorem about the sign of the derivative, find the global extrema and their positions. Then calculate the derivative at the maximum you found: is it 0?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The centre 3 m lies outside the new domain. The distance to the centre remains between 1 m and 2 m.

Hint 2

Bound the square of this distance, then subtract it from 9 m². Look for where equality is attained.

Compare with the solution

On this domain, the distance to 3 m ranges from 1 m to 2 m. Its square is therefore between 1 m² and 4 m². The area identity gives 9 − 4 ≤ A(x) ≤ 9 − 1, or 5 ≤ A(x) ≤ 8, in square metres.

The global minimum is 5 m² at x = 1 m. The global maximum is 8 m² at x = 2 m; it corresponds to the rectangle with adjacent side 4 m. Each equality is attained at an allowed position, so these are indeed extrema.

The derivative at the maximum is 6 − 2 × 2 = 6 − 4 = 2 m. It is nonzero. The maximum is at an endpoint of the domain: the stated necessary condition for an interior extremum does not apply. Larger positions that would improve the area are forbidden here.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the new domain and exclude x = 3 m from the allowed candidates.
  • Justify the bounds on every area, then give the positions where equality holds.
  • Explain why the derivative 2 m does not contradict the maximum at the endpoint.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

The square is excluded: can you still do better?

The same rope must now form a rectangle with x in [1, 3) m: the left endpoint is included and the right endpoint excluded. What is the global minimum? Is there a global maximum? To decide, start from any allowed x and propose another allowed setting with a greater area. Distinguish the supremum from the maximum.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Set h = 3 − x, a strictly positive length. The area is 9 minus h squared.

Hint 2

Choose y halfway between x and 3 m. Its squared distance to the square’s position is smaller, and y stays strictly below the excluded endpoint.

Compare with the solution

The minimum remains 5 m² at x = 1 m. This position is included, and the bounds from the original domain remain valid. The other minimum position from the old domain is now forbidden.

Let x be an allowed setting and h = 3 − x > 0, in metres. Set y = (x + 3)/2, also in metres. Then x < y < 3 and y belongs to the new domain. The distance from y to the centre is h/2, strictly less than h.

A(y)=9−h24>9−h2=A(x)

The numerical equality uses metres for h and square metres for A. Every candidate x therefore has an allowed competitor y with a greater area: no global maximum exists. This reasoning holds for any x, not just for the trials in the notebook.

The areas remain strictly below 9 m², but we can reduce h as much as we want and make h squared as small as desired. Thus 9 m² is the supremum: the smallest value that bounds all these areas from above. This bound is not a maximum, because no allowed position attains it.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the minimum attained at x = 1 m.
  • Construct y for an arbitrary x and check that it belongs to the domain.
  • Prove A(y) > A(x), then distinguish a value that can be approached from a value that is attained.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What information must you give when stating the maximum for the rectangle?

The domain D = [1, 5] m, the position x = 3 m, the value A(x) = 9 m², and the comparison with every x in D. The position and the value have different units.

Recall 2What do a local extremum and a global extremum compare?

A local extremum compares allowed positions sufficiently close by, within a neighbourhood of positive size. A global extremum compares all positions in the domain. Here the endpoints are also compared relative to the domain. Global implies local; the reverse implication is not guaranteed.

Recall 3Why does A′(x) = 0 not finish our search?

It is a necessary condition in the interior if the derivative exists. It certifies neither a maximum nor a minimum nor a global extremum, and it does not address endpoints. Here the identity “9 minus a square” proves the global maximum and its unique position.

Recall 4On [1, 3) m, why is 9 m² not a maximum?

This area requires x = 3 m, which is forbidden. Every allowed x can be replaced by the midpoint between x and this endpoint, giving a greater area. 9 m² is a supremum that can be approached but is not attained.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without rereading: state the allowed settings and the quantity being compared; distinguish the position from the value; explain the difference between local and global; propose candidates; prove the comparison and check attainment. Then revisit the case where the square is forbidden, without inventing a maximum.

Reading guide · best encountered and proven optimum

What you understand
An optimal value and the parameters that attain it are distinct objects. Comparing trials retains the best encountered; reaching a global conclusion requires a proof over the entire domain. The rectangle is a teaching model and does not claim to represent the program’s objective.
