Level 2 · C068·M2

Bernoulli, Beta and pseudo-counts

Your goalDistinguish the binary outcome of a trial from uncertainty about its probability, then build and check a Beta prior.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional workshop, method A consists of properly reconnecting a lamp’s cable. After the same check, the lamp either stays on or does not stay on. You can record this outcome. However, you cannot directly see the probability that the method will succeed on another comparable lamp.

We therefore need to keep distinct objects separate: the trial outcome, which is binary, and the still plausible values of its probability of success. The Bernoulli distribution describes the former; a Beta distribution can represent our uncertainty about the latter. Here, an arm means a method we could choose, as in the previous unit; we study only the profile for method A.

A prior is a distribution chosen before the observations under consideration. To build it, we will state a mean and a concentration. The mean locates the probability around which the belief is distributed; the concentration controls its spread with the mean held fixed. By itself, it does not count lamps that have actually been tested.

The scenario, the choice of prior and all its numerical parameters are fictional. No workshop outcomes have been collected. The calculations describe a working belief without claiming any real performance for this method.

The rule to remember

Bernoulli concerns the trial outcome; Beta concerns the unknown probability. State the prior mean, its concentration and the observations actually available separately.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Build the profile for method A before the trials

For this fictional example, we choose a mean m = 2/5, or 0.40 with no units, and a concentration k = 5. Let us build the corresponding Beta distribution, check that it is a density, then examine the effect of another choice, k = 50. The number of actual trials available remains 0.

  1. Separate the outcome from its parameter

    Y denotes the outcome of the next trial: 1 if the criterion “stays on at the end of the check” is met, 0 otherwise. θ, the letter theta, denotes its probability of success, between 0 and 1, with no units. P means probability and the vertical bar means “assuming θ is fixed”.

    P(Y=1∣θ)=θ;P(Y=0∣θ)=1−θ

    This is the Bernoulli distribution with parameter θ. The outcome and its code have no physical units. We assume an unchanged criterion and the same probability θ for comparable lamps; the trials are independent conditional on this value. A distribution over θ describes our lack of knowledge of this fixed value, without assuming that the method changes in quality from one lamp to the next.

  2. Define the Beta distribution and its density

    Writing that θ follows Beta(α, β) means that the possible values of θ are assigned a density f. α, alpha, and β, beta, are strictly positive parameters with no units; they can be real numbers and need not be integers. The variable t denotes a possible value of θ inside the interval from 0 to 1.

    f(t)=tα−1(1−t)β−1B(α,β)
    B(α,β)=∫01tα−1(1−t)β−1 dt

    The symbol ∫ adds up areas under a curve; dt indicates the variable of this continuous addition. B is the area under the expression in the numerator: dividing by B makes the total area under f equal to 1. The probability of an interval is its area under f, never just the height of the curve. The density is expressed per unit of t; since t has no physical units, neither does f.

  3. Go from the mean and concentration to the parameters

    m denotes the prior mean of θ; k = α + β is the concentration. To set m strictly between 0 and 1 and k strictly positive, we divide this mass k into a proportion m and its complement. Under the convention used on this page, α and β are the masses of pseudo-successes and pseudo-failures.

    α=mk;β=(1−m)k

    The complement is 1 − 2/5 = 3/5. Thus α = 2/5 × 5 = 2 and β = 3/5 × 5 = 3. The profile is Beta(2, 3). Check: 2 + 3 = 5, the requested concentration. These fictional masses are not a trial log.

  4. Check the area rather than guess a coefficient

    The exponents are α − 1 = 1 and β − 1 = 2. The numerator is therefore t times (1 − t) squared. Expanding it gives t − 2t squared + t cubed. Its integral from 0 to 1 is calculated term by term:

    B(2,3)=12−23+14=1/12

    Each power of t contributes its area: t gives 1/2, the subtracted term gives 2/3 and the last term gives 1/4. The intermediate calculation is -1/6 + 1/4 = 1/12. Dividing by this area is equivalent to multiplying by 12.

    f(t)=12t(1−t)2

    Check: f is nonnegative on the interval and its area is 12 × 1/12 = 1. The coefficient is therefore neither a probability nor a repair outcome: it normalizes the density.

  5. Calculate what the profile tells us and what it does not

    E denotes expectation, a weighted average under the distribution; Var denotes variance, the average squared deviation from that expectation. For a Beta distribution, these quantities are expressed as follows:

    E[θ]=αα+β=m
    Var(θ)=αβ(α+β)2(α+β+1)=m(1−m)k+1

    Mean: 2/(2 + 3) = 2/5, or 0.40 with no units. This is also the predictive probability of success for the next trial before observation, because we average θ. This never requires the binary outcome Y to take the value 0.40.

    Variance: the numerator is 2 × 3 = 6. In the denominator, the square of the sum is 25 and the sum increased by 1 is 6; their product is 150. We obtain 6/150 = 1/25, or 0.04 with no physical units.

    Independent check using the density: integrating t f(t) gives 2/5 and integrating t squared times f(t) gives 1/5. The variance is therefore also 1/5 − 4/25 = 1/25. This is the variance of θ in our belief, not the variance of the outcome Y.

  6. Change the strength of the belief without shifting its mean

    Keep m = 2/5, but now choose k = 50. The same division gives α = 2/5 × 50 = 20 and β = 3/5 × 50 = 30. The sum is 50 and the mean is 20/50 = 2/5: it has not changed.

    The variance becomes (20 × 30)/(50 squared × 51) = 600/(2500 × 51) = 600/127500 = 2/425, or approximately 0.0047 with no units. This is less than 1/25. With m fixed, the numerator m(1 − m) is constant and the denominator k + 1 increases: the belief narrows.

    Yet we have not tested any additional lamps. This narrowing expresses the choice of a stronger prior; its relevance would need justification beyond the calculation. The following units will cover Thompson sampling and then the arrival of observations.

  7. Interpret pseudo-counts within their proper limits

    Under our convention, the sum α + β is an equivalent mass of information for the Bernoulli–Beta model. Saying “2 pseudo-successes and 3 pseudo-failures” describes the setting without claiming that these events occurred. Here, the number of actual trials remains 0 for each profile.

    The exponents α − 1 and β − 1 belong to the expression for the density. They must be distinguished from the masses α and β used to calculate the mean. For example, Beta(1, 1) has a uniform density with 0 exponents; its mean is 1/2 and its concentration is 2 under our convention. This does not prove that any actual trial took place.

    Final check: the mean is between 0 and 1, the parameters are positive and the variance is positive. These checks validate our operations; they do not prove that θ stays constant for new types of lamps or that future trials are independent.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading the formulas, reconstruct α and β from m and k. Why is the mean not enough to describe the profile? Then explain how the variance can decrease while the trial log remains empty.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The mean is 0.40, so the next outcome will be 0.40.”

    Y only takes the value 0 or 1. Here, the mean of θ gives a predictive probability of success without producing a fraction of a successfully repaired lamp. Return to the branching diagram and separate outcome, parameter and belief.

    Review what a trial actually produces

  • “Beta(20, 30) proves that 50 lamps have been tested.”

    These parameters were chosen before the trials. Their sum measures a concentration under the stated convention. Only a record of observations could establish a number of lamps tested; no such record is provided here.

    Reread the limits of pseudo-counts

  • “The exponents are α − 1 and β − 1, so I must also subtract 1 in the mean.”

    The exponents define the shape of the density; the Beta mean remains α/(α + β). The uniform case Beta(1, 1) gives 1/2, whereas subtracting 1 from both parameters would lead to division by 0. Keep the same convention throughout the calculation.

Compare with the solution

Y is 1 if the lamp stays on, 0 otherwise. Before the trial, the predictive probability of success is 2/(2 + 3) = 2/5. The probability of failure is 1 − 2/5 = 3/5, or 0.60 with no units.

α/β compares the masses of pseudo-successes and pseudo-failures with each other. A success proportion is calculated relative to their sum. Neither the mean nor the ratio of the masses is an observed outcome.

Exercise 2 · Application · check a density

A height greater than 1

For the same Beta(2, 3) profile, calculate f(1/2). Someone rejects the profile because this height exceeds 1. Respond by distinguishing height, area over an interval and the probability of the exact value θ = 1/2.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Use f(t) = 12 × t × (1 − t) squared.

Hint 2

The probability of an interval corresponds to an area; a single value has no width.

Compare with the solution

At t = 1/2, the complement is also 1/2. Its square is 1/4; the product with t is 1/8. Thus f(1/2) = 12 × 1/8 = 3/2, or 1.50 per unit of t, with no physical units.

This height can exceed 1: the total area must equal 1, as the normalization calculation has verified. To obtain the probability of an interval, we would need to integrate f over that interval. Since Beta is a continuous distribution with a density, the probability of an exact value, here θ = 1/2, is 0; this does not make its neighborhood impossible.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 3/2 while showing the square and the product.
  • Do not equate a density with the probability at a single point.
  • Explain why normalization concerns an area.

Does a stronger setting provide more evidence?

The trial log for method A is empty. A colleague replaces k = 5 with k = 50, keeping m = 2/5, then announces: “The profile is more precise, so the method has proved itself.” Reconstruct the parameters, compare the means and variances, then correct this conclusion. Also specify what would need to be reconsidered if the next batch contained lamps of another type.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Divide each concentration into proportions m and 1 − m. Calculate the mean using the sum of the parameters.

Hint 2

With the mean fixed, Var(θ) = m(1 − m)/(k + 1). Separate the profile setting from the trial log.

Compare with the solution

With k = 5, the parameters are 2 and 3. With k = 50, they become 20 and 30. The sums are 5 and 50 respectively; the means remain 2/5 and 2/5.

The variances are 1/25 and 2/425, or 0.04 and approximately 0.0047 with no physical units. The second belief is less spread out under the model. But the 50 units of mass come from the fictional setting, not additional trials: the number of observations remains 0.

A justified conclusion would be: “I have chosen a more concentrated prior; I still need to justify this choice and collect authorized observations.” For other types of lamps, the assumption of a common probability θ would need to be reconsidered. The validity of the calculation does not establish the validity of this assumption.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do Bernoulli and Beta concern in the workshop?

Bernoulli describes Y, the binary outcome of a trial conditional on θ. Beta describes the belief about the unknown probability θ. An outcome, a probability and a distribution over that probability are distinct objects.

Recall 2How do you recover α and β from a mean m and a concentration k?

α = mk and β = (1 − m)k, with 0 < m < 1 and k > 0. Check that α + β = k and α/(α + β) = m. The parameters are strictly positive and have no physical units.

Recall 3What do pseudo-counts prove? Why must they not be confused with the exponents?

Under our convention, α and β are masses of pseudo-successes and pseudo-failures. They do not prove that any actual event occurred. The exponents α − 1 and β − 1 define the density; they do not replace the parameters in the mean formula.

Recall 4With the mean fixed, what effect does a larger concentration k have?

The variance m(1 − m)/(k + 1) decreases. The belief is more concentrated without its mean changing. If k is only an initial choice, this decrease proves neither a new observation nor any real improvement in the method.

Recall 5Why does a density height greater than 1 not invalidate the Beta distribution?

The probability of an interval is an area under the density. The total area equals 1; a height is not a probability. The probability of an exact value is 0 for this continuous distribution.
