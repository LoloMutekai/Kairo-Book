Expertise and pressure

Your goalDescribe a drop in performance on a well-practised movement, compare explanations based on attention and specify what the observations allow us to conclude.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A pianist knows a passage well enough to follow the musical phrase without naming each finger movement. She plays the passage easily in rehearsal. Before an audition panel, she suddenly wonders what people think of her and monitors every key. She makes a mistake. Has she lost her skill, or have the conditions in which she performs changed?

Here, expertise means a skill built through practice, precise and suited to a familiar task. Performance is what is produced in a particular take. A take provides only an indication of that skill; knowing this passage does not guarantee mastery of the whole repertoire or success under all stage conditions.

Automaticity means that a well-practised sequence can be performed without consciously deciding on each of its elements. Attention remains useful for following the music, listening and adapting. Making a sequence automatic therefore does not mean playing without any control. Evaluation pressure refers to the stakes attached to being judged and to the consequences; it cannot be read directly from the number of mistakes.

We examine 2 possible explanations. Distraction would divert attention towards being judged or the consequences. Overcontrol would involve consciously breaking down an already well-coordinated movement, risking disruption of its sequence. These hypotheses can coexist; the score alone cannot distinguish them. Breaking down a new movement can, by contrast, help someone learn it: the amount of practice matters.

The entire scene and all numerical observations are fictional. We assume neither an inevitable decline under pressure nor a universal stress threshold. The lesson concerns the distinction between skill, context and performance, then the scope of a small comparison. It provides neither a therapeutic method nor a guaranteed recipe for an audition.

The rule to remember

First describe what the pianist did and under what conditions. An observed decline is not enough to establish either a loss of skill or the mechanism that might explain it.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Compare takes without turning the score into a diagnosis

The pianist plays the same passage of 40 expected note positions at the prescribed tempo of 80 beats per minute. In each session, a take is recorded in rehearsal and another before the panel. The order is rehearsal then panel in A, panel then rehearsal in B. The error counts are respectively 1 and 4 in A, 2 and 2 in B. Let us calculate the mean difference: panel minus rehearsal.

  1. Define exactly what we are counting

    An error is a wrong pitch or a missing note at a position in the score. Each position counts as at most 1 error. Extra notes are recorded separately and none occur in this scene. Rhythm and expression are not assessed by this count: fewer pitch errors do not capture overall musical quality.

    E denotes the error count for a take; its unit is the error. The subscripts r and j identify rehearsal and panel. The subscript s denotes session A or B. N = 40 is the number of expected note positions per take; n = 2 is the number of sessions. The tempo is the same instruction in both conditions, not a measurement of the speed actually played.

    Comparing these counts assumes the same score, the same number of note positions, the same definition of an error and consistent scoring of the recordings. A difference in difficulty or stricter scoring of panel takes would make their interpretation ambiguous. These conditions are fixed in the exercise; checking them would be necessary in practice.

  2. Choose the direction of the difference

    d denotes the paired difference: we subtract counts from the same session to preserve their link. A positive d indicates more errors before the panel; a 0 d indicates equal counts. A negative d would indicate fewer errors before the panel. The sign describes the result, not the emotional state.

    ds=Ej,s−Er,s

  3. Calculate within each session

    In A: dA = 4 − 1 = 3 errors. In B: dB = 2 − 2 = 0 errors. The difference is therefore not identical in each session. Keeping the rows separate avoids describing a systematic decline that the scene does not show.

    The bar above d in the next step will mean the arithmetic mean: add the differences and divide by the number of sessions. This is neither a percentage nor a direct measure of stress.

  4. Summarise without rounding during the calculation

    The sum is 3 + 0 = 3 errors. Dividing by n = 2 gives a mean difference of 3/2 errors per take, or 1.5 errors per take. This fraction is a summary: no take produced a fraction of a wrong note.

    d¯=dA+dBn=3+02=32 erreur par prise

  5. Check by grouping the counts another way

    In rehearsal, the total is 1 + 2 = 3 errors, so the mean is 3/2 = 1.5 errors per take. Before the panel, 4 + 2 = 6 errors, so 6/2 = 3.0 errors per take. Subtracting these means again gives 3.0 − 1.5 = 1.5 errors per take.

    This check works because each context has the same number of takes and the pairs are complete. The mean difference also lies between 0 and 3, the extreme differences. Counts remain between 0 and 40 errors per take. These checks verify the arithmetic, not the psychological explanation.

  6. State what this difference establishes

    In these fictional observations, the panel context accompanies more errors on average, with a 0 difference in B. This demonstrates neither a lasting loss of the learned passage, nor a causal effect of the panel, nor distraction rather than overcontrol. We collected neither thoughts during the takes nor a direct measure of attention.

    Reversing the order between A and B prevents the panel take from always coming after rehearsal. This does not eliminate fatigue, improvement, expectations or differences between sessions. The 40 notes in a take are not that many independent pianists. We describe a single pianist, without testing generalisation to a population.

    To examine the context, we would need to plan further comparable sessions, allocate orders in advance without always favouring the same context and score the recordings without knowing their condition. Checking how the pianist experiences the stakes would also help distinguish the presence of the panel from felt pressure. Distinguishing the attentional mechanisms would require additional targeted observations or manipulations. No benefit from an intervention has been calculated here.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the counts. Reconstruct the meaning of d, the difference for each session, their mean and the check using the context means. Then explain how a skill can remain available while a take deteriorates, and why mistakes alone do not reveal the pianist’s thoughts.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If she were truly an expert, the panel would make no difference.”

    Expertise concerns a skill and the conditions in which it is used. A take also depends on context and attention. Look for stable skill across comparable repeat takes before concluding that it has disappeared or been maintained.

    Return to the distinction between expertise and performance

  • “More mistakes prove that she is thinking too much about her fingers.”

    Overcontrol is a hypothesis; concern about being judged could also divert attention. Other changes could play a role. The counts describe what was produced; an observation that distinguishes the explanations is missing.

    Reread what the comparison allows us to conclude

  • “The positive mean means that her performance declines in every take.”

    Return to the pairs: 3 additional errors in A and 0 in B together give 1.5 additional errors per take on average. The summary does not erase the 0 result in B and does not predict the next audition.

    Recalculate the differences before their mean

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What distinction prevents us from concluding that a failed audition erases expertise?

Expertise is a developed, specific skill; performance is what is produced in a take under particular conditions. Comparable observations are needed to assess whether the skill is available beyond this take.

Recall 2How can we distinguish distraction from overcontrol in the piano scene?

Distraction would divert attention towards being judged; overcontrol would consciously break down an already coordinated movement. These explanations can coexist. The number of mistakes alone does not reveal which path was taken.

Recall 3Why calculate panel minus rehearsal within each session, then keep the differences visible?

Each pair preserves the link between takes from the same session. Here, 3 errors in A and 0 in B give 1.5 errors per take on average: this positive mean does not mean a decline in every session.

Recall 4What does a positive difference add, and what is missing before we can discuss a mechanism?

It describes more errors before the panel according to our indicator and in the takes compared. Observations or manipulations distinguishing the mechanisms are missing, as are controls for other possible changes. A score does not directly measure felt pressure.
