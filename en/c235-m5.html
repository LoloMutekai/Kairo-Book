Level 4 · C235·M5

Divergence

Your goalDefine divergence as the adjoint of the Malliavin derivative, justify its domain, and calculate its correction in a single Gaussian mode.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

On a stationary measurement bench, a sensor displays a deviation X from a known reference position. We assume that this deviation comes entirely from noise. Its theoretical standard deviation is σ = 2 mm. In our fictional reading, X = 4 mm: the normalized deviation Z is therefore 2, with no unit.

To examine a large noise excursion, we look at its square. But even a perfectly centered sensor produces a positive square on average. The standard normal distribution assigns this square a mean of 1. Subtracting this contribution gives a score whose theoretical mean is 0; it does not remove any noise from the sensor.

Why does this subtraction appear in Malliavin calculus? The derivative measures how sensitive a quantity is to a perturbation of the noise. Divergence transfers this derivative to the other side of an average through a duality relation. When the coefficient being transferred depends on the same noise, its own sensitivity produces a correction.

We stay within the space generated by this single Gaussian noise variable. The bench, reading, and scale are fictional; the normal distribution is an assumption of the exercise. Neither an actual calibration nor a sensor improvement has been demonstrated.

The rule to remember

For a polynomial coefficient f of the normalized noise, divergence in the unit direction is Z × f(Z) − f′(Z). Justify this formula through duality and check that the result is square integrable.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The square of the noise from the same sensor

With X = σZ, σ = 2 mm, and the reading X = 4 mm, let us calculate the divergence of the field u = Zh. The letter h will denote a noise direction with norm 1. We will then check the result using its mean and a duality test.

  1. Specify the space and units

    Z is a standard normal random variable: E[Z] = 0 and E[Z squared] = 1. E denotes expectation under this distribution, a theoretical average over all possible realizations. Lowercase z denotes a particular realization of Z. Here, z = X/σ = 4/2 = 2.

    H is the real line spanned by h, equipped with the dot product ⟨ah, bh⟩H = ab for real numbers a and b; thus ⟨h, h⟩H = 1. A random field u here is a coefficient depending on Z multiplied by h. We work on the Gaussian space generated by Z: no other source of randomness is involved.

    Z, z, h, and the field coefficients have no units. Only X and σ are in millimeters; their squares are in square millimeters. The letters f and g will denote smooth real functions whose derivatives grow at most polynomially. All polynomials used below satisfy this assumption.

  2. Define the adjoint and its domain

    For a test random variable F = g(Z), the Malliavin derivative is DF = g′(Z)h; the prime denotes the ordinary derivative of g. We take the closure of this derivative in the norm given by the square root of the sum E[F squared] + E[‖DF‖H squared]. This specifies the domain of D beyond the test functions alone.

    A square integrable field u belongs to the domain of divergence if a finite constant Cᵤ, independent of F, satisfies the following inequality for all smooth tests. The notation ‖F‖ denotes the square root of E[F squared].

    |E[⟨DF,u⟩H]|≤Cu‖F‖

    In this case, δ(u) is the unique square integrable random variable that satisfies the duality relation below. Density of the tests gives uniqueness. δ is the adjoint of D for the dot products defined by expectation; this convention also fixes its sign.

    E[Fδ(u)]=E[⟨DF,u⟩H]

    The essential point about the domain is that the bound uses the norm of F alone. A bound in terms of the norm of DF is insufficient. Thus, finiteness of E[‖u‖H squared] does not automatically allow us to write δ(u).

  3. Recover the correction using the Gaussian density

    Let φ denote the density of the standard normal distribution and t its real integration variable. It satisfies φ′(t) = −tφ(t). For u = f(Z)h, the side containing the derivative is E[g′(Z)f(Z)]. Ordinary integration by parts gives:

    ∫ℝg′(t)f(t)φ(t)dt=∫ℝg(t)[tf(t)−f′(t)]φ(t)dt

    The boundary term vanishes: the product g(t)f(t)φ(t) tends to 0 at both infinities because the Gaussian density decays faster than any power. Differentiating the product fφ gives f′φ − tfφ; the sign from integration by parts then gives tf − f′.

    δ(f(Z)h)=Zf(Z)−f′(Z)

    For polynomial f, the candidate result is a polynomial in Z and is therefore square integrable. Applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to E[F × this candidate] gives exactly the required constant Cᵤ: we can choose the square root of the expected square of the candidate. The formula therefore also establishes membership in the domain in our examples. It does not justify blind substitution of a singular function.

  4. Apply the formula to the coefficient Z

    Here f(t) = t and f′(t) = 1. We have DZ = h, then ⟨DZ, h⟩H = 1. This contraction, namely the dot product of the two directions, must be subtracted from the raw product Z × Z.

    δ(Zh)=Z2−1

    For the constant coefficient f(t) = 1, the same formula gives δ(h) = Z because f′ = 0. We cannot deduce δ(Zh) = Zδ(h): the coefficient Z varies with the noise, and its derivative contributes precisely the term subtracted.

  5. Evaluate the fictional reading

    For z = 2, the square is 2 × 2 = 4. The contraction is 1, giving the score 4 − 1 = 3. These numbers have no units.

    The score 3 is the value of the random variable δ(u) at this realization. It is neither its expectation, nor a probability, nor a new sensor reading.

  6. Return to millimeters while keeping the calculation the same

    The square of the reading is X squared = 16 square millimeters. The mean noise contribution is σ squared = 4 square millimeters. Their difference is 12 square millimeters.

    X2−σ2=σ2δ(Zh)

    Check by another route: σ squared = 2 × 2 = 4; multiplying this square by the score gives 4 × 3 = 12 square millimeters. We recover 16 − 4 = 12. This centered difference can be negative for another reading; it is not a variance.

  7. Check the theoretical mean

    The constant test F = 1 has DF equal to 0. Duality therefore requires E[δ(u)] = 0. Our result satisfies this constraint: E[Z squared] − 1 = 1 − 1 = 0.

    The raw product Z squared would have mean 1 and would already fail this test. A 0 mean is, however, only a necessary check: it does not characterize a divergence on its own.

  8. Check duality with a nonconstant test

    Take F = Z squared. Its derivative is DF = 2Zh. The side E[⟨DF, u⟩H] is therefore 2 E[Z squared] = 2.

    On the Fδ(u) side, we need the moment of order 4. Gaussian integration by parts E[Za(Z)] = E[a′(Z)], applied to a(t) = t to the power 3, gives E[Z to the power 4] = 3 E[Z squared] = 3. The function a is used only to calculate this mean.

    E[Z2(Z2−1)]=3−1=2

    We therefore obtain E[Fδ(u)] = 3 − 1 = 2, matching the other side. The general equality was established by integration by parts; this additional numerical test helps detect an error in a sign or coefficient.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, explain where the subtracted term comes from and why the constant test is enough to reject the raw product. Then distinguish the score at the reading, its theoretical mean, and the difference in square millimeters.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Since δ(h) = Z, I can take Z outside and write δ(Zh) = Z squared.”

    Taking a random coefficient outside ignores its sensitivity. The formula gives Z × f(Z) − f′(Z). For f(t) = t, we must subtract 1; the raw product has mean 1, whereas the constant test requires 0.

    Review the formula and the mean check

  • “The score is a squared noise value, so it must stay positive.”

    The score is a square minus its theoretical mean. At the normalized reading 0.5, it is -0.75. This negative sign indicates a square below its mean under the model; it does not describe a negative variance.

    Solve the exercise with a reading closer to the reference

  • “If u is square integrable, δ(u) must exist.”

    The domain requires a bound on E[⟨DF, u⟩H] by a constant times the norm of F for all tests. For our polynomial coefficients, integration by parts gives a square integrable candidate, then Cauchy–Schwarz provides this bound. Without a similar justification, existence remains to be established.

    Reread the domain definition and its justification

Compare with the solution

Normalization gives z = 1 ÷ 2 = 1/2, or 0.5. Its square is 1/4. The score is therefore 1/4 − 1 = -3/4, or -0.75, with no unit.

In the physical unit, 4 × -3/4 = -3 square millimeters. The observed square is below the mean square predicted by the noise distribution, so a negative difference is consistent. No negative variance has been calculated.

Exercise 2 · Applying the rule

Multiply the coefficient of the same noise

Return to the reading z = 2. The field becomes v = 2Zh, keeping both the distribution of Z and the norm of h unchanged. Calculate δ(v) as a random variable and then its value at the reading. Justify its existence and check linearity.

Hint 1

In the divergence rule, take f(t) = 2t; calculate its derivative before substituting z for t.

Hint 2

Compare the result with 2 times δ(Zh), multiplying the correction term as well.

Compare with the solution

The derivative f′ is 2. The divergence is 2 times Z squared − 2, or 2 times (Z squared − 1). This polynomial is square integrable under the Gaussian distribution; the duality relation we proved therefore establishes that v belongs to the domain.

At the reading, the result is 2 × 3 = 6, with no unit. The factor is deterministic: the relation δ(2u) = 2δ(u) is indeed satisfied. Multiplying only the raw product without multiplying the subtracted term would violate this relation.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and checking

A coefficient that is itself a square

On the same Gaussian space, define the field w = f(Z)h with f(t) = t squared. Calculate δ(w), evaluate it for z = 2, then check duality with F = Z. Why would checking the mean alone fail to detect omission of f′ here?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The raw product is Z cubed. The derivative of the coefficient is 2Z.

Hint 2

With F = Z, DF = h: the side containing the derivative is E[Z squared]. Use the moment of order 4 calculated in the example.

Compare with the solution

The formula gives δ(w) = Z cubed − 2Z. This polynomial is square integrable, so it also justifies membership in the domain. At the reading: 8 − 4 = 4, with no unit.

On the left, E[⟨DF, w⟩H] = E[Z squared] = 1. On the right, E[Zδ(w)] = E[Z to the power 4] − 2 E[Z squared] = 3 − 2 = 1. Both sides are equal.

The raw product Z cubed and the actual divergence are both odd in Z; their means are therefore 0 by symmetry. The constant test does not distinguish them. With F = Z, the raw product would give 3 instead of 1: the nonconstant test detects the omission.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which identity defines divergence and fixes its sign?

For all tests F, E[Fδ(u)] = E[⟨DF, u⟩H]. δ(u) must be square integrable. The derivative is transferred to the field under the dot product defined by expectation.

Recall 2What bound does the domain require beyond integrability of the field?

The absolute value of E[⟨DF, u⟩H] must be bounded by Cᵤ times the square root of E[F squared], with a finite constant independent of the test F. Only the norm of F enters this bound.

Recall 3In our unit direction, how can you reconstruct δ(f(Z)h) for polynomial f?

Integrate by parts against the Gaussian density: δ(f(Z)h) = Z × f(Z) − f′(Z). The candidate is polynomial and therefore square integrable; duality and Cauchy–Schwarz justify membership in the domain.

Recall 4Why is a 0 mean insufficient to identify a divergence?

It checks only the constant test. For the field with a squared coefficient, the raw product and the correct divergence both have 0 mean; the test F = Z detects the missing derivative term.

Recall 5What distinguishes δ(Zh) from X squared − σ squared on the bench?

The former is a normalized score with no unit. The latter is this score multiplied by σ squared, in square millimeters. They are centered differences under the model; they can be negative at a reading.
