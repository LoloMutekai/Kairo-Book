Areas and volumes

Your goalMeasure the base of a cardboard box using unit squares, then its contents using unit cubes; convert this volume to litres and predict how enlargement affects area and volume.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

The cardboard box on this page is a fictional example. Its internal dimensions are 30 cm long, 20 cm wide and 10 cm high. There are two distinct questions. How many small squares are needed to cover its base? How many small cubes are needed to fill it?

A length is measured in a single direction, using a ruler marked in centimetres. For the base, we choose a unit square with sides of 1 cm; its area is called a square centimetre, written cm². We cover the base without gaps or overlaps, then count the squares: this number is the area of the base.

For the inside, we choose a unit cube with edges of 1 cm; its volume is called a cubic centimetre, written cm³. A layer of cubes placed on the base contains exactly one cube above each square. We stack layers up to the top, then count the cubes: this number is the internal volume.

The exponent in the unit tells us how many directions are measured together: 2 for an area, 3 for a volume. This is more than a change of symbol. A centimetre, a square centimetre and a cubic centimetre measure different things; we never add them to one another.

Model assumptions. The box is a cuboid: its six faces are rectangles that meet at right angles. The dimensions are internal, and the thickness of the walls is ignored. Each dimension is a whole number of centimetres, so no square or cube is cut at the edge. The values are chosen for the calculations; they are not measurements of a real object.

The rule to remember

Area of a rectangle: length × width, in square units. Volume of a cuboid: base area × height, that is, length × width × height, in cubic units. The three lengths must first be expressed in the same unit.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The 30 by 20 by 10 centimetre box

Fictional data: internal dimensions L = 30 cm, l = 20 cm and h = 10 cm. Find the area of the base, the internal volume in cm³ and then in litres, and the effect of making a copy with every length multiplied by 2.

  1. Name the quantities and their units

    L is the internal length, l the internal width and h the internal height: these are lengths, all in centimetres. A denotes the area of the base, in cm², and V the internal volume, in cm³.

    Why require a common unit? A unit square only makes sense if both its sides are measured with the same ruler. If a dimension were given in metres, it would first have to be converted to centimetres, before any multiplication.

  2. Cover the base in rows

    Along L, a row contains 30 squares with sides of 1 cm. Along l, we place 20 identical rows side by side. Counting 20 groups of 30 squares is exactly what multiplication does.

    A=L×l=30 cm×20 cm=600 cm²

    Check: adding would give 30 + 20 = 50. This number is a length, half the perimeter of the base in centimetres; it does not count any squares. Multiplying the units, cm × cm, does give cm².

  3. Stack the layers

    A layer of cubes with edges of 1 cm placed on the base contains one cube above each square, giving 600 cubes. The height of 10 cm provides room for 10 identical layers, without cutting any cubes.

    V=A×h=L×l×h=600 cm²×10 cm=6000 cm³

    Multiplying by h adds a direction: each square on the base becomes a cube when given a thickness of 1 cm, and each layer is repeated 10 times.

  4. Check by dividing the box another way

    Count another way, using vertical slices parallel to the shorter side. A slice has the shape of the side face, 20 cm by 10 cm: it contains 20 × 10 = 200 cubes. We line up 30 slices along L.

    V=(l×h)×L=200 cm²×30 cm=6000 cm³

    We obtain the same total: the volume does not depend on which face is chosen as the base. A different result would indicate a counting error or a unit error.

  5. Convert to litres

    A decimetre equals 10 cm. By definition, a litre is the volume of a cube with edges of one decimetre. This cube contains 10 × 10 = 100 cubes with edges of 1 cm per layer, and 10 layers.

    1 L=10×10×10 cm³=1000 cm³
    V=6000 cm³1000 cm³ par litre=6 L

    Independent check: arrange cubes of one litre directly. Along L: 30 ÷ 10 = 3; along l: 20 ÷ 10 = 2; in height: 10 ÷ 10 = 1. This gives 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 cubes of one litre, the same number of litres.

  6. Enlarge every length

    A copy of the box has every length multiplied by the same factor k = 2, as in a similar shape with scale factor 2: 60 cm, 40 cm and 20 cm.

    Base: 60 × 40 = 2400 cm², which is 4 times 600 cm². Volume: 2400 × 20 = 48000 cm³, or 48 L, which is 8 times the original volume.

    V′=(kL)×(kl)×(kh)=k×k×k×V=k3V

    Check: 2 × 2 = 4 for the area, because only two directions are enlarged in the base; 2 × 2 × 2 = 8 for the volume, because all three factors double. These ratios describe the cuboid model; they say nothing about the strength or weight of a real cardboard box.

Explain it in your own words

Explain to someone why the area of the base is calculated by multiplication rather than addition. Then explain why the enlarged copy contains 8 times as many cubes, rather than 2 times as many.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A squared unit is just another symbol.”

    The unit cm² counts squares of 1 cm by 1 cm, so it involves two directions. To convert, convert each side: one metre equals 100 cm, and one square metre contains 100 rows of 100 squares, giving 10000 cm², rather than 100.

    Review the unit square

  • “If I double the sides, I double everything.”

    Each doubled length contributes a factor of 2. The base receives two such factors, giving ×4; the volume receives three, giving ×8. Doubling just one factor, as in the first exercise, doubles the volume.

    Return to the enlarged copy step

  • “The internal volume tells me how much cardboard the box needs.”

    Volume counts cubes that fill the box; the material covers faces and is measured by an area, with thickness and flaps still to be specified. The two numbers do not even have the same unit.

    Do the faces exercise

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Lengthen just one side

Another fictional box has internal dimensions of 20 cm by 20 cm by 10 cm. Calculate its volume in cm³ and then in litres. It is then lengthened to 40 cm, with width and height unchanged: by what factor is its volume multiplied?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with the area of the base, then stack the 10 layers.

Hint 2

One litre equals 1000 cm³. For the comparison, look at how many factors have changed.

Compare with the solution

Base: 20 × 20 = 400 cm². Volume: 400 × 10 = 4000 cm³, or 4000 ÷ 1000 = 4 L.

Lengthened box: 40 × 20 = 800 cm², then 800 × 10 = 8000 cm³, or 8 L. Ratio: 8000 ÷ 4000 = 2. Only one factor has doubled: the volume only doubles; it is not multiplied by 8.

Check your reasoning

  • Units cm², then cm³, then L, each in the correct place.
  • Conversion using 1000 rather than 100.
  • Ratio justified by the single factor that changed.

Exercise 2 · Paper challenge

One square metre in square centimetres

Draw a square with sides of one metre, without drawing it to actual size. How many unit squares with sides of 1 cm are needed to cover it? Explain your drawing.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First convert each of the two sides to centimetres.

Hint 2

Count the squares in one row, then the number of rows. Multiplying only by 100 covers just one row.

Compare with the solution

One metre equals 100 cm. A row along one side contains 100 squares; we place 100 rows along the other side. Total: 100 × 100 = 10000 cm².

The conversion factor applies once per direction: 100 for a length, 100 × 100 for an area. We are counting whole squares, so the answer is an exact whole number.

Check your reasoning

  • Drawing showing a row and the number of rows.
  • Both sides converted, rather than just one.
  • Result in cm², rather than cm.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

Filling or covering

We want to make a closed box with exactly the internal dimensions of the box in the example, ignoring thickness and flaps. What area of cardboard covers its six faces? Compare this number with the volume. What happens to each of the two numbers for the copy enlarged by 2?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The faces come in identical pairs: base and lid, front and back, two sides.

Hint 2

For the copy, an area contains 2 length factors, while a volume contains 3.

Compare with the solution

Base: 600 cm². Front face: 30 × 10 = 300 cm². Side face: 20 × 10 = 200 cm². One face of each kind: 600 + 300 = 900, then 900 + 200 = 1100 cm². Each face has a matching face, so 2 × 1100 = 2200 cm².

The volume is 6000 cm³. These two numbers cannot be compared directly: one counts squares that cover, the other counts cubes that fill. A real box would also require allowance for thickness, flaps and cutting waste.

Enlarged copy: both sides of each face are doubled, so the surface area is multiplied by 4 and is 8800 cm²; the volume is multiplied by 8 and is 48000 cm³.

Check your reasoning

  • All six faces counted, in pairs.
  • Surface area in cm² and volume in cm³, never added together.
  • Factor 4 for surface area and 8 for volume, with an explanation.
  • Limitation stated: thickness and flaps ignored.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the area of the base of the box count?

The number of unit squares that cover it without gaps or overlaps; with squares whose sides are 1 cm, it is expressed in cm².

Recall 2Why does the volume of a cuboid equal base area × height?

Each layer contains a unit cube above each square on the base, and the height, measured in the same unit, gives the number of layers.

Recall 3How many cm² are there in one m², and why?

10000 cm²: one metre equals 100 cm in each of the two directions, giving 100 rows of 100 squares.

Recall 4What is one litre in cm³?

1000 cm³: it is the volume of a cube with edges of 10 cm.

Recall 5Every length doubles: what happens to the area and volume?

The area is multiplied by 4 and the volume by 8, because each length that is multiplied contributes a factor of 2.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Choose the unit shape before counting; cover the base with rows of squares for area; stack layers of cubes for volume; check by dividing the box another way; convert in each direction involved; enlarging every length multiplies the area by the square of the factor and the volume by its cube; never confuse the contents with the material.
