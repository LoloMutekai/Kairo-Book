Identification

Your goalPropose ARIMA orders from temporal correlations, distinguishing evidence for identification from validation of the model.

Before you start
c090-m5 : stationarity · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c101-m1 : AR and MA · c101-m2 : ARIMA

✿01 See the idea

A sensor records the temperature of a room every minute. When a reading is above the mean, does the next one tend to be above it too? And if readings further apart seem related, does that relationship add anything beyond the intermediate reading? These questions help us choose a model structure before fitting its coefficients.

Autocorrelation compares a series with its own copy shifted in time. Here, a lag counts minutes. We start by subtracting the mean: we compare deviations rather than raw temperatures. Deviations with the same sign give a positive product; opposite signs give a negative product. Taken together, these products measure a linear relationship without establishing a cause.

Partial autocorrelation looks for the relationship that remains after removing the linear explanation provided by the intermediate lags. A relationship over a longer interval can therefore be transmitted entirely through the preceding minute. Counting only the nonzero autocorrelations does not give the number of autoregressive terms.

All temperatures on this page are fictional and chosen for exact calculation by hand. We study a nonseasonal model with regular time steps, assuming a stable mean and stable covariances. This short segment does not verify that assumption: it helps us propose candidates and never certifies stationarity.

The rule to remember

Set d according to the stationarity sought; examine autocorrelations and partial correlations together to propose p and q; keep these orders as hypotheses to be checked.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Identify an initial candidate for the sensor

The 8 readings, in order, are 18, 18, 19, 21, 20, 22, 21, 21 °C, spaced 1 minute apart. Let us look for candidate nonseasonal orders. No future temperature is used, and no coefficient has been fitted yet.

  1. Define what we are identifying

    In ARIMA(p, d, q), p counts the lags of the series in the autoregressive part, d counts successive differencing operations, and q counts the lags of the shocks in the moving average part. These orders are unitless counts. Identification here means choosing a few candidate structures; estimation will then mean finding their coefficients.

    t indexes the minutes; x(t) is the reading in °C. If the levels are stationary, d = 0 is a consistent option: we do not difference the series. With d = 1, we would study the differences x(t) − x(t − 1), in °C, taken over an interval of 1 minute. We do not add a differencing operation to obtain a more appealing plot.

    Here, d = 0 remains conditional on the stated assumption. Neither the temperature level nor these 8 observations are enough to decide it in a real application. A persistent trend, seasonality, or irregular time steps would require us to reconsider the setup before this nonseasonal identification.

  2. Center the whole series once

    n is the number of readings. The sample mean x̄ is their sum divided by n. We obtain 2 × 18 + 19 + 3 × 21 + 20 + 22 = 160 °C, then x̄ = 160/8 = 20 °C.

    zt=xt−x¯

    z(t), or z with subscript t, is therefore a deviation in °C. In order, the deviations are -2, -2, -1, 1, 0, 2, 1, 1 °C. Their sum is 0 °C: this check detects a missed centering step. We keep this same mean for all lags.

  3. State the calculation convention and its denominator

    h is an integer lag between 0 and n − 1, counted in minutes here. S(h) sums the products of deviations separated by h steps. The symbol ∑ means to add the indicated terms. For each h, we keep only the available pairs; we do not invent any value before the start of the series.

    S(h)=∑t=h+1nztzt−h

    The full definition is S(h) = the sum of z(t) × z(t − h), for t running from h + 1 to n. Our sample autocorrelation is r(h) = S(h)/S(0). It is unitless: both numerator and denominator are in °C². This convention uses the same divisor n for all sample covariances; that divisor cancels in their ratio.

    At 0 lag, we add the squares: S(0) = 4 + 4 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 4 + 1 + 1 = 16 °C². Another grouping is 3 × 4 + 4 × 1 = 12 + 4 = 16 °C².

    S(0) is strictly positive, so the ratios are defined. For a constant series, it would be 0: we would report an undefined autocorrelation, never an automatically 0 correlation.

  4. Shift, multiply, then normalize

    At lag 1, the successive products are 4, 2, -1, 0, 0, 2, 1 °C². We add them because all pairs must contribute: S(1) = 8 °C². We then divide by the total dispersion rather than the mean temperature:

    r(1)=816=1/2=0.50

    At lag 2, the products are 2, -2, 0, 2, 0, 2 °C². The first two cancel; the others give S(2) = 4 °C². Thus r(2) = 4/16 = 1/4 = 0.25, unitless.

    Check: deviations with opposite signs did contribute negatively. Both ratios lie between −1 and 1. Do not replace each sum with an average using n − h while keeping S(0) unchanged: that would be a different convention.

  5. Remove the relationship transmitted through the intermediate minute

    κ(h) denotes an estimate of the partial autocorrelation. At the first lag, there is no intermediate lag: κ(1) = r(1). For lag 2, we use the Yule–Walker convention, which constructs this estimate from the same autocorrelations:

    κ(2)=r(2)−r(1)21−r(1)2

    The square of r(1) is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 and represents the transmitted relationship in this calculation. The numerator is 1/4 − 1/4 = 0. The denominator is 1 − 1/4 = 3/4; it is positive. The result is κ(2) = 0, unitless.

    We therefore do more than subtract the square: normalization is part of the formula. In a stationary population, partial correlation is the correlation of the residuals obtained by removing the linear explanation provided by the intermediate lags from both endpoints. In a short sample, an estimate from separate regressions can differ from our convention. The 0 obtained here is not proof of independence.

  6. Connect the pattern to a candidate model

    To understand the AR(1) reference pattern, let W(t) = X(t) − μ, where X is the random temperature and μ its population mean, in °C. The model is W(t) = φW(t − 1) + ε(t). φ is unitless; ε(t) is a shock in °C with 0 mean and constant positive variance, independent of the past. We assume |φ| is less than 1 and the process is in its stationary regime.

    ρ(h), the Greek letter rho, is the theoretical correlation between W(t) and W(t − h). Multiplying the model by the past value and taking the means of the products eliminates the independent shock. We obtain ρ(1) = φ, then ρ(2) = φρ(1), or φ squared. The relationship at lag 2 is then transmitted through lag 1: the theoretical partial correlation is 0.

    Our pair r(1) = 1/2 and r(2) = 1/4 resembles this reference pattern. This motivates including ARIMA(1, 0, 0) among the candidates. Provisionally taking φ = 1/2 explains this pattern; it is neither a least squares fit nor an estimate of all parameters.

    For comparison, an MA(1) model is written W(t) = ε(t) + θε(t − 1), with independent shocks of the same variance and unitless θ. Beyond lag 1, the shocks no longer overlap: its theoretical autocorrelations are 0. Yet the sample value r(2) = 1/4, based on 8 readings, is not enough to rule out ARIMA(0, 0, 1). It comes with no decision threshold established here.

  7. Present a proposal with its limitations

    As theoretical reference patterns, a pure causal AR of order p has a cutoff in partial correlation beyond p; a pure MA of order q has a cutoff in autocorrelation beyond q. A cutoff means “0 at subsequent lags in the population,” rather than “a few small bars in our plot.” For models combining AR and MA, both patterns can decay without a clear cutoff.

    The resulting proposal is therefore: d = 0 assuming stationarity in levels; first candidate ARIMA(1, 0, 0); a low-order alternative MA(1). We have calculated only the first clues and cannot yet distinguish between these structures. The exercise on lag 3 will even show a departure from the ideal pattern.

    We still need to fit the candidates, examine their fitting errors, then evaluate forecasts for later dates held out from identification. Residual diagnostics and rolling-origin forecasting belong to the following units. This page establishes neither a winning model nor forecasting quality.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results. Reconstruct the steps from temperatures to products, then from products to the pair of correlations. Why can a nonzero second raw correlation accompany a 0 partial correlation? Finish by stating what your proposed order leaves to be checked.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The first bars are positive, so I count that many AR lags.”

    Autoregressive dependence propagates indirectly. Distinguish r(h), the raw relationship, from κ(h), the relationship after removing the intermediate lags. In our calculation, r(2) = 1/4 while κ(2) = 0: counting the raw bars does not give p.

    Revisit the transmitted relationship and the autoregressive reference pattern

  • “An exactly 0 partial correlation proves the model order.”

    This is an exact result for these fictional values and this convention. It does not turn the other lags into known observations, prove a population cutoff, or rule out other models. Calculate the next lag to test this overly strong conclusion.

    Try the calculation at the next lag

  • “The room is warmer, so we need more differencing.”

    A high level and a trend over time are different. Adding the same constant to each reading shifts the mean but leaves all centered deviations unchanged. The order d concerns the stationarity sought, rather than the size of the temperature.

Exercise 2 · Application · check an interpretation

The next lag does not match the ideal plot

In the same centered list, calculate S(3), then r(3). Compare this result with the theoretical reference φ to the power 3 for an AR(1) with φ = 1/2. Does this difference justify declaring the candidate impossible?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The first product pairs the deviation of the fourth reading with that of the first. Positions at the edge with no partner are left out.

Hint 2

The available products are -2, 0, -2, 1, 0 °C². Then compare a sample estimate with a population reference.

Compare with the solution

The sum is S(3) = -2 + 0 + -2 + 1 + 0 = -3 °C². Therefore r(3) = -3/16 = -3/16 ≈ -0.1875, unitless.

The population reference would be φ to the power 3, or 1/2 × 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/8 = 0.125, unitless. Our sample result even has the opposite sign. This prevents us from presenting the entire observed curve as exact geometric decay.

However, a short sample does not reproduce population correlations exactly. Without analyzing its uncertainty, this difference does not demonstrate that the candidate is impossible. We keep a cautious proposal and gather the remaining checks; we do not choose an additional order to make every irregularity disappear.

Check your reasoning

  • The pairs are separated by the requested lag and the sum is -3 °C².
  • Fractions remain exact until the comparison.
  • The answer distinguishes an ideal pattern, a sample estimate, and a decision that has not yet been established.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · argue without recalculating all pairs

A constant sensor error

In a variation of the same scene, the sensor displays each original reading increased by 10 °C. Someone claims that the higher mean requires d = 1 and changes the candidate orders. Calculate the new mean and use centering to explain what happens to S(h), r(h), and κ(2).

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The mean receives the same offset as each observation.

Hint 2

Expand (x(t) + offset) − (x̄ + offset), then recover the original deviations.

Compare with the solution

The new mean is 20 + 10 = 30 °C. Each new deviation is (x(t) + 10) − (x̄ + 10) = x(t) − x̄. All centered products and their sums S(h) therefore remain the same.

We recover r(1) = 1/2, r(2) = 1/4, r(3) = -3/16 and κ(2) = 0, unitless. This constant shift does not create a temporal trend or justify changing d. The absolute temperature is wrong, but our argument about centered dependence is unchanged.

This still does not prove stationarity or the correct order: it only shows that the choice of d does not follow from a high mean. A sensor error that varied over time would require a different analysis.

Check your reasoning

  • The shifted mean is 30 °C.
  • The cancellation of the offset is written out before stating invariance.
  • The reasoning separates the temperature level, temporal dependence, and the limitations of identification.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do we choose during identification, and what remains to be estimated?

We propose orders p, d, q: autoregressive lags, differencing operations, and shock lags. The coefficients remain to be fitted. The proposal must then be checked against diagnostics and forecasts for other dates.

Recall 2How do we recover our autocorrelation at lag h from the temperatures?

Subtract the same mean from all readings. Add the products of deviations separated by h steps to form S(h), then divide by S(0), the sum of squares of the entire list. The ratio is unitless; if S(0) is 0, it is undefined.

Recall 3Why can a raw relationship at lag 2 accompany a 0 partial relationship?

The intermediate lag can transmit the entire linear relationship. With our convention, we subtract the square of r(1) from r(2), then divide by 1 minus that same square. The 0 result describes this calculation; it proves neither independence nor a definite order.

Recall 4Which cutoff suggests a pure AR, and which suggests a pure MA?

For a causal AR of order p, the theoretical partial correlation is 0 beyond p. For an MA of order q, the theoretical autocorrelation is 0 beyond q. In a sample, these patterns help us propose candidates without mechanically counting nonzero bars.

Recall 5What does the partial correlation of 0 obtained from our 8 readings fail to demonstrate?

It demonstrates neither stationarity, uniqueness of the model, nor forecasting quality. The next lag already differs from the ideal pattern. Fitting, diagnostics, and evaluation on held-out dates remain to be done.
