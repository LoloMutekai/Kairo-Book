Level 0 · C005·M1

Angles

Your goalMeasure how far a door is open, distinguish this opening from the length of the drawing, and calculate the angle still needed to reach a right angle or a straight angle.

Before you start
c004-m5 : rational equations

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a door seen from above. The hinge stays in place; the door swings away from its closed position. To describe how far it opens, look at its movement around the hinge, rather than the size of the door.

The hinge is the vertex of the angle. The direction of the closed door and the direction of the open door form its sides. Imagine extending them endlessly: each is a ray, a line that starts at the vertex and continues in one direction. The angle describes the opening between them.

In the first drawing, we lengthen the lines without turning them. The arc near the hinge stays the same. In the second, we keep the same door but change the target: stopping at a right angle or continuing to a straight angle requires different amounts of movement still to go.

The openings and lengths in this scene are fictional. We use an idealized rotation in the plane of the floor, from the closed position to a half turn; we ignore real obstacles. The lengths are drawing units, rather than metres measured on a door.

The rule to remember

To compare angles, compare the directions at the vertex. To calculate an opening still to go, first name the target, then subtract the opening already covered.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The door open through 30°

A protractor reads 30° from the closed position. How much further must the door open to reach a right angle, then a straight angle? We always turn in the opening direction and measure from the same closed position.

  1. Read the opening in the right place

    O is the vertex, at the hinge. θ, the Greek letter theta, represents the measure of the opening from the closed position. Its unit is the degree, written °. By convention, a full turn is divided into 360 equal parts; each measures 1°.

    To measure, centre the protractor on O, align its 0° mark with the reference side, and read the mark crossed by the door. Choose the scale that starts at 0° on this reference: here, it gives θ = 30°. The drawing represents this value; it does not replace a precise measurement.

    A closed position corresponds to 0°. Our opening increases up to a straight angle, without continuing through an additional turn. The length of the door plays no part in reading θ.

  2. Build the targets from a full turn

    A right angle corresponds to a quarter turn: the door is perpendicular to its closed position. A straight angle corresponds to a half turn: the sides lie on the same line and point in opposite directions. We therefore divide the same full turn to find the measures of these targets.

    Droit : 3604=90°
    Plat : 3602=180°

    An opening strictly between 0° and 90° is an acute angle; strictly between 90° and 180°, an obtuse angle. At 0°, the angle is a 0 angle. The values 90° and 180° give a right angle and a straight angle respectively. Our door at 30° therefore forms an acute angle.

  3. Complete the opening to a right angle

    c represents the measure, in degrees, still to go to reach a right angle. Two angles are complementary when their measures add up to 90°. In our drawing, the openings follow each other without overlapping: the current opening plus the remainder must equal this target.

    θ+c=90°;c=90°−θ

    Subtract the part already opened from the target, rather than subtracting the target from the part. The complement is 90° − 30° = 60°.

    We allow a 0 remainder. The complement defined in this way exists as a nonnegative angle only for an opening between 0° and 90°, including both endpoints. Beyond a right angle, continuing to open the door can no longer lead to a right angle.

    c=90°−30°=60°

  4. Complete the opening to a straight angle

    s represents the measure, in degrees, still to go to reach a straight angle. Two angles are supplementary when their measures add up to 180°. The target is further away than a right angle, so the previous complement is not enough.

    θ+s=180°;s=180°−θ

    The supplement is 180° − 30° = 150°. It remains nonnegative throughout our interval, from 0° to 180°. An angle can therefore exceed a right angle and still have a supplement.

    s=180°−30°=150°

  5. Check by reconstructing the target

    Now add, using the inverse operation of subtraction. With the complement: 30° + 60° = 90°. With the supplement: 30° + 150° = 180°. Each sum returns its own target.

    Another check: the turn from a right angle to a straight angle is 180° − 90° = 90°. The supplement must therefore exceed the complement by this same amount: 150° − 60° = 90°. This check also distinguishes the two names.

    At the endpoints, a door already at a right angle has no further movement to make to reach a right angle: its complement is 0°. Already at a straight angle, its supplement is 0°. You must state the target even when the result is 0.

  6. Change the drawing, then distinguish the units

    We change from lines 1 unit long to lines 3 drawing units long, keeping the vertex and directions. The length factor is 3 ÷ 1 = 3, with no unit. The drawn length increases by 2 drawing units.

    The door has not turned: the opening after this change remains 30°. Its change is 30° − 30° = 0°. The complement therefore remains 60° and the supplement 150°. Drawing units are never added to degrees.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations. Start again from the hinge, name the targets, and find both remainders. Explain why multiplying the lengths by 3 does not change the remainders, then check each result by adding.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If the line is longer, the angle is larger.”

    Place the protractor back on the hinge. Lengthening the lines leaves the same directions: the arc at the vertex and its measure do not change. To change the angle, you must turn one side relative to the other.

    Compare the arcs in the first drawing

  • “An opening cannot exceed a right angle.”

    A right angle is a reference point, rather than the maximum in this scene. Between 90° and 180°, the door forms an obtuse angle. Its nonnegative complement no longer exists, but its supplement can still be calculated.

    Try the door beyond a right angle

  • “Complement or supplement, I always subtract from the same value.”

    First write the target: 90° for the complement, 180° for the supplement. Then subtract the current opening. The checking addition must return the chosen target, rather than the other one.

    Revisit the targets and the checking additions

Exercise 1 · Identify and compare

The drawing gets longer

The door is open through 30°. You replace the lines of 1 unit with lines of 3 units, without moving the hinge or turning the directions. Give the new opening and its change. Why is the distance between the endpoints not enough to read the angle?

Hint 1

Imagine the protractor staying in the same place: does the door cross a different mark?

Hint 2

Distinguish the change in length, in drawing units, from the change in opening, in degrees.

Compare with the solution

The opening remains 30°. Its change is 30° − 30° = 0°. The factor 3 multiplies the lengths, rather than the angle.

The distance between the endpoints grows when the lines are lengthened, even without turning. It therefore also depends on the chosen lengths and cannot provide the angle measure on its own. Check: the vertex and directions can still be superimposed, and the same arc can be copied near the hinge.

Check your reasoning

  • You give 30° and a change of 0°.
  • You justify the result using the directions, without multiplying the angle by the length factor.
  • You explain what changes in the drawing and what is used to measure the opening.

Exercise 2 · Calculate and check

Another position of the same door

The protractor now reads 35°. Calculate the complement and the supplement. For each, write the target, the operation, the result with its unit, and a checking addition.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The complement leads to a right angle; the supplement leads to a straight angle.

Hint 2

Subtract 35° from each target. Then add this opening to the remainder you found.

Compare with the solution

Complement: the target is 90°. Subtract the opening already covered: 90° − 35° = 55°. Check: 35° + 55° = 90°.

Supplement: the target is 180°. The remainder is 180° − 35° = 145°. Check: 35° + 145° = 180°. The results differ because the targets differ; the initial door position is the same.

Check your reasoning

  • You associate 55° with the complement and 145° with the supplement.
  • You subtract the opening from the target in that order.
  • Your sums return 90° and 180° respectively, with degrees throughout.

Exercise 3 · Justify a limit

The door has passed a right angle

The same door reaches 120°. Is this an acute, right, or obtuse angle? Can you still find a nonnegative complement by continuing to open it? Calculate the supplement and explain why the two questions have different answers about whether an angle still to go exists.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Locate 120° relative to a right angle and a straight angle before subtracting.

Hint 2

An angle still to go in the opening direction must be positive or 0. What does a negative result tell you?

Compare with the solution

The opening exceeds 90° by 30° and remains below 180°: the angle is obtuse. The formal subtraction 90° − 120° gives -30°. This number indicates that a right angle has already been passed; it does not constitute a nonnegative complement.

Returning to a right angle would require closing the door, which changes the requested direction of movement. By continuing to open it, however, you can reach a straight angle: 180° − 120° = 60°. Check: 120° + 60° = 180°.

The supplement exists within our interval even when the nonnegative complement does not. The sign of the calculation must be interpreted with the assumptions; producing a number is not enough to answer the geometric question.

Check your reasoning

  • You classify the angle as obtuse using the endpoints of the intervals.
  • You reject presenting -30° as a nonnegative opening still to go.
  • You obtain a supplement of 60° and check it with the sum 180°.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1For the door seen from above, what does the angle measure, and where is its vertex?

It measures the opening between the closed direction and the direction of the door. The vertex is the hinge; lengthening the sides without turning them preserves this angle.

Recall 2How can you find the measures of a right angle and a straight angle from a full turn?

A full turn is 360°. A right angle is a quarter turn: 360 ÷ 4 = 90°. A straight angle is a half turn: 360 ÷ 2 = 180°.

Recall 3How do you calculate the complement, and when is it nonnegative?

Subtract the opening from 90°. The result is a nonnegative complement for an opening between 0° and 90°, including both endpoints; the opening and its complement must total 90°.

Recall 4Which target distinguishes the supplement from the complement?

The supplement completes the opening to 180°, the complement to 90°. For the supplement, subtract the opening from 180°, then check that the addition returns 180°.

Recall 5Which reference points must you align before reading a protractor?

Its centre with the vertex and its 0° mark with the reference side. Then read where the other side crosses, on the scale starting from this same 0°.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, redraw the door and its hinge. Define the angle, find the right angle and the straight angle from a full turn, then reconstruct the complement and the supplement with a checking addition. Finish by explaining what happens if you lengthen the lines or if the door passes a right angle.
