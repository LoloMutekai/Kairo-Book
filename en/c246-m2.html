Level 4 · C246·M2

Likelihood weights

Your goalCalculate the sensitivity of a threshold cost by differentiating outcome probabilities, then distinguish this exact derivative from its Monte Carlo estimate.

Before you start
c176-m5 : correlated simulation · c240-m5 : forward smile and VIX · c186-m5 : risk near the barrier · c182-m5 : trinomial tree and admissibility · c246-m1 : pathwise differentiation under conditions

✿01 See the idea

A small station has a single backup unit. During a time slot, each of its 2 customers may need it. If 2 customers request it, the station must rent additional equipment for 12 €. With a single request, the backup unit is enough. The entire scenario, its costs and the draws presented are fictional.

Each customer’s probability of making a request is called θ. If θ increases slightly, the rental rate stays the same, but the costly outcome becomes more frequent. We want the slope of the mean cost with respect to θ. Differentiating only the rate observed in a realization does not describe this change in frequency.

The idea is to keep the possible outcomes fixed and examine how quickly their probabilities change. The likelihood weight divides this rate of change by the outcome’s probability: it measures a local relative change. An outcome that becomes less probable receives a negative weight. Multiplying the cost by this weight lets us recover the slope of the mean cost.

In the figures, K counts the requests. The customers are assumed to be independent and have the same probability θ, strictly between 0 and 1. The threshold, number of customers and rental rate are fixed. This small model allows an exact check; the next aim is to understand the mean of contributions that we would calculate by simulation.

The rule to remember

Differentiate the outcome distribution while holding the outcome fixed, divide by its probability, then average cost × weight. The weight is signed; it is not a probability.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The slope of the station’s cost

Set θ = 1/3 and C = 12 €, where C is the rental rate. K is the number of requests; h(k) equals C if k = 2, and 0 € otherwise. Let us calculate the derivative of the mean cost V(θ), then an estimate over 4 fictional time slots: K = (0, 0, 1, 2). This list is a prescribed teaching sample, rather than the output of a generator run.

  1. Write the distribution and its units

    k denotes a possible value of K; it is a count with no physical unit. pθ(k) is its probability. The subscript θ indicates that this distribution depends on the parameter. The 2 requests are independent events: we multiply their probabilities. For a single request, the cases “first customer only” and “second customer only” are disjoint: we add their probabilities.

    pθ(0)=(1−θ)2;pθ(1)=2θ(1−θ);pθ(2)=θ2

    At our θ, the probabilities are 4/9, 4/9 and 1/9. Normalization check: 4/9 + 4/9 = 8/9, then 8/9 + 1/9 = 1. V(θ) = Eθ[h(K)] is the mean cost, in euros per time slot; Eθ means averaging under the distribution pθ. The derivative V′(θ) measures euros per time slot per unit of θ. As a probability, θ has no unit; a unit of θ is a change in probability, rather than a percentage point.

  2. Move the derivative to the probability

    The prime denotes differentiation with respect to θ while holding k fixed. Here h does not depend directly on θ. A denotes the support: the fixed set of possible outcomes 0, 1, 2. The symbol ∑ adds the terms for all k in A. We can differentiate each term in this finite sum. Since every pθ(k) is strictly positive, let us multiply and divide its derivative by pθ(k).

    V′(θ)=∑k∈Ah(k)pθ′(k)=Eθ[h(K)Sθ(K)]

    The weight Sθ(k) is defined by Sθ(k) = p′θ(k)/pθ(k) = ∂θ ln pθ(k). ln is the natural logarithm and ∂θ denotes differentiation with respect to the parameter. This weight is also called the score. Its unit is the inverse of the parameter’s unit: here, per unit of θ, with no physical dimension. p′θ(k) = pθ(k)Sθ(k) lets us write the derivative as a new expectation of h(K)Sθ(K). The cost can therefore jump at a threshold: we do not differentiate it with respect to k.

    This weight is the local derivative of a ratio of distributions: the derivative of p(θ+ε)(k)/pθ(k) with respect to ε at ε = 0 is the score Sθ(k). ε denotes a small change in the parameter. A ratio of probabilities at two parameter values is positive; its derivative can be negative. Do not confuse these two objects or normalize the scores as probabilities.

  3. Calculate the weights while holding the outcome fixed

    The logarithmic derivative of a product adds the logarithmic derivatives of its factors. Each request contributes 1/θ; each absence of a request contributes −1/(1 − θ). The factor that counts the cases is independent of θ, so its derivative is 0. With k requests among 2 customers:

    Sθ(k)=kθ−2−k1−θ

    Intermediate values: 1 − θ = 2/3; 1/θ = 3; 1/(1 − θ) = 3/2. For k = 0, the weight is −2/(2/3) = -3. For k = 1, it is 3 − 3/2 = 3/2, or 1.50 per unit of θ. For k = 2, it is 2/θ = 6.

    The negative sign for no requests indicates that this outcome becomes less probable as θ increases. The weights are not required to lie in the probability interval. The formula is used only in the interior: at the endpoints, some probabilities vanish and this division is no longer justified.

  4. Average the contributions, then check another way

    Call Y = h(K)Sθ(K) the contribution to sensitivity, in euros per unit of θ for a time slot. Its values are 0 × (-3) = 0, 0 × 3/2 = 0, and 12 × 6 = 72. The exact mean multiplies each contribution by its probability. Only the last contributes here:

    V′(1/3)=1/9×72=8 €/unité de θ

    Independent check: the station pays only when the customers make requests together. Thus V(θ) = C × θ × θ, and V′(θ) = 2Cθ. We recover 12 × 2/3 = 8 €/unit of θ. At the chosen point, the mean cost is C × pθ(2) = 4/3 €, or approximately 1.33 € per time slot. Mean cost and slope are different quantities.

    Another structural check: the mean score is 0, because the sum of the probabilities stays at 1. The weighted average of the score vanishes; the unweighted sum of the weights need not vanish. The reading exercise carries out this check term by term.

  5. Move from enumeration to Monte Carlo

    If there are too many outcomes to enumerate, draw M independent realizations Kᵢ from pθ. M is the number of simulated time slots and i is their index. The empirical mean, denoted D̂ with a hat, estimates the derivative. Dividing by M is enough: the draw frequencies already represent the probabilities. Do not multiply each contribution by pθ(Kᵢ) a second time.

    D^=1M∑i=1Mh(Ki)Sθ(Ki)

    For our fictional list (0, 0, 1, 2), the contributions are (0, 0, 0, 72). Their mean is 72/4 = 18 €/unit of θ. The difference from the exact derivative is 10 €/unit of θ. This list illustrates a possible realization; it does not demonstrate the estimator’s properties experimentally.

    Under truly independent draws from the stated distribution, Eθ[D̂] = V′(θ): this is unbiasedness. It does not mean that a sample gives exactly V′. If Y has finite variance, Var(D̂) = Var(Y)/M; Var means variance. The score can become large when certain outcomes are rare: the formula promises no variance reduction compared with finite differences.

  6. Check the conditions before reusing the method

    Here, the support is the fixed set of possible values 0, 1, 2. Their probabilities are positive and differentiable for θ in the interior; h is fixed and the sum is finite. These facts justify all our equalities. For a continuous variable with density pθ(x), the same identity replaces the sum with an integral. x then denotes the outcome, and the score remains ∂θ ln pθ(x).

    In this continuous case, sufficient conditions are a fixed support, a density positive almost everywhere on that support, differentiability with respect to θ, and an integrable function that bounds |h(x)∂θpθ(x)| from above in a neighbourhood of the parameter. This domination allows differentiation under the integral. A moving support requires examining boundary terms; calculating the logarithm alone is no longer enough.

    Link to the previous unit: we could generate each request by drawing for its customer a variable U uniformly distributed between 0 and 1, independently of the other customer’s variable. A request occurs if U is less than θ. These variables have no unit. For fixed U values, the cost is piecewise constant in θ; its derivative is 0 away from the thresholds. Averaging it would give 0, whereas our exact derivative is 8. Interchanging differentiation and expectation is not justified for this pathwise calculation. The score works here because it differentiates the probabilities on the fixed support.

    One last condition to check: if the cost hθ itself depends on θ, the product rule adds its explicit derivative. The complete formula becomes Eθ[∂θhθ(K) + hθ(K)Sθ(K)]. The transfer exercise uses the same station to make this term visible. Other weighting methods and replication are left for later units.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the formulas, explain what is held fixed when differentiating, why a weight can be negative, and why enumeration uses probabilities while the mean of draws does not apply them a second time. Then recover the derivative directly from the only costly outcome.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A negative weight is an impossible probability; it must be removed.”

    The score is a local relative change. The outcome with no requests becomes less probable as θ increases, which gives -3. Keep the sign and check the mean score using the probabilities, rather than its unweighted sum.

    Review the weight calculation and its check

  • “I normalize the scores, or multiply each draw by its probability again.”

    Enumeration weights each outcome by pθ; draws from pθ already provide this weighting through their frequencies. For Monte Carlo, average the contributions hS by dividing by M. The sum of the scores can be 0: it must not be used as a denominator.

    Compare the exact mean with the mean of draws

  • “The score formula lets me differentiate any cost under any distribution.”

    First identify the parameter, the support and all the ways the cost depends on the parameter. A moving support can introduce a boundary term; a cost hθ introduces ∂θhθ. The presence of a logarithm does not justify interchanging differentiation and expectation.

    Work through the surcharge in the transfer exercise after the example’s conditions

Exercise 1 · Reading and checking

A positive score for a single request

At the same θ = 1/3, recalculate Sθ(1), then Eθ[Sθ(K)]. Why does this outcome’s positive weight not assign a rental cost to it?

Hint 1

A request contributes 1/θ and an absence of a request contributes −1/(1 − θ).

Hint 2

Average the scores using the probabilities 4/9, 4/9 and 1/9; then look at h(1).

Compare with the solution

Sθ(1) = 3 − 3/2 = 3/2, or 1.50 per unit of θ. This outcome becomes locally more probable, but the backup unit is enough: h(1) = 0 €, so its contribution hS stays at 0 €/unit of θ.

The products probability × score are -4/3, 2/3 and 2/3. The first terms give -2/3; adding the last gives a sum of 0. This is the derivative of the total mass 1, which is constant.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 3/2 and justify the signs of the terms.
  • Recover 0 through an explicit weighted average.
  • Separate the change in probability, the cost and the contribution to sensitivity.

Exercise 2 · Application on paper

Why does the simulated answer differ?

A colleague obtains 18 €/unit of θ using the list in the example and claims that the derivative 8 is wrong. Reconstruct the colleague’s mean and the exact derivative, then respond. If the simulated time slots shared a common disruptive event, could the colleague keep the formula Var(Y)/M without justification?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The list contains a single nonzero contribution, equal to 72. Enumeration assigns it the probability 1/9.

Hint 2

Unbiasedness concerns the theoretical mean across possible samples. For the variance of a sum, consider what happens to the covariances.

Compare with the solution

For the list: (0 + 0 + 0 + 72)/4 = 18 €/unit of θ. For the distribution: 1/9 × 72 = 8 €/unit of θ. The frequency of the costly outcome in this list is not its exact probability.

The derivative can also be checked through V(θ) = C × θ × θ: V′ = 2Cθ = 8. A small sample can differ from it; its difference 10 is neither proof of bias in the method nor a measure of its overall quality.

If the contributions are dependent, the covariances between time slots enter the variance of their mean. The independence that allows Var(Y)/M must then be checked; knowing M alone is not enough. Dependence between time slots is distinct from the independence of customers within a time slot.

Check your reasoning

  • Divide by the number of draws for the list.
  • Recover the derivative through the probabilities and through the mean cost formula.
  • Distinguish sample deviation, bias and the independence assumption.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same scenario

The rate now depends on the parameter

The station keeps the rental cost C at the same threshold and adds a surcharge Bθ to every time slot, with B = 3 € per unit of θ. Its cost becomes hθ(k) = h(k) + Bθ. Calculate the derivative of the new mean cost at θ = 1/3. What would be missing if we calculated only Eθ[hθ(K)Sθ(K)]?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The new mean cost is C × θ × θ + Bθ. First check its derivative directly.

Hint 2

In the score term, Bθ is constant with respect to the outcome k; use Eθ[Sθ(K)] = 0.

Compare with the solution

The surcharge is Bθ = 1 € per time slot at the chosen point. The new mean cost is 4/3 + 1 = 7/3 €, or approximately 2.33 €. Its derivative is 2Cθ + B = 8 + 3 = 11 €/unit of θ.

The score term alone gives Eθ[hS] + BθEθ[S] = 8 + Bθ × 0 = 8. It captures only the change in distribution. The explicit derivative of the cost is missing: ∂θhθ(k) = B, identical for every outcome.

The complete rule therefore adds Eθ[∂θhθ(K)] = 3 €/unit of θ and recovers 11. The change concerns only the surcharge at the same station; the support and the request distribution remain those of the example.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify both dependencies on θ: distribution and cost.
  • Obtain 11 by direct differentiation and by the complete rule.
  • Explain why the 0 mean of the score eliminates the surcharge from the hθSθ term alone.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the likelihood weight differentiate, and what is held fixed?

Hold the outcome k fixed and differentiate its probability with respect to θ: Sθ(k) = p′θ(k)/pθ(k) = ∂θ ln pθ(k). The score measures a local relative change; it can be negative.

Recall 2Under what conditions is our derivative the mean of hS?

Here: a fixed finite support, positive differentiable probabilities, and a cost independent of θ. Differentiate the sum of cost times probability. For an integral, differentiation under the integral must be justified; a moving support requires examining the boundaries.

Recall 3How do enumeration and draws differ when calculating a sensitivity?

Enumeration multiplies each h(k)Sθ(k) by pθ(k). Draws from pθ provide this weighting through their frequencies: simply average their contributions. A realization of the estimator can differ from the exact derivative.

Recall 4Why is the mean score 0 in our model?

The sum of the probabilities is constantly 1. Its derivative is 0, and p′θ(k) = pθ(k)Sθ(k). It is a weighted average that vanishes, rather than necessarily the unweighted sum of the scores.

Recall 5Which term must be added if the cost itself depends on the parameter?

The explicit derivative of the cost: V′(θ) = Eθ[∂θhθ(K) + hθ(K)Sθ(K)], under the conditions for differentiation. The score alone describes the change in distribution and does not replace ∂θhθ.
