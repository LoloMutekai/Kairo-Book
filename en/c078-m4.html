Level 2 · C078·M4

External model and adaptation

Your goalConvert the landmarks predicted by an external model into the image coordinate system, then check this adaptation without confusing correct conversion with accurate prediction.

Before you start
c027-m5 : complex numbers · c040-m5 : least squares · c036-m5 : annotated graphs · c022-m5 : untrusted inputs and injection · c078-m1 : pixels, coordinates and normalization · c078-m2 : coordinate systems, segments and angles · c078-m3 : degenerate geometry and missing points

✿01 See the idea

You want to draw the segments of a jointed arm on a photograph of it. An external model proposes positions for the shoulder, elbow and wrist. However, it received only a cropped area, scaled down before analysis. If you place its points directly on the full photograph, they may end up in the wrong place, even if the model located the joints perfectly within the area it received.

Here, an external model is a separately supplied prediction program whose parameters have already been learned from other images. We use its outputs without studying that learning process. The adapter translates between what this program provides and what our geometry calculation expects: point names, order, units and coordinate system. This adaptation does not change the parameters learned by the model.

Imagine an address marked on a map of a neighbourhood: you need to know where that neighbourhood lies in the city to transfer the address to the city map. The crop plays this role. Keeping its origin and dimensions lets you place the points back in the full image; losing them prevents you from determining their positions in that image with certainty.

The whole scene is fictional: the photograph, dimensions and outputs of the Arti model, version A. This model is an invented exercise contract, rather than a tool claimed to be available. E, C and P denote the shoulder, elbow and wrist, respectively, of the same jointed arm. A predicted point remains a proposal; an exact conversion does not guarantee that it identifies the correct joint.

Before calculating, follow the diagrams: the model proposes points, the adapter converts and checks them, then the geometry uses the accepted points. If the output is missing or incompatible, the process stops with an explicit reason; it does not invent a position.

The rule to remember

An external output has meaning only with its contract: landmark identities, coordinate format and image transformations. Translate this contract before taking measurements.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Recover the wrist position after cropping

The fictional photograph measures 800 × 600 px. The analysed area starts at (100 ; 50) px and measures 400 × 200 px. It is scaled down to 200 × 100 px for the model. The output gives, in this order: wrist (0.75 ; 0.75), shoulder (0.25 ; 0.25), elbow (0.25 ; 0.75). These pairs are normalized by the dimensions of the received image, so they are dimensionless. Let us recover E, C and P in pixels of the full photograph.

  1. Establish the contract before using the numbers

    Our fictional contract associates each output position with its name: first P, then E, then C. The geometry calculation expects the names E, C, P; the adapter therefore reorders the complete associations, never the numbers in isolation. With a real provider, the list of names and the output version must come from a reviewed source. They cannot be guessed from an image.

    In the contract for this scene, u denotes the normalized x-coordinate pointing right and v the normalized y-coordinate pointing down, from the top left corner of the received area. The values are finite and between 0 and 1, inclusive. The adapter also checks that the 3 expected landmarks are each present exactly once. This choice of bounds belongs to our exercise; it is not imposed on every external model.

    We use continuous positions: the right edge is at the rectangle’s width and the bottom edge at its height. These are not integer indices used to read an array element. Scaling down adds no padding and applies neither rotation nor reflection. These assumptions make the conversion below valid. With added padding or a flipped image, those transformations would first have to be reversed.

  2. Keep the crop dimensions and origin

    W and H denote the width and height of the full photograph, in pixels: W = 800 px and H = 600 px. The top left corner of the crop has coordinates x0 = 100 px and y0 = 50 px. The crop rectangle has width w = 400 px and height h = 200 px, measured in the original photograph.

    The right edge of the crop rectangle is at 100 + 400 = 500 px; the bottom is at 50 + 200 = 250 px. They lie within the photograph. The dimensions are strictly positive. We keep this information with the output: a normalized pair alone does not say which area it refers to.

  3. Derive the conversion, then apply it to the wrist

    x and y will be the point’s coordinates in the full photograph, in pixels. Multiplying u by w recovers its horizontal displacement within the original crop; multiplying v by h recovers its vertical displacement. Adding the corner of the crop rectangle then expresses the position relative to the photograph’s origin.

    x=x0+uw;y=y0+vh

    For P, the displacements are 3/4 × 400 = 300 px and 3/4 × 200 = 150 px. Each multiplication combines a dimensionless number with a dimension in pixels. We do not use W and H: u and v describe the cropped area.

    P=(100+300;50+150)=(400;200) px

    The scaled input dimensions do not appear in the final formula: their effect cancels when the output is already normalized by those dimensions, under our assumptions. This would not hold for coordinates supplied directly in pixels of the model’s input image.

  4. Convert the other landmarks and put the names in order

    For E, 1/4 × 400 = 100 px and 1/4 × 200 = 50 px. After adding the origin, E = (200 ; 100) px. For C, the normalized x-coordinate is the same as E’s; its final x-coordinate is therefore 200 px. Its y-coordinate is 50 + 3/4 × 200 = 200 px.

    The named internal output is therefore E = (200 ; 100) px, C = (200 ; 200) px, P = (400 ; 200) px. The segment from C to P is horizontal, with length 400 − 200 = 200 px. The segment from E to C is vertical, with length 200 − 100 = 100 px. The values can be compared in the same unit and coordinate system.

    The adapter has not decided that these positions are the correct joints. It has converted the positions proposed by the model. An identification error by the model would simply be carried through to this point.

  5. Check through another calculation route

    Now let us go through the pixels of the scaled image. The wrist is at 3/4 × 200 = 150 px and 3/4 × 100 = 75 px in that image. To return to the original crop rectangle, the horizontal ratio is 400/200 = 2 and the vertical ratio is 200/100 = 2. These ratios are dimensionless.

    We recover the displacements 150 × 2 = 300 px and 75 × 2 = 150 px, then the point (100 + 300 ; 50 + 150) = (400 ; 200) px. The result matches the direct route.

    Let us also check the direction: u = 0 would give the left edge x0, u = 1 the right edge x0 + w; the middle of the crop rectangle must fall halfway between these edges. For v, the same check concerns the top and bottom. These reference points check the origin and scale; merely checking that a point stays inside the photograph would be less demanding.

  6. Choose the internal output and keep the limits explicit

    Our geometry calculation expects pixels of the full image. If it expected normalized coordinates for that image, we would still have to divide x by W and y by H. For P: 400/800 = 1/2, or 0.50 dimensionless, and 200/600 = 1/3, exactly, dimensionless. We therefore could not pass it the initial pair (0.75 ; 0.75).

    If a landmark is missing, a value is non-finite or the version does not establish the order, the conversion does not provide a usable set: we return “landmarks unavailable” with the reason. We never replace a missing point with (0 ; 0), which is a possible actual position. Degenerate segments must still be rejected before calculating an angle, as in the previous unit.

    Checking the adaptation concerns the contract and the change of coordinate system. The accuracy of the predicted landmarks requires a separate comparison with suitable references. Our fictional calculations establish neither model performance, nor real measurement accuracy, nor any effect on the arm.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why we multiply by the crop dimensions, why we add its origin, then why reordering names does not improve the accuracy of predicted points. Recover the wrist position through both routes.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Normalized always means normalized by the full photograph.”

    Ask which image served as the model’s reference. Here, its pairs describe the scaled crop. Work through u × w, v × h, then add the corner of the crop rectangle. The order and units of the operations are justified in the example.

    Revisit the conversion and the check through scaled image pixels

  • “The first point can become E, since my calculation expects E first.”

    In our contract, the first point is P. Associate each pair with its original name before arranging E, C, P. If the version changes without a description of the order, reject the input rather than guessing a correspondence.

    Explicitly reorder the associations in the first exercise

  • “The point is inside the photograph, so the adaptation and model are validated.”

    The last exercise constructs an incorrect conversion that stays inside the photograph. A known reference point provides a better check of the coordinate system. Even correct adaptation does not prove that the model found the correct joint; these checks concern different things.

    Diagnose the plausible conversion in the last exercise

Exercise 2 · Independent calculation

The wrist changes position in the same image

In a fictional variant of the same photograph, keep exactly the same crop and scaling. Only the wrist prediction changes to u = 0.50 and v = 0.25, dimensionless. Calculate its coordinates in the full photograph. Check the x-coordinate separately using the middle of the crop rectangle.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The horizontal displacement is u times the width of the crop rectangle; then add the x-coordinate of its origin.

Hint 2

The horizontal midpoint lies halfway between 100 px and 500 px.

Compare with the solution

Horizontally, the displacement is 1/2 × 400 = 200 px. Relative to the photograph’s origin, x = 100 + 200 = 300 px. Vertically, y = 50 + 1/4 × 200 = 50 + 50 = 100 px. The new wrist position is therefore (300 ; 100) px.

Another check of x: (100 + 500)/2 = 300 px. This is indeed the horizontal midpoint of the crop. The y-coordinate lies a quarter of its height down from its top edge; it is not a quarter of the full photograph’s height.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the dimensions of the original crop.
  • Add both coordinates of its origin.
  • Give both final coordinates in pixels.
  • Check the x-coordinate using the edges of the crop rectangle, without repeating the same multiplication.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and limits of evidence

An incorrect conversion can stay within bounds

Return to the initial wrist position, u = v = 0.75. An adapter multiplies directly by the width and height of the full photograph and reports P = (600 ; 450) px. It checks only that the point stays inside the photograph. Explain the fault, calculate the differences from the correct conversion and propose a check that detects it. Then state what to return if the elbow is missing from the output.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate the difference from the wrist in the example, coordinate by coordinate.

Hint 2

Which corner in the photograph should the normalized corner (0 ; 0) correspond to?

Hint 3

Do a 0 coordinate and an unknown coordinate mean the same thing?

Compare with the solution

The program used the full image as its normalization reference: 3/4 × 800 = 600 px and 3/4 × 600 = 450 px. The correct conversion returns (400 ; 200) px. The signed differences are therefore 600 − 400 = 200 px horizontally and 450 − 200 = 250 px vertically. These are differences between two conversions of the same point, rather than a measurement of the model’s error on a real joint.

The incorrect point stays between the photograph’s edges: the stated check passes despite the fault. Yet it is outside our crop. A test of the local corner (0 ; 0) must return (100 ; 50) px; the faulty adapter would return (0 ; 0) px. This known case detects the lost origin. Testing the opposite corner must also recover (500 ; 250) px to check the scale.

If C is missing, return an indication that the segments depending on C are unavailable, naming the missing landmark. E and P may be kept as available points according to the internal contract, but no elbow angle is reported and no fictional C is created at the origin. Reading a solution or producing an answer after a hint still counts as an assisted answer.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify the wrong reference image and the lost origin.
  • Calculate the conversion differences in both directions, in pixels.
  • Explain why staying inside the photograph is not enough.
  • Propose reference points with expected positions and keep the missing elbow explicit.
  • Avoid treating the conversion diagnosis as validation of the model’s accuracy.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What information gives meaning to the pairs from an external model?

The version and landmark names, their order, the units, the origin and axis directions, the reference image and the transformations applied to that image. Rules for presence and validity are also part of the contract.

Recall 2How do you recover a point in the full image from an output normalized to the crop?

With no padding, rotation or reflection: multiply the normalized x-coordinate by the width of the original crop rectangle and the y-coordinate by its height, then add the coordinates of the rectangle’s top left corner. The final positions are in pixels of the full image.

Recall 3What check detects a forgotten crop origin?

The local corner (0 ; 0) must recover the corner of the crop rectangle, here (100 ; 50) px. An incorrect point can stay inside the photograph; this bounds check alone is insufficient.

Recall 4Why should you not replace a missing elbow with a 0 pair?

The pair (0 ; 0) denotes a position, whereas absence means that no usable position is available. Inventing this point could produce segments and an angle without a basis.

Recall 5What does a correct geometric conversion prove, and what remains to be checked?

It establishes the conversion of the supplied points under a given contract. The accuracy of the model’s landmarks, its actual use and the quality of a real measurement require their own evidence. Reading or giving an assisted answer does not establish an attained level.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the route from photograph to segment: name the image actually analysed, attach each pair to its landmark, reverse the scaling and cropping, state the final coordinate system, check against known positions, then preserve missing points as missing. Finally, explain why an exact conversion can carry through an incorrect prediction. Repeat this reasoning without help at least 2 days later; reading and immediate success are not enough to establish an attained level.
