Level 2 · C066·M4

Heteroscedasticity

Your goalIdentify spread that depends on batch type, measure it using residuals, and explain what it changes about uncertainty in a regression.

Before you start
c065-m5 : prediction interval · c050-m5 : conditioning · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c066-m1 : matrix OLS · c066-m2 : collinearity · c066-m3 : indicator variables

✿01 See the idea

In a packing workshop, small batches almost always take the expected time. Large batches sometimes take less time, and sometimes much more. Even when the mean time for each category is predicted correctly, the uncertainty margin need not be the same for both.

Heteroscedasticity means that the spread of errors varies with the characteristics used for prediction. Homoscedasticity is the opposite assumption: a common error variance. A higher mean for large batches is not enough to distinguish between them; we need to examine deviations around each mean.

Our scenario and all its times are fictional. We observe 6 batches, assumed to be independent, classified as small or large. The diagram already subtracts the group mean from each time: the points show residuals, in minutes. Two neighboring points at the same height represent equal times, not a change of category.

Variance is a probabilistic average of squared deviations from an expected value. Here it is expressed in square minutes, written min². A residual is a deviation calculated after fitting; it helps us examine the unknown error variance without itself being that variance.

The rule to remember

Examine the spread of deviations after accounting for the mean. An overall mean of squares can hide very different spreads across batches.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Compare deviations after fitting the means

Small batches: 9, 9, 11, 11 min. Large batches: 17, 23 min. Batch type is the only characteristic in this particular case of the linear model. We fit an intercept and an indicator variable, then describe the residuals; we calculate neither a significance test nor a confidence interval.

  1. Separate the mean time from the error

    For batch i, yᵢ is the time in minutes, and dᵢ is the known, unitless indicator for a large batch: 0 for small, 1 for large. The unknown parameters a and b are in minutes: a is the mean for small batches, and b is the difference in means between large and small batches. εᵢ denotes the random error around this mean, in minutes. The index i identifies an observation; n denotes the number of observations.

    yi=a+bdi+εi

    D denotes the set of fixed indicator values for the sample. We assume independent errors with finite variances and 0 conditional mean: E(εᵢ | D) = 0 min. E means expectation, that is, the mean under the probabilistic model; the bar | means “given.” The presence of both categories makes the intercept and indicator columns linearly independent here.

    Var denotes conditional variance. The notation σᵢ squared represents the variance of εᵢ, in min². Under homoscedasticity it is the same for all batches; under heteroscedasticity it can depend on the category. Neither normality nor equal variances are needed for the group means we are about to fit.

    Var(εi|D)=σi2

  2. Fit the mean for each category

    Ordinary least squares chooses the coefficients that minimize the sum of squared residuals. With an intercept and this indicator, this amounts to choosing each group mean separately. A hat distinguishes the fitted coefficient from the unknown parameter.

    Small batches: the 2 times of 9 min total 18 min; the 2 times of 11 min total 22 min. Their sum is 40 min, divided by 4 observations: the fitted mean is 10 min.

    Large batches: 17 + 23 = 40 min; 40 ÷ 2 = 20 min. Thus â = 10 min and b̂ = 20 − 10 = 10 min. The fitted time ŷᵢ is â + b̂dᵢ: 10 min for a small batch and 20 min for a large one.

  3. Calculate the residuals with their signs

    eᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ is the residual, in minutes. It is observable after fitting. The error εᵢ is relative to the true unknown mean: we cannot equate the two on the basis of these data alone.

    Small batches: 9 − 10 = -1 min, then 11 − 10 = 1 min; each value appears 2 times. Large batches: 17 − 20 = -3 min and 23 − 20 = 3 min.

    Check the centering: a pair of small-batch residuals totals 0 min; the large-batch pair totals 0 min. The mean residual is therefore 0 min in each group. This centering comes from fitting the means here: it does not prove the probabilistic assumption about εᵢ.

  4. Measure spread without letting signs cancel

    To describe the size of the residuals in group G, we use D_G, their mean square. G represents a category here, and n_G is its number of observations; the sum runs over observations i in that category. D_G is in min². Squaring prevents negative and positive residuals from canceling out.

    DG=1nG∑i∈Gei2

    For small batches, (-1) squared gives 1 min² and (1) squared gives 1 min². A pair contributes 2 min²; with the 2 pairs, the sum is 4 min². D_P = 4 ÷ 4 = 1 min², where P denotes small batches.

    For large batches, the squares are 9 and 9 min²: their sum is 18 min². D_G = 18 ÷ 2 = 9 min². Their ratio is 9 ÷ 1 = 9, with no unit. The mean residuals were equal, but their descriptive spreads are not.

    We divide by the number of observations to describe this sample. These D values are not unbiased estimators of the error variances: the means were fitted using the same observations. This ratio is not a test for heteroscedasticity.

  5. See what pooling hides

    Pooling the categories adds 4 + 18 = 22 min² of squares. The overall mean square is 22 ÷ 6 = 11/3 min², or approximately 3.67 min². It is a weighted average based on group sizes, not the simple average of the group D values.

    Check using another expression: (4 × 1 + 2 × 9) ÷ 6 = 11/3 min². This value is indeed between 1 and 9 min², but it loses the category information. Using it as a common spread would overestimate the descriptive spread of small batches and underestimate that of large batches.

    This overall number remains useful as a summary. The problem is using it to claim that the same uncertainty applies everywhere. It must not be confused with the usual estimate of a common variance, whose denominator accounts for the number of fitted coefficients.

  6. Distinguish coefficients from coefficient uncertainty

    Allowing different variances does not change the ordinary least squares criterion: our fitted means remain 10 and 20 min. With a correctly specified conditional mean and errors whose conditional mean is 0, heteroscedasticity alone does not bias these coefficients. However, the usual uncertainty formulas based on a common variance can become incorrect.

    A heteroscedasticity-robust covariance estimate estimates coefficient uncertainty while allowing the contribution of observations to vary. The covariance matrix is a table containing their variances and how their estimation errors vary together. In our parameterization, its entries are in min². Changing this covariance estimate while keeping the ordinary least squares fit does not move the coefficients.

    This uncertainty correction fixes neither an incorrect mean nor dependence between batches. Its usual justification concerns large samples; our small fictional dataset cannot guarantee an interval or a coverage probability. Calculating standard errors and using formal diagnostics belong to the next unit.

  7. State a diagnosis that the data can support

    The calculation establishes that the residuals in this sample have different spreads depending on batch type. This motivates examining the conditional variance of the errors; it does not by itself establish that this variance differs in the population. Even with a common variance, a small sample can display unequal spreads.

    Before drawing a conclusion, check that the mean uses the right characteristics, then examine whether the spread changes across more comparable observations. An omitted curvature can leave residuals that shift systematically; related batches can violate independence. The diagram is a starting point for these checks, not their result.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Large batches take longer, so they are heteroscedastic.”

    A difference in means is already represented by the indicator. First subtract the predicted mean time for each category, then compare the residual spreads. In our example, the signs average out in both categories, but the squares differ.

    Compare heights around the same 0

  • “The mean residual is 0, so the residuals have the same variance.”

    Cancellation of signs concerns centering. Revisit the squares: D_P is 1 min² and D_G is 9 min². They describe spread that the signed sum erased.

    Recalculate the squares by category

  • “A robust covariance estimate makes the regression correct in every case.”

    It targets coefficient uncertainty under specific assumptions. By itself, it does not change the fitted means, remove bias due to an omitted characteristic, or automatically handle dependence. Our sample guarantees no interval coverage.

    Reread the consequences and limitations

The batch finished early

Subtract the fitted time from the observed time.

Hint 2

Squaring multiplies the deviation by itself; both factors keep their signs.

Compare with the solution

The residual is 17 − 20 = -3 min: the batch finished earlier than predicted. Its square is (-3) × (-3) = 9 min².

The sign of the residual gives the direction of the deviation. Its square is positive; a variance does not become negative because an observation is below its mean. This single square does not measure the variance of all large batches.

The stopwatch switches to seconds

Hint 1

Each residual is multiplied by 60, so its square receives this factor twice.

Hint 2

In the ratio of the spreads, the same factor appears in the numerator and denominator.

Compare with the solution

Changing units does not change the relative difference in spread. The factor for the squares is 60 × 60 = 3600. Thus D_P becomes 1 × 3600 = 3600 s²; D_G becomes 9 × 3600 = 32400 s².

The ratio is 32400 ÷ 3600 = 9, as in minutes. Multiplying only by 60 would confuse a deviation with a squared deviation.

  • Conversion factor squared.
  • Results 3600 s² and 32400 s².
  • Unchanged ratio 9, with no unit and with a justification.

An overly reassuring summary

A colleague pools the residuals and announces: “The spread is 3.67 min² for all batches. The mean residual is 0, so the usual intervals must be valid. Otherwise, a robust covariance estimate will also correct the means.” Respond using the calculation by group, what remains unknown, and what should be examined next.

Hint 1

The overall summary keeps the squares but loses their category.

Hint 2

Separate three things: fitted mean, unknown error variance, and estimated coefficient uncertainty.

Compare with the solution

The overall summary is exactly 11/3 min², rounded to 3.67 min². Yet the mean squares by group are 1 and 9 min². The common summary hides this difference; 0 centering imposes no equality of spreads.

These residuals do not suffice to establish population error variances or the validity of the intervals. We need to examine the specified mean, the conditional spread across more comparable batches, and the independence of observations.

With the ordinary least squares fit unchanged, a robust covariance estimate changes the estimated coefficient uncertainty, not the fitted times of 10 and 20 min. If the mean is misspecified, it needs to be reexamined; covariance robustness does not fix it.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do we compare when discussing heteroscedasticity?

The variance of the errors conditional on the characteristics used for prediction. It varies with these characteristics. A difference in mean time between small and large batches is not enough.

Recall 2Why must we distinguish εᵢ from eᵢ?

εᵢ is the deviation from the true unknown mean; eᵢ is the deviation from the mean fitted using the sample. Residuals can be calculated, but they do not reveal the errors or their population variances exactly.

Recall 3In the workshop, why does a 0 mean residual fail to guarantee a common spread?

The signs cancel out in both groups. Yet their squares give 1 min² for small batches and 9 min² for large batches, a ratio of 9. These descriptive means are not a formal test.

Recall 4Does heteroscedasticity necessarily bias ordinary least squares coefficients?

No. With a correctly specified mean, errors with 0 conditional mean, and linearly independent predictor columns, it does not by itself create bias. It can invalidate an uncertainty estimate that assumes a common variance.

Recall 5What does a robust covariance estimate change, and what does it fail to fix?

With the ordinary least squares fit unchanged, it changes the estimated coefficient uncertainty. It fixes neither an incorrect mean nor, automatically, dependence, and it does not guarantee interval coverage for our small sample.
