Level 3 · C171·M3

Ito integral

Your goalConstruct a Brownian accumulation from coefficients chosen before each fluctuation, then define its limit in mean square.

Before you start
c170-m5 : Strong Markov property · c057-m5 : Hessian · c165-m5 : Duality · c171-m1 : Adapted integrands · c171-m2 : Isometry

✿01 See the idea

A laboratory sensor accumulates a voltage from a highly irregular fluctuation. Its sensitivity can change, but only using information already received. We must therefore set the sensor, let the fluctuation occur, multiply it by the setting held during that interval, then add this contribution to the running total.

In our fictional scene, the sensitivity is initially 2 mV/√s. After 1 s, it becomes 1 mV/√s if the cumulative fluctuation is positive or 0, and 3 mV/√s otherwise. The setting then stays unchanged until 2 s. These values are invented for the calculation; no real sensor was measured.

The fluctuation is modelled by Brownian motion. We observe its increments: the difference between the end and the start of an interval. Multiplying a setting by this difference gives a contribution in millivolts, positive or negative. We do not use an instantaneous velocity of Brownian motion.

The new idea is to extend this procedure to sensitivities that do not remain piecewise constant. We approximate them by step settings, then require the mean of the squared error between the accumulations to tend to 0. A single plotted path does not prove this convergence.

The rule to remember

Decide before the increment; sum the products; to take a limit, control a mean square error rather than relying on an impression from a sample path.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What the sensor accumulated and what the model predicts

The time horizon T is 2 s. The fictional readings are W at the start = 0 √s, W at 1 s = 1/2 √s, W at T = -1/4 √s. Let us apply the sensor rule, then define what the integral means when the setting becomes more general.

  1. Define the objects and units

    Ω denotes the set of possible outcomes and P their probability law; E is the average under this law. The outcome ω represents a realisation. The filtration Ft is the information available up to time t, measured in seconds. Wt is a standard Brownian motion with respect to this filtration: it starts at 0, and its increments are independent of the past, Gaussian, with mean 0 and variance equal to the elapsed time.

    With this convention, W is measured in √s. Ht is the sensitivity in mV/√s and It the accumulation in mV. The horizon T is deterministic and finite. We first work with a step process H: on each interval (tᵢ, tᵢ+1], its value is ξᵢ, a square-integrable random variable known from F at time tᵢ. The partition is deterministic. Values at the partition points do not change the integral.

    For the extension, our chosen setting is the closure of these step processes under the expected time integral of the square. In other words, we explicitly assume that a sequence of adapted step processes approximates H in this sense, with E[∫ H² dt] finite over the horizon T, in mV². The statement “H depends on the past” alone does not replace this assumption or integrability.

  2. Define the integral of a step setting

    n is the number of intervals; i runs through them. With t at the start equal to 0 and t at the end equal to T, the definition is a finite sum of products. ΔWᵢ denotes W at the end minus W at the start of the same interval.

    IT(H):=∫0THtdWt:=∑i=0n−1ξi(Wti+1−Wti)

    The symbol dW here denotes the Brownian integrator; it requires no derivative of W. We multiply by ΔW, rather than by the duration Δt. Duration enters the variances, since E[(ΔW)²] = Δt, in seconds.

  3. Choose the coefficient without looking ahead

    The first interval lasts 1 s and uses ξ at the start = 2 mV/√s. At time 1 s, the reading is 1/2 √s, so it is greater than 0 √s. The second sensitivity is therefore 1 mV/√s, held for 1 s.

    This second coefficient is random before the intermediate reading; it becomes known at that reading. The rule is therefore adapted. Looking at W at 2 s to choose this coefficient would use information that is not yet available.

  4. Form the signed increments

    We subtract successive positions, because only the fluctuation experienced during each setting should contribute. First increment: 1/2 − 0 = 1/2 √s. Second increment: -1/4 − 1/2 = -3/4 √s.

    The second increment has a negative sign: the sensor loses part of what it had accumulated. Check the data: 1/2 + (-3/4) = -1/4 √s, the difference between the final reading and the start.

  5. Multiply, then add the contributions

    The first contribution is 2 × 1/2 = 1 mV. The second is 1 × (-3/4) = -3/4 mV. The √s cancel with those in the denominators of the sensitivities.

    IT(H)=1+(-3/4)=14 mV=0.25 mV

    This is exactly the integral of the step process defined by the rule, evaluated on these readings. It is not an approximation of this particular integral. It does not give the integral of another setting that would vary between readings.

  6. Check using another expression

    On this branch, expanding the products gives 2 W at the intermediate reading + 1 (final W − intermediate W). The net coefficient of the intermediate reading is 2 − 1 = 1 mV/√s. We therefore obtain 1/2 + (-1/4) = 1/4 mV, as with the sum of contributions.

    Another check is to subdivide each interval without changing its setting: the intermediate differences cancel in pairs. The refinement exercise makes this cancellation explicit. Independence from the partition for the same step process avoids defining several integrals for a single setting.

  7. Separate realisation, energy and variance

    The isometry from the previous unit states E[It(H)²] = E[∫ H² dt], with the same time bounds. Also, E[It(H)] = 0 mV: each future increment has conditional mean 0 given the coefficient already known. The second moment is therefore the variance here. We do not assume that the coefficient chosen at the intermediate time is independent of the first increment.

    W at time 1 s has a non-degenerate Gaussian distribution with mean 0. Each of its signs has probability 1/2; the probability of being exactly 0 is 0. The mean square of the second sensitivity is (1 + 9)/2 = 5 mV²/s. Hence the variance: 4 × 1 + 5 × 1 = 9 mV².

    For the observed branch alone, the time integral of H² dt is 4 × 1 + 1 × 1 = 5 mV². This realised energy is not the average over all branches. The result 1/4 mV, its square, the energy 5 mV² and the variance 9 mV² answer different questions.

  8. Extend from step processes to the general integral

    Hⁿ is the nth adapted step process in an approximation; here n indexes the sequence rather than the intervals of its partition. We require E[∫ |Hⁿ − H|² dt] → 0 mV² over the fixed horizon. The space L² of square-integrable random variables is complete: every Cauchy sequence in it has a limit. Being Cauchy here means that the mean of the squared difference between the terms with indices n and m tends to 0 as both indices grow independently.

    E[|IT(Hn)−IT(Hm)|2]=E[∫0T|Hn−Hm|2dt]→0

    This equality follows from linearity and the isometry. The right side is bounded above by 2 times the approximation error of Hⁿ, plus 2 times that of Hᵐ: both tend to 0 as n and m grow. The integrals therefore form a Cauchy sequence in L². Their limit defines the terminal integral of H, denoted in the formula by I with subscript T.

    Two sequences approximating the same H give the same limit, because the same bound applied to their differences makes their distance in L² vanish. Uniqueness is up to almost sure equality: the random variables may differ on a set of probability 0. The isometry and the 0 mean pass to the limit. This construction does not promise convergence for every sample path or for every choice of evaluation points.

    The construction using step processes and its extension are cross-checked against David Gamarnik’s notes for the MIT course on advanced stochastic processes, in the sessions on constructing and defining the integral. The references consulted are recorded in the report; the scene and its calculations were created for this unit.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain when each sensitivity is chosen, why the second difference is negative, then why the isometry lets us define a limit independent of the sequence of step processes. Do not use the single sample path to justify a theoretical average.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I choose the sensitivity using the value at the end of the same interval.”

    Choose ξ using the information at the start, then hold it until the end. The rule at the intermediate time uses W already observed; it does not consult final W. A choice that looks at this future lies outside the framework constructed here.

    Review the order of decisions

  • “I multiply H by the duration to find the accumulated voltage.”

    The contribution along a sample path is H × ΔW, in mV. H² × Δt enters the quadratic energy, in mV². Review the units before choosing the operation.

    Review increments and contributions

  • “My curve looks stable when I refine the partition: the general integral is proved.”

    Check that the settings approach the same H in the expected time integral of the squared difference. The isometry turns this control into L² convergence of the accumulations. A stable result on a single realisation does not prove this probabilistic control.

    Establish an approximation certificate

Exercise 1 · Direct application

The intermediate reading becomes negative

The same sensor follows the same rule, but the fictional readings become W at the start = 0 √s, W at 1 s = -1/2 √s, W at 2 s = 1/4 √s. Choose the second sensitivity, then calculate both contributions and the accumulation.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The sign at 1 s selects the branch; it does not give the sign of the next increment.

Hint 2

Subtract the negative reading from the final reading before multiplying.

Compare with the solution

The second sensitivity is 3 mV/√s, because the intermediate reading is negative. The increments are -1/2 √s then 1/4 − (-1/2) = 3/4 √s.

The contributions are 2 × (-1/2) = -1 mV and 3 × 3/4 = 9/4 mV. Their sum is 5/4 mV, or 1.25 mV. The probabilistic rule stays the same: another realisation does not change its theoretical variance 9 mV².

Check your reasoning

  • Choose the branch using only the intermediate reading.
  • Subtract a negative value correctly.
  • Give signed contributions and a sum in mV.

Exercise 2 · Algebraic check

Observe more often without changing the setting

Return to the realisation in the example. Add W at 1/2 s = 1/4 √s and W at 1 + 1/2 s = 0 √s. Keep the sensitivity 2 until 1 s, then 1 until 2 s. Calculate the contributions on the four subintervals and explain why the result stays the same.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

An added observation is not a decision to change the setting.

Hint 2

On each original interval, add the two increments: the middle value should disappear.

Compare with the solution

The successive increments are 1/4, 1/4, -1/2, -1/4, in √s. We multiply the first two by 2 and the last two by 1: 1/2, 1/2, -1/2, -1/4, in mV.

The contributions from the first interval total 1 mV; those from the second, -3/4 mV. Overall: 1/4 mV. Algebraically, (middle W − starting W) + (ending W − middle W) = ending W − starting W. This cancellation works because the coefficient stays the same on both subintervals.

If you recalculated H at each new reading, you would define a different setting. The equality would no longer follow from this simple cancellation; you would need to examine the new process.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the stated coefficients despite the new observations.
  • Obtain 1/4 mV.
  • Justify the equality by cancellation, then identify what would make it fail.

Exercise 3 · Construction and proof

Prove that a small setting error vanishes

For the same sensor, write a = 1 mV/√s and define Hⁿ = H + a/n over the entire horizon T = 2 s, where n is a positive integer. Iⁿ denotes the integral of Hⁿ. Find Iⁿ − I and its mean square. On the readings from the example, calculate Iⁿ for n = 2. Is the value on this sample path what proves convergence?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Use linearity and the fact that integrating a constant coefficient multiplies final W minus W at the start by that coefficient.

Hint 2

For the mean of the square, use E[final W²] = T, then let n grow.

Compare with the solution

The difference in sensitivities is the constant coefficient a/n, known from the start. By linearity, Iⁿ − I = (a/n)(final W − starting W) = a final W/n, because W starts at 0.

E[|In−I|2]=a2Tn2→0

For n = 2, the shift on our readings is -1/4/2 = -1/8 mV; the approximating accumulation is 1/4 + (-1/8) = 1/8 mV. Yet the mean square error over all outcomes is 1/2 mV² for this n.

Convergence is proved by the formula a²T/n², which tends to 0. The particular observed value is not enough. Here each Hⁿ is already an adapted step process; taking the limit concerns its sensitivities and does not require new time points.

Check your reasoning

  • Define the constant coefficient of the difference and its units.
  • Find the exact difference and 1/8 mV for n = 2.
  • Prove convergence in L² using an average over the outcomes.
  • Do not treat an assisted answer or a calculation for a single case as an attained level.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do we define the Ito integral of a step setting?

Multiply each coefficient known at the start by the Brownian increment of its interval, then add. For a fixed step process, this is the exact definition, rather than an approximation of its own integral.

Recall 2Which units distinguish contribution and energy in our sensor?

H is in mV/√s and ΔW in √s: H ΔW is in mV. By contrast, H² Δt is in mV². Duration is used in the quadratic calculation; it does not replace the observed increment.

Recall 3Why does the energy of the observed branch not directly give the variance?

The chosen sensitivity is random. We must average the energy H² dt over all branches. In our model the integral has mean 0; its second moment, equal to this average energy, is its variance.

Recall 4Which property turns an approximation of the sensitivities into a definition of the integral?

The isometry turns the expected time integral of the squared difference between sensitivities into a mean square error between integrals. Completeness of L² provides a limit; the same control proves that it does not depend on the chosen sequence of step processes.

Recall 5Does refining the observations always preserve the integral?

If the same coefficient stays applied to every part of an interval, the increments recombine exactly. If the extra observations change the sensitivity rule, the integrand changes too: a new analysis is needed.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without support: describe the sensor with its units; fix each coefficient before the fluctuation; form the differences and their contributions; separate the observed result from the variance; use the isometry and completeness to explain the limit.
