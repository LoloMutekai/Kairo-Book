Complex numbers

Your goalRepresent an arrow in the plane by a complex number, then calculate and check a quarter turn combined with a reduction in length.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

On a dial marked with a grid, an arrow starts at the centre O. Its tip P is 3 divisions to the right and 4 upwards. A command must turn it a quarter turn counterclockwise, then divide its length by 2. How can we keep direction and length together in the calculation?

The first drawing keeps the horizontal and vertical displacements separate. The second shows what happens to them: what pointed right now points up; what pointed up now points left. The reduction then brings the tip closer to the centre without changing its new direction.

A complex number combines these displacements in a form we can calculate with. Adding a complex number will move the tip; multiplying by a complex number can turn the arrow and change its length. We will build this distinction without taking the word “imaginary” to mean an uncertain reading.

The dial and its values are fictional. The axes are perpendicular, have the same scale, and upwards is positive. The coordinates count divisions and have no physical unit; lengths will be given in grid units. We are modelling neither a motor nor the motion of a real device.

The rule to remember

Write the components, expand the product by replacing i squared with minus one, then check length and direction separately.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The dial command: turn and shorten

The initial arrow is (3 ; 4). We want a quarter turn to the left and a length divided by 2. Let us find its final complex form, then check the drawing with another calculation.

  1. Define the number that represents the arrow

    No square of a real number is negative. We introduce a new number i defined by i × i = -1. A complex number is written z = a + ib, where a and b are real numbers; a is its real part and b its imaginary part. The imaginary part is the coefficient b, not the term ib. Two complex numbers are equal when their real parts are equal and their imaginary parts are also equal.

    i2=-1
    z=a+ib↔(a;b)

    Here, a = 3 and b = 4, so z = 3 + 4i. The letters z, a and b denote numbers with no physical unit; i is also dimensionless. The complex plane is this coordinate system, with the real part on the horizontal axis and the imaginary part on the vertical axis. A real number is the case b = 0.

  2. Connect this form to length and angle

    The modulus |z| is the length of the arrow starting at O. In our coordinate system with the same scale on both axes, Pythagoras’ theorem gives |z| = √(a × a + b × b). We take the nonnegative square root. Here, a × a = 9, b × b = 16, their sum is 25, and 5 × 5 = 25: the modulus is therefore 5 grid units.

    |z|=a2+b2

    For a nonzero arrow, r denotes its modulus and θ its angle measured counterclockwise from the direction pointing right, in radians. Such an angle is called an argument of z; adding a full turn describes the same arrow. The known projections give a = r cos θ and b = r sin θ, hence the trigonometric form:

    z=r(cosθ+isinθ)

    In this scene, cos θ = 3/5 = 0.60 and sin θ = 4/5 = 0.80, both dimensionless. We do not need to calculate θ. The 0 complex number has 0 modulus but no direction: we do not assign it an argument.

  3. Distinguish displacement from rotation

    Addition works component by component: (a + ib) + (c + id) = (a + c) + i(b + d), where c and d are the real-valued components of another complex number. Adding i, whose components are (0 ; 1), would only move the tip one division upwards.

    To multiply, expand as in algebra and replace each i × i with -1. With c and d real and dimensionless here:

    (a+ib)(c+id)=(ac−bd)+i(ad+bc)

    Indeed, expansion gives ac + iad + ibc + (i × i)bd; the last term becomes −bd. Multiplying by i alone gives i(a + ib) = −b + ia, or (a ; b) → (−b ; a). Right becomes up, and up becomes left, with the same length: this is the required quarter turn about O.

  4. Choose the factor and work out the products

    After the turn, dividing each component by 2 keeps the direction and divides the length by 2. The whole command is therefore multiplication by q = i/2 = 0 + 1/2i. Its real part c is 0 and its imaginary part d is 1/2. The factor q is dimensionless.

    Let us expand zq. Real product ac: 3 × 0 = 0. Product bd: 4 × 1/2 = 2. The term (i × i)bd therefore contributes −2 to the real part. Product ad: 3 × 1/2 = 3/2. Product bc: 4 × 0 = 0; these last two products are the coefficients of i.

  5. Assemble the final components

    The real part becomes ac − bd = 0 − 2 = -2. The imaginary part becomes ad + bc = 3/2 + 0 = 3/2. Write z′ for the complex number after the command, with the prime meaning “after”:

    z′=(3+4i)i2=-2+32i

    The final tip Q is therefore (-2 ; 3/2), or (-2 ; 1.5) in French decimal notation. It is to the left of and above O, as expected. The components still count divisions.

  6. Check using the drawing, the length and the inverse operation

    Another approach: the turn alone sends (3 ; 4) to (-4 ; 3), then division by 2 gives (-2 ; 3/2) directly. This geometric calculation agrees with the expansion.

    Length: the square of the final modulus is 4 + 9/4 = 25/4. Since 5/2 × 5/2 = 25/4, the positive length is 5/2 = 2.5 grid units. The modulus of q is 1/2, and its square is 1/4: 25 × 1/4 = 25/4, the same squared length. We verify this in this case without assuming that addition would have this effect.

    Check by reversing the operation: multiplying the final vertical coordinate by 2 recovers 3, and multiplying the final horizontal coordinate by −2 recovers 4. The centre has stayed at O. The correct length alone does not guarantee the correct direction: the last exercise will show this limitation.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The imaginary part of z is 4i.”

    In z = a + ib, the imaginary part is the real number b: here 4. The full imaginary term is ib. Write the pair (a ; b) to separate the coefficient from the symbol i.

    Review the definition of the components

  • “i squared is 1, so the result stays on the right.”

    The definition is i × i = -1. In the product, i × ib = −b; this sign is what turns up into left. Go through the expansion again before grouping the components.

    Review the expansion of the product

  • “Adding i and multiplying by i both turn the arrow.”

    Adding i changes only the vertical coordinate. Multiplying by i swaps the components and changes one sign: (a ; b) becomes (−b ; a). Draw both tips from the same centre, then compare their lengths.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · critique a check

Is the correct length enough?

For the single command in the example, someone proposes w = 2 + 3/2i. They calculate the correct squared length, 25/4, and declare the answer verified. Explain why this check passes, then fix the answer. Give a direction check that distinguishes the two tips.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Changing the sign of a coordinate does not change its square. Now work out which side of O the tip must reach.

Hint 2

To undo the command, multiply the final vertical coordinate by 2 and the final horizontal coordinate by −2. Do you recover the initial coordinates?

Compare with the solution

The horizontal sign has been lost: w is to the right of O, whereas the required quarter turn places the tip on the left. The square of 2 is the same as the square of -2, namely 4. The other component is unchanged, so the squared length remains 25/4.

The corrected answer is z′ = -2 + 3/2i, obtained by keeping i × i = -1. The geometric check (a ; b) → (−b ; a), followed by division by 2, determines both the sign and the length.

A numerical check repeats the inverse operation from the example. With the correct answer, 2 × 3/2 = 3 and −2 × (-2) = 4: we recover the initial arrow. With w, the first calculation still gives 3, but the second gives −2 × 2 = −4, instead of 4. This check detects the lost sign.

The correct length does not determine a direction. The mapping (a ; b) → (−b ; a), followed by the reduction, determines the components and the direction of the quarter turn. Recovering the initial components provides a separate check from calculating the squares.

Check your reasoning

  • You explain why squaring removes the sign without invalidating the length calculation.
  • You fix the horizontal sign and connect this repair to the square of i.
  • You check the direction of rotation using the components and show why reversing the operation from w fails.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In z = a + ib, what do a, b and i represent?

a and b are real numbers: a is the real part and b the imaginary part. The number i is defined by i × i = -1. In the plane, z corresponds to the pair (a ; b). The term ib is not the imaginary part b.

Recall 2How can we recover length and direction from z = a + ib?

The modulus |z| = √(a × a + b × b) is the length in a coordinate system with the same scale on the perpendicular axes. For nonzero z, an argument θ indicates its direction: z = r(cos θ + i sin θ), with r = |z|. The 0 complex number has no direction.

Recall 3Where does the minus sign in the product (a + ib)(c + id) come from?

The term (ib)(id) equals (i × i)bd = −bd. After expansion, the product is (ac − bd) + i(ad + bc). We group the real part and the coefficient of i separately.

Recall 4Why does multiplying by i turn the arrow, while adding i moves it?

i(a + ib) = −b + ia transforms (a ; b) into (−b ; a), a quarter turn counterclockwise. Adding i only changes b into b + 1. The centre O remains the starting point of the represented arrow.

Recall 5Why is the correct modulus not enough to verify our command?

The modulus does not determine direction: changing the sign of a component preserves its square. We must also check the quarter turn and its direction using the components. Recovering the initial arrow provides another separate check.
