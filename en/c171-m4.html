Level 3 · C171·M4

Ito's formula

Your goalTransform a scalar process using Ito's formula, justify the curvature term, and distinguish a continuous identity, an expectation, and a calculation over a finite step.

Before you start
c170-m5 : Strong Markov property · c057-m5 : Hessian · c165-m5 : Duality · c171-m1 : Adapted integrands · c171-m2 : Isometry · c171-m3 : Ito integral

✿01 See the idea

A bead agitated in a liquid moves horizontally around a reference point. We track its signed coordinate X, in centimetres, and the square of its distance from the reference point, Y. The jolts have no preferred direction. Is that enough to keep the mean of Y constant?

Imagine a small displacement ε to the right, then its opposite to the left, from the same position x. In the square of the new position, the contributions proportional to ε have opposite signs. The contribution ε × ε is positive in both cases. Agitation can therefore increase the mean of the square without changing the mean position.

For smooth motion, the squares of small displacements vanish when we sum over increasingly fine steps. For Brownian motion, their sum retains a limit. Ito's formula adds precisely the effect of these squares to the chain rule for differentiation.

The bead and all the values on this page are fictitious. The model is continuous and one-dimensional, with no walls; it does not claim to describe every microscopic collision. We study a scalar transformation, without solving a family of stochastic equations or moving to the multidimensional case.

The rule to remember

Before transforming noisy motion, identify the slope, curvature, and quadratic variation. Centred agitation can leave a drift term after a curved transformation.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The square of the distance of an agitated bead

The bead starts at x = 3 cm. Its agitation has coefficient σ = 2 cm per square root of a second, with no position drift. Over T = 1 s, let us calculate the mean of the square of its distance, then check what happens to the reasoning over a finite step.

  1. Specify the motion and the available information

    t denotes time, in seconds; T is the fixed, positive final duration. W is a standard Brownian motion, with W initially equal to 0. Here W has units of the square root of a second. Over a duration h, its increment ΔW is independent of the past, with mean 0 and variance h. E denotes expectation, meaning the mean under the probabilistic model.

    Xt=x+σWt;Yt=Xt2

    X is the bead's signed coordinate, in cm. x is its deterministic initial value; σ is the constant agitation coefficient, in cm per square root of a second. Y is in cm squared. The filtration describes the information available over time; W is Brownian with respect to this information. An adapted process uses only information already available. These concepts and the Ito integral were introduced in the prerequisites.

  2. See why the square of the small displacement remains

    For any step, ΔX is the displacement and ΔY is the change in the square. The following expansion is an exact algebraic identity, with no approximation:

    ΔY=(X+ΔX)2−X2=2XΔX+(ΔX)2

    On a partition of time, we sum these identities using X at the start of each step, as for the Ito integral. The quadratic variation [X] is the limit in probability of the sums of squared increments as the maximum step size tends to 0. For X = x + σW, it is [X] at t = σ squared × t. It is not a variance calculated from a list of positions.

    The usual notation “(dW) squared = dt” summarises this limit. It does not mean that (ΔW) squared = h over every finite step. The ordinary chain rule would lose a term whose sum does not vanish here.

  3. State the scalar formula and its assumptions

    More generally, write dX = a dt + b dW. a is the drift, in cm per second; b is the noise coefficient, in cm per square root of a second. On the interval from 0 to T, assume a and b are continuous and adapted, with the time integrals of |a| and of b squared finite almost surely. This defines X through the time integral and the Ito integral. “Almost surely” allows an exception with probability 0.

    f(t,x) transforms time and position into a quantity with unit U. It is deterministic, 1 times continuously differentiable in time and 2 times in position: f_t, f_x, and f_xx are its continuous partial derivatives. Their units are U per second, U per cm, and U per cm squared. The subscripts t and x indicate the variable of differentiation, rather than a numerical value.

    df(t,Xt)=(ft+atfx+bt22fxx)dt+btfxdWt

    All derivatives on this line are evaluated at (t, X at t). The notation d is shorthand for an identity between integrals, valid almost surely. It is not a time derivative of W. The last term is an Ito integral; the term with half of b squared multiplies time, even when the drift a is 0.

    The curvature term comes from the quadratic variation d[X] = b squared dt. Quadratic terms containing dt vanish in the limit. The argument using the square explains why this correction is needed; by itself, it does not prove the theorem for every function f. The multidimensional version belongs to the next unit.

  4. Calculate slope, curvature, and correction for the square

    Our function is f(t,x) = x squared, independent of time: f_t = 0, f_x = 2x, and f_xx = 2. Here U is cm squared; f_x is in cm and f_xx is dimensionless. At the initial position, the slope is 2 × 3 = 6 cm. The drift a is 0 cm per second and b = σ.

    dYt=12×σ2×2dt+2σXtdWt

    The half and the curvature cancel: 1/2 × 2 = 1. Then σ squared = 2 × 2 = 4 cm squared per second. The drift coefficient of Y is therefore 4 cm squared per second. We must square σ because it multiplies the displacement before that displacement is squared.

  5. Integrate exactly, then take the mean

    s is a time variable inside the integrals. The integrated form retains X at every time s in the random term:

    YT=x2+σ2T+2σ∫0TXsdWs

    The integral has units of cm times the square root of a second. After multiplication by 2σ, all terms are in cm squared. To take the expectation of the Ito term as 0, we need square integrability, rather than adaptation alone. It holds here: over a finite duration, X = x + σW has a finite second moment whose time integral is finite.

    The initial square is 3 × 3 = 9 cm squared. The mean increase is 4 × 1 = 4 cm squared. Thus E[Y at T] = 9 + 4 = 13 cm squared. The mean of X at T remains 3 cm; the square of this mean is only 9 cm squared.

  6. Check by another method

    Expand X at T = x + σW at T directly before taking the expectation. Squaring gives x squared + 2xσW at T + σ squared × (W at T) squared. Now E[W at T] = 0 and E[(W at T) squared] = T. The cross term therefore has mean 0; the last term has mean σ squared × T.

    This method recovers 9 + 4 × 1 = 13 cm squared without using Ito's formula. It also confirms that Var(X at T), the mean squared deviation of X from its mean, is 4 cm squared. The equality between this variance and [X] at T depends on the present model; these objects have different definitions.

  7. Distinguish the finite step from the continuous identity

    From the starting point, take a fictitious step h = 0.25 s and the fixed increment ΔW = -0.25 square root of a second. The exact displacement is ΔX = 2 × (-1/4) = -1/2 cm. The final position of this step is 3 + (-1/2) = 2.50 cm, and its square is 25/4 cm squared, or 6.25 cm squared.

    The algebraic expansion gives 6 × (-1/2) = -3 cm squared for the linear part, and (-1/2) × (-1/2) = 1/4 cm squared for the square of the displacement. The sum is -11/4 cm squared. Direct check: 25/4 − 9 = -11/4 cm squared.

    If we freeze X at its initial value in the differential formula, we obtain only an approximate calculation: -3 + 4 × 1/4 = -2 cm squared. The difference from the exact change is 3/4 cm squared. The exact Ito formula has not replaced the integral of X by x × ΔW; this additional approximation explains the difference.

    In this example, the square of ΔX is 1/4 cm squared, whereas σ squared × h is 1 cm squared. They differ on this fixed step, although their means are equal if ΔW follows the Brownian distribution for the step.

  8. Check the units and the scope of the result

    If σ is 0, the motion in our model becomes constant and the mean increase vanishes. If we track an affine function of X, its curvature is 0 and the Ito correction also vanishes. A different transformation can have negative curvature: the positive sign found for the square is not universal.

    The mean 13 cm squared exceeds the square 9 cm squared of the mean position, as required here by a nonnegative variance. This check does not say that Y increases on every sample path or that a real bead follows our model. The last exercise makes this distinction explicit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The noise is centred, so the ordinary chain rule is enough.”

    An increment with mean 0 can have a square with positive mean. Start again from the exact expansion of (X + ΔX) squared. The sum of the squares retains the quadratic variation and supplies half of b squared times the curvature.

    Review the square of the displacement and the scalar formula

  • “I can replace the square of every ΔW by the step duration.”

    Quadratic variation describes a limit of sums over fine partitions, rather than each fixed increment. Compare the exact change and the approximate calculation over the fictitious step; an integral with varying X does not become exactly x times ΔW.

    Review the finite step calculation

  • “The drift of the square is positive, so the bead moves farther away every time.”

    The drift gives a contribution over time; the Ito integral fluctuates and can outweigh it in the opposite direction. Compare a mean with the value on a sample path, then work through the final case in the transfer exercise.

Exercise 1 · Application

Stronger agitation, the same bead

The initial position and duration stay the same, but σ becomes 3 cm per square root of a second. Calculate the drift of the square and E[Y at T]. Justify squaring σ and give the units.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The function being observed is still the square: its curvature does not change.

Hint 2

Square the agitation coefficient before multiplying by the duration.

Compare with the solution

The curvature is still 2 and the half cancels it. The drift is therefore σ squared = 3 × 3 = 9 cm squared per second.

The mean increase over 1 s is 9 cm squared. Adding the initial square 9 cm squared gives 18 cm squared. The mean position remains 3 cm: changing the dispersion adds no drift to X.

Check your reasoning

  • Square σ and justify this operation using the square of the displacements.
  • Distinguish the drift per second from the increase over the entire duration.
  • Add quantities in cm squared.

Exercise 2 · Written diagnostic

The right sign, the wrong claim of exactness

With the initial parameters, return to h = 0.25 s and ΔW = -0.25 square root of a second. Someone claims that ΔY is exactly -2 cm squared because they wrote 2xσΔW + σ squared × h. Calculate the exact change and identify the unjustified step.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate X at the end of the step, then its square.

Hint 2

The integrated formula contains X at every instant. Replacing it with its value at the start of the step is an additional operation.

Compare with the solution

ΔX = -1/2 cm, so final X = 2.50 cm and final Y = 6.25 cm squared. The exact change is 25/4 − 9 = -11/4 cm squared.

The exact expansion also gives -3 + 1/4 = -11/4 cm squared. The claimed value -2 differs by 3/4 cm squared. The person froze the integrand X in the Ito integral, then treated the approximation as an equality.

The problem does not refute Ito's formula: it refutes using it as an identity over a finite step with frozen coefficients. The square of a particular increment is not its Brownian expectation.

Check your reasoning

  • Find the exact change by calculating the final square.
  • Identify freezing the integrand as an approximation.
  • Distinguish the observed square of an increment from its expectation.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and checking

A square below its starting value

At T = 1 s, suppose the total Brownian increment on a fictitious sample path is W at T = -0.50 square root of a second, with the initial parameters. Calculate X at T, Y at T, and the integral of X dW from 0 to T using the integrated formula. Does this case contradict the calculated mean?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The final position is x + σW at T.

Hint 2

Isolate the integral after subtracting the initial square and the term σ squared × T.

Compare with the solution

The total displacement is 2 × (-1/2) = -1 cm. Thus X at T = 2 cm, Y at T = 4 cm squared, and the change is -5 cm squared.

Let J denote the integral of X dW, in cm times the square root of a second. The identity gives 4 = 9 + 4 + 4J, in the corresponding units. Thus 4J = -9 cm squared and J = -9/4 cm times the square root of a second, or -2.25 cm times the square root of a second.

Check: 9 + 4 + 4 × (-9/4) = 4 cm squared. The random contribution is -9 cm squared and outweighs the positive term in the opposite direction. A sample path with final Y = 4 can coexist with E[final Y] = 13; an expectation does not predict the value on every sample path.

We used a special property of the square of an affine Brownian motion: here the integrated identity determines J from the endpoint. We cannot conclude that every stochastic integral can be calculated using only the starting and ending points.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain the position and then the final square, with their units.
  • Isolate J while retaining the quadratic variation term.
  • Recover the final square by substitution and distinguish a sample path from an expectation.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why does the Ito chain rule have a curvature term?

The squares of Brownian increments have a sum that does not vanish: the quadratic variation. The second-order expansion therefore leaves half of b squared times f_xx, multiplied by dt.

Recall 2Recall the contributions to df(t,X) when dX = a dt + b dW, and their assumptions.

The time part is f_t + a f_x + (b squared / 2) f_xx; the Ito part is b f_x dW. The derivatives are evaluated at the current time and position. f has the required continuous derivatives; the adapted coefficients satisfy the integrability conditions of the model. Subsequently taking the mean of the integral to be 0 requires square integrability.

Recall 3For X = x + σW, reconstruct the integrated identity for the square and its mean.

Y at T = x squared + σ squared × T + 2σ times the integral of X dW. This integral has mean 0 in our model with a finite second moment. E[Y at T] = x squared + σ squared × T; this differs from the square of E[X at T].

Recall 4What does the symbolic square of dW equal to dt mean, and what does it not allow?

It summarises the limit of sums of squared increments over partitions whose maximum step size tends to 0. It does not allow us to impose (ΔW) squared = h on a particular increment. Freezing the integrand over a finite step adds an approximation.

Recall 5Why does a positive drift of Y not ensure that it increases on every sample path?

The Ito integral can give a negative contribution stronger than the positive contribution over time. The mean of the square increases in our model, but a realisation of this square can decrease.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without aids: start from the square of a displacement, explain quadratic variation, write the contributions from time, slope, and curvature, then integrate for the bead. Recover the mean by direct expansion. Finally, explain why neither a particular sample path nor a step with a frozen integrand can be equated with this mean or with the continuous identity.

What you understand
The square of a fluctuation can leave a positive mean contribution. Quadratic variation sums squared increments in a time limit; an empirical variance describes the dispersion of a list around its mean. Recognising a square is not enough to identify Ito's formula.
