Level 2 · C022·M2

Identity and permissions

Your goalDecide whether an identified person may perform a specific action on a logbook, separating proof of identity from granted permission.

Before you start
c021-m5 : origin, authentication and authorization · c018-m5 : contract tests and test doubles · c012-m5 : counterexamples · c022-m1 : asset, threat and scope

✿01 See the idea

In a workshop, Lea consults the digital repair logbook. The application has recognized her as Lea. She opens a record, then asks to edit one of its instructions. The logbook gatekeeper must still check this second action: recognizing Lea does not, by itself, give her permission to change the logbook.

Identity answers “who is asking?”. An identifier is the label for an account; authentication is the check that links the request to that account. Writing “I am Lea” in a request does not perform this check. In our scene, we assume that a verified session provides this link; we do not build the mechanism that proves it.

A permission answers “may this person perform this action on this object?”. Authorization consists of consulting the applicable rule and making the decision. Reading and editing are different actions, even when they concern the same logbook and the same person.

The scene, accounts and permissions are fictional. The register maintained by the person in charge of the workshop grants Lea permission to read the logbook, without granting her permission to edit it. Here, it is the complete, fixed list of permissions; the text Lea sends cannot change this list. A different permission or a new request for consent belongs to a separate step.

The rule to remember

Link the request to a verified identity, then look up the permission for the exact triple: person, action, resource.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Lea wants to read, then edit the logbook

The verified session identifies Lea. The register grants this account permission to read the workshop logbook and no permission to edit it. Let us decide separately for these 2 requests, without performing the actions in a real application.

  1. Specify the person, action and object

    p denotes the account of the person whose identity must be checked; a denotes the requested action; r denotes the target resource, here the workshop logbook. These letters name objects, not physical quantities. The triple (p, a, r) keeps them in order: the person performs the action on the resource.

    D denotes the register of authorized triples. In the scene, D = {(Lea, read, workshop logbook)}. Parentheses group the elements of a triple; braces group the entries in the register. The register is complete within our scope, with no inherited role, exception or hidden rule. It stays unchanged while the decisions are made.

  2. Encode proof of identity and permission separately

    I(p) is 1 if the session check links this request to account p; otherwise it is 0. Lea’s session therefore gives I(Lea) = 1. This value comes from the session check, which we assume to be reliable, not from a “name” field filled in within the message.

    d(p, a, r) is 1 when the triple is in D, and 0 otherwise. The symbol ∈ means “belongs to”. Thus, (Lea, read, workshop logbook) ∈ D gives d = 1. The triple with edit does not belong to D: d = 0. Looking for “Lea” alone in the register would lose the action and the object.

    I and d are indicators with no unit, limited to 0 or 1. They are neither probabilities nor trust scores. We assume that the identity check is reliable and that the register is supplied by the workshop authority; the formula alone cannot make these assumptions true.

  3. Require the conditions together

    A(p, a, r) is the authorization decision in this model: 1 means allowed, 0 means denied. A also has no unit. We require a verified identity AND permission for the exact triple.

    A(p,a,r)=I(p)×d(p,a,r)

    Why multiply? With these codes, only 1 × 1 gives 1. If a condition is 0, the product is 0. Adding would not represent this AND: the presence of one condition could then hide the absence of the other. The formula describes a decision, not the execution of the action.

    In a program, if identity is not established, the check may stop before consulting permissions. Writing the product does not require this lookup to be performed despite the failure.

  4. Calculate authorization to read

    Keep p = Lea and r = workshop logbook, then set a = read. The session evidence gives 1; the corresponding register entry gives 1. Multiply to require the conditions together.

    Alecture=1×1=1

    Result: reading is allowed under our rule. This does not mean that the logbook has already been read: the decision authorizes the action; it is not evidence that the action occurred.

  5. Change only the action

    The new request keeps the same person and resource, but a becomes edit. Identity remains verified; permission must be looked up for this new triple. It is not granted.

    Amodification=1×0=0

    Result: editing denied. This denial does not mean that Lea was incorrectly identified; it means that being recognized as Lea does not give her this permission. Permission to read has not been extended to another action.

  6. Check by returning to the register

    Another way to check: hide the products and go through the requested actions. For Lea, the register contains read on the logbook; it does not contain edit on the logbook. With her verified session, reading is exactly the request we find to be allowed.

    Count this small batch: 1 + 0 = 1 authorization; 2 − 1 = 1 denial. These numbers count fictional decisions, with no physical unit. They measure neither completed accesses nor the effectiveness of a system.

    Cross-check: remove the session evidence while keeping the name Lea on the read request. I drops to 0. Even though the register still grants reading to Lea’s account, 0 × 1 = 0: no permission can be exercised under this account without a verified link.

Hide the calculations. Explain why the read and edit requests have different outcomes despite the same identity. Then describe what is missing when the message contains only the name Lea.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “She is logged in, so she can edit.”

    The session answers the identity question. Next, reconstruct the triple with edit and consult the corresponding permission: it is missing from our register. Proof of identity does not create a permission.

    Repeat the calculation with edit as the action

  • “The name Lea is written in the request: identity is proven.”

    A typed name is a claim. Look for the check that links the request to the account, here the verified session. If you have only the name, I is 0, even if this account has read permission.

    Recall the difference between an identifier and authentication

  • “The register contains Lea and the logbook: the action does not matter.”

    Rewrite the complete entry with the action between the person and the resource. The read entry does not answer an edit request. Comparing isolated names loses the scope of the permission.

    Test this confusion using the logbook requests

The gatekeeper’s shortcut

Hint 1

Compare with the solution

The shortcut sees I = 1 and allows editing. The complete rule keeps d = 0, because the edit triple is not in D. It gives A = 1 × 0 = 0, hence a denial.

The shortcut has removed the permission tied to the action on the resource. The edit request is a counterexample: identity is correctly verified, but the action is not authorized. To fix the rule, restore the lookup for the exact triple after the session check.

Hint 1

Hint 2

Compare with the solution

The register still grants reading to Lea’s account: d = 1. But nothing links this request to that account. For the claimed identity Lea, I = 0. The name copied into the message does not replace this proof.

The logical result is A = 0 × 1 = 0, hence denial. The check may stop as soon as identity verification fails; the calculation explains why a known permission does not rescue the request.

The missing evidence is the result of an authentication check that links this request to the account. Adding a permission to the register would not produce this evidence. Conversely, restoring a verified session would allow only the actions already granted to this account.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 0 while justifying the factor linked to identity.
  • Require a verified link between the request and the account, without inventing proof or extending permissions.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why is the name Lea in a message not enough to identify its author?

The name is a claim. Authentication must link the request to the account; in our scene, this link comes from a verified session.

Recall 2What information must be kept together to check Lea’s permission on the logbook?

The person, action and resource: (Lea, read, workshop logbook). Replacing read with edit changes the triple, so a new permission check is required.

Recall 3Why does A = I × d represent the rule in this scene?

I and d are each 0 or 1, with no unit. The product is 1 only if identity is verified and the exact permission is granted. These codes are not probabilities.

Recall 4Recall a case where identity is verified but the request is denied.

Lea requests to edit the logbook. Her session gives I = 1, but permission is missing: d = 0, so A = 0.

Recall 5What does an allowed result prove, and what evidence remains separate?

It gives the decision under our assumptions of a reliable session and a complete register. It does not demonstrate that reading was carried out or that a real application correctly applies this model.
