Independent and paired comparisons

Your goalChoose a comparison based on how the data were collected, calculate differences within pairs, and explain which measure of spread should accompany their mean.

Before you start
c055-m5 : confidence interval · c056-m1 : hypotheses · c056-m2 : decision errors · c056-m3 : power

✿01 See the idea

A library is trying guides A and B for locating books. The time needed to find a book is recorded. Someone who knows the shelves well may be quick with both guides; someone else may be slow with both. To compare the guides, should we preserve this link between the times?

If the same readers try A and B, each reader provides a pair: their times belong together. A paired comparison starts with the difference between them. The reader labels P, Q, and R are fictional; in the diagram, each line connects measurements from the same person and does not represent a time sequence.

Another way to collect the data would give A to one group and B to other readers, with no planned matching between them. We would call these independent groups if the observations from individuals were independent, both within and between groups. Equal group sizes do not create pairs. Readers who whisper answers to each other do not satisfy this assumption.

All times on this page are fictional and expressed in seconds, written s. The diagrams contrast possible designs for collecting data in the same library. The main calculation uses the same 3 readers with A and B. We are preparing to choose a comparison; we do not calculate a decision probability or a standardized effect size.

The rule to remember

Choose according to how the data were collected: linked measurements require you to preserve the pairs; independent groups are compared using their means and their own measures of spread. The most favourable numerical result must never determine the design.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Keep each reader in their own comparison

P records A = 20 s and B = 18 s; Q records A = 30 s and B = 27 s; R records A = 40 s and B = 36 s. Each person carries out searches of comparable difficulty with guides A and B. These fictional data are complete. Let us calculate B − A, its mean, and then the appropriate measure of spread.

  1. Define what belongs together

    The index i identifies a reader, from 1 to n; n = 3 is the number of pairs and has no physical unit. Aᵢ and Bᵢ are that reader’s times in seconds. We define dᵢ = Bᵢ − Aᵢ, also in seconds. A negative difference means the search with B was shorter. A bar over a letter will denote its arithmetic mean.

    Observations from the same reader may be linked. However, we assume that readers are independent of one another and follow the same protocol. Repeating measurements on the same reader does not create new independent readers. Pairing can also be planned between different people according to a rule set before the results; here, the link is simply the person’s identity.

    di=Bi−Ai

  2. Subtract within pairs

    For P: 18 − 20 = -2 s. For Q: 27 − 30 = -3 s. For R: 36 − 40 = -4 s. Each subtraction compares a person with themselves; their general speed is no longer a difference between people that could be confused with a difference between guides.

    We have 6 raw times, but 3 differences from independent readers. For the paired mean, the sample size is therefore n = 3. Do not divide the sum of the differences by the number of times.

  3. Average the differences

    Each reader receives the same weight. The symbol ∑ instructs us to add the terms dᵢ for all the indicated readers. The sum is -2 + (-3) + (-4) = -9 s; we divide it by 3 pairs.

    d¯=∑i=1ndin=-93=-3 s

    For these fictional readers alone, the time with B is shorter by an average of 3 s. This describes the table. The population mean, meaning the mean difference expected for the readers targeted by the study, remains unknown; the hypothesis of no difference would concern this mean being equal to 0 s.

  4. Check using the column means

    Column A totals 20 + 30 + 40 = 90 s, so its mean is 30 s. Column B totals 18 + 27 + 36 = 81 s, so its mean is 27 s. Their difference is 27 − 30 = -3 s: it agrees with our mean of the differences.

    d¯=B¯−A¯

    This identity follows from distributing the sum across the subtraction: all pairs are complete and have the same weight. It is an arithmetic check. It does not prove that measurements have been correctly linked to people: permuting B would also leave its mean and sum unchanged.

  5. Measure the spread of the differences

    The sample variance v_d measures the spread of the dᵢ around their mean; it is expressed in seconds squared. We square the deviations to prevent their signs from cancelling out. We divide by n − 1: after estimating the mean, the sum of the deviations is constrained to 0, and only n − 1 deviations are free to vary. This is the sample variance used here to estimate the population variance.

    vd=∑i=1n(di−d¯)2n−1

    Around -3 s, the deviations are 1 s, 0 s, and -1 s. Their squares are 1, 0, and 1 seconds squared. Their sum is 2 seconds squared. The denominator is 3 − 1 = 2, giving v_d = 2/2 = 1 second squared.

    The standard error, labelled ES in the formulas, estimates the spread of the mean if data collection were repeated with new readers under the same design. This differs from the spread of individual differences. For independent differences with the same variance, its square is v_d/n; ES itself is the square root and is expressed in seconds.

    ES2=vdn=13 s2≈0.333 s2

    Check the units: a variance remains in seconds squared after division by a sample size with no physical unit. Its square root, rather than this square, would be used in a margin expressed in seconds. We keep the exact fraction 1/3 second squared to avoid rounding during the calculation.

  6. Recognize what independent data collection would change

    Now suppose the lists in the second diagram really come from different people: A from P, Q, R; B from U, V, W. msoehl dnqqxn iqbuin bfemvv brydsd giefzd fzvq The difference between the means remains -3 s, but no individual difference Bᵢ − Aᵢ then represents a change for the same reader.

    Let v_A and v_B denote the groups’ sample variances, in seconds squared. In A, the deviations from 30 s are −10, 0, and 10 s. The sum of their squares divided by 2 gives v_A = 100 seconds squared. In B, the deviations from 27 s are −9, 0, and 9 s; the same calculation gives v_B = 81 seconds squared.

    ES2=vAnA+vBnB

    When the groups are independent, the estimated variances of their means add. Here: 100/3 + 81/3 = 181/3 = 181/3 seconds squared, or approximately 60.33 seconds squared. This rule allows the groups to have different variances.

    The two designs describe the same mean difference with different measures of spread. In our main data collection, people who are slow with A remain slow with B: their differences vary little. This situation explains the value of the individual link; pairing does not automatically improve precision in every study. In particular, erasing the names does not make the independent formula correct for paired data.

  7. Keep the conclusion within what the evidence supports

    To proceed to a test of means, a paired t test would work on the list of differences; a Welch test would compare independent groups using their separate measures of spread. These names refer to decision methods, not ways to create pairs after seeing the results. With such a small sample, it is particularly necessary to justify a suitable distribution model: normally distributed differences for the paired t test, and appropriate group distributions for the Welch approximation. The table alone does not verify these assumptions.

    Even with the same people, order can matter: repeating a search may give the reader practice or cause fatigue. A protocol can vary the order of the conditions and use comparable searches. The individual link alone does not remove these influences. Our descriptive calculation therefore proves neither a causal effect of B nor a difference in the population.

    If a time is missing, preserve the identity of each row and define how incomplete pairs will be handled; do not shift the remaining values. Data collection and assumptions come before the formula. The next unit will address effect size; it does not replace this choice of design.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The columns have the same length, so I can pair them.”

    Look for the link planned when collecting the data: the same person, or a pair defined in advance by a relevant rule. Position in a table and sorting the times do not create this link. Without it, you have no individual change to interpret.

    Compare the links in the two diagrams

  • “I have 6 recorded times, so I divide the sum of the differences by 6.”

    Each difference uses the A and B times from the same reader. This leaves 3 differences, not 6 independent people. Divide their sum -9 s by 3; then use their variance to estimate the spread of the mean.

    Review the pair count and variance

  • “The means are unchanged after my sorting, so my pairs are correct.”

    A permutation preserves the column means but changes the differences and their spread. Check the matching by identifier as well as the arithmetic equality. The final exercise shows what a check of the means can miss.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What feature of data collection distinguishes a paired comparison from a comparison of independent groups?

Pairing relies on a planned link between observations, here the same reader with A and B. Independent groups have no such matching and assume independent observations. Equal sample sizes do not establish a link.

Recall 2What do we calculate first with the same readers under A and B, and what does n count?

We calculate dᵢ = Bᵢ − Aᵢ for each pair, in seconds, then average these differences. n counts pairs that are independent of one another; times from the same person do not each represent an independent person.

Recall 3Which measure of spread do we use for the paired mean, and which for the difference between independent means?

The square of the paired standard error is the sample variance of the differences divided by the number of pairs. For independent groups, add each group’s sample variance divided by its sample size. These squares are in seconds squared for times measured in seconds; their square roots are in seconds.

Recall 4Why does equality between the mean of the differences and the difference of the means fail to validate the pairs?

With complete columns of the same size and equal weights, this equality holds even after permuting a column. You must also check the matching by identifier; the spread can change while the means remain identical.

Recall 5Why does a favourable paired result alone fail to demonstrate a causal effect of the guides?

Repetition, order, search difficulty, or fatigue can also explain the differences. The protocol must address these influences and justify the assumptions for inference. A descriptive mean alone demonstrates neither causality nor a population difference.
