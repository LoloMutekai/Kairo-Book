Monotone and dominated convergence

Your goalChoose between monotone convergence and dominated convergence to interchange a limit and an integral, check every assumption for the sequence xⁿ on the interval from 0 to 1, then reject the interchange when an assumption is missing.

✿01 See the idea

The whole course uses the same setting: the interval from 0 to 1, equipped with Lebesgue measure, and the sequence of functions fn(x) = xⁿ. For each x strictly less than 1, xⁿ approaches 0 as n increases; at x equal to 1, the value stays 1. The curves approach the horizontal axis, except very close to the right endpoint.

The question concerns an interchange: does the area under fn approach the area under the limit function? In other words, do we obtain the same number by first taking the limit in n and then integrating, or by first integrating and then taking the limit? The calculation below will give a positive answer for this sequence. This is not a general rule.

A counterexample shows this. Take rectangular pulses kn of height n, supported on an interval of width equal to the reciprocal of n, next to x equal to 0. Their area stays equal to 1. Yet at each strictly positive point x, the pulse eventually lies to the left of x and the value falls back to 0. The limit is the 0 function, whose area is also 0: one unit of area has disappeared in the limit.

Two conditions allow the interchange. With monotone convergence, the functions are nonnegative and keep increasing: nothing can disappear along the way. With dominated convergence, the same integrable function g bounds all the functions in absolute value: nothing can escape to infinity. These are sufficient conditions, stated below; their proofs are not repeated in this unit.

Everything that follows is an exact mathematical exercise: the functions are chosen for study, and no measured data are involved.

The rule to remember

Before interchanging a limit and an integral: check measurability, almost everywhere convergence, then either nonnegativity and increasing behavior (monotone convergence theorem), or a common integrable bound for all terms (dominated convergence theorem).

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Interchanging a limit and an integral for xⁿ on the interval from 0 to 1

On X, the interval from 0 to 1, with Lebesgue measure λ, we study three sequences: fₙ(x) = xⁿ, hₙ(x) = 1 − xⁿ and the rectangular pulse kₙ from the introduction. For each, decide whether the integral of the limit is the limit of the integrals, and which theorem justifies this. The calculations are exact.

  1. Specify the setting, symbols and assumptions

    X is the underlying set, and λ is Lebesgue measure: an interval with endpoints a and b has measure b − a. Thus λ(X) = 1 − 0 = 1. A single point has measure 0. “Almost everywhere” means “except on a set of measure 0”, such as the point with coordinate 1.

    A measurable function f is integrable if the integral of |f| is finite; ∫ f dλ is then its integral, a number without a unit here, since the functions are dimensionless. The continuous functions and step functions in this setting are measurable. Assumption for this lesson: for a continuous function on X, the Lebesgue integral equals the integral calculated using an antiderivative. If this connection has not yet been established in your studies, accept it as a given here.

  2. State the two theorems

    Monotone convergence theorem. Let fn be measurable functions with 0 ≤ f1 ≤ f2 ≤ … almost everywhere and fn tending to f almost everywhere. Then the integral of f is the limit of the integrals of fn, and this limit may be +∞.

    0≤f1≤f2≤…, fn→f p.p.⟹∫fdλ=limn→∞∫fndλ

    Dominated convergence theorem. Let fn be measurable functions tending to f almost everywhere, and let g be a measurable function, the same for all n, with |fn| ≤ g almost everywhere and ∫ g dλ finite. Then f is integrable and the integral of fn tends to that of f.

    |fn|≤g p.p., ∫gdλ<+∞⟹limn→∞∫fndλ=∫fdλ

    In both cases, check the assumptions one by one; the conclusion is not an assumption. Neither theorem states a rate of convergence.

  3. Choose the theorem for fₙ(x) = xⁿ

    Each fn is continuous, hence measurable. It is nonnegative, but it decreases: the next term is no greater than the previous one at every point of X, so the sequence is not increasing and the monotone convergence theorem, as stated, does not apply. Look for an upper bound: for x in X, 0 ≤ xⁿ ≤ 1. Take g to be the constant function equal to 1, the same for all n. Its integral is 1 × λ(X) = 1, hence finite.

    Limit: for x strictly less than 1, xⁿ tends to 0; at x equal to 1, fn is 1 for all n. The limit f is therefore 0 everywhere except at a single point of measure 0: f is 0 almost everywhere. Check at x equal to 1/2: f at index 4 is 1/16 and f at index 9 is 1/512, two values smaller than 1 that approach 0.

  4. Calculate the integrals of xⁿ

    An antiderivative of xⁿ is the next power of x divided by n + 1, which gives, between 0 and 1, the value 1 divided by n + 1.

    ∫xndλ=1n+1

    For n equal to 4: 4 + 1 = 5, so the integral is 1/5. For n equal to 9: 9 + 1 = 10, giving 1/10. For n equal to 99: 99 + 1 = 100, giving 1/100.

    Check: lower and upper Riemann sums with equal step sizes bound each of these values, and their difference is exactly the step size (verified in calculs.py). The numbers 1/5, 1/10 and 1/100 do decrease.

  5. Conclude using the dominated convergence theorem

    The three assumptions hold: measurability, almost everywhere convergence to the 0 function, and a bound g equal to 1, integrable and common to all n. Thus the limit of the integrals is the integral of the limit. On the left, 1/5, 1/10, 1/100 approach 0; on the right, the integral of the 0 function is 0 × 1 = 0.

    Connection with uniform convergence: the supremum of the distances between xⁿ and the limit on the interval closed at 0 and open at 1 stays equal to 1 for all n, because xⁿ approaches 1 near the right endpoint. The convergence is therefore not uniform, yet the interchange is valid: domination does not require uniform convergence.

  6. Increasing version: hₙ = 1 − xⁿ and the monotone convergence theorem

    hn is continuous, hence measurable, and 0 ≤ hn ≤ 1. Since xⁿ decreases as n increases, hn increases: at x equal to 1/2, h at index 4 is 1 − 1/16 = 15/16 and h at index 9 is 1 − 1/512 = 511/512, with 15/16 < 511/512 < 1. The limit is 1 except at x equal to 1, where every hn is 0: it equals 1 almost everywhere, with integral 1.

    Integrals: 1 − 1/5 = 4/5; 1 − 1/10 = 9/10; 1 − 1/100 = 99/100. They increase to 1, exactly as the monotone convergence theorem predicts.

    Check: hn + fn = 1 everywhere, so 4/5 + 1/5 = 1 and 99/100 + 1/100 = 1, the area of X. At index 99, the remaining gap to the limit is 1/100.

  7. See the interchange fail for the rectangular pulses kₙ

    kn equals n on the interval open at 0 and closed at the reciprocal of n, and 0 elsewhere. It is a step function: its integral is height × width. For n equal to 4: 4 × 1/4 = 1. The same equality holds for all n.

    Limit: if x is strictly positive, kn(x) equals 0 as soon as n exceeds the reciprocal of x. The limit is the 0 function, with integral 0, whereas the integrals equal 1: the difference is 1. The interchange is false.

    Which assumption is missing? If an integrable upper bound g existed, the dominated convergence theorem would give a limit of the integrals equal to 0; however, it equals 1. By contraposition, no integrable upper bound exists. Increasing behavior is also missing: on the interval from 1/3 to 1/2, k at index 2 equals 2 and k at index 3 equals 0, on a set of nonzero measure.

    Numerical check: the pointwise maximum of the first four rectangular pulses has integral 1/2 + 1/3 + 1/4 + 1 = 25/12, greater than 1; any upper bound must be at least as large as this envelope. This integral increases with the number of pulses: it is the harmonic sum, whose unboundedness is a standard fact accepted here. A single example is enough to refute a general implication, as in the unit on counterexamples.

Explain it in your own words

Why does xⁿ fail to meet the assumptions of the monotone convergence theorem while 1 − xⁿ meets them? Which assumption of the dominated convergence theorem does the rectangular pulse kₙ violate, and how do you know without looking for an explicit upper bound?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If fₙ tends to f at every point, then the integral of fₙ tends to that of f.”

    The rectangular pulse kₙ converges to 0 at every point, but its integral stays 1. An additional assumption is needed: nonnegativity with increasing behavior, or an integrable upper bound common to all n.

    Review the rectangular pulse counterexample in the last step

  • “For xⁿ, which is nonnegative and converges, the monotone convergence theorem applies.”

    The monotone convergence theorem requires an increasing sequence. xⁿ decreases: use the bound g equal to 1, or switch to 1 − xⁿ, which increases and whose integrals 4/5, 9/10, 99/100 rise to 1.

    Review the choice of theorem for xⁿ

  • “Without uniform convergence, a limit and an integral cannot be interchanged.”

    On the interval from 0 to 1, xⁿ does not converge uniformly (the supremum of the distances stays 1), yet the interchange is valid by domination. Uniform convergence on a set of finite measure is sufficient, but not necessary; domination is another sufficient condition.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

With u = xⁿ, d u equals gₙ(x) dx, and the endpoints 0 and 1 remain 0 and 1.

Hint 2

If the interchange failed, which theorem would be ruled out by contraposition?

Hint 3

At x equal to 1/2, calculate g₂, then g₃, and compare.

Compare with the solution

With u = xⁿ, d u = gn(x) dx. As x goes from 0 to 1, u also goes from 0 to 1. The integral therefore equals that of the constant 1 between 0 and 1, giving 1 − 0 = 1. Check for n equal to 3: g3(x) = three times the square of x has the cube of x as an antiderivative, so the value is the cube of 1 minus the cube of 0 = 1.

Almost everywhere limit: 0, with integral 0. However, the limit of the integrals is 1. The interchange fails: by contraposition of the dominated convergence theorem, no common integrable upper bound exists. The dominated convergence theorem does not apply.

Monotonicity: g2(x) = 2 x, so g2 at 1/2 equals 2 × 1/2 = 1; g3 at 1/2 equals 3 × 1/4 = 3/4. Since 1 > 3/4 and these functions are continuous, g2 > g3 on a neighborhood of 1/2, of nonzero measure: the sequence is not increasing, so the monotone convergence theorem does not apply either. Neither sufficient condition holds; this is not a general proof, only an assessment of this sequence.

Check your reasoning

  • The endpoints for u are recalculated and the result is 1.
  • Contraposition is used in the correct direction.
  • Failure to be increasing is justified on a set of nonzero measure, rather than at a single point.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What assumptions does the monotone convergence theorem require, and what does it conclude?

Measurable functions that are nonnegative and increasing almost everywhere, with limit f almost everywhere. Then the integral of f is the limit of the integrals, possibly infinite.

Recall 2What assumptions does the dominated convergence theorem require?

Measurable functions that converge almost everywhere to f, and the same function g, independent of n, with |fₙ| ≤ g almost everywhere and the integral of g finite. Then f is integrable and the integrals of fₙ tend to that of f.

Recall 3Why does the rectangular pulse kₙ of height n fail to allow the interchange?

It converges to 0 almost everywhere, but its integral stays 1. By contraposition of the dominated convergence theorem, no common integrable upper bound exists; the sequence is not increasing either.

Recall 4Is uniform convergence necessary to interchange a limit and an integral?

No. xⁿ converges nonuniformly on the interval from 0 to 1, and the interchange is valid by domination. On a set of finite measure, uniform convergence is sufficient, but it is only a sufficient condition.

Recall 5What does “almost everywhere” mean, and why does the point with coordinate 1 cause no problem for xⁿ?

Except on a set of measure 0. The point with coordinate 1 has measure 0, so the limit of xⁿ, which is 0 everywhere else, is 0 almost everywhere.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Specify the measure space and the sequence; check measurability and the almost everywhere limit; look for either nonnegativity and increasing behavior (monotone convergence theorem), or a single integrable upper bound independent of n (dominated convergence theorem); calculate the integrals; compare the limit of the integrals with the integral of the limit; if neither set of assumptions holds, do not interchange them, and look for a counterexample such as the rectangular pulse kₙ.
