Green

Your goalConstruct the Green's function for a rod with fixed end temperatures, then use superposition to obtain the temperature due to several point heaters.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A thin metal rod, 4 m long, has both ends held at the same reference temperature. Two small devices each heat a tiny region: one at 1 m from the left end, the other at 3 m. Once nothing changes over time, what temperature is measured at each point? All values in this scene are fictional: they are used for calculation, not to describe a real rod.

We could solve the problem for this arrangement, then start again whenever a heater is moved or its power changes. The Green's function avoids starting again. We solve the case of a heater of unit intensity at any point s just once; its response, written G(x, s), gives the temperature at x. Any arrangement can then be reconstructed by adding these responses, each multiplied by the intensity of its heater.

This addition is possible because the law is linear. Let u(x) be the temperature difference from the reference, in kelvins (K), and f(x) the heat input per unit length, scaled by the conductivity, in K/m². In the steady state, u satisfies −u″ = f, where u″ is the second derivative of u with respect to x. Doubling the sources doubles the difference; adding two sets of sources adds the differences, provided that the ends remain at 0 difference. We also assume a uniform material and conduction only along the rod.

A tiny heater can be described without new notation. If its width w is small and f equals q/w across that width, the intensity q is the integral of f over the heater, in K/m. Integrating −u″ = f across the heater gives a drop in the slope of u equal to q, while u remains continuous. Outside the heaters, f is 0, so u″ is 0: u is a straight line. The required profile is a piecewise linear curve, with a kink at each heater.

For the unit response, G is therefore 0 at both ends, straight on each side of s, continuous at s, and its slope drops by one at s. These conditions determine it completely; the example constructs it.

The rule to remember

The temperature is the sum, over the heaters, of their intensity multiplied by the unit response G(x, s): a piecewise linear curve that is 0 at the ends, continuous at s, and whose slope drops by one at s.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Two heaters on a rod of length 4 m

A fictional rod of length L = 4 m has 0 temperature difference at its ends. A heater of intensity q₁ = 8 K/m is placed at s₁ = 1 m, and a second of intensity q₂ = 4 K/m at s₂ = 3 m. Let us calculate the temperature difference u at the probe x = 2 m using the Green's function, then check the entire profile.

  1. Define the quantities and assumptions

    x is the position along the rod, in metres, measured from the left end; L is the length. u(x) is the temperature difference from the reference, in K; f(x) is the source, in K/m². The problem is as follows.

    −u″(x)=f(x),u(0)=0,u(L)=0

    Each heater is characterised by a position s in m and an intensity q in K/m. We assume a steady state, conduction only along the rod, a uniform material, and heaters thin enough to be treated as points. Outside these assumptions, the construction below must be reconsidered.

  2. Express a heater as a kink in the slope

    Across a heater of width w, f equals q/w. Integrate −u″ = f from s − w/2 to s + w/2: the integral of −u″ is the slope before minus the slope after, and the integral of f equals q. As w becomes small, u remains continuous and its slope drops by q.

    u′(s+)−u′(s−)=−q

    Outside the heaters, u″ = 0: u is affine between two heaters and between a heater and an end. The units agree: a slope is expressed in K/m, like q.

  3. Construct G for unit intensity

    Place a single heater of unit intensity at s. The response G is expressed in metres, since u = q G gives K = (K/m) × m. To the left of s, G is a straight line that is 0 at x = 0: G = a x. To the right, it is a straight line that is 0 at x = L: G = b (L − x). Continuity at s requires a s = b (L − s). The slope is a on the left and −b on the right: a drop of one requires a + b = 1. These two equations give the following solution.

    a=L−sL et b=sL
    G(x,s)=x(L−s)L pour x ≤ s
    G(x,s)=s(L−x)L pour x ≥ s

    Check: at x = s, both formulas give s (L − s)/L. Since a and b are positive, G increases up to s, then decreases: G(s, s) is its maximum as x varies. Swapping x and s leaves the value unchanged: G(x, s) = G(s, x). The response at x to a source at s equals the response at s to a source at x. This reciprocity can be proved by two integrations by parts, the one-dimensional version of Green's theorem from the prerequisites; here we observe it in the formula without proving it.

  4. Calculate the slopes of each unit response

    Heater 1, at s1 = 1 m: a1 = (L − s1)/L = 3/4 = 0.75 and b1 = s1/L = 1/4 = 0.25. These slopes have no units, since both G and x are in metres. Their sum is 1, as required.

    Heater 2, at s2 = 3 m: a2 = (L − s2)/L = 1/4 = 0.25. The slope b2 is not needed for this probe.

  5. Evaluate each response at the probe and superpose

    The probe is at x = 2 m. For heater 1, x ≥ s1: use the right branch, G = b1 (L − x), with L − x = 2 m, so G = 0.25 × 2 = 0.5 m. For heater 2, x ≤ s2: use the left branch, G = a2 x = 0.25 × 2 = 0.5 m. The branch depends on the probe's position relative to each heater, not on the heater's number.

    By linearity, the temperature is the sum of the responses multiplied by the intensities.

    u(x)=q1G(x,s1)+q2G(x,s2)
    u(2)=8×0.5+4×0.5=4+2=6 K

    The units check out: (K/m) × m = K. The addition assumes that both responses share the same end conditions and that the state is steady.

  6. Check the entire profile

    Values of G at the heaters. G(s1, s1) = a1 s1 = 0.75 × 1 = 0.75 m. G(s2, s1) = b1 (L − s2) = 0.25 × 1 = 0.25 m. G(s1, s2) = a2 s1 = 0.25 × 1 = 0.25 m: this is the same value as G(s2, s1), obtained by a different calculation, which confirms reciprocity in this example. G(s2, s2) = a2 s2 = 0.25 × 3 = 0.75 m.

    Thus u(s1) = 8 × 0.75 + 4 × 0.25 = 6 + 1 = 7 K, and u(s2) = 8 × 0.25 + 4 × 0.75 = 2 + 3 = 5 K. The profile is therefore 0 at x = 0, 7 K at 1 m, 6 K at 2 m, 5 K at 3 m, then 0 at 4 m.

    Checking the slopes. The temperature rises at 7 K/m up to s1, then falls at 1 K/m between s1 and s2 (a drop of 2 K over 2 m), then at 5 K/m up to the end. The slope change at s1 has magnitude 7 + 1 = 8 K/m, equal to q1; at s2, its magnitude is 5 − 1 = 4 K/m, equal to q2.

    A second method for the probe: the straight line between s1 and s2 has midpoint value (7 + 5)/2 = 12/2 = 6 K, identical to superposition. This check validates the calculation within its model; it does not prove that the model represents a real rod.

Explain it in your own words

Why is knowing G for a source of unit intensity enough for two sources of different intensities? Which property of the equation is used, and why must the slope drop by exactly the intensity at a heater?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “G(x, s) is already the temperature produced by my heaters.”

    G responds to unit intensity and is expressed in metres. The temperature in kelvins is obtained by multiplying each response by its intensity in K/m, then adding: at the probe, 4 K + 2 K = 6 K, rather than 0.5 m + 0.5 m.

    Revisit superposition

  • “It is enough for G to be 0 at both ends.”

    0 values at both ends leave both slopes free. Continuity at s and a slope drop of one are also needed: two equations for two slopes. For a heater at 1 m on a rod of length 4 m, they give 0.75 and 0.25, with sum 1.

    Review the conditions on G

  • “The left formula holds for every x.”

    It holds only for x ≤ s. At the probe x = 2 m, with the heater at 1 m, it would give 2 × 0.75 = 1.5 m, greater than G(s, s) = 0.75 m, which is the maximum of G as x varies: this is impossible. Always compare x and s before choosing the branch.

    Practise choosing the branch again

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Switch off a heater

In the rod from the example, heater 2 is switched off and heater 1 is kept on (q₁ = 8 K/m at s₁ = 1 m). Calculate the temperature difference at x = 2 m, then at x = 3 m, and state how the difference at x = 2 m changes compared with the rod with two heaters.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Only one response needs to be multiplied by q₁; the slopes a₁ and b₁ from the example still apply.

Hint 2

For both probes, the heater is to the left of the probe: choose the right branch, G = b₁ (L − x).

Compare with the solution

At x = 2 m: G = 0.5 m, so u = 8 × 0.5 = 4 K. At x = 3 m: G = b1 (L − s2) = 0.25 × 1 = 0.25 m, so u = 8 × 0.25 = 2 K.

With both heaters, the difference at x = 2 m was 6 K. Switching off heater 2 removes its contribution of 2 K: 6 − 2 = 4 K, the contribution of heater 1 alone. This is superposition: each heater acts independently.

Check your reasoning

  • Choose the branch according to the positions of x and s.
  • Multiply G by the intensity and give the result in K.
  • Relate the difference between the two rods to the contribution of the heater that was removed.

Exercise 2 · Symmetry and interpretation

Swap the source and the probe

A source of intensity q = 6 K/m is alone at s = 2 m, in the middle of the rod of length 4 m. Calculate the temperature difference at x = 1 m. Then compare G(1, 2) with the value G(2, 1) from the example: what do you notice? Fictional values.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The probe is to the left of the source: use the left branch, G = a x, with a = (L − s)/L.

Hint 2

Compare your G with the value 0.5 m obtained for heater 1 at the probe.

Compare with the solution

Here s = 2 m, so a = (4 − 2)/4 = 2/4 = 0.5. At x = 1 m, x ≤ s: G = a x = 0.5 × 1 = 0.5 m, so u = 6 × 0.5 = 3 K.

The value G(1, 2) = 0.5 m equals G(2, 1) = 0.5 m, calculated in the example using the other branch: swapping source and probe leaves G unchanged. This equality between two distinct calculations is a useful check; it does not prove reciprocity in general, which requires integration by parts.

Check your reasoning

  • Recognise that the probe is to the left of the source.
  • Obtain G, then u, with the units m and K.
  • Present the equality as a check rather than a general proof.

Exercise 3 · Guided construction

A longer rod

Fictional setting: a rod of length L′ = 6 m, with 0 temperature difference at its ends, has a heater of unit intensity at s = 2 m. Determine the slopes a (on the left, G = a x) and b (on the right, G = b (L′ − x)) using the two equations below, then calculate G(s, s) and G at x = 5 m.

as=b(L′−s) et a+b=1
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write the continuity condition at s, then replace b by 1 − a.

Hint 2

You should find a L′ = L′ − s, so a = (L′ − s)/L′; deduce b from this.

Hint 3

For G at x = 5 m, x is to the right of s: use the right branch.

Compare with the solution

Continuity: a × 2 = b × 4. With b = 1 − a: a × 2 = (1 − a) × 4, so a × 6 = 4 and a = 2/3. Then b = 1 − 2/3 = 1/3, or s/L′, as in the general formula.

G(s, s) = a s = 2/3 × 2 = 4/3 m, approximately 1.33 m. At x = 5 m, to the right of s: L′ − x = 1 m and G = b × 1 = 1/3 × 1 = 1/3 m, approximately 0.33 m. The value is less than G(s, s), as expected since G is maximal at x = s.

Check your reasoning

  • Write continuity and the slope drop as two equations.
  • Solve for a and b, remembering that their sum equals one.
  • Choose the right branch for x greater than s and keep the unit m.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does G(x, s) represent for the rod in the example?

The temperature at x due to a point heater of unit intensity placed at s, with 0 temperature difference at the ends. It is expressed in metres.

Recall 2Why can the responses of several heaters be added?

The equation and the end conditions are linear: adding sources adds temperatures, and each unit response is multiplied by the intensity of its heater.

Recall 3What condition determines the slope of G at s?

G is continuous at s and its slope drops by one there. For a source of intensity q, the slope of u drops by q, as obtained by integrating minus the second derivative of u across the heater.

Recall 4How do you choose the formula for G at the probe?

Compare x and s. For x less than or equal to s, G is x times L minus s, divided by L. For x greater than or equal to s, G is s times L minus x, divided by L.

Recall 5What does the equality G(x, s) = G(s, x) say?

The response at x to a source at s equals the response at s to a source at x. Here it can be read from the formula; its general proof uses integration by parts.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Write the problem with its units and end conditions; express a point heater as continuity of u and a slope drop q; construct G for unit intensity (straight lines, 0 at the ends, a slope drop of one); choose the branch according to x and s; multiply by the intensities and add; check using the values at the heaters, the slope drops, and reciprocity.

What you understand
A Green's function is the response to an excitation of unit intensity: calculate it once, then combine copies of it according to the intensities. In the example, the excitation was a point heater in space. In the code being read, the related idea is an exact response in time: a difference from a baseline decays by an exponential factor.
