Costs and events

Your goalApply corporate actions and then execution costs to a long-short portfolio over a simulated day, then check cash, positions and net wealth in two ways.

✿01 See the idea

A backtesting engine replays the past day by day. For a given day, it does more than compare two prices: in an order decided in advance, it applies what happens to the securities, then the cost of each executed order. We use a single fictional example: a long-short portfolio of two stocks, A and B, replayed over one simulation day.

The portfolio is long 100 shares of A, meaning it owns them, and short 40 shares of B, meaning it has borrowed and then sold them. Cash is 1000 € before the day begins. The starting prices are 50 € for A and 80 € for B. These numbers are invented for the calculation; they do not describe any real security.

Two kinds of action occur. A corporate action changes the security without any decision from us: here, both A and B go ex-dividend. An order is our decision: the strategy rule reduces A by 20 shares and buys back 10 shares of B to reduce the short position. Each executed order has a cost.

Receiving a dividend does not by itself create a gain. In this scenario, the holder receives cash and the share price falls by the same amount. The short seller pays the dividend and benefits from the fall in price. Costs are actual losses: they reduce cash without adding anything to the positions.

Two ideas guide this page. First, the sequence on the clock: the corporate action is applied before the orders for the day, and this convention is written down because a different convention would give a different dividend. Second, the check: if the corporate actions are neutral, net wealth can fall only by the total cost of the orders.

The rule to remember

First apply corporate actions to the quantity held, then execute each order and pay a proportional commission and a half-spread, then check that net wealth has fallen only by the total cost.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A rebalancing day with two dividends

Return to the fictional portfolio: long 100 shares of A at 50 €, short 40 shares of B at 80 €, and cash of 1000 €. During the day, A goes ex-dividend with a dividend of 2 € per share and B with 1 € per share; the rule then sells 20 shares of A and buys back 10 shares of B. Each order pays a commission proportional to the notional amount, at a rate τ of 10 basis points, or 10 divided by 10000, plus a half-spread of 0.05 € per share traded. Let us calculate cash, positions and net wealth at the end of the day.

  1. Define the symbols and assumptions

    The subscript l denotes the long leg (stock A), and the subscript s the short leg (stock B). Nl and Ns are unitless share counts, with the short quantity counted as positive. Pl and Ps are prices in € per share; Dl and Ds are dividends in € per share; C is cash in €. Net wealth is E, in €.

    E=C+NlPl−NsPs

    The short position contributes negatively to E. Buying back shares of B at the current price exchanges cash for an equal reduction in debt: E does not change, apart from costs. This scenario is fictional. Assumptions: the dividend is paid in cash, when the engine applies the corporate action, on the quantity held at that instant; the price falls by exactly the dividend amount; orders execute at the price after the corporate action, without market impact; financing for cash and the short position, covered in the previous unit, is assumed to be 0 during this day. Outside these assumptions, the formulas change.

  2. Apply the corporate action before the orders

    On the daily clock, the corporate action comes first: the quantity that counts is the one before the orders. The long position receives Dl per share held, and the short position pays Ds per share borrowed.

    Dnet=NlDl−NsDs=100×2−40×1=160 €

    Cash rises from 1000 € to 1000 + 160 = 1160 €. The prices after the corporate action are P′l = 50 − 2 = 48 € and P′s = 80 − 1 = 79 €. These prices are used for orders and valuation.

  3. Check that the dividend makes nobody wealthier

    Before any order, the long leg is worth 100 × 50 = 5000 €, then 100 × 48 = 4800 € after the corporate action: it loses 100 × 2 = 200 €, exactly the dividend received. The net effect on A is 0.

    The short leg is worth 40 × 80 = 3200 € before, then 40 × 79 = 3160 € after: the debt falls by 40 × 1 = 40 €, a gain that exactly offsets the dividend paid. The net effect on B is 0. Initially, the positions are worth 5000 − 3200 = 1800 € and E is 1000 + 1800 = 2800 €.

  4. Calculate the cost of an order

    The cost of an executed order adds two positive parts: a proportional commission τ·V, where V = n × P is the notional amount in € (n shares traded at price P) and τ is a unitless rate, and a half-spread h in € per share traded. The spread is the difference between the quoted buying price and the quoted selling price; in this scenario, an immediate order pays half of it per share.

    K=τV+hn avec V=nP

    Sale of 20 shares of A: V = 20 × 48 = 960 €, commission 0.96 €, half-spread 1.00 €.

    Kl=0.001×960+0.05×20=1.96 €

    Buyback of 10 shares of B: V = 10 × 79 = 790 €, commission 0.79 €, half-spread 0.50 €.

    Ks=0.001×790+0.05×10=1.29 €

    The total cost is 1.96 + 1.29 = 3.25 €. Buying back a short position incurs costs like an ordinary purchase: both parts remain positive.

  5. Update cash and positions

    Selling A brings in 960 € and buying back B pays out 790 €: the balance is 960 − 790 = 170 €. After costs: 170 − 3.25 = 166.75 €. Final cash is 1160 + 166.75 = 1326.75 €.

    The quantities become 100 − 20 = 80 shares of A and 40 − 10 = 30 shares of B sold short. Values at the prices after the corporate action: A is worth 80 × 48 = 3840 €, B is worth 30 × 79 = 2370 €, so the positions are worth 3840 − 2370 = 1470 €. Net wealth becomes 1326.75 + 1470 = 2796.75 €.

  6. Check wealth in two ways

    First way: wealth has fallen by 2800 − 2796.75 = 3.25 €, while cash has increased from 1000 to 1326.75 €. Second way: the costs of the two orders total 1.96 + 1.29 = 3.25 €.

    Edébut−Efin=2800−2796.75=3.25 € =Kl+Ks

    The two ways agree because the corporate actions are neutral on each leg and each order exchanges cash for shares of equal value at the valuation price: only costs remain. With no costs, wealth would have stayed the same. This check proves that the accounting is consistent, but does not prove that the assumed costs represent a real market.

Explain it in your own words

Why does net wealth fall by 3.25 € while cash increases? Also state which convention for the sequence of the corporate action and orders the calculation assumes, and what would change without it.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A dividend received is a gain.”

    In this scenario, the price falls by the dividend amount: cash in the long position gains 200 € but the value of A loses 200 €, for a net effect of 0. The short position pays the dividend and gains from the price fall. Always check both effects before calling something a gain.

    Review the dividend check

  • “Buying back a short position costs nothing, or makes money.”

    An executed order incurs costs in both directions because the proportional commission and the half-spread are two positive parts. Buying back B costs 1.29 € in addition to the 790 € paid out.

    Review the cost calculation

  • “The sequence of operations during a day is a minor detail.”

    It is a convention that changes the result: applying the corporate action after the sale would give a dividend of 160 € instead of 200 €. Write down the convention before simulating and keep it throughout the series.

    Repeat the exercise on the sequence of operations

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

A wider half-spread

In the same scenario, suppose the half-spread per share is multiplied by 2 (a fictional value), with everything else unchanged, including the notional amount of the sale of A. Recalculate the cost of this sale in €, then compare it with the cost in the example.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Multiply the half-spread by the number of shares traded, rather than the notional amount.

Hint 2

The proportional commission does not depend on the half-spread: it stays the same.

Compare with the solution

Half-spread in the example: 1.00 € for 20 shares; multiplied by 2, it is 2.00 €. The proportional commission remains 0.96 €.

Cost: 0.96 + 2.00 = 2.96 €, higher than the 1.96 € in the example. Check: only the term linked to the half-spread has changed, and the cost remains positive.

Check your reasoning

  • The half-spread multiplier is applied only to the part that depends on it.
  • The proportional commission is kept unchanged.
  • Comparison with the cost in the example, in €.

Exercise 2 · Variant to justify

Change the sequence of operations

Suppose the engine first executes the sale of 20 shares of A, then applies the dividend of 2 € per share to the remaining quantity. Calculate the dividend received on A, compare it with the one in the example, then explain why the convention must be written down.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

How many shares of A remain after the sale?

Hint 2

Multiply this quantity by the dividend per share, then compare with 200 €.

Compare with the solution

80 shares of A remain. Dividend: 80 × 2 = 160 €, compared with 200 € in the example. Difference: 200 − 160 = 40 €.

Neither convention is generally the correct one: actual entitlement depends on the market rules, which must be reread and then implemented in the engine. The lesson is that an unwritten choice changes cash by 40 € here.

Check your reasoning

  • The remaining quantity is used in the variant.
  • The difference is calculated with the correct sign.
  • The convention is presented as a choice to write down, without claiming that it is universal.

Exercise 3 · Transfer with a pitfall

A stock split

A corporate action splits stock B: each share becomes 2 shares and the price is divided by 2. Before the split, the short position is 40 shares at 80 €. Calculate the new quantity, the new price and the value of the short position. Then calculate the difference in value if the engine divided only the price without changing the quantity. This corporate action is fictional and independent of the example.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A split changes the quantity and the price, but leaves the total value unchanged.

Hint 2

For the pitfall, multiply the old quantity by the new price.

Compare with the solution

Quantity: 40 × 2 = 80 shares. Price: 80 ÷ 2 = 40 €. Value: 80 × 40 = 3200 €, the same as before.

With only the price divided: 40 × 40 = 1600 € instead of 3200 €. The engine would treat the short debt as having shrunk, implying a gain with no market movement. Fees, dividends and rounding of fractional shares are ignored here.

Check your reasoning

  • The quantity is multiplied and the price divided by the same ratio.
  • The value of the position is checked to be unchanged.
  • The pitfall is quantified and interpreted as a false gain for the short position.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What two parts make up the cost of an order in this scenario?

A commission proportional to the notional amount V = n × P and a half-spread per share traded. The sum is positive for both a purchase and a sale.

Recall 2What happens to a dividend when the stock goes ex-dividend, for the long leg and the short leg?

The long position receives cash, and the short position pays it. In this scenario, the price falls by the dividend amount, so the dividend alone does not increase wealth, before costs.

Recall 3Why fix the sequence of corporate actions and orders on the clock?

Because the quantity to which the corporate action applies depends on it. An unwritten convention changes cash, as shown by the 40 € difference in the exercise.

Recall 4What check links net wealth to costs in this scenario?

If corporate actions are neutral and orders execute at the valuation price, the fall in net wealth equals the total cost, or 3.25 € here.

Recall 5What must a stock split preserve?

The total value of the position: the quantity is multiplied by the ratio and the price divided by it.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Set out the scenario and its assumptions; fix the sequence of corporate actions followed by orders; apply the dividend to the quantity held and adjust prices; calculate each order cost as a proportional commission and a half-spread; update cash and quantities; check that net wealth has fallen only by the total cost.
