Level 1 · C031·M2

Limit of a function

Your goalJustify a finite limit at a given time, even when the function is undefined at that time.

Before you start
c006-m5 : composition · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c011-m5 : binomial · c014-m5 : invariants · c031-m1 : limit of a sequence

✿01 See the idea

A tank is filling. You know the volume at time a = 2 s and compare it with the volume at a nearby time t. The difference in volume, divided by the elapsed time, gives an average flow rate. What happens to this quotient when the times get as close together as you wish?

In our fictional model, the tank contains 4 L at 2 s and 4.41 L at 2.1 s. Between these times, the average flow rate is 4.1 L/s. As the times get even closer together, the average flow rates approach 4 L/s. This value that they approach is a limit.

Making the times exactly equal would mean dividing a volume difference of 0 by a time interval of 0. The original quotient then gives no value. A limit concerns nearby times where the calculation makes sense; it does not require a calculation at the target point.

The scene and all its values are fictional. We assume an exact volume law, with no measurement noise or overflow, near the time under study. We are looking for a finite limit near a finite time; behaviour over a very long time will be a separate question.

The rule to remember

To prove a limit, control all values close enough to the target point, excluding that point. A few calculations suggest a candidate; a relationship valid throughout a neighbourhood provides a proof.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Bring the times closer together without making the interval 0

The filling model is V(t) = c·t·t, with c = 1 L/(s·s), and the fixed time is a = 2 s. We study the average flow rate f(t) between a and t, for t positive or 0 and different from a. All fractions remain exact throughout the calculation.

  1. Define the function and its domain

    t and a are times in seconds; V(t) is the volume in litres; c is a fixed positive coefficient, in litres per second squared. The notation s·s means second squared. Thus c·t·t does have the unit litre. f(t) is an average flow rate in litres per second. The parentheses in V(t) and f(t) mean “the value of the function for input t”.

    f(t)=V(t)−V(a)t−a=c(t2−a2)t−a

    The domain D, the set of allowed inputs, contains here the times t ≥ 0 s with t ≠ a. If t is before a, the numerator and denominator are both negative: their quotient still gives the average flow rate between the times. We do not reverse just one of the differences.

    At t = a, the numerator and denominator are each 0, in their respective units. The expression 0/0 is not a value of f. We do not assign it any result by convention.

  2. State precisely what “approaches” means

    limt→af(t)=ℓ

    This notation is read: “the limit of f(t), as t approaches a, is ℓ”. ℓ, a lowercase L, is the limiting flow rate in L/s; the arrow → expresses the approach. t can take all allowed values close enough to a, without being equal to a. It need not follow a single list of times or actually move.

    For every strictly positive tolerance ε on the flow rate, we must be able to choose a strictly positive distance δ in time such that every t in the domain less than δ from a gives a flow rate less than ε from ℓ. ε is read “epsilon”, in L/s, and δ “delta”, in s. We assume that the domain provides inputs different from a as close to a as we wish; that is indeed the case here.

    0<|t−a|<δ⇒|f(t)−ℓ|<ε

    The bars |…| give a distance, hence an absolute value; ⇒ means “implies”. The condition on the left excludes the point a. We choose δ after receiving ε, and the guarantee must then hold for all the relevant t. It requires neither that f(a) exist nor that f(t) reach ℓ.

  3. Carry out a first complete calculation

    Set t = 2.1 s, a gap h = 1/10 s = 0.1 s after a. Here h denotes the chosen positive time interval. The reference volume is V(a) = 1 × 2 × 2 = 4 L, using c in L/(s·s) and a in seconds. For the second volume, first square the time: 2.1 × 2.1 = 4.41 s·s, then multiply by c, giving V(t) = 4.41 L.

    Subtract the volumes to keep only the water added: 4.41 − 4 = 0.41 L. Subtract the times in the same order: 2.1 − 2 = 0.1 s. Finally, divide the increase in volume by the time interval:

    f(2.1)=0.41 L0.1 s=4.1 L/s

    Reverse check: 4.1 L/s × 0.1 s = 0.41 L, exactly the volume added. The result applies to this specific interval; this isolated calculation does not yet prove any limit.

  4. Look for a candidate using closer times

    For t = 1.9 s, the quotient is 3.9 L/s; for t = 2.1 s, it is 4.1 L/s. With the gap reduced to 0.01 s, t = 1.99 s gives 3.99 L/s, and t = 2.01 s gives 4.01 L/s. We obtain these values from the same volume law; the following simplification will allow us to check them quickly.

    The candidate is ℓ = 4 L/s: the flow rate error goes from 0.1 L/s to 0.01 L/s as the distance from time a decreases. But these times are only a sample. Another function could have the same values at these points and behave differently between them. We still need to cover all allowed nearby times.

  5. Simplify where the quotient exists

    The difference of squares identity gives t·t − a·a = (t − a)(t + a). We can check it by expanding the product: the cross terms cancel. For t ≠ a, the factor t − a is nonzero and can therefore be cancelled:

    f(t)=c(t−a)(t+a)t−a=c(t+a)

    For example, for t = 1.9 s, the sum t + a is 3.9 s, and c times this sum is 3.9 L/s. For the closer times, the sums are 3.99 s and 4.01 s; we recover 3.99 L/s and 4.01 L/s. This equality also explains the graph in the figure.

    As t approaches a, we therefore expect c(a + a) = 2ca. Here ca = 2 L/s, so the candidate ℓ is 4 L/s. We must still guarantee this approach using the exact error. The simplification does not add a to the domain of the original function.

  6. Turn the candidate into a guarantee

    |f(t)−2ca|=|c(t−a)|=c|t−a|

    The last equality uses the fact that c is positive. It directly relates the flow rate error to the distance in time, for every t in the domain. For any tolerance ε > 0 L/s, choose δ = ε/c. This quotient is a time interval, since (L/s)/(L/(s·s)) gives seconds. Whenever 0 < |t − a| < δ, the flow rate error is less than cδ = ε. We have the required guarantee for every positive tolerance, and hence the stated limit.

    limt→af(t)=2ca=4 L/s

    Example of how to read this: for ε = 0.05 L/s, δ = 0.05 s works. Every t between 1.95 s and 2.05 s, excluding the endpoints and with t ≠ 2 s, gives a flow rate less than 0.05 L/s from 4 L/s. Checking a single tolerance would not suffice to define the limit; the rule δ = ε/c covers all positive tolerances.

  7. Check the result and its scope

    At t = 2.1 s, the simplified form gives c(t + a) = 4.1 L/s, as does dividing the volume by the time interval. The error law gives c·h = 0.1 L/s, equal to |4.1 − 4| L/s. These checks use another form of the calculation.

    The excluded point remains excluded: f(a) has no value, while its limit is 4 L/s. The nearby flow rates can approach this limit without ever equalling it. This result concerns our exact model; it does not prove that a real tank follows the assumed law. The connection with an instantaneous flow rate will be studied through the derivative, after this chapter.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the formulas. Explain why making the time interval 0 prevents the quotient but does not prevent its limit. Then reconstruct the relationship between the flow rate error and the distance in time, giving their units.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The calculation gives 0/0, so the limit does not exist.”

    This expression only says that the quotient cannot be calculated at the point. Factorise for t different from a, then study nearby values using the error law. Here the limit is 4 L/s.

    Return to “Simplify where the quotient exists”

  • “The calculated values get closer together: I have proved the limit.”

    A finite sample can suggest the correct value without controlling the times between its inputs. We need a relationship valid for all times close enough; here |f(t) − ℓ| = c|t − a| provides that control.

    Return to “Turn the candidate into a guarantee”

  • “After simplification, f(a) must be 4 L/s.”

    The equality with c(t + a) was obtained under the condition t ≠ a. Assigning a value at the point would require defining a new function. The exercise on the added value explicitly separates the two operations.

    Try “Add a value at the excluded point”

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Another interval for the same tank

Keep the law V(t) = c·t·t, c = 1 L/(s·s) and a = 2 s. Calculate f(t) for t = 2.5 s from the volumes, then check using c(t + a). Is this result the limit as t approaches a?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate V(t) and V(a), then subtract the volumes and the times in the same order.

Hint 2

The limit concerns an approach arbitrarily close to a; this calculation fixes one particular input.

Compare with the solution

The square of the time is 6.25 s·s. The volume V(t) is therefore 6.25 L, while V(a) = 4 L. The increase is 2.25 L and the time interval is 0.5 s. Their quotient is 4.5 L/s.

The check c(t + a) also gives 4.5 L/s; in reverse, 4.5 L/s × 0.5 s = 2.25 L. This is the average flow rate over this interval. The proved limit remains 4 L/s, rather than this result obtained at a fixed input.

Check your reasoning

  • Present both differences and the quotient, with their units.
  • Recover 4.5 L/s by another route.
  • Distinguish the value at a fixed input from the limit as the input approaches a.

Exercise 2 · Guarantee on a neighbourhood

Choose the precision in time

Without relying only on the times already calculated, guarantee |f(t) − ℓ| < 0.05 L/s. Give a sufficient distance δ in time and the corresponding interval around a = 2 s. Specify the excluded points and explain why the guarantee covers all other times in this interval.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start again from the equality |f(t) − ℓ| = c|t − a|.

Hint 2

Divide the flow rate tolerance by c, checking the unit of the result.

Compare with the solution

Take δ = ε/c = 0.05 s. The interval is 1.95 s < t < 2.05 s, with t ≠ 2 s. For each of these times, c|t − a| < cδ = 0.05 L/s; the error identity transfers this inequality to |f(t) − ℓ|.

The endpoints are excluded because we require an error strictly less than the tolerance. The centre is excluded because the original quotient is undefined there. A smaller positive distance also works. This calculation handles the requested tolerance; the general proof requires that we can make this choice again for every positive ε.

Check your reasoning

  • Express δ in seconds and ε in litres per second.
  • Exclude the endpoints and the centre for distinct reasons.
  • Justify all the relevant times using the error equality, without restricting yourself to a few trials.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

Add a value at the excluded point

The tank software defines a new function g: g(t) = f(t) for allowed times different from a, and g(a) = 0 L/s. Someone claims: “The software displays 0 at a, so the limit of g is 0.” Respond by distinguishing the added value, the nearby values and the limit.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The condition 0 < |t − a| excludes precisely the time whose value the software has changed.

Hint 2

Write |g(t) − ℓ| for t different from a, then reuse the previous guarantee.

Compare with the solution

The function g is defined at the point and its value g(a) is 0 L/s. For every nearby time different from a, g(t) = f(t), so |g(t) − 4 L/s| = c|t − a|. The same rule δ = ε/c proves that its limit is 4 L/s.

The value displayed at the point does not determine the nearby values. Thus g(a) can differ from the limit of g. For f, the value at the point is missing; for g, it has been chosen. Both limits are identical. We are not deciding here whether this software choice is suitable for a real device.

Check your reasoning

  • State g(a) = 0 L/s and the limit 4 L/s without confusing them.
  • Explain that the values away from the centre are unchanged.
  • Reuse a proof on the neighbourhood, rather than the software display.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does a finite limit ℓ guarantee as t approaches a?

For every positive tolerance on the output, a sufficiently small positive distance on the input guarantees this tolerance for every allowed input different from a that satisfies this distance condition.

Recall 2In the tank model, why does the absence of f(a) not prevent the limit?

The quotient is undefined for a time interval of 0, but the limit controls nearby times different from a. For them, factorisation gives f(t) = c(t + a) and the error approaches 0.

Recall 3Which relationship turns the numerical candidate into a proof, and how should δ be chosen?

For t different from a, |f(t) − ℓ| = c|t − a|, with ℓ = 2ca and c positive. For every positive ε in L/s, δ = ε/c in seconds suffices.

Recall 4Why are a few very close values insufficient to prove a limit?

They leave other inputs uncontrolled, as close to the point as we wish. The definition requires a guarantee for all allowed inputs close enough, for every positive tolerance.

Recall 5Does changing only the value of the function at the point a change its limit at a?

No: the inputs used in the definition of the limit are different from a. If their values remain identical, the same limit guarantee remains valid.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the reasoning in pencil: name the input, the output and their units; write the domain and the excluded point; use a few values to propose a limit; factorise while keeping the restriction; recover the error law and choose δ after ε; finally distinguish the limit from the value at the point.
