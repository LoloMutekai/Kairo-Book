Level 3 · C165·M2

Completeness

Your goalProve that a Cauchy sequence in L¹ converges in L¹, by following continuous ramps that narrow towards a jump, and understand why the continuous functions equipped with the same norm do not form a complete space.

Before you start
c164-m5 : Fubini-Tonelli · c040-m5 : least squares · c142-m5 : convergence criteria · c165-m1 : Lp spaces

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a switch that changes from off to on at the midpoint of a segment. We can approximate it with increasingly steep continuous ramps: the value stays at 0 up to the midpoint, rises along a straight line, then stays equal to 1. Each ramp is a function with no jump. Yet as the slope gets steeper, the ramps become more alike: the area between them becomes as small as we want.

A sequence whose terms eventually all become close to one another is called a Cauchy sequence. In the real numbers, it always has a limit: this is the completeness studied in C142·M3. This unit asks the same question at the level of functions: does a sequence of functions that become close to one another, with distance measured by area, have a limit that remains a function in the same family?

The answer depends on the family. For continuous functions, no: the limit of the ramps is the jump, which is not continuous. For integrable functions, identified when they are equal almost everywhere, yes. The key to the proof is to extract terms from the sequence whose successive differences are summable: the series of these differences then converges almost everywhere.

The numbers in this scene are exact mathematical values, rather than measurements. We work on the segment from 0 to 1 with Lebesgue measure. The functions have no units here; the L¹ norm has their unit multiplied by a length, so it is also dimensionless in this scene.

The rule to remember

In L¹, a sequence whose terms become close to one another converges: we extract terms with summable differences, their series converges almost everywhere, and the tail of this series measures the final error.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Continuous ramps converging in L¹ to a jump

On the segment from 0 to 1, for an integer n at least equal to 2, the ramp fₙ equals 0 up to the midpoint 1/2, rises with slope n, then stays equal to 1. We show that (fₙ) is Cauchy in L¹, that it converges there, and that its limit is not continuous. The indices followed in detail are 4, 8 and 16.

  1. Set out the framework, symbols and claim to be proved

    x is a point on the segment from 0 to 1, with no units here. L¹ denotes the measurable functions f for which the integral of |f| is finite, with functions equal almost everywhere identified; the norm, studied in C165·M1, is the following Lebesgue integral.

    ‖f‖1=∫01|f(x)|dx

    A sequence (fn) is Cauchy if, for every strictly positive ε, there is an index N such that ‖fn − fm‖1 < ε whenever n and m are at least equal to N. The space is complete if every Cauchy sequence in it converges in norm to an element of the space. We prove that L¹ is complete. Assumptions: Lebesgue integration and monotone convergence (C164·M4), completeness of ℝ (C142·M3), the triangle inequality for the norm (C165·M1).

    The ramp with index n is defined by the following formula: it is continuous, 0 up to the midpoint, has slope n, and is capped at 1.

    fn(x)=min(1,max(0,n(x−12)))

  2. Calculate the distance between two ramps

    For n < m, the ramp fm lies at or above fn at every point: it rises faster and reaches the same cap. Thus |fm − fn| = fm − fn, and the distance is the difference between the integrals. The integral of fn equals 1/2 minus the area of the small triangle that the ramp leaves below the cap, with base 1/n and height 1, giving 1/(2n).

    ‖fm−fn‖1=12n−12m pour n<m

    At index 4, the base of the triangle is 1/4 and its area is 1/4 × 1 × 1/2 = 1/8. At index 8, the area is 1/16. The distance between these two ramps is therefore 1/8 − 1/16 = 1/16. Between indices 8 and 16, the area changes from 1/16 to 1/32 and the distance is 1/32.

  3. Find a Cauchy index

    Fix ε = 1/10, or 0.1 in decimal form. For n < m, the distance 1/(2n) − 1/(2m) is strictly less than 1/(2n), and this upper bound decreases as n increases. It therefore suffices to have 1/(2n) ≤ ε, that is, n ≥ 1/(2ε).

    Here 2ε = 1/5, so the index is 1 divided by 1/5, giving 5. Check: 2 × 5 = 10, and 1 divided by 10 indeed gives ε again. For the pair of indices 5 and 10, the distance is 1/10 − 1/20 = 1/20, well below 1/10.

    The same reasoning holds for every strictly positive ε: the sequence is Cauchy in L¹.

  4. Extract terms with summable differences

    A Cauchy index does not yet provide a limit function. We therefore choose strictly increasing indices nk such that the difference between two consecutive selected terms is below a bound εk, which the Cauchy property allows by taking ε = εk.

    ‖fnk+1−fnk‖1≤εk=2−k

    For the ramps, we can take nk equal to 2 to the power (k + 1), giving indices 4, 8 and 16 when k is 1, 2 and 3. The steps are 1/16 then 1/32, below the bounds 1/2 and 1/4, whose sum is 3/4.

    Let g be the sum of the absolute values of these differences. It is a nonnegative function taking values in [0, ∞].

    g=∑k≥1|fnk+1−fnk|

    Monotone convergence allows us to integrate a series of nonnegative functions term by term. The integral of g is therefore the sum of the norms of the differences, at most equal to the sum of the bounds.

    ∫g≤∑k≥12−k=1

    A nonnegative function with finite integral can be infinite only on a set of measure 0: g is finite almost everywhere. Check with our ramps: the steps never cancel out, because the ramps are ordered, so 1/16 + 1/32 = 3/32 and 1/8 − 3/32 = 1/32, which is exactly 1/32.

  5. Pass to the limit and measure the error

    At a point x where g(x) is finite, the series of differences converges absolutely: its partial sums form a Cauchy sequence of real numbers, which converges because ℝ is complete. Now fnk(x) is the first selected term plus the partial sum of the differences. We therefore define f(x) to be the limit of fnk(x) wherever it exists, and 0 elsewhere, on a set of measure 0 that does not change the equivalence class of f.

    We now bound the tail. Almost everywhere, |f − fnk| is at most the sum of the absolute values of the differences with index at least k. Integrating gives the following bound.

    ‖f−fnk‖1≤∑j≥k2−j=21−k

    This bound tends to 0 as k increases, and f belongs to L¹ because |f| ≤ |fnk| + |f − fnk|. For the ramp with index 16, the tail is bounded by 1/4 whereas the exact error is 1/32: the bound is a guarantee, rather than the actual value.

    Finally, for every n, ‖f − fn‖1 ≤ ‖f − fnk‖1 + ‖fnk − fn‖1. The first term tends to 0 as k increases; the second is small as soon as n and nk exceed the Cauchy index. Thus fn converges to f in L¹.

  6. Compare with continuous functions

    Here f equals 0 on [0, 1/2] and 1 on ]1/2, 1]. No continuous function g is equal to f almost everywhere. If g is 0 almost everywhere on the open interval from 0 to 1/2, the set where it is nonzero is an open set of measure 0, hence empty: g is 0 everywhere on that interval. Similarly, g equals 1 everywhere on the open interval from 1/2 to 1. Continuity at 1/2 would force 0 = 1, which is impossible.

    Each ramp is continuous and the sequence is Cauchy for ‖·‖1; yet it has no limit among the continuous functions. The space of continuous functions equipped with this norm is therefore not complete, whereas L¹ is.

    For an exponent p in [1, ∞[, the approach is the same: replace ‖·‖1 with ‖·‖p, use the triangle inequality for this norm in place of that for L¹, and apply monotone convergence to the pth powers. We do not develop this general proof here, and the infinite exponent is not covered.

  7. Check in three ways

    First way: the area of the missing triangle at index 4, with base 1/4 and height 1, is half the product, giving 1/8. Second way: the exact integral of the missing part, with h = 1/n, gives the same value.

    h−nh22=12n avec h=1n

    Third way: the telescoping sum 1/16 + 1/32 + 1/32 = 1/8. All three ways give 1/8 at index 4. The file calculs.py also checks each distance using the trapezoidal rule, which is exact for piecewise affine ramps.

Explain it in your own words

Why is it not enough for the difference between two consecutive terms to tend to 0 for a sequence to be Cauchy? And exactly where does the proof use the completeness of ℝ and monotone convergence?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Every Cauchy sequence in a normed space converges.”

    Completeness is a property of the space equipped with its norm, rather than of the norm alone. The ramps are Cauchy for ‖·‖₁ among the continuous functions, but their limit is a jump outside this family. Always check whether the candidate limit belongs to the space.

    Review the step on continuous functions

  • “Convergence in the L¹ norm means convergence at every point.”

    The norm measures an area of difference, rather than a value at a point. The conveyor belt in the exercise converges to 0 in norm without converging at any point; only a selected subsequence converges almost everywhere.

    Redo the conveyor belt exercise

  • “If the difference between two consecutive terms tends to 0, the sequence is Cauchy.”

    You must control all pairs of terms far enough along the sequence. In the real numbers, the partial sums of the harmonic series have steps tending to 0, but the difference between index n and index 2n remains at least equal to 1/2: they diverge. First look for an index N common to all pairs.

    Reread the intuition about terms becoming close to one another

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Distance between two ramps

Use the ramps from the example with n = 2 and m = 10. Calculate ‖fₘ − fₙ‖₁, then compare it with ε = 1/10. Is index 2 at least equal to the Cauchy index found for this ε?

Hint 1

For n < m, the distance is the difference between the areas that the two ramps leave below the cap.

Hint 2

The missing area for a ramp with index n is 1/(2n).

Compare with the solution

Index 2: 2 × 2 = 4, so the missing area is 1/4. Index 10: 2 × 10 = 20, so the missing area is 1/20. The distance is 1/4 − 1/20 = 1/5.

This distance exceeds 1/10. This is consistent: index 2 is smaller than the Cauchy index 5, so the guarantee from the example does not apply there. This does not mean that the sequence is not Cauchy.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the difference between areas, rather than values at a point.
  • Compare the distance with ε on the same scale.
  • Do not conclude that the sequence is not Cauchy.

Exercise 2 · Application

A Cauchy index for a smaller ε

With ε = 1/100, find the smallest index N such that ‖fₙ − fₘ‖₁ < ε for all n and m at least equal to N. Explain why index 49 does not work.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Solve 1/(2n) ≤ ε for n, as in the example.

Hint 2

For the index just below it, calculate 1/(2n) and see what happens as m increases.

Compare with the solution

We have 2ε = 1/50, so n ≥ 1 divided by 1/50, giving 50. Check: 2 × 50 = 100, and 1 divided by 100 indeed gives ε again. Since the distance is strictly less than this upper bound, the strict inequality is satisfied at index 50.

For n = 49, 2 × 49 = 98, giving the bound 1/98, which exceeds ε. The distance ‖fm − fn‖1 approaches this value as m increases: a sufficiently large m puts it above ε. Index 49 therefore does not guarantee the condition.

Check your reasoning

  • Isolate n in the inequality before calculating.
  • Justify rejecting the neighbouring index using an upper bound approached as m increases, rather than a single trial.
  • Check the equality at the index found.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and justification

A conveyor belt that converges in norm but nowhere pointwise

Block k, for k equal to 0, 1, 2, 3 and so on, contains the indicator functions of 2 to the power k intervals of equal length that partition the segment from 0 to 1. Block 0 is the indicator function of the whole segment. Arrange these functions one after another in a sequence (hₙ). (a) How many functions do blocks 0 to 3 contain? (b) At a fixed point, how many equal 1? (c) Show that hₙ tends to 0 in the L¹ norm without converging at any point. (d) Take the first interval of each block for k equal to 1, 2, 3: add their norms and compare with 1.

Ik,i=[i2k,i+12k[
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The norm of the indicator function of an interval is its length. Block k contains 2 to the power k intervals of length 1 over 2 to the power k.

Hint 2

The intervals in a block partition the segment: a fixed point belongs to exactly one interval per block.

Hint 3

For (d), recognise the technique from the example: summable differences along a subsequence.

Compare with the solution

(a) The blocks contain 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 functions, giving 16 − 1 = 15.

(b) The intervals in a block partition the segment: a fixed point belongs to exactly one interval per block, so exactly one function per block equals 1 at that point. Among these 15 functions, there are 4, one per block, because there are 4 blocks.

(c) The norm of a function in block k is 1 over 2 to the power k, which tends to 0: the sequence tends to 0 in the L¹ norm. However, for every point, each block contains a function equal to 1 at that point and, as soon as k is at least equal to 1, another equal to 0: the values oscillate indefinitely and have no limit. The sum of the norms of an entire block is 1 (block 3: 8 × 1/8 = 1), so the series of norms of the whole sequence diverges: the technique from the example does not apply to the whole sequence.

(d) The norms are 1/2, 1/4 and 1/8, with sum 7/8, leaving 1 − 7/8 = 1/8 before reaching 1. These norms are summable: the subsequence converges almost everywhere to 0, as the proof predicts, but the whole sequence does not.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish convergence in norm from convergence at a point.
  • Count only one function per block for a fixed point.
  • Relate the subsequence to the technique of summable differences.
  • Do not claim that the whole sequence converges almost everywhere.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does it mean for a sequence (fₙ) to be Cauchy for a norm?

For every strictly positive ε, there is an index N such that ‖fₙ − fₘ‖ < ε whenever n and m are at least equal to N. All pairs of terms far enough along the sequence become close, rather than just neighbouring terms.

Recall 2What is a complete space, and what does this unit establish about L¹?

A space in which every Cauchy sequence converges to an element of the space. L¹ is complete; the continuous functions equipped with the L¹ norm are not, as the ramps show.

Recall 3Why extract terms whose successive differences are summable?

The series of differences then has a finite integral by monotone convergence, so it is finite almost everywhere; its partial sums converge in ℝ, providing the limit function, and its tail bounds the error.

Recall 4Does convergence in L¹ imply convergence at every point?

No. The conveyor belt converges to 0 in norm without converging at any point. Only a suitably chosen subsequence converges almost everywhere.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define a Cauchy sequence and a complete space; extract terms with summable differences; bound the integral of the series of differences using monotone convergence; deduce the limit almost everywhere using the completeness of ℝ; measure the error using the tail of the series; conclude that L¹ is complete; check that the continuous ramps have no continuous limit.
