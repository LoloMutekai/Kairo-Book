Level 4 · C236·M4

Brackets

Your goalCalculate a Lie bracket, check its sign using a loop of movements, and recognize the direction it adds locally.

Before you start
c235-m5 : divergence · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c231-m5 : geodesics · c236-m1 : integration by parts · c236-m2 : Clark-Ocone · c236-m3 : Malliavin covariance

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a hypothetical pen plotter. A command U moves its pen horizontally. A command V moves it vertically, faster the farther to the right it is. To the left of the origin, V moves it downward; on the vertical axis, it does nothing. All coordinates and command parameters are scaled and therefore dimensionless: each physical length has been divided by the same fixed reference length.

Moving right and then up does not lead to the same point as moving up and then right: in the first order, the upward movement benefits from the new x-coordinate. The diagram compares these orders starting from (1 ; 2), using the same dimensionless step h = 0.50. Before reading the values, predict which path ends higher.

Next, apply U, then V, then their opposite commands in the same order. The pen returns to its starting x-coordinate but remains higher: the downward movement took place at a smaller x-coordinate than the upward movement. The Lie bracket describes the direction of this small closure defect after division by the square of the step.

Opposite commands are allowed in this reversible mathematical model. This loop represents neither an observed Brownian path nor an actual control protocol. We will explain the geometric mechanism; the regularity of probability distributions remains the topic of the next unit.

The rule to remember

Fix the convention [U,V]f = U(Vf) − V(Uf), keep the movements in chronological order, and compare the closure defect with h squared.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Reverse the commands without closing the path

On the hypothetical plotter, start from p = (1 ; 2), take the dimensionless step h = 1/2 = 0.50, and apply, in chronological order, U with parameter h, V with parameter h, U with parameter −h, and V with parameter −h. Let us calculate the bracket and the final displacement by two different methods.

  1. Describe the fields and assumptions

    p = (x,y) is a scaled position in the real plane. A field gives the velocity with respect to its own command parameter. Here U(x,y) = (1,0), V(x,y) = (0,x). The horizontal component is written first. These affine fields are defined on the whole plane and differentiable to every order; their movements exist for every real parameter, positive or negative.

    x, y, the parameter s of U, the parameter t of V, and the step h are dimensionless. The components of U and V, like those of the bracket in these scaled coordinates, are therefore dimensionless. This analysis of units would need to be reconsidered if the parameters were physical times.

  2. Fix the convention through its action on a function

    For a dimensionless scalar function f, U f denotes its derivative in the direction U: U f = Uˣ ∂xf + Uʸ ∂ᵧf. The superscripts x and y label components; they are not powers. ∂x and ∂ᵧ mean differentiation with respect to the indicated coordinate. We take f to have continuous second derivatives so that its mixed derivatives can be interchanged.

    [U,V]f=U(Vf)−V(Uf)

    With this convention, [U,V] = DV · U − D(U) · V. D(U) and DV are the Jacobian matrices: their columns are the derivatives of the field with respect to x, then y; the dot · means applying the matrix to the vector. The bracket differentiates the fields before subtracting. It is not the vector U − V. The opposite convention would change the sign, but not the direction spanned.

  3. Calculate the bracket and see the second derivatives cancel

    Here U f = ∂xf and V f = x ∂ᵧf. The product rule gives U(Vf) = ∂ᵧf + x ∂x∂ᵧf, whereas V(Uf) = x ∂ᵧ∂xf. The mixed derivatives are equal, so their difference vanishes. This leaves [U,V]f = ∂ᵧf. The commutator of these differentiation operators therefore remains a first-order vector field.

    DU=(0000),DV=(0010)

    The matrix method confirms this: D(U) · V is 0, DV · U = (0 ; 1), hence [U,V] = (0 ; 1). We differentiate the expression for the field V before evaluating it at the origin. Replacing V with just its 0 value at the origin would lose precisely its variation.

  4. Find the exact movements

    The flow Φ of U is the movement that solves dx/ds = 1 and dy/ds = 0. The flow Ψ of V solves dx/dt = 0 and dy/dt = x: during this command, x is constant. The flows are therefore given without approximation by:

    Φs(x,y)=(x+s,y)
    Ψt(x,y)=(x,y+tx)

    The loop is Ch(p) = Ψ-h(Φ-h(Ψh(Φh(p)))). Read the maps from the inside outward: positive U, positive V, negative U, negative V. Ch names this composition; it is not a new field.

  5. Apply the positive commands

    U adds h to the x-coordinate: 1 + 1/2 = 3/2. The first state is (3/2 ; 2). V keeps this new x-coordinate: its upward movement is h × x = 1/2 × 3/2 = 3/4. The y-coordinate becomes 2 + 3/4 = 11/4. We are at (3/2 ; 11/4).

    Why multiply by the new x-coordinate? Because V depends on where the command is applied. With V first, we would have added only 1/2 × 1 = 1/2, then U would have left y unchanged: endpoint (3/2 ; 5/2). The difference between the orders is already 11/4 − 5/2 = 1/4.

  6. Return with the opposite commands

    U with parameter −h subtracts 1/2 from 3/2: the x-coordinate returns to 1, without changing the y-coordinate 11/4. V with parameter −h then subtracts h × 1 = 1/2. The x-coordinate used in this calculation is the one on the return movement, rather than the upward movement. The final state is (1 ; 11/4 − 1/2) = (1 ; 9/4).

    The total displacement is Δx = 1 − 1 = 0 and Δy = 9/4 − 2 = 1/4 = 0.25, both dimensionless. Here Δ means “final value minus initial value”. The first-order vertical contributions cancel; what remains is the extra displacement created by the change in x-coordinate.

  7. Check using algebra and a coordinate function

    Keeping x, y, and h symbolic, the successive y-coordinates are y, y + h(x + h), y + h(x + h), then y + h(x + h) − hx = y + h × h. Thus Ch(x,y) = (x,y + h × h) exactly in this model. The square results from two successive commands, rather than elapsed physical time.

    Ch(p)−ph2=(0,1)=[U,V](p)

    Numerically, the square of the step is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, so Δy/(h × h) = 1/4/1/4 = 1. This result has the sign of the bracket calculated by differentiation. Another check: for f(x,y) = y, V f = x, U(Vf) = 1, whereas U f = 0, so V(Uf) = 0. The vertical component of the bracket is indeed 1.

    For general smooth fields, with this same chronological composition, the displacement divided by the square of the step tends to [U,V](p) as h tends to 0. Equality for a finite step is specific to our example; it does not justify treating any finite loop as an exact bracket.

  8. Identify the additional direction before applying any theorem

    At the origin o = (0 ; 0), U(o) = (1 ; 0), V(o) = (0 ; 0). Their values span only a line. Adding [U,V](o) = (0 ; 1), the determinant of the columns U(o), [U,V](o) is 1 × 1 − 0 × 0 = 1: it is nonzero. These columns are independent and their rank, meaning the number of independent directions, is 2.

    This is local information about the fields and their variations. For a diffusion, we will need to specify which fields represent noise or drift, which family of brackets to use, and which analytic assumptions to adopt. This calculation alone proves neither a smooth density nor a global accessibility result. These implications belong to the next unit.

Without rereading, draw the loop and explain at which x-coordinate the upward and downward movements take place. Then recover the sign of the bracket using f(x,y) = y. Why does V being 0 at a point not imply that its bracket is 0 there?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The bracket is just U minus V at the chosen point.”

    It measures a difference between successive differentiations: DV · U − D(U) · V. At the origin V is 0, but its derivative with respect to x is not. Keep the expression for the field until after differentiation.

    Review the definition and the calculation of the Jacobians

  • “Changing the order does not change the sign, since the same commands are used.”

    The definition gives [V,U] = −[U,V]. You must also fix the chronological order of the loop: a map written on the left in a composition is applied after the one on the right. Checking on the function y lets you recover the sign without memorizing a diagram.

    Check the sign in the first exercise

  • “The bracket adds a direction, so the probability distribution already has a smooth density.”

    We have calculated a local rank for deterministic fields. A conclusion about a diffusion requires identifying its fields, the relevant family, and the assumptions of the theorem. Even finding a 0 bracket does not allow us to stop searching: iterated brackets may add a direction.

    Examine the iterated bracket in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Application · convention and check

Swap the two commands

Keep the plotter fields U and V. Calculate [V,U] and check its vertical component on f(x,y) = y. Then compare the direction spanned by this bracket with that of [U,V].

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write V(Uf) − U(Vf), with the operators in this order.

Hint 2

On the function y, U f is 0 and V f is x.

Compare with the solution

V(Uf) = 0, whereas U(Vf) = 1. Thus [V,U]f = 0 − 1 = -1. The horizontal component is 0: [V,U] = (0 ; -1) = −[U,V].

The orientation of the vector reverses; the vertical line spanned stays the same, because a vector and its opposite are multiples of each other. The sign matters when predicting the oriented displacement, even though this change does not alter the rank.

Check your reasoning

  • Write the subtraction in the order specified by the convention.
  • Recover the vertical component -1 by acting on y.
  • Distinguish the orientation of the vector from the line it spans.

Exercise 2 · Exact calculation · changing the step

Divide the step while keeping its square

Start again from (1 ; 2) with the same fields. Replace h with h′ = h/2 = 1/4. Without rounding, find the final state of the loop, the ratio of the two vertical defects, and the defect normalized by the square of h′. Predict the changes before calculating.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The symbolic composition gave a vertical defect h × h.

Hint 2

Dividing the step by 2 divides its square by 4. Then check using the exact movements.

Compare with the solution

We expect a smaller defect with the same sign. The new square is 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16. The final state is (1 ; 2 + 1/16) = (1 ; 33/16), giving a dimensionless y-coordinate of 2.0625.

The ratio new defect / old defect is 1/16/1/4 = 1/4, rather than 1/2. The defect is divided by 4. The normalized quotient is 1/16/1/16 = 1: the bracket has not changed.

To check on paper, compose Φ, then Ψ, then Φ with the opposite parameter, then Ψ with the opposite parameter, keeping h′ symbolic: y + h′(x + h′) − h′x = y + h′ × h′. The linear terms cancel as before.

Check your reasoning

  • Predict that the defect is divided by 4 before calculating.
  • Keep the exact fraction 33/16 until reading its decimal value.
  • Explain why the defect changes while its normalized coefficient remains 1.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · looking beyond the first bracket

The plotter changes its upward movement rule

On the same plotter, keep U and replace V with Ṽ(x,y) = (0, x × x). Calculate C = [U,Ṽ], then D = [U,C]. Evaluate them at the origin. Does the first bracket being 0 at this point suffice to conclude that all brackets are useless there? Justify the rank using U and D.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The command U differentiates with respect to x; its own Jacobian is 0.

Hint 2

First differentiate x × x, then differentiate the resulting expression, and only then evaluate at the origin.

Compare with the solution

Since D(U) is 0, C = DṼ · U = (0,2x). At the starting point x = 1, its vertical component would be 2; at the origin it is 0. Evaluating this field as 0 at the origin before differentiating it would erase its variation.

D = [U,C] = DC · U = (0,2). This is an iterated bracket: we have applied the same operation again to a field already obtained. At the origin, Ṽ and C are 0, but D is not.

The determinant of the columns U(o), C(o) is 0, whereas that of U(o), D(o) is 1 × 2 − 0 × 0 = 2. The latter is nonzero: U and D span the plane at the point considered. The first failure did not allow a conclusion. This remains a geometric calculation, with no claim about the probability distribution of a diffusion.

Check your reasoning

  • Differentiate the fields before evaluating them.
  • Obtain C = (0,2x) and D = (0,2).
  • Justify the rank using the nonzero determinant 2 and leave probabilistic conclusions open.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which convention defines the bracket here, and how is it expressed in coordinates?

[U,V]f = U(Vf) − V(Uf). In coordinates, [U,V] = DV · U − D(U) · V: differentiate the fields before evaluating them at the point.

Recall 2Which loop order produces the positive bracket here, and what should its displacement be normalized by?

Chronologically: U with parameter h, V with parameter h, U with parameter −h, V with parameter −h. Divide the final displacement by the square of the step h. In our model, the quotient is exactly (0,1); in general, take the limit as h tends to 0.

Recall 3Why can V be 0 at the origin and still have a nonzero bracket with U?

The bracket also uses the variation of the field around the point. Here V = (0,x) vanishes at the origin, but its derivative with respect to x is (0,1).

Recall 4With Ṽ = (0, x × x), why must we continue after the first bracket is 0 at the origin?

[U,Ṽ] = (0,2x) vanishes at the origin, but [U,[U,Ṽ]] = (0,2) does not vanish there. Together with U, it provides independent directions.

Recall 5What does our rank calculation prove, and what remains to be established for a diffusion?

It proves the independence of directions obtained from the fields and their brackets at the point studied. For a diffusion, we still need to specify the fields, the family of brackets, and the assumptions that allow a conclusion about its probability distribution.
