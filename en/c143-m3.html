Level 2 · C143·M3

Riemann integration

Your goalConstruct a volume using bounds, define the Riemann integral, and justify why the choice of sample points has no effect in the limit.

Before you start
c142-m5 : convergence criteria · c045-m5 : introductory quadrature · c046-m5 : approximation error · c143-m1 : rigorous continuity · c143-m2 : compactness

✿01 See the idea

A tank receives water for 2 min. The flow rate increases steadily: it starts at 0 L/min and reaches 2 L/min. What volume has entered? Multiplying the final flow rate by the entire duration would assume that the flow was already that strong at the start.

Divide the time into intervals. On each interval, keeping the smallest flow rate gives less water than the tank receives; keeping the largest gives more. This produces a lower and an upper bound for the volume, which we then try to bring closer together.

The diagrams show flow rate as height and time as width. A rectangle therefore has units L/min × min = L. Its area represents a volume, even though the diagram is a surface on the page. Both figures describe the same tank and the same partition.

The scenario and all its values are fictional. We assume that the model specifies the flow rate exactly, with no leaks, overflow, or water flowing out. We calculate the volume received, rather than a fill level, which would also require the shape of the tank.

The rule to remember

Bound each contribution between minimum flow rate × duration and maximum flow rate × duration; make the gap arbitrarily small to define a unique volume.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From provisional rectangles to a defined volume

The elapsed time t runs from 0 to T = 2 min. The model is q(t) = αt, with α = 1 litre per minute squared. Start with n = 4 equal intervals, then justify what happens as the intervals become small.

  1. Name the model and its units

    t is elapsed time, T is the total duration, q(t) is the instantaneous flow rate, and α is its rate of increase. t and T are in minutes; q is in litres per minute; α is in litres per minute squared. Here α is positive, so q is increasing and continuous on the closed interval [0, T].

    The symbol I will denote the total volume to be defined, in litres. No measurements of the tank are used: every physical conclusion remains conditional on the flow rate model.

  2. Construct bounds on each interval

    A partition P is a finite list of strictly increasing times tᵢ, from the first, with index 0, to the last, with index n. Its endpoints are 0 min and T. n is a positive integer with no units; i numbers the intervals from 1 to n. The width Δtᵢ is the difference between tᵢ and the previous time: it is a strictly positive duration, in minutes.

    mᵢ denotes the infimum of the flow rates on interval i and Mᵢ their supremum, in L/min. Each interval includes its endpoints. An infimum is the greatest lower bound; a supremum is the least upper bound. For our continuous flow rate on a compact interval, these bounds are attained: they are a minimum and a maximum. Since q is increasing, they occur at the left and right endpoints, respectively.

    mi=q(ti−1);Mi=q(ti)

    L(P), the lower sum, adds the small rectangles; U(P), the upper sum, adds the large ones. The symbol Σ means to add the terms for all the indicated indices. Each term and each sum are in litres.

    L(P)=∑i=1nmiΔti
    U(P)=∑i=1nMiΔti

  3. Set the times before multiplying

    The common width is h = T/n = 2/4 = 1/2 min, or 0.5 min. The times are 0; 0.5; 1; 1.5; 2 min. Applying q(t) = αt gives flow rates of 0; 0.5; 1; 1.5; 2 L/min.

    Each rectangle uses a width h and a flow rate, without adding these quantities. The choice of endpoint is justified only by the stated increase in flow rate.

  4. Add the lower volume estimates

    At the left endpoints, the successive products flow rate × duration are: 0 × 1/2 = 0 L; 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 L; 1 × 1/2 = 1/2 L; 3/2 × 1/2 = 3/4 L.

    We add volumes: 1/4 + 1/2 = 3/4 L, then 3/4 + 3/4 = 3/2 L. The first rectangle, with 0 area, does not change the sum. We obtain L(P) = 1.50 L.

  5. Construct the upper bound and check the bounds

    At the right endpoints, the volumes are 1/4; 1/2; 3/4; 1 L, because the last product is 2 × 1/2 = 1 L. This list takes the previous volumes, removes the 0 term, and adds this last rectangle. Thus U(P) = 3/2 + 1 = 5/2 L, or 2.50 L.

    The gap is U(P) − L(P) = 5/2 − 3/2 = 1 L. Once integrability is established, the volume I will lie between these bounds. Their midpoint C = (L(P) + U(P))/2 is 4/2 = 2 L and guarantees |C − I| ≤ 1/2 L using the bounds alone.

    This last quantity is an error bound, rather than an observed error. A single pair of bounds is still insufficient to define an integral: the bounds must be able to approach each other as closely as required.

  6. State exactly what “integrable” means

    For a function q bounded on [0, T], choose a time ξᵢ in each interval. The Riemann sum S(P, ξ) uses the flow rates chosen at these times; ξ is the list of these times. Whatever the list, L(P) ≤ S(P, ξ) ≤ U(P).

    S(P,ξ)=∑i=1nq(ξi)Δti

    The mesh |P| is the largest of the durations Δtᵢ. The function is Riemann integrable if there is a number I such that, for every tolerance ε > 0 in litres, we can choose a duration η > 0 in minutes for which every partition with mesh less than η, with any choice of points ξᵢ, satisfies |S(P, ξ) − I| < ε. The same value I must work for all these choices.

    I=∫0Tq(t)dt

    The sign ∫ denotes this integral; dt indicates that the variable of integration is time. The equivalent Darboux criterion is as follows: for every ε > 0, there is a partition P such that U(P) − L(P) < ε. It uses bounds over entire intervals, rather than isolated readings. The assumption “q is bounded” alone does not guarantee this criterion.

  7. Make the uniform bounds tend to the same value

    For n equal intervals, tᵢ = ih and h = T/n. The index n in Ln and Un denotes this uniform partition. The heights are α(i − 1)h on the left and αih on the right. Each area therefore contains h squared.

    The sum 0 + 1 + … + (n − 1) is n(n − 1)/2: writing the terms in reverse order and then adding term by term gives n pairs, each equal to n − 1. The sum from 1 to n follows by adding n. This gives the fractions below.

    Ln=αT22(1−1n)
    Un=αT22(1+1n)

    Their difference is α × T × T/n = 4/n L for the values in the scenario. For n = 4, we recover 1 L; for n = 8, we obtain 1/2 L. For every positive tolerance, choosing an integer n > (α × T × T)/ε makes this gap smaller than ε. The Darboux criterion is therefore satisfied.

    Both bounds converge to (α × T × T)/2 = 2 L. The integral is this common limit: the bounds determine I without having to use an antiderivative.

  8. Check nonuniform partitions as well

    For our affine flow rate and any partition, the variation in flow rate over interval i is αΔtᵢ. Thus U(P) − L(P) = αΣ(Δtᵢ × Δtᵢ) ≤ α|P|ΣΔtᵢ = αT|P|. We used Δtᵢ ≤ |P| and the fact that the durations add up to T. The unit of αT|P| is indeed the litre.

    On an interval with endpoints a and b, in minutes, the average of the lower and upper areas is α(a + b)(b − a)/2 = α(b × b − a × a)/2. Adding these differences cancels all the interior terms: (L(P) + U(P))/2 = (α × T × T)/2 = I. Every sum S(P, ξ) is therefore at most half the gap between the bounds away from this same value I.

    In particular, |S(P, ξ) − I| ≤ αT|P|. Taking η = ε/(αT) is enough to verify the definition for all partitions and all chosen points. α and T are strictly positive here. Adding many subdivision points elsewhere without reducing the longest interval does not give this guarantee.

    Independent geometric check: the graph encloses a triangle with base 2 min and height 2 L/min. Its area is 2 × 2/2 = 4/2 = 2 L. This recovers the volume defined by the sums. The exact equality of the midpoint of the bounds with I comes from the affine flow rate here; it does not hold for every integrable flow rate.

Explain it in your own words

Start again from the tank without looking at the formulas. Explain why the heights alone do not give a volume, where the increase in flow rate is used, and why a proof for all sufficiently fine meshes goes beyond calculating a single sum. Then reconstruct the bounds and their limit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I add the flow rates and have the volume.”

    A flow rate is still in L/min. Multiply each by the duration of its interval to obtain litres. On our partition, forgetting h = 1/2 min changes every contribution. Recalculate the products before adding anything.

    Review the rectangles and their units

  • “The left endpoint always gives the lower bound.”

    This rule uses the increase in our flow rate. For a decreasing flow rate, the roles are reversed; if the flow rate rises and then falls, a minimum may occur inside the interval. The definition uses bounds over the whole interval.

    Reread what the rectangles must bound

  • “Many intervals, or a sum that converges, are enough.”

    The number of intervals does not control the longest one. The definition covers every sufficiently fine partition and all chosen points. The last exercise keeps a wide interval to make this flaw visible.

    Examine the partition that leaves a large interval

Exercise 1 · Application

Double the number of intervals

Keep the same flow rate and total duration, but take n = 8 equal intervals. Calculate h, Lₙ, and Uₙ. Compare their gap with that in the example and check that 2 L remains between the bounds.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Express the bounds in terms of (α × T × T)/2 and the term (α × T × T)/(2n).

Hint 2

Half the gap is 2/n L for this model.

Compare with the solution

h = 2/8 = 1/4 min, or 0.25 min. Half the gap is 2/8 = 1/4 L. Thus Ln = 2 − 1/4 = 7/4 L and Un = 2 + 1/4 = 9/4 L.

The displayed values are 1.75 L and 2.25 L. The gap is 9/4 − 7/4 = 1/2 L, half the initial 1 L. The volume 2 L remains between the bounds; the flow rate has not changed, only the precision of the guarantee has changed.

Check your reasoning

  • Deduce the duration of an interval from the total duration.
  • Give both bounds in litres and justify their meaning.
  • Compare the gaps without attributing a change in volume to the flow rate.

Exercise 2 · Justified choice

Choose a partition for an error guarantee

Using only the general rule |C − I| ≤ (Uₙ − Lₙ)/2, where C is the midpoint of the bounds, find a sufficient condition on n to guarantee an error strictly less than 1/8 L. Propose n = 20 and verify the guarantee. We are practising reasoning with bounds, even though the affine geometry has already given I.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Half the gap is (α × T × T)/(2n); here it is 2/n L.

Hint 2

Solve 2/n < 1/8 with n positive. A strict inequality requires exceeding the threshold.

Compare with the solution

The sufficient condition is 2/n < 1/8, so n > 2/(1/8) = 16. At n = 16, the guarantee gives only ≤ 1/8 L: this is insufficient to deduce the required strict inequality from this guarantee.

For n = 20, h = 1/10 min, the bounds are 19/10 L and 21/10 L, or 1.90 L and 2.10 L. Their gap is 1/5 L. Their midpoint is 2 L and its guaranteed error is at most 1/10 L = 0.10 L, less than 0.125 L.

The guaranteed bound 1/10 L is conservative: for this affine flow rate, C equals I exactly. An error bound gives a guarantee, rather than a measurement of an error actually made.

Check your reasoning

  • State that the condition is sufficient for the chosen guarantee.
  • Handle the threshold and the strictness of the inequality correctly.
  • Distinguish an error bound from the actual error.

Exercise 3 · Justification

The number of intervals can be misleading

A classmate subdivides [0, 1] min into as many pieces as they wish, but leaves [1, 2] min whole. They claim that the number of intervals tending to infinity is enough to force all Riemann sums on these partitions to have a common limit. Refute this proof for our flow rate, then explain how to repair it.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The mesh is the maximum of the widths, rather than their average.

Hint 2

On the interval left whole, compare the rectangle at the left endpoint with the one at the right endpoint.

Compare with the solution

The mesh remains 1 min: the interval [1, 2] min does not shrink. Its extreme flow rates are 1 and 2 L/min. Its contribution to U(P) − L(P) is (2 − 1) × 1 = 1 L. The total gap therefore remains at least 1 L.

We can choose identical points in all the other intervals, then choose the left or right endpoint in this fixed interval. The resulting sums then differ by 1 L, regardless of the number of subdivisions elsewhere. They cannot all tend to the same value. This invalidates the classmate’s argument; the integrability of the flow rate, proved in the lesson, remains true.

Repair: subdivide this large interval as well, so that the mesh of the whole partition tends to 0 min. The bound U(P) − L(P) ≤ αT|P| then ensures that all choices of points become consistent with I.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify the interval responsible and its width.
  • Calculate a persistent gap in litres.
  • Repair the family of partitions and preserve the distinction between a flawed proof and a nonintegrable function.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does each term of a Riemann sum multiply in the tank scenario?

The flow rate q(ξᵢ), in L/min, by the duration Δtᵢ of its interval, in min. The term is a volume in litres; ξᵢ is chosen in the interval.

Recall 2What must you know to form the lower and upper bounds?

The bounds on the flow rate over each entire interval and the width of that interval. In our increasing model, the left and right endpoints give these extrema; isolated readings do not guarantee this for an arbitrary flow rate.

Recall 3Which choices must the Riemann definition make irrelevant in the limit?

All partitions with sufficiently small mesh and all points chosen in their intervals must give sums close to the same I. For every positive tolerance, a sufficient mesh bound must exist.

Recall 4Which gap is enough to control to establish integrability of a bounded function?

For every positive tolerance, find a partition whose gap U(P) − L(P) is smaller than that tolerance. For our affine flow rate, this gap is (α × T × T)/n on a uniform partition.

Recall 5Is half the gap between the bounds the exact error of their midpoint?

It bounds this error once I lies between the bounds. It may exceed the actual error: the midpoint gives I exactly in our affine model, while half the gap remains positive for a finite partition.
