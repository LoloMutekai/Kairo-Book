Level 2 · C052·M1

Euler

Your goalConstruct an approximation using explicit Euler, recalculate the slope at each step, and distinguish checking the calculation from the accuracy of the trajectory.

Before you start
c049-m5 : series solutions · c051-m5 : error diagnostics · c013-m5 : recursion

✿01 See the idea

A cup is at 60 °C in a room at 20 °C. Initially, our model predicts a slope of -10 °C/min: the temperature is decreasing. To predict where it will be after 1 min, imagine that this slope stays constant throughout this short interval. The change would be -10 °C; the predicted temperature would be 50 °C.

This is the explicit Euler procedure: start from a known point, advance using its slope, then repeat from the resulting point. The cup cools more slowly as it approaches the surrounding air temperature. The slope must therefore be recalculated at the start of the next step. The segments in the diagram form an approximation, even if their endpoints were calculated without arithmetic errors.

The scene and all its values are fictional. We assume a uniform temperature in the cup, a constant air temperature, and no heat input. The chosen cooling rule is used to learn the calculation; it has not been fitted to a real cup.

The rule to remember

At each step: evaluate the slope at the starting point, multiply it by the duration, add this change to the current state, then advance the clock.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Predict cooling over 2 min

The cup starts at 60 °C at t = 0 min, the air stays at 20 °C, and the cooling rate is k = 0.25 per minute. Using a step h = 1 min, let us calculate the approximate temperatures at times 1 min and 2 min.

  1. State the law and its assumptions

    t is time in minutes. T(t) is the continuous temperature predicted by the model, in °C. A is the constant air temperature, in °C. k is a positive rate, in inverse minutes, held constant. The symbol T′ denotes the derivative with respect to time: a slope in °C/min. The chosen law states that the slope is proportional to the difference from the air temperature, with a sign that brings the temperature back toward A.

    T′(t)=−k(T(t)−A)

    Here A = 20 °C, k = 1/4 per minute, and T(0) = 60 °C. Above A, the slope is negative; at A, it is 0. These properties describe our mathematical law under the stated assumptions for the cup.

  2. Move from the tangent to a calculation rule

    Over a short duration h, replacing the trajectory with its tangent at the start gives T(t + h) ≈ T(t) + hT′(t). The symbol ≈ means “approximately equal.” This replacement neglects the change in slope during the step; it becomes an equality over an interval where the slope is constant.

    Write f(t, y) = −k(y − A). The function f gives a slope in °C/min from a time t and a temperature y. It does not depend explicitly on t in this example. n is the step number, a dimensionless integer starting at 0; tn is its starting time, in minutes; yn is the approximation of T(tn), in °C. h is a strictly positive duration, in minutes. The index n + 1 denotes the next point.

    tn+1=tn+h
    yn+1=yn+hf(tn,yn)

    This equality defines the Euler sequence exactly, rather than the continuous solution. “Explicit” means that the entire right-hand side is known before calculating the next point. We start at time 0 min with the initial temperature 60 °C.

  3. Calculate the first slope, then the change

    Calculate the signed difference toward the air temperature: A − y = 20 − 60 = -40 °C. Multiplying by k gives the starting slope: 0.25 × (-40) = -10 °C/min. This is the same law as −k(y − A), rewritten to keep the sign visible.

    A slope is not yet a change in temperature. Multiply it by the duration h: 1 min × (-10 °C/min) = -10 °C. The minutes cancel. We can then add this change to the starting temperature.

    y1=60+1×(-10)=50 °C

    The clock also advances: 0 + 1 = 1 min. We keep the time–temperature pair: (1 min ; 50 °C).

  4. Start again from the resulting temperature

    At the start of the next step, the difference is 20 − 50 = -30 °C. The new slope is 0.25 × (-30) = -7.5 °C/min. Its absolute value is smaller: the cup is closer to the air temperature. Reusing the first slope would ignore this change.

    The new change in temperature is 1 × (-7.5) = -7.5 °C. The temperature becomes 50 + (-7.5) = 42.5 °C, or exactly 85/2 °C. The time becomes 1 + 1 = 2 min.

    The procedure can be repeated: keep the old pair, calculate its slope and the resulting change, then produce the new pair. If f also depended on time, it would need the old time tn along with the old temperature yn.

  5. Check using another form

    Subtract A from the Euler update. The temperature excess above the air temperature is multiplied by the dimensionless factor 1 − kh. We have only rearranged the same equality; this form provides another calculation method.

    yn+1−A=(1−kh)(yn−A)

    Here kh = 0.25 × 1 = 0.25, so the factor is 0.75. The initial excess is 60 − 20 = 40 °C. After the first step: 0.75 × 40 = 30 °C, then 20 + 30 = 50 °C. After the next step: 0.75 × 30 = 22.5 °C, then 20 + 22.5 = 42.5 °C.

    The two methods agree. With this factor between 0 and 1, the calculated temperatures stay above 20 °C and decrease; if we started exactly at A, the slope would remain 0. These checks can detect, for example, a reversed sign or a change added to the wrong state.

  6. State precisely what has been checked

    Fractions make the operations in this example exact. They do not remove the Euler approximation: the model’s slope changes during each interval, whereas the step uses only its starting value. The previous check confirms the calculated sequence; it does not measure its difference from T(t).

    To compare different step sizes, we must reach the same final time. The last exercise will make this comparison using the same cup. Global error, stability, and a convergence protocol will be studied in the units that follow. No real readings have been used here.

Explain it in your own words

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The slope is already the decrease to add.”

    The slope is in °C/min. Multiply it by the duration in minutes before adding it to a temperature. With 0.5 min, the same initial slope gives a change of -5 °C, rather than -10 °C. The coinciding numerical values for 1 min can hide this error.

    Review the slope–duration–change diagram

  • “I can keep the initial slope for every step.”

    The law depends on the current temperature. Keeping the first change would give 50 + (-10) = 40 °C at 2 min. Euler recalculates the slope at 50 °C and gives 42.5 °C. Use the resulting pair, then evaluate the law again.

    Return to the start of the cup’s second step

  • “The numbers are exact and the curve goes down: I have the true solution.”

    Exact arithmetic and a plausible evolution do not make the slope constant in the model. Compare a full step and half-steps at the same time. Their difference reveals an effect of the time subdivision; it does not directly give the error relative to the continuous solution.

    Compare the two time subdivisions in the last exercise

Exercise 3 · Diagnostic · compare the same duration

A full step or half-steps?

Starting from 60 °C, compare the temperature at 1 min obtained with one step of 1 min and the temperature obtained with 2 steps of 0.5 min. The first half-step gives 55 °C. Calculate the second by reevaluating the slope. Give the difference between the two final temperatures. Is this difference the exact Euler error?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

After the first half-step, the slope is no longer -10 °C/min: evaluate f at 55 °C.

Hint 2

The last half-step still lasts 0.5 min. Compare its final temperature with 50 °C, rather than the temperature in the example at 2 min.

Compare with the solution

At 0.5 min, the difference toward the air temperature is 20 − 55 = -35 °C. The slope is 0.25 × (-35) = -8.75 °C/min. The next change is 0.5 × (-8.75) = -4.375 °C.

After the 2 half-steps, the time is indeed 2 × 0.5 = 1 min, and the temperature is 55 + (-4.375) = 50.625 °C, or 405/8 °C. The full step gave 50 °C at the same time. The difference is 50.625 − 50 = 0.625 °C, or 5/8 °C.

The result depends on the time subdivision. This difference compares two approximations, neither of which has been taken as an exact reference. By itself, it is therefore not the exact error of either approximation and does not demonstrate convergence. It also does not prove that the law is appropriate for a real cup.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you reconstruct an explicit Euler step using a slope law f?

From the current pair (tₙ, yₙ), calculate f(tₙ, yₙ), then h times this slope. Add the change to yₙ and h to tₙ. The right-hand side uses only the known starting pair.

Recall 2Which units distinguish slope, change, and temperature in the cup?

The slope is in °C/min. Multiplied by h in minutes, it gives a change in °C. This change is added to the current temperature, also in °C; it does not replace it.

Recall 3Why reevaluate the slope at each starting point, even with a constant step size?

Here the law depends on temperature. The new point is closer to the air temperature: its slope is less negative. A constant step size fixes the duration of each interval, rather than the slope of all the segments.

Recall 4Do exact operations on fractions give the exact continuous solution?

They give the values of the Euler sequence exactly. At each step, this sequence replaces the trajectory with a slope taken at the start; it remains an approximation when the slope changes during the step.

Recall 5What can the difference between a full step and half-steps tell us?

At the same final time, it shows here that the time subdivision changes the result. It compares two approximations; without further justification, it is neither the exact error nor a proof of convergence.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the formulas, reconstruct the cup’s trajectory: law and units, starting point, slope, duration, change, new state, and updated clock. Then explain why the slope must be reevaluated and propose the check using the excess above the air temperature. Finish by distinguishing exact operations, approximation of the continuous solution, and the model’s suitability for reality.
