Power series

Your goalRecognize a power series, find its radius of convergence, and check the endpoints separately before assigning it a sum.

Before you start
c031-m5 : asymptotes · c045-m5 : introductory quadrature · c046-m1 : series tests

✿01 See the idea

You line up pieces of ribbon end to end. The first measures 80 cm. To get the next piece, divide the length of the previous one by 2: 40 cm, then 20 cm, then 10 cm. The ribbon keeps getting longer, but the additions get smaller. Can we give this construction a limiting length without ever finishing the list?

The scene and its values are fictional. We ignore thickness and joins, and allow pieces as small as needed. The infinitely many pieces belong to the mathematical model, rather than a construction we could actually make. The sum of the first few lengths is visible; the limiting length needs a proof.

Now replace the ratio 1/2 with a variable setting x: the ratio of a length to the previous length. For each fixed x, we obtain a numerical series, an addition whose partial sums we study. By allowing x to vary, we obtain a power series: the same coefficients multiply successive powers of the variable.

For the ribbon, x is positive or 0. To study the whole series on the real line, we will also allow negative x: the terms then become signed contributions, rather than negative physical lengths. In the first diagram, n indicates the index of the piece, starting at 0; the setting is x = 0.50. The second diagram describes the full mathematical domain; R denotes the distance from the center to an endpoint, called the radius of convergence.

The rule to remember

Fix the coefficients and the center; determine where the partial sums converge; examine the endpoints separately. A sum formula holds only on the domain where its equality with the series has been proved.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From ribbon to a function of its setting

The first piece measures L = 80 cm; each subsequent length is multiplied by x, which is dimensionless. At the setting x = 1/2, let us calculate the sum up to index N = 3, then the infinite sum if it exists. We will also determine the settings for which this infinite sum makes sense.

  1. Distinguish coefficient, term, and sum

    A real power series is an expression of the following form. The symbol Σ means that we add; n runs through the integers starting at 0. The center c is fixed. The variable x and the center have the same unit here: none. The coefficients a subscript n are fixed before choosing x; in this lesson, they are in centimeters.

    ∑n=0∞an(x−c)n;bn(x)=an(x−c)n

    We will use b subscript n for the term actually added, S subscript N for the finite sum of the terms from index 0 to N, and S for the limit of these sums when it exists. N is a nonnegative integer. At the center, the constant term is a subscript 0 and all terms with a strictly positive index vanish: no ambiguous power is needed.

    SN(x)=∑n=0Nbn(x);S(x)=limN→∞SN(x)

    For the ribbon, c = 0 and all coefficients equal L = 80 cm. This does not make all the pieces equal: the coefficient remains 80 cm, while the power of x reduces the term. A power series need not converge for every x. A formal series keeps only the list of coefficients, without asserting that a numerical sum exists.

  2. Define the radius before looking for a sum

    The radius of convergence R is the boundary in terms of distance from the center: if |x − c| < R, the series converges absolutely, meaning that the sum of the absolute values of the terms is finite. If |x − c| > R, it diverges. At a distance of exactly R, each endpoint must be studied separately. The bars | | denote absolute value.

    A radius can equal 0: only the center is then guaranteed. It can also be infinite: all real x then work. In the case of a finite, strictly positive radius studied here, the endpoints are c − R and c + R. R is dimensionless, like x. The radius theorem describes the shape of the domain without automatically giving its value.

    A tool from series tests lets us calculate it in a common case. If the coefficients are nonzero from some index onward and the ratio of their absolute values tends to a finite, strictly positive number ℓ, the radius is its reciprocal. ℓ denotes this limit, which is dimensionless here.

    ℓ=limn→∞|an+1||an|;R=1ℓ

    Why? For x different from the center, the ratio of the absolute values of the terms tends to ℓ|x − c|. The ratio test gives convergence if this limit is less than 1, and divergence if it is greater than 1. At equality, this test is inconclusive. If its assumptions about the coefficients fail, this procedure does not apply as stated.

  3. Find the radius for the ribbon

    The coefficients are constant and nonzero: their ratio is 80/80 = 1 at every index. So ℓ = 1 and R = 1/1 = 1. For x different from 0, we can also calculate the ratio of the terms directly: its absolute value is |x|.

    |Lxn+1||Lxn|=|x|

    Thus, for |x| < 1, the series converges absolutely; for |x| > 1, the terms do not even tend to 0. At the center x = 0, only the first piece remains and the sum is 80 cm. The radius is independent of the choice of 80 cm as the nonzero initial length. The endpoints still need to be examined.

  4. Calculate what the finite sum contains

    The setting x = 0.50 satisfies |x| < 1. Each multiplication adds a power of x: x squared is 1/4, and x cubed is 1/8. The terms are therefore 80 cm, 80 × 1/2 = 40 cm, 80 × 1/4 = 20 cm, and 80 × 1/8 = 10 cm.

    S3=80+40+20+10=150 cm

    The addition proceeds as follows: 80 + 40 = 120 cm; 120 + 20 = 140 cm; 140 + 10 = 150 cm. This value adds 4 terms. It cannot be the infinite sum, since another piece of 5 cm will still be added.

  5. Take the limit starting from a finite equality

    The geometric formula can be proved without subtracting two unknown infinite sums. Multiplying the finite sum by x shifts each term by one index. On subtraction, the intermediate terms cancel: the first term remains, minus the term with index N + 1.

    (1−x)SN=L(1−xN+1)

    At our index N = 3, x to the power 4 is 1/16. Check the equality: x times the partial sum is 1/2 × 150 = 75 cm. Subtraction gives 150 − 75 = 75 cm; the other side gives 80 − 5 = 75 cm. Both calculations agree.

    When |x| < 1, x to the power N + 1 tends to 0 as N increases without limit. Since 1 − x is nonzero, we can divide the finite equality and then take the limit. We then obtain the following equality, valid only on this domain.

    S(x)=L1−x pour |x|<1

  6. Obtain the limiting length and check it

    The denominator is 1 − 1/2 = 1/2. Dividing the initial length by this number gives the sum: 80 ÷ (1/2) = 160 cm. The parentheses indicate division by the whole fraction. We keep exact fractions throughout the reasoning; in French decimal notation, the setting is 0.50, dimensionless, and the result is 160.00 cm.

    Independent check: if the infinite construction has length S, removing the first piece leaves a copy of the same construction scaled by x. We must therefore have S − xS = L. Our answer satisfies (1 − 1/2) × 160 = 80 cm, the length of the first piece. The partial sum 150 cm remains less than 160 cm, which is appropriate since all additions here are positive.

    This algebraic check does not replace the proof of convergence: an equation can have a solution even when the series diverges. We first established |x| < 1. General control of truncation error will be studied later; here we only distinguish the finite sum from the limit.

  7. Examine the endpoints without extending the formula

    At x = 1, every term equals 80 cm: the terms do not tend to 0 and the series diverges. At x = -1, the terms alternate between 80 cm and -80 cm. The partial sums alternate between 80 cm and 0 cm; they have no limit. Alternating signs alone therefore do not ensure convergence.

    The real domain of convergence is therefore (-1, 1). The physical ribbon uses only the nonnegative settings in this interval. Yet the fraction L/(1 − x) can be evaluated at x = -1: it equals 80/2 = 40 cm. This number is not the sum of the divergent series. The domain of a rational expression can be larger than the domain of its series expansion.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations. Explain why the coefficient is constant while the pieces get shorter, why the radius does not decide what happens at the endpoints, and why the fraction that can be evaluated at the negative endpoint does not give a sum. Then reconstruct the subtraction of the finite sums.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The coefficient at index 2 is 20 cm, since that is the length of the piece.”

    The coefficient is what multiplies the power, before choosing x: it remains 80 cm. The term at the setting 1/2 is 80 × 1/4 = 20 cm. Separate the rule from its value at the chosen setting.

    Review the definitions of coefficient and term

  • “R = 1 means that both endpoints are included.”

    The theorem guarantees convergence at distances strictly less than the radius. At an equal distance, replace x with each endpoint and study the resulting numerical series. Here, the terms do not tend to 0 at either endpoint.

    Check both endpoints in the last exercise

  • “If L/(1 − x) gives a number, the series must equal that number.”

    The formula was obtained using a power that tends to 0, which here requires |x| < 1. At the negative endpoint, the fraction gives 40 cm, but the partial sums oscillate. Calculating a fraction does not create the missing limit.

    Find the assumption needed to take the limit

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · read the symbols

What is fixed and what is evaluated

In the ribbon series, give the center, the coefficient at index 2, the term at that index for x = 1/2, and the sum up to that index. How many pieces does this sum contain? Explain the difference between coefficient and term.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The coefficient is fixed before choosing the setting. To obtain the term, multiply it by the power corresponding to the index.

Hint 2

The initial index is 0: list the indices before counting the pieces.

Compare with the solution

The center is 0. The coefficient at index 2 is 80 cm, like all the others. The term is 80 × (1/2) to the power 2, or 80 × 1/4 = 20 cm. The setting affects the term, rather than the coefficient.

The sum up to index 2 is 80 + 40 + 20 = 140 cm. It contains 3 pieces, with indices 0, 1, and 2. The last term 20 cm is neither the coefficient nor the sum.

Check your reasoning

  • Name the center 0 and the coefficient 80 cm.
  • Calculate the term 20 cm, then the sum 140 cm, using different operations.
  • Count 3 pieces, including the initial index.

Exercise 2 · Application · change the setting

Pieces that get shorter more slowly

Keep the first piece at 80 cm, but take x = 3/4. Without repeating the whole theory, justify convergence, calculate the terms up to index 2, their sum, and the limiting length. Before calculating, predict whether this limit will be greater or less than 160 cm.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The coefficients have not changed: recall the radius, then compare |x| with that radius.

Hint 2

A larger ratio retains more length at each index. Calculate the finite sum and L/(1 − x) separately.

Compare with the solution

The radius remains 1 and |3/4| < 1: the series converges. The subsequent pieces are longer than those in the example, so the limiting length must increase. The square of the new setting is 9/16.

The terms are 80 cm, 80 × 3/4 = 60 cm, and 80 × 9/16 = 45 cm. Addition first gives 140 cm, then 185 cm. This is the finite sum up to index 2.

The complement is 1 − 3/4 = 1/4. The limit is 80 ÷ (1/4) = 320 cm. It does exceed 160 cm and the finite sum 185 cm. Check: 1/4 × 320 = 80 cm, the first piece. Changing x changes the sum while preserving the power series and its radius.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify convergence using a strict inequality before using the formula.
  • Obtain 185 cm as the finite sum and 320 cm as the limit, without confusing them.
  • Predict and explain the direction of the change, then check the equation for the sum.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · check an argument

Is a fraction that can be evaluated enough?

Someone claims: “The radius is 1, so the endpoints work. At x = -1, the sum is 40 cm because the fraction L/(1 − x) exists.” Evaluate the terms and the partial sums separately at both endpoints. Give the exact domain of convergence and explain where the argument loses its justification.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A necessary condition for convergence is that the term being added tends to 0.

Hint 2

At the negative endpoint, write the successive sums after one piece and then after two pieces. Do they approach the same number?

Hint 3

In the proof of the formula, find the power that had to tend to 0.

Compare with the solution

At the positive endpoint x = 1, each term equals 80 cm. The partial sums grow without limit. At the negative endpoint x = -1, the terms alternate between 80 cm and -80 cm; the partial sums alternate between 80 cm and 0 cm. Neither series converges.

The domain is (-1, 1). The fraction exists at the negative endpoint and equals 40 cm, but the power of x does not tend to 0. The step of taking the limit is therefore no longer valid. The radius guaranteed nothing at the endpoints, and the existence of a fraction does not prove the existence of the sum we wanted to assign to it.

Check your reasoning

  • Study both endpoints, instead of concluding from the ratio test alone in the equality case.
  • Use either the failure of the term to tend to 0 or the partial sums to prove divergence.
  • Give the open interval and identify precisely the assumption lost in the formula.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What varies and what stays fixed in a power series?

The variable x varies; the center c and all coefficients are fixed. For each x, we study the limit of the sums of the coefficients multiplied by successive powers of x − c. Writing the expression alone does not guarantee this limit.

Recall 2For the ribbon at the setting 1/2, why do 80 cm and 20 cm refer to different things?

80 cm is the fixed coefficient at index 2. 20 cm is the term evaluated at that index: the coefficient multiplied by 1/4. The sum up to that index also includes the preceding terms.

Recall 3What does the radius guarantee, and what remains to be checked?

Absolute convergence at distances from the center strictly less than the radius; divergence at greater distances. For a finite positive radius, each endpoint must be examined separately. The radius alone does not say whether these endpoints are included.

Recall 4Under what assumptions does taking the reciprocal of the coefficient ratio limit give the radius?

The coefficients must be nonzero from some index onward; the ratio of their absolute values must tend to a finite, strictly positive limit. The radius is then the reciprocal of this limit. The test leaves the endpoints to be studied.

Recall 5Why does L/(1 − x) fail to give the ribbon sum for every x where this fraction exists?

The proof uses the power of x tending to 0 as the index increases, here for |x| < 1. At x = -1, the fraction equals 40 cm, but the partial sums oscillate between 80 cm and 0 cm. They have no limit.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the chain: fixed coefficients and center → term at the chosen setting → partial sums → radius → endpoint checks → sum function on its domain. For the ribbon, find the radius again and justify the formula by subtracting finite sums. Then explain why its fraction can exist outside the domain of the series.
