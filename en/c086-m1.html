Level 2 · C086·M1

Homeostasis

Your goalReconstruct a homeostatic loop, calculate a temperature adjustment and distinguish observed stability from demonstrated regulation.

Before you start
c085-m5 : development and environment · c083-m5 : transfer · c084-m5 : misleading decline

✿01 See the idea

After a walk, a person enters a room and stops. Their heat production and heat exchange with the air change. To stay within a range compatible with its functioning, their body adjusts its heat exchange. Homeostasis is this active maintenance of internal conditions despite disturbances; it does not require conditions to remain perfectly unchanged.

Let us focus only on internal temperature. Sensors sensitive to temperature provide signals. Thermoregulatory centres, including the hypothalamus, a region of the brain, integrate these signals. Effectors are the tissues that carry out the response: for example, sweat glands produce sweat, whose evaporation can remove heat. Heat exchange also depends on conditions in the room.

The response changes what will be measured next: this is feedback. It is called negative when it opposes the initial deviation. If the temperature exceeds the reference, a response that helps lower it reduces this deviation. “Negative” describes the direction of this loop; it means neither a bad response nor a negative temperature.

The person, temperatures and coefficients on this page are fictional. We will use a reference c = 37.0 °C and an initial temperature T = 38.0 °C. These choices serve the calculation; they define neither an individual norm nor a clinical threshold. The diagram combines several biological mechanisms into a simplified loop.

The rule to remember

Name the regulated quantity, its measurement, its reference and the effectors; check that their action reduces the deviation, then measure again. A stable value alone does not demonstrate this loop.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A thermoregulation step in the same room

Our model moves a fraction g = 1/2 of the deviation back towards the reference during Δt = 1 min. Initially, T = 38.0 °C and c = 37.0 °C. During this step, the disturbing input p is fixed at 0 °C. Let us calculate the predicted temperature, then check the direction and size of the change.

  1. Give each symbol a meaning

    T is the internal temperature at the start of the step; T′, with a prime, is the temperature predicted at its end. c is a regulation reference that is fixed over this step. These temperatures are in degrees Celsius. e = T − c is the signed deviation: it is positive above the reference. A temperature difference is also expressed in °C.

    Δt denotes the duration of the step, in minutes. g is the fraction of the deviation corrected over this step and has no unit. u is the temperature change attributed to the regulatory response during this step, in °C. It is neither a sweat flow rate nor a quantity of heat. p is an additional disturbing temperature change during the same step, in °C; a positive p represents a net input that warms the body. p does not denote the air temperature.

    Calculation assumptions: exact measurement of T; constant reference; response proportional to the deviation, with no delay; effectors capable of providing this response; no additional change outside p. We assume 0 < g < 1 for this rule. The coefficient is defined for Δt = 1 min: changing the duration would require revising the coefficient.

    The body is not actually a uniform reservoir with a perfect sensor. The following rule is a simplified model of the direction of feedback, with effects grouped into temperature changes; it does not predict a person’s recovery.

  2. Measure the deviation from the correct reference

    We subtract the reference from the internal temperature. This shows which side of the reference the system is on. Comparing T with the room temperature would answer a different question: that of heat exchange with the surroundings.

    e=T−c=38.0−37.0=1.0 °C

    The deviation is +1.0 °C. To reduce it, the corrective change must therefore lower T.

  3. Choose the sign and size of the response

    The rule u = −ge takes the opposite sign to the deviation. We can write it as u = g(c − T), which directly shows the change needed to reach the reference. The coefficient g sets the fraction of this change that is made.

    u=g(c−T)=12×(37.0−38.0)

    The signed change c − T is -1.0 °C. Half of it gives u = -1/2 °C, or -0.5 °C over the step. Multiplying a fraction with no unit by a difference in °C does indeed produce a change in °C.

  4. Account for all changes over the step

    The next temperature starts from T, then includes the corrective change u and the disturbance p. We add changes to a temperature, all expressed in the same unit.

    T′=T+u+p
    T′=38.0+(-0.5)+0=37.5 °C

    The total change u + p is -0.5 °C. The predicted temperature after 1 min is 37.5 °C. We have not yet observed this value in a person.

  5. Check the result using another expression

    Without a disturbance, expanding T + g(c − T) gives (1 − g)T + gc. Here, the weights are equal: the result must be the average of the initial temperature and the reference.

    T′=38.0+37.02=37.5 °C

    The remaining deviation is 37.5 − 37.0 = 0.5 °C. The ratio of remaining deviation to initial deviation is 1/2, equal to 1 − g = 1/2. The result lies between the initial temperature and the reference, and the deviation has decreased: the sign and size are consistent.

    This check applies to this step and these assumptions. Feedback acting in the correct direction may regulate poorly if its response arrives too late or if the effectors are insufficient; the name “negative” alone does not guarantee stability.

  6. Close the loop with a new measurement

    To continue, the sensors must provide the temperature at the start of the next step. In our simplified model with perfect measurement and no additional event, it is 37.5 °C. The deviation must be recalculated from this value, rather than repeating the first corrective change.

    The temperature can approach a reference without being exactly equal to it. In biology, a regulated range can also vary with the context; the fixed reference here belongs to the model. This unit stops at the loop and its balance of changes, before reward, motivation and stress.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Homeostasis means that the temperature never changes.”

    A disturbance can move the quantity away from its reference while regulation acts. In our step, T goes from 38.0 to 37.5 °C: the deviation decreases without disappearing. Reconstruct the roles and the return of measurement information instead of looking for a perfectly horizontal line.

    Review the loop and the change in temperature

  • “The deviation is positive, so I add a positive corrective change.”

    With e = T − c, a positive deviation means that T is too high relative to c. Taking u = +ge here would give 38.5 °C, with a deviation of 1.5 °C: this makes it larger. To reduce it, use u = −ge and check the result.

    Review the sign and the balance of changes

  • “The temperature is stable, so the effectors are no longer doing anything.”

    A response can exactly compensate for a disturbing input. Conversely, passive cooling can also change T without a regulatory loop. The curve alone cannot distinguish these mechanisms; the measurement, response and disturbance must be specified.

Exercise 1 · Identification and justification

The curve is not enough

In the same scene, we observe only a decrease in temperature after the walk. Name the regulated quantity, the sensors, the comparison and the effectors in our explanation. Is this decrease enough to establish negative feedback? Give another possible explanation without changing the scene.

Hint 1

Ask yourself what provides information and what changes heat exchange.

Hint 2

The room can allow heat loss even if no change in the regulatory response has been demonstrated.

Compare with the solution

The regulated quantity is internal temperature. Sensors sensitive to temperature provide the signals; thermoregulatory centres integrate them in relation to a reference. Sweat glands are possible effectors: the sweat they produce can remove heat as it evaporates.

The decrease alone does not demonstrate the loop. After walking stops, heat production can decrease and passive heat exchange with the room can contribute to the decrease. To establish feedback, we would need to link the measured deviation to an appropriate response, then to its effect on the quantity, while accounting for these other contributions.

Check your reasoning

  • You distinguish sensors, centres and effectors, with a role for each.
  • You relate the direction of the response to the deviation from the reference.
  • You propose a competing explanation and do not turn an observed decrease into proof of the mechanism.

Start again at T = 37.5 °C with c = 37.0 °C, g = 1/2 and p = 0 °C over the same step Δt = 1 min. Calculate the new deviation, u and T′. Explain why repeating the first corrective change would be an error.

Hint 1

The new value of T replaces the initial temperature in the example. The reference does not change.

Hint 2

First calculate c − T, then its fraction g; then add the corrective change to T.

Compare with the solution

The new deviation is e = 37.5 − 37.0 = 0.5 °C. The change needed to reach the reference is c − T = -0.50 °C.

The corrective change is u = 1/2 × (-1/2) = -1/4 °C, or -0.25 °C. The balance gives T′ = 37.50 + (-0.25) + 0 = 37.25 °C.

Check: the final deviation is 0.25 °C, again half the previous deviation. We also find 37.25 °C as the midpoint between 37.50 °C and 37.00 °C. Repeating -0.5 °C would ignore the new measurement: the rule requires a fraction of the new deviation.

  • You start from 37.5 °C and define the new deviation.
  • You obtain a corrective change of -0.25 °C, then 37.25 °C.
  • You check using the final deviation or the midpoint, explaining why the response changes.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same scene

A constant temperature with an active response

A variation on the same minute: the person starts at T = 37.5 °C, with the same reference and the same g. A constant disturbing input adds p = +0.25 °C during each step. Calculate u, then T′. Can we say “their temperature stays at this value because regulation does nothing”? Are they exactly at the reference?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The corrective change still depends on c − T, while p is added separately in the balance.

Hint 2

Compare the value of u with that of p before calculating the new temperature.

Hint 3

If T′ = T with the same conditions in the next step, the same balance can repeat; also check the deviation from c.

Compare with the solution

The initial deviation is 0.5 °C. The predicted response is u = 1/2 × (37.0 − 37.5) = -0.25 °C. The input p is +0.25 °C: the changes cancel each other out.

The balance is u + p = -0.25 + 0.25 = 0 °C. So T′ = 37.5 + 0 = 37.5 °C. The value remains constant under these conditions, with a nonzero corrective response.

The deviation from the reference remains 0.5 °C. In this proportional rule, that deviation sustains the response that compensates for the input. Heat exchange continues despite a net change of 0: this is dynamic maintenance, not a halt in the processes. This does not demonstrate that this model describes all biological mechanisms.

Check by removing the disturbance: we return to the case in the previous exercise and T falls to 37.25 °C. The input therefore changes the balance without changing the corrective direction of the response.

Check your reasoning

  • You calculate u and p separately and find a total change of 0.
  • You distinguish an unchanged temperature, a nonzero response and a deviation from the reference.
  • You limit the conclusion to the model and the stated conditions, without attributing an intention to the body.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does homeostasis maintain, and why should it not be defined as a perfectly unchanging state?

It maintains internal conditions within a range compatible with functioning despite disturbances. The quantity can vary while regulatory responses adjust; in our scene, a temperature can also remain constant thanks to heat exchanges that balance each other.

Recall 2Reconstruct the temperature loop, from the quantity to the new measurement.

Internal temperature → sensors → thermoregulatory centres and reference → effectors → change in heat exchange → new temperature and new measurement. Effectors carry out the response; they do not replace the sensors.

Recall 3With e = T − c, why does the corrective change u = −ge oppose a positive deviation when g is positive?

A positive deviation places T above c. The corrective change becomes negative and lowers T. The word negative describes this opposition to the deviation; it does not by itself guarantee stability if the assumptions about delay and effector capacity do not hold.

Recall 4What balance must be written before concluding that a stable temperature means there is no response?

T′ = T + u + p. A stable temperature means that the changes u and p cancel each other out in this balance, not that each is 0. The regulatory response must be distinguished from disturbances, and the mechanism must be checked beyond the curve.

Recall 5Why do our calculation and an observed decrease in temperature not prove the same thing?

The calculation deduces a temperature under assumptions and fictional values. An observed decrease shows a change but can have several causes, including passive heat exchange. Demonstrating regulation requires evidence about the return of measurement information, the response and its effect.
