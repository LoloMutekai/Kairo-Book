Level 1 · C030·M1

Discrete distributions

Your goalBuild a discrete distribution from equally likely outcomes, then calculate the probability of a set of values.

Before you start
c029-m5 : Bayes

✿01 See the idea

At a workshop counter, a fictional game asks you to draw a ticket that states a waiting time. Tickets A, B, C, D and E give 0 min; F, G and H give 2 min; I and J give 5 min. The tickets are distinct, but several state the same waiting time.

Assume that every ticket has exactly the same chance of being drawn. Look at the groups: waiting 2 min corresponds to more tickets than waiting 5 min. The waiting times therefore do not all have the same chance, even though the tickets do.

A discrete probability distribution brings together the possible values and the probability of each. It keeps the information we want, the waiting time, by grouping the letters that give that time. It describes what can happen before the draw; it does not choose the next ticket.

The waiting times, tickets and their chances are entirely fictional. Equal likelihood is an assumption of the game, rather than a result measured at a real counter. The times are exact in the model, with no rounding or hidden intermediate waiting time.

The rule to remember

Set the rule that turns an outcome into a number; group outcomes with the same value; add their probabilities; check that all the probability has been allocated.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Build the distribution without missing or counting a ticket twice

The draw uses A to J, with equal chances. The 5 tickets A to E state 0 min, the 3 tickets F to H state 2 min, and the 2 tickets I and J state 5 min. Let us build the waiting time distribution, then calculate the chance of having to wait.

  1. Define the outcome, the variable and its distribution

    An outcome here is the letter drawn. Ω, read as capital omega, denotes the set of letters A to J; ω, lowercase omega, denotes a letter in that set. An event is a set of outcomes, such as “draw F, G or H”.

    X is the random variable: the rule set before the draw that assigns each letter its waiting time, in minutes. X(ω) is the waiting time attached to the letter ω. The rule does not change at random; it is the letter to be drawn that is still unknown. If G is drawn, the observed value of X is 2 min.

    S denotes the support of the distribution, meaning the values with strictly positive probability. Here S contains 0, 2 and 5, expressed in minutes. A distribution is discrete when all its probability is allocated to values that can be listed. The list is finite here; it can also continue indefinitely, a case this page does not calculate.

    x is a candidate waiting time, in the same unit as X. P denotes the probability the model assigns to an event. The notation X = x denotes the event “the waiting time obtained equals x”. The function p with subscript X assigns to x the probability of that event:

    pX(x)=P(X=x)

    X and x are waiting times; p_X(x) has no unit. The distribution is not a list of letters: it is the allocation of probability among the waiting time values.

  2. Calculate the probability of an outcome

    The groups are disjoint and cover all the tickets. We add their counts to obtain the total number of outcomes: 5 + 3 = 8, then 8 + 2 = 10 tickets.

    The game assumes equally likely tickets, meaning tickets with the same probability. All the probability, 1, is therefore shared equally among 10 tickets. Each letter receives 1/10, or 0.10, with no unit. Without this assumption, a simple ratio of counts would not be enough: we would need to add the individual probabilities of the letters.

    P(billet G)=110=0.10

  3. Group letters with the same waiting time

    To obtain 0 min, you must draw A, B, C, D or E: 5 favourable outcomes out of 10. Their total probability is 5/10 = 1/2. This grouping preserves all the chances of the letters without giving the waiting time a new weight.

    For 2 min, the outcomes are F, G and H: 3/10, or 0.30. For 5 min, they are I and J: 2/10 = 1/5. The values are placed opposite their probabilities in the following table. The fractions are exact.

    Valeur x, en min025P(X=x)510310210Sans unité0.500.300.20

    Keeping only the values without their probabilities would lose the distribution. The probability of a letter also differs from that of a waiting time: G has probability 1/10, but the waiting time of G has probability 3/10 because other letters give the same time.

  4. Check a complete distribution

    Each probability must lie between 0 and 1, including both bounds. The sum over all values in the support must equal 1. The symbol ∑ here means “add for each value x in S”; the sign ∈ means “belongs to”.

    0≤pX(x)≤1;∑x∈SpX(x)=1

    In our table, 0.50 + 0.30 = 0.80, then 0.80 + 0.20 = 1.00. All terms are valid. A check using the tickets gives the same total: (5 + 3 + 2)/10 = 10/10 = 1.

    This numerical check goes alongside checking the ranking by waiting time: each letter appears once and only once. A correct total would not be enough if letters had been assigned to the wrong waiting times. Finally, any time outside the support receives probability 0; for example, P(X = 1 min) = 0 in this game.

  5. Answer a question about several values

    “Having to wait” means X > 0 min. The event combines X = 2 min and X = 5 min. These events are mutually exclusive: the ticket drawn cannot state these different times at the same time. That is why their probabilities add; no independence assumption is needed.

    P(X>0)=P(X=2)+P(X=5)=0.50

    The numerical values of X are expressed in minutes in the formula. The calculation is 3/10 + 1/5 = 1/2. Returning to the letters, F to J represent 3 + 2 = 5 tickets: 5/10 = 1/2. The two methods agree.

    Another check uses the complementary event “not waiting”: 1 − 1/2 = 1/2. This subtraction is valid because waiting and not waiting cover all tickets without overlapping.

  6. Ask a different question about the same draw

    Let Y be the number that equals 1 if the ticket requires a wait and 0 otherwise. Y is another random variable, with no unit: it only indicates whether the event occurred. The experiment is the same, but the information retained is different.

    The distribution of Y is P(Y = 0) = 1/2 and P(Y = 1) = 1/2. Positive waiting times are combined into the same value of Y. The distribution of Y answers “wait or not”, but no longer distinguishes 2 min from 5 min.

  7. State what the result establishes

    The probability of waiting is 0.50, with no unit, under the stated rules. This means neither a wait of 0.50 min nor certainty about the next letter. If J is drawn, the observed waiting time will be 5 min; the distribution also described that possibility.

    The distribution is calculated exactly for our game. It is not a frequency measured over real visits. To apply a distribution to a real wait, we would need to justify the chosen values and their probabilities; arithmetic validity alone does not do that.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the figures, explain why equally likely tickets can give waiting times with different chances. Then recover the probability of waiting using the letters, the distribution of X and the complement.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The waiting times have the same chance because they appear in the same list.”

    Equal chances are assigned to the tickets, rather than the values. Group the letters: 2 min comes from 3 tickets, whereas 5 min comes from 2 tickets. Their probabilities are therefore 3/10 and 1/5.

    Review the mapping from tickets to waiting times

  • “The probability P(X = 2 min) equals 2 min.”

    2 min is the value of interest. Its probability is the weight of tickets F, G and H: 3/10, or 0.30, with no unit. Read the row of waiting times and the row of probabilities separately.

    Reread the table and its units

  • “If the sum equals 1, my distribution must be correct.”

    This total is necessary, but also check the bounds of each probability and the rule for ranking tickets by waiting time. Every ticket must appear once and only once, in the correct group. Equal likelihood must be justified by the model; a correct total does not prove it.

    Repeat the checks on the complete distribution

Exercise 1 · Reading and justification

A letter or a waiting time?

In the initial game, give the probability of drawing G and of obtaining 2 min. Explain why they differ. State the unit of the waiting time and of the probabilities.

Hint 1

Find all the letters that state the same waiting time as G.

Hint 2

A ticket is an outcome; the waiting time of G also groups together F and H.

Compare with the solution

G is a particular ticket among 10 equally likely tickets: its probability is 1/10, or 0.10. Obtaining 2 min corresponds to F, G or H: 3/10, or 0.30.

The waiting time is in minutes; the probabilities have no unit. The event “draw G” is contained in the event “obtain 2 min”, which has other outcomes. This explains the difference between the probabilities.

Check your reasoning

  • Give 1/10 for the letter and 3/10 for the waiting time.
  • Name F, G and H to justify the grouping.
  • Distinguish minutes from probability with no unit.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and checking

At most the short waiting time

Still using the initial game, calculate P(X ≤ 2 min) from the distribution, then check using the tickets or a complement. What is P(X = 1 min)? Explain every inclusion and exclusion.

Hint 1

“At most” includes the bound: keep the short waiting time and all lower values in the support.

Hint 2

The complement here is the only waiting time strictly greater than 2 min.

Compare with the solution

The qualifying values are 0 min and 2 min. We add their probabilities because these waiting times are mutually exclusive: 1/2 + 3/10 = 4/5 = 0.80, with no unit.

Tickets A to H are exactly the favourable outcomes: 8/10 = 4/5. Checking by the complement also gives 1 − 1/5 = 4/5.

No ticket states 1 min. This value is outside the support, so P(X = 1 min) = 0. Having waiting times on either side does not create an intermediate waiting time.

Check your reasoning

  • Include the bound 2 min and the waiting time 0 min.
  • Find 4/5 and provide a separate check.
  • Assign probability 0 to 1 min based on the rules of the game.

Exercise 3 · Modification and reasoning

A ticket changes its waiting time

Only the inscription on ticket J is replaced: it now gives 2 min instead of 5 min. The letters and their chances remain the same. Rebuild the distribution of X and check its total. Does the probability of having to wait change? Does the probability of waiting 5 min change?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Move J between groups without adding or removing a ticket from the draw.

Hint 2

Compare the “long waiting time” group with the larger “strictly positive waiting time” group.

Compare with the solution

The group with no wait keeps A to E, or 5 tickets. The 2 min group contains F, G, H and J: 3 + 1 = 4 tickets. The 5 min group now contains only I: 2 − 1 = 1 ticket. The total remains 10 equally likely tickets.

The new distribution is P(X = 0 min) = 1/2; P(X = 2 min) = 4/10 = 2/5; P(X = 5 min) = 1/10 = 0.10. The probabilities have no unit.

Check: 0.50 + 0.40 = 0.90, then 0.90 + 0.10 = 1.00. Each letter is still assigned once and only once.

Having to wait retains probability 1/2: J remains in the group of positive waiting times. We again find 2/5 + 1/10 = 1/2. However, the probability of 5 min changes from 1/5 to 1/10. The same probability of waiting can therefore occur with different waiting time distributions.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the denominator 10 and the group with no wait.
  • Find the new counts 4 and 1 before dividing.
  • Check the probabilities, their total and the ranking of each letter by waiting time.
  • Distinguish the event “wait” from the value “wait 5 min”.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the difference between an outcome, the variable X and its observed value?

The outcome is the letter drawn. X is the fixed rule that turns each letter into a waiting time, in minutes. The observed value is the waiting time of the letter actually obtained; it is not the entire rule.

Recall 2In the initial game, how can you recover P(X = 2 min) from the tickets?

Group F, G and H, then add their probabilities. With 10 equally likely tickets, this gives 3/10, or 0.30, with no unit. The values of X are not assumed to be equally likely.

Recall 3What checks should you make on a finite discrete distribution?

Each probability is between 0 and 1; their sum over the support equals 1. Also check that all outcomes are assigned once to the correct value and that their probabilities follow from the stated assumptions. A value outside the support has probability 0.

Recall 4How do you calculate the probability of waiting from the distribution of X?

Select the strictly positive waiting times, then add their probabilities: 3/10 + 1/5 = 1/2. The addition follows from the values being mutually exclusive in the same draw. The complement 1 − 1/2 gives the same result.

Recall 5What does the distribution say before the draw, and what does it not give?

It assigns a probability to each possible waiting time under the rules of the game. It does not give the letter on the next ticket and is not a measurement of real waiting times. A realised waiting time remains different from the distribution that made it possible.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Hide the page and reconstruct the sequence “letter → waiting time → probability”. Finally, explain why modifying J changes the waiting time distribution without changing the probability of a positive wait.
