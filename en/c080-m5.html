Level 1 · C080·M5

Synapse

Your goalFollow transmission through an excitatory chemical synapse, calculate its delay on a fictional timeline, and distinguish a local response from a new action potential.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

An action potential arrives at the end of neuron A’s axon. Opposite it, a small region of neuron B’s membrane then begins to depolarize: the inside becomes locally less negative relative to the outside. Yet the membranes of A and B remain separate. How does the signal cross this gap?

The synapse is this specialized region of communication. In our example, it is chemical: A releases glutamate, a molecule that acts as a messenger, called a neurotransmitter. Glutamate diffuses across the synaptic cleft, the narrow space between the membranes, then binds to a receptor on B. A receptor is a protein that recognizes a molecule and whose activity can change when that molecule binds to it.

On A’s side, called presynaptic, glutamate is stored in vesicles, small sacs enclosed by a membrane. The arrival of the action potential opens channels that allow calcium to enter. This entry promotes the fusion of vesicles with the membrane and the release of their contents into the cleft. On B’s side, called postsynaptic, our receptor is also a channel: glutamate binding allows the channel to open and an ionic current to flow across B’s membrane.

This synapse is excitatory: under the chosen conditions, the current produces a local depolarization that brings B closer to triggering an action potential. A postsynaptic potential is this local change in voltage. Its amplitude is not necessarily sufficient to trigger a new action potential. Glutamate does not need to enter B: its binding on the outside acts on the channel, while ions pass across the membrane.

We use this single synapse from A to B throughout the page. The scene and its times are fictional, designed to teach you to follow a chain of transmission; no duration given here is a biological measurement or a typical value. The mechanism studied concerns a chemical synapse; electrical synapses do not follow this sequence of release and binding.

The rule to remember

At a chemical synapse, the electrical signal in A leads to a chemical release; reception in B produces a local electrical response. Check each step before claiming that a new action potential has occurred.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From arrival in A to B’s local response

On our fictional clock, the arrival of the action potential at A’s axon terminal is marked at 8000 μs, glutamate release at 8400 μs, its binding to B at 8500 μs, and the start of B’s local response at 8700 μs. Let us work through the mechanism and the delay between the first and last events.

  1. Define the symbols, units, and scope

    Let t_A be the time of arrival in A, t_L the time of release, t_F the time of binding, and t_R the time when the local response begins. These are positions on the same clock, expressed in microseconds, symbol μs. A millisecond, symbol ms, contains 1000 μs. The subscript letters name the events; they do not represent numbers to multiply.

    The total delay Δt is the duration between t_A and t_R. To divide it into parts, d_P is the duration before release, d_F the duration between release and binding, and d_R the duration between binding and the start of the response; all are in μs. We assume successful transmission and an unambiguous order of events. The intervals follow one another with no gaps or double counting. These choices make our addition exact; in a real synapse, molecular events can overlap, and an arrival does not guarantee release.

    We start at A’s axon terminal and end at the start of a local response in B. The travel time along the axon before this terminal and any triggering of an action potential in B fall outside this interval.

  2. In A: making release possible

    The arrival of the action potential changes the voltage across A’s membrane. Channels sensitive to this voltage open; calcium ions enter along their electrochemical gradient, which combines a concentration difference and an electrical effect. This entry triggers the machinery for vesicle fusion. Fusion connects the contents of the vesicle to the outside and releases glutamate: this is exocytosis.

    To obtain the duration of this phase on our timeline, subtract the starting time from the release time. Subtracting points in time gives a duration; adding the times would not answer this question.

    dP=tL−tA=8400−8000=400 μs

  3. In the cleft: reaching the receptor

    The released glutamate diffuses: molecular motion spreads it through space. Some molecules encounter a compatible receptor on B’s membrane and bind to it. The cleft is therefore not an extension of the inside of the axon: the chemical messenger moves through it outside the cells.

    In our division into phases, d_F covers diffusion and then the binding event chosen as a time marker. The duration is t_F − t_L = 8500 − 8400 = 100 μs. This number is a constructed interval; it does not by itself measure a property of the molecule. Since arrival, the elapsed time has reached 400 + 100 = 500 μs.

  4. In B: turning binding into current

    The receptor chosen for this scene is a channel whose opening depends on glutamate binding. The open channel lets ions pass; under the conditions of our excitatory synapse, the net current locally depolarizes B. The energy for ion movement comes from electrochemical gradients: glutamate controls opening; it does not itself supply the charge that crosses the channel.

    The chosen duration between binding and the start of the response is d_R = t_R − t_F = 8700 − 8500 = 200 μs. We have added no amplitude measurement: this interval tells us when the response begins, not whether it is sufficient to make B fire an action potential.

  5. Add the successive durations

    We add the durations because they cover successive parts of the same timeline, from t_A to t_R. Shared boundaries are counted only once.

    Δt=dP+dF+dR=400+100+200=700 μs

  6. Check using the endpoints and convert

    Another approach leaves out the intermediate events and subtracts the endpoints directly: Δt = t_R − t_A = 8700 − 8000 = 700 μs. It gives the same result as the earlier sum. When the intervals are expanded, t_L and then t_F each appear with opposite signs and cancel: this explains the check; it is not a numerical coincidence.

    To convert to milliseconds, divide the duration in microseconds by the number of microseconds in a millisecond. The unit becomes ms. The fraction remains exact; the decimal comma notation used here is also exact.

    Δt=7001000 ms=0.7 ms

  7. Interpret the result without equating a response with firing

    Our result links arrival at 8.0 ms to the start of the response at 8.7 ms. Release occurred at 8.4 ms and binding at 8.5 ms. These conversions give the same event times as the diagram. None of these times demonstrates that an action potential was triggered in B: we would need to check that depolarization reaches the threshold, meaning the voltage level required for triggering in the region concerned.

    The local response is not permanent. Glutamate can detach from receptors; diffusion out of the cleft and uptake by transporters reduce its availability. The channels can then close again, and the membrane can return toward its previous state. The single transmission we are following does not demonstrate a lasting change in the effectiveness of this synapse.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the equations. Explain where calcium ions enter, where glutamate moves, and where the postsynaptic current flows. Reconstruct the delay using the intervals, then the endpoints. Why does neither calculation prove that a new action potential has occurred in B?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The action potential simply jumps from A to B.”

    In the chemical synapse studied here, the membranes remain separate. The arriving electrical signal in A triggers release; glutamate diffuses across the cleft, and its binding changes a channel on B. Reconstruct how the signal changes form instead of extending the axon through the cleft.

    Trace the path from A to B again on the diagram

  • “Since B responds, B must have fired an action potential.”

    The last event marks a local depolarization whose amplitude is not given. An excitatory response brings the membrane closer to the threshold without guaranteeing that it is reached. Distinguish the start of a local response from the triggering of an action potential.

    Review the interpretation of the last event

  • “Calcium and glutamate pass through the same opening into B.”

    Separate the locations: calcium enters A’s axon terminal; glutamate leaves A for the cleft and binds to B; the ions carrying the postsynaptic current cross B’s membrane through its channel. In this example, glutamate acts from outside B.

    Locate each participant again

Also place diffusion across the cleft in the sequence. For each event, indicate whether it concerns A, the cleft, or B.

Hint 1

Start with the arriving electrical signal. Which event makes vesicle fusion possible?

Hint 2

The messenger must be released before it can diffuse and encounter its receptor.

Compare with the solution

In A: arrival of the action potential, opening of voltage-sensitive channels and calcium entry, then vesicle fusion and glutamate release. In the cleft: glutamate diffusion. At B’s surface: binding to the receptor channel, channel opening, then current across B’s membrane and a local electrical response.

Calcium is involved in presynaptic release; glutamate carries the extracellular chemical signal. Giving the order without distinguishing their locations would leave the confusion between these participants unresolved.

Check your reasoning

  • You place calcium entry before release and binding before the local response.
  • You correctly locate A, the cleft, and B’s membrane.
  • You distinguish ions passing through a channel from glutamate binding to a receptor.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and checking

Lengthen only the last phase

In a fictional variation of our timeline, arrival, release, and binding keep their original times. Only the duration between binding and the start of the response increases by 200 μs. Before calculating, predict whether the total delay will increase or decrease. Find the new duration of this phase, the final time, and the total delay in μs, then in ms. Check using the endpoints.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add 200 μs to a duration, and keep the other phases unchanged.

Hint 2

Find the final time by adding the new duration of the last phase to the binding time. Then subtract the arrival time.

Compare with the solution

The delay increases: only one phase is lengthened, and no other phase is shortened. The last duration becomes 200 + 200 = 400 μs. The new response time is 8500 + 400 = 8900 μs, or 8.9 ms.

Using the phases: 400 + 100 + 400 = 900 μs. Using the endpoints: 8900 − 8000 = 900 μs. Conversion: 900/1000 = 9/10 ms = 0.9 ms. The total increase is exactly the specified addition; it must not be applied to every phase.

This variation changes a time in an exercise. It represents neither an actual biological intervention nor a known effect of a substance.

Check your reasoning

  • You state whether the delay increases or decreases before giving the result.
  • You obtain 400 μs for the last phase and 8900 μs for the final time.
  • You distinguish the final time from the total delay of 0.9 ms.
  • You obtain the same delay by adding durations and by subtracting points in time.

Exercise 3 · Reasoning and limits of evidence

Can the stopwatch continue if release fails?

Return to the original scene. Suppose the action potential arrives in A, but the calcium entry required for release is prevented and no vesicle releases glutamate for this event. No other input is considered. Someone nevertheless keeps the binding and response times and reports the same synaptic delay. Explain what no longer works. Which event still needs to be verified before a response in B can be attributed to this transmission?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The times in the example assume successful transmission. Which step made the messenger’s presence in the cleft possible?

Hint 2

An absent event does not simply have a shorter duration; its time may not exist along the path being studied.

Compare with the solution

Without glutamate release for this event, we cannot retain its binding to B or the response attributed to that binding. The corresponding times are undefined for this interrupted chain. We do not replace their absence with a 0 delay, and we do not reuse the 0.7 ms delay of a successful transmission.

Evidence of release and then reception is needed first to link arrival in A to a response in B. If B nevertheless showed a voltage change, that change alone would not repair the chain of evidence: its origin would need to be investigated. This does not demonstrate that B is unable to respond under other conditions.

Check your reasoning

  • You identify the break between calcium entry, fusion, and release.
  • You keep the missing times undefined for this event.
  • You ask for evidence linking the response to this path before attributing it to A.
  • You avoid concluding that neuron B is generally unable to respond.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In our synapse, what do presynaptic, cleft, and postsynaptic refer to?

Presynaptic refers to the sending side A; the cleft is the extracellular space between the membranes; postsynaptic refers to the receiving side B. Glutamate is released by A and binds to a receptor on B.

Recall 2How does the arrival of the electrical signal in A lead to glutamate release?

The action potential opens voltage-sensitive channels. Calcium enters A and triggers the machinery for vesicles to fuse with the membrane; their contents are released into the cleft by exocytosis. This chain describes successful transmission, not guaranteed release on every arrival.

Recall 3Does glutamate have to enter B to produce the response in our example?

No. It binds from the outside to a receptor that is also a channel. Opening allows an ionic current across B’s membrane; under the chosen excitatory conditions, this current produces a local depolarization.

Recall 4How can you find the fictional delay without adding the event times?

Subtract the arrival time from the final time: 8700 − 8000 = 700 μs = 0.7 ms. Check using the successive intervals: 400 + 100 + 200 = 700 μs. The times must belong to the same path and the same clock.

Recall 5Why does an excitatory response in B not demonstrate a new action potential?

It brings the membrane locally closer to the firing threshold, but its presence does not prove that this threshold is reached in the region where an action potential can begin. The start of a response, sufficient amplitude, and triggering are distinct facts.
