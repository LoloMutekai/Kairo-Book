Level 2 · C059·M3

Lagrange

Your goalConstruct a Lagrangian for an equality constraint, solve its equations and verify that the candidate does minimize the cost over the allowed allocations.

Before you start
c057-m5 : Hessian · c048-m5 : positivity · c054-m5 : Newton’s method · c059-m1 : gradient and Hessian · c059-m2 : convexity

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional workshop, a day allocates exactly 6 h to two tasks: preparing parts and applying their finish. Let x be the preparation time and y the finishing time. Extending one task requires shortening the other: the allowed allocations stay on the line x + y = 6 h.

We choose a cost score purely for teaching: the square of the time for each task is multiplied by its coefficient, then the contributions are added. Finishing has the larger coefficient. This model penalizes concentrating time on one task, but nothing here measures actual fatigue, quality or productivity.

At the best allocation, transferring a very small amount of time from finishing to preparation must no longer lower the score. The marginal costs, meaning the slopes of the cost with respect to each duration, balance. The cost gradient can still be nonzero: only its component along the allowed movements must vanish.

The Lagrange method expresses this balance using an auxiliary unknown, the multiplier. It lets us write the cost and the constraint in the same system of equations, while keeping the total duration. Read the diagrams as an allocation and a direction of movement; the calculation will justify the chosen point.

The rule to remember

For a regular equality constraint: keep the constraint, balance the gradients using a multiplier, then check the nature of the candidate over the allowed points only.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Allocating the workshop day

The total duration is T = 6 h. The preparation cost has coefficient a = 1 point per hour squared; the finishing cost has coefficient b = 2 points per hour squared. Durations are continuous and nonnegative. Let us find the allocation that minimizes this defined score.

  1. Separate the objective from the equality

    x and y are durations in hours. f is the cost in points; a and b are strictly positive coefficients. g is the duration residual in hours: an allocation is feasible if g equals 0 h. T is fixed throughout the solution.

    f(x,y)=ax2+by2
    g(x,y)=x+y−T=0

    We first search over the entire line, mathematically allowing real coordinates. If its minimum then has nonnegative durations, it also minimizes the cost over the segment that corresponds to the workshop. This approach avoids dealing here with an active bound, which is studied in the next unit.

  2. Understand why the gradients align

    The symbol ∇ denotes the gradient, the vector of partial derivatives. A partial derivative measures the slope when only the specified variable changes. Here ∇g = (1, 1), with no units, and it never vanishes: the equality constraint is said to be regular.

    A transfer of t hours replaces (x, y) with (x + t, y − t). The direction vector is (1, −1). The slope of the cost in this direction is ∂f/∂x − ∂f/∂y, in points per hour. At a minimum in the interior of the segment, it must be 0, since small transfers in both directions are allowed.

    The components of the gradient of f are therefore equal. The gradient becomes parallel to (1, 1), the normal to the line. A coefficient λ can then offset this gradient: ∇f + λ∇g = (0, 0).

    More generally, for one equality constraint and functions whose partial derivatives exist and are continuous, a regular local optimum satisfies this balance. This is a necessary condition; on its own, it does not prove that a candidate is a minimum.

  3. Construct the Lagrangian and keep every equation

    We choose the convention ℒ = f + λg. ℒ, the Lagrangian, is an auxiliary function in points. The multiplier λ is in points per hour: its product with g must have the units of f. For an equality constraint, its sign is unrestricted.

    ℒ(x,y,λ)=ax2+by2+λ(x+y−T)

    Stationary here means that the partial derivatives of ℒ vanish. Differentiating with respect to x or y gives the slope balances. Differentiating with respect to λ recovers the constraint; this last equation is essential.

    ∂ℒ∂x=2ax+λ=0∂ℒ∂y=2by+λ=0∂ℒ∂λ=x+y−T=0

    The first rows have units of points per hour; the last has units of hours. We solve these equations to find a candidate. We are not seeking a minimum of ℒ with respect to all its variables: away from the line, the linear term λg can become arbitrarily negative.

  4. Eliminate the multiplier and solve for the durations

    The first equations give λ = −2x and λ = −4y, with the derivative coefficients given respectively by 2a and 2b. Equating the two expressions eliminates λ: 2x = 4y. Dividing by 2 gives x = 2y.

    Substitute this ratio into the duration equality: 2y + y = 6 h, so 3y = 6 h. Division gives y = 2 h, then x = 2 × 2 h = 4 h. The task with the higher coefficient receives less time; an equal allocation was not required.

    x⋆=4 h,y⋆=2 h

  5. Recover λ and check the equations

    The star denotes the candidate values: x⋆ = 4 h and y⋆ = 2 h. The multiplier is λ⋆ = −2 × 4 = -8 points per hour. It is negative under the chosen convention; this sign does not rule out the candidate.

    Perform separate checks. Duration: 4 + 2 = 6 h; residual: 6 − 6 = 0 h. The durations are strictly positive, so small transfers in both directions are allowed.

    Slopes: ∂f/∂x = 2 × 4 = 8 points per hour, and ∂f/∂y = 4 × 2 = 8 points per hour. Adding λ⋆ makes each 0. The gradient of f is therefore (8, 8), which is nonzero; its projection onto the transfer direction vanishes because 8 − 8 = 0.

  6. Calculate the cost and distinguish it from λ

    Squaring each duration gives 16 and 4 hours squared. Multiplying by a and b converts the contributions to points: 1 × 16 = 16 points and 2 × 4 = 8 points. The total cost is 16 + 8 = 24 points.

    On the allowed line, g = 0 h, so ℒ = f. This does not allow us to remove λg before differentiating: we would lose the effect of the constraint. An equal allocation of 3 h per task would cost (1 + 2) × 9 = 27 points. This comparison allocation costs 3 more points; this comparison alone does not yet prove a global minimum.

  7. Prove the minimum over the entire line

    Every allocation on the line can be written as (x⋆ + t, y⋆ − t), where t is a signed displacement in hours. Expanding f gives the linear term in t as (2ax⋆ − 2by⋆)t. It vanishes because of the slope balance already checked. The coefficient of the square is a + b = 3 points per hour squared.

    f(x⋆+t,y⋆−t)−f(x⋆,y⋆)=(a+b)t2≥0

    A square is nonnegative, and a + b is strictly positive. The cost difference is therefore positive for every nonzero displacement: the candidate is the unique minimum over the entire line. For physical durations, t stays between −4 h and 2 h; the same proof applies to this whole segment. We have proved the minimum of the model, without measuring anything in a real workshop.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I set the cost gradient equal to 0, as in the unconstrained case.”

    You would get x = y = 0 h, which violates the duration T = 6 h. Look instead for a 0 slope along the allowed transfers. At the chosen allocation, the cost gradient remains (8, 8) points per hour.

    Review the line and the transfer direction

  • “Once λ is introduced, I can forget the duration equality.”

    The slope balances fix only a ratio between the durations. The derivative with respect to λ, which equals g, fixes their total. Write all the equations again before substituting values.

    Revisit the construction of the Lagrangian

  • “The Lagrange equations are satisfied: the minimum is proved.”

    Under the stated regularity assumptions, these equations are necessary for a local optimum, but they do not classify all candidates. Here, expanding the cost into a positive square along the line proves the minimum and its uniqueness.

    Revisit the proof using a feasible displacement

Compare with the solution

The new Lagrangian is f + λ(x + y − T), with T = 9 h. The balances still require x = 2y. Thus 3y = 9 h: y⋆ = 3 h and x⋆ = 6 h.

The total 6 + 3 equals 9 h. Both slopes are 12 points per hour; λ⋆ = -12 points per hour offsets them. The durations are positive.

Their squares are 36 and 9 hours squared. The cost is 36 + 18 = 54 points. For any other allocation with the same total, the linear term of the displacement vanishes and the difference is (a + b) multiplied by the square of t, with a + b = 3 points per hour squared. It is strictly positive if t is nonzero: the minimum is unique.

Exercise 3 · Apply the reasoning in a new setting

The same line, a different multiplier

Return to T = 6 h. A colleague writes the equality as g̃ = 2g = 0 h, and chooses ℒ̃ = f + μg̃. μ is their multiplier. Without finding the durations again, find μ⋆ and explain whether the minimum or the proof changes. Does the number λ⋆ have a meaning independent of how the constraint is written?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Multiplying an equality by a nonzero number preserves its solutions.

Hint 2

In the gradient balance, replace ∇g̃ with 2∇g and compare with the original system.

Compare with the solution

g and g̃ vanish on the same line, since 2 is nonzero and has no units. The cost does not change. The durations therefore remain x⋆ = 4 h and y⋆ = 2 h; the cost remains 24 points and the proof using the square is unchanged.

The gradient of g̃ is 2 times that of g. Keeping the same balance requires 2μ⋆ = λ⋆. Thus μ⋆ = -8 ÷ 2 = -4 points per hour. Check: 2 × (-4) = -8, and this term still offsets 8 points per hour in each coordinate direction.

The multiplier depends on the normalization and sign convention of the constraint. It is distinct from both a duration and the cost value. Its change here does not indicate any change in the optimal allocation.

  • Divide the multiplier by the normalization factor.
  • Distinguish the multiplier, the optimal point and the minimum cost.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why can the cost gradient be nonzero at a constrained minimum?

Only movements that respect the equality are allowed. At a regular minimum, the gradient is normal to their directions; the slope of the cost along these directions is 0. In the workshop, increasing x requires decreasing y by the same duration.

Recall 2How do you construct the system with the convention ℒ = f + λg?

Setting the derivatives with respect to the problem variables to 0 gives ∇f + λ∇g = (0, 0). Setting the derivative with respect to λ to 0 recovers g = 0. The multiplier is an auxiliary unknown and the constraint residual remains an equation.

Recall 3Under what assumptions do we invoke the Lagrange condition here?

The partial derivatives of f and g are continuous and the gradient of g does not vanish at the point being studied. We seek a local optimum on the equality constraint, with small movements allowed in both directions. This necessary condition is not enough to prove a minimum.

Recall 4What proof classifies the workshop candidate as the unique minimum?

Write any other allowed point as (x⋆ + t, y⋆ − t), expand the cost and cancel the linear term using the slope balance. The remaining difference is (a + b) times the square of t, strictly positive for nonzero t since a and b are positive.

Recall 5What must be specified before comparing two multipliers?

The sign convention of the Lagrangian, the normalization of g and the units. Here λ is in points per hour. Multiplying g by 2 divides the multiplier by 2 without changing the optimal allocation.
