CRPS, Brier and forecast resolution

Your goalResolve a recorded forecast, recalculate its Brier score and CRPS, then explain what these scores allow us to conclude.

✿01 See the idea

A workshop is waiting for a delivery. Before departure, a forecast of its duration is recorded: a probability mass of 1/4 at 10 min and a mass of 3/4 at 30 min. These masses add up to 1. The model is deliberately coarse: it gives weight only to these durations, without claiming that other durations would be physically impossible. The entire scene and all its values are fictional.

The parcel arrives after 20 min. Was the forecast any good? For the question “delivered within 20 min”, it stated a probability of 1/4, and the event occurred. The Brier score measures the difference between this probability and the binary outcome. To assess the duration as a whole, the CRPS examines the questions “arrived by this threshold?” across the entire duration scale.

Resolving this forecast means linking the observed duration to the correct recorded forecast, according to the measurement rule already set. Resolution follows observation; it preserves the original forecast. Replacing the forecast with certainty after arrival would remove the very object we want to evaluate.

Here a score is a loss: the smaller it is, the better the forecast agrees with the outcome. Calibration means agreement, over a series of cases, between stated probabilities and observed frequencies. An isolated result demonstrates neither this calibration nor a general ability to anticipate outcomes. The units and the question being evaluated must accompany the number.

The rule to remember

Keep the forecast intact, establish the outcome, then calculate: Brier for a defined event; CRPS for the full distribution of an ordered quantity.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Score the parcel forecast without rewriting it

Fictional forecast fixed before departure: probability 1/4 at 10 min and 3/4 at 30 min. Duration is measured from departure to handover of the parcel, in minutes, with an observation assumed to be exact in this exercise. The outcome is y = 20 min. The threshold a = 20 min and the rule “by this time”, including the boundary, were chosen before arrival.

  1. Establish the outcome and the symbols

    Y is the future delivery duration; y is its observed value. The variable threshold t, the chosen threshold a and the durations Y and y are in minutes. F(t) is the stated probability that Y is less than or equal to t. F describes the forecast, rather than a true distribution whose accuracy we have proved.

    Hᵧ(t) is the observation step function: it equals 0 if t is less than y, and 1 if t is greater than or equal to y. F and H are dimensionless. For the event Y ≤ a, we write p = F(a) for the stated probability and o = Hᵧ(a) for the binary outcome. Here p = 1/4 and o = 1, since arrival at 20 min meets the inclusive boundary of 20 min.

    The name q will refer only to the complementary mass 3/4 at 30 min. The masses are positive and their sum is 1. The delivery is uniquely identified; if no outcome is available or the measurement is ambiguous, the forecast remains pending, with no final score.

  2. Calculate the Brier score for the chosen event

    B(p, o) denotes the Brier loss of probability p for outcome o of the same event.

    B(p,o)=(p−o)2

    Subtract the outcome from the probability to measure the difference: 1/4 − 1 = -3/4. Square this difference to obtain a nonnegative loss: B = -3/4 × -3/4 = 9/16 = 0.5625, with no unit.

    Independent check by expanding the square: 1 − 2 × 1/4 + 1/16 = 9/16. The value lies between 0 and 1, the bounds for this binary Brier score. We use the convention for a single event; we do not add the loss for its complement a second time.

  3. Define the CRPS as a sum over thresholds

    CRPS(F,y)=∫−∞∞(F(t)−Hy(t))2 dt

    The integral sign adds contributions over thresholds t; the bounds −∞ and +∞ indicate that all real thresholds are included. Here the element dt carries the unit minute. At each threshold, the square is the Brier score for the question Y ≤ t. The CRPS therefore has the unit of Y, the minute. It remains defined for our discrete distribution even though the measured quantity can take other values. We assume a finite first absolute moment: the average of the absolute values of the durations under the forecast distribution must be finite, which its finite support guarantees here.

    No complicated integration is needed: F and H are constant on intervals. Before 10 min, both equal 0; from 30 min onward, both equal 1. The only contributions come from intervals between these bounds. The value taken exactly at a jump does not change the integral.

  4. Calculate the contributions of each interval

    From 10 min inclusive to 20 min exclusive, the forecast gives F = 1/4, while the observed arrival time has not yet been reached: H = 0. The squared difference is 1/16. The width is 20 − 10 = 10 min; the contribution is 10 × 1/16 = 5/8 min.

    From 20 min inclusive to 30 min exclusive, F remains 1/4, but H equals 1. The squared difference is now 9/16. The width is 30 − 20 = 10 min; the contribution is 10 × 9/16 = 45/8 min.

    CRPS=10×1/16+10×9/16=25/4 min

    The exact result is 25/4 min, or 6.25 min. Do not forget the widths or simply square the error of a mean duration. The Brier score 0.5625 concerned a single threshold; the CRPS adds the losses over all thresholds.

  5. Check using distances between draws

    Another expression for the same CRPS lets us check the interval calculation. X and X′ are two independent draws from the forecast distribution, in minutes; the prime distinguishes the second draw. E means the weighted average under this distribution, and the bars | | mean absolute distance. These draws are calculation tools, rather than additional observed deliveries.

    CRPS(F,y)=E|X−y|−12E|X−X′|

    Distance to the outcome: |10 − 20| = 10 min and |30 − 20| = 10 min. The weighted average is therefore 1/4 × 10 + 3/4 × 10 = 5/2 + 15/2 = 10 min.

    Between draws, identical pairs have 0 distance. The short–long pair has probability 1/4 × 3/4 = 3/16 and distance 20 min. The long–short pair contributes the same amount: E|X − X′| = 2 × 3/16 × 20 = 15/2 min.

    Subtract half of this average: 10 − 1/2 × 15/2 = 10 − 15/4 = 25/4 min. Both methods give exactly 6.25 min. The subtracted term depends on the forecast dispersion; it prevents the score from reducing to a simple average distance to the outcome.

  6. Interpret the score and complete the resolution

    The resolution preserves the parcel identifier, the original forecast, its date, the definitions of departure and arrival, the observed duration, its provenance and the date when it became known. It then records Brier = 0.5625 with no unit for the chosen threshold and CRPS = 6.25 min for the duration. Measurement quality is part of this evidence; the calculation does not guarantee it.

    These scores are proper: under a given true distribution, forecasting that distribution minimizes the expected average loss. This does not mean that every realization favors the correct forecast. For the Brier score, if π denotes the true probability of the event, the following expansion shows the penalty added by a p different from π. The expectation E is then over the actual outcomes o; π and p are dimensionless.

    EB(p,o)=π(1−π)+(p−π)2

    The first term does not depend on the forecast; the second is minimized when p = π. The same principle of minimization in expectation holds for the CRPS on distributions with a finite first moment. A single parcel cannot establish this true distribution or calibration. Comparing forecasts with a reference on cases defined in advance belongs to the next unit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Since the parcel arrived, its probability was certain.”

    The outcome is 1 for the event, but the forecast remains p = 1/4. A probability describes what was stated before the result. Keep both: the Brier score uses their difference, rather than a probability rewritten afterward.

    Review p, o and forecast resolution

  • “The CRPS is the sum of the differences at the thresholds I choose.”

    It adds the squared CDF differences across the entire scale. With our step functions, each square is multiplied by the exact width of its interval. An arbitrary selection of thresholds without widths does not reproduce this integral.

    Repeat the calculation interval by interval

  • “A Brier score smaller than the CRPS proves that the first model is better.”

    The dimensionless Brier score 0.5625 and the CRPS 6.25 min concern different objects. Converting minutes to hours changes the CRPS number without improving the forecast. Check the object, the unit and the observed cases before comparing numbers.

Compare with the solution

Before 10 min, F = H = 0. From 10 min inclusive to 30 min exclusive, F = 1/4 and H = 0. From 30 min onward, F = H = 1. This leaves one interval of width 30 − 10 = 20 min, with squared difference 1/16.

The CRPS is 20 × 1/16 = 5/4 min = 1.25 min. If you keep a division at 20 min, both parts give the same square and combine into this interval.

Another method: E|X − y| = 1/4 × 20 + 3/4 × 0 = 5 min. Half the distance between draws remains 15/4 min. We find 5 − 15/4 = 5/4 min, identical to the step function calculation.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the binary Brier score compare, and in what unit?

The stated probability p and the outcome o, which equals 0 or 1, for the same event. Their difference is squared. The score has no unit; smaller means a lower loss.

Recall 2How can you reconstruct the CRPS from questions about thresholds?

Compare the forecast CDF F with the observation step function H, square the difference and integrate it over all thresholds. For step functions, multiply each square by the width of its interval. The unit is that of the forecast quantity.

Recall 3What other method can check the CRPS?

Take the average distance from a draw from the forecast distribution to the outcome, then subtract half the average distance between two independent draws from that same distribution. Keep the units of the quantity.

Recall 4What changes, and what is preserved during resolution?

The established outcome, its provenance and the resolution date are attached to the identified, fixed forecast. The original probabilities remain intact. Without an established outcome, the forecast remains pending.

Recall 5Why is a good score for this parcel insufficient to conclude that forecasts are good in general?

Proper scores favor the true distribution on average, without guaranteeing the ranking for every outcome. An isolated case proves neither calibration nor quality on other cases; the measurement and evaluation conditions must also be established.
