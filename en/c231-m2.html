Local manifolds

Your goalDescribe a surface using local charts, verify their inverses, and change coordinates without changing the point.

Before you start
c127-m5 : Green and Stokes · c059-m6 : deterministic discrete Bellman · c143-m5 : uniform convergence · c049-m5 : series solutions · c165-m5 : duality · c231-m1 : multilinearity and tensors

✿01 See the idea

You need to find a small marker painted on a smooth globe. A sheet showing two coordinates can give its address without physically flattening the globe. When the address stops working near a pole, you change sheets; the marker stays on the same surface.

This sheet acts as a local chart: it assigns a pair of numbers to each point in a region of the globe and lets you return to the point. Nearby points must have nearby addresses, in 2 directions. A simple list of numbers is not enough.

The surface has 2 independent directions of local movement, although its position in space requires 3 coordinates. This is the difference between local dimension and ambient dimension. The numbers on the chart are coordinates; their distance on paper does not, by itself, give a length on the globe.

The globe and its marker are fictional. We idealize the surface as a perfectly smooth sphere with no thickness or boundary. The example and exercises will use this same sphere. We will construct the charts, their inverses, and their transition map; measuring lengths belongs to later units.

The rule to remember

A chart gives a reversible, continuous address on an open subset of the surface. Several compatible charts cover the surface; a coordinate failure does not prove that the surface has a singularity.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Finding the address of a marker on the globe

The globe has radius R = 5 cm. In a Cartesian coordinate system centered on the globe, the painted marker has position (X, Y, Z) = (3 cm, 0 cm, 4 cm). We will give its address in 2 charts and verify that the addresses refer to the same point.

  1. Set the units and the surface

    X, Y, and Z measure a position in centimeters; R is the radius in the same unit. We divide by R to work on a unit sphere: x = X/R, y = Y/R, and z = Z/R are dimensionless. The normalized point P is therefore (3/5, 0, 4/5), or (0.60 ; 0.00 ; 0.80), with no units.

    M={(x,y,z)∈ℝ3:x2+y2+z2=1}

    M is the set of points on this sphere. For our P, y = 0; checking membership gives x squared + y squared + z squared = 9/25 + 0 + 16/25 = 1. The idealization is therefore consistent with the data. The squares are dimensionless. No rounding was used in this check.

  2. State exactly what a chart is

    An open subset U of M is a region that is open in the topology of the surface: U is the intersection of M with an open subset of the ambient space. Each point of U therefore has a small neighborhood on the surface that is still contained in U. An open subset of the plane contains a small disk around each of its points, entirely within that subset.

    A chart (U, φ) is a map φ from U to an open subset V of ℝⁿ that is bijective, continuous, and has a continuous inverse. Bijective means that each address in V corresponds to exactly one point in U. Continuity in 2 directions makes neighborhoods correspond; this is called a homeomorphism. The number n is the local dimension, here n = 2; ℝⁿ denotes the space of n real coordinates.

    A topological manifold of dimension n has such a chart around every point. It must also be Hausdorff and second countable: two distinct points have disjoint neighborhoods, and a countable family of open subsets generates all open subsets by unions. Our sphere inherits these properties from Euclidean space: sufficiently small balls separate points, and intersections with balls whose centers have rational coordinates and whose radii are rational give a countable base.

    The word “smooth” will add a condition on chart transitions. A chart does not require the surface to become flat in space; it gives coordinates to calculate with.

  3. Construct the north chart and calculate the address

    N = (0, 0, 1) is the north pole and S = (0, 0, −1) is the south pole. The letters N and S name points, not units. Take Un = M with N removed. This is an open subset of M. The small subscript n in φn means “north”; it does not denote the dimension here.

    The line N + t(P − N) passes through N and P. Its parameter t is dimensionless. To meet the plane at 0 height, we solve 1 + t(z − 1) = 0, so t = 1/(1 − z). The coordinates in this plane give the stereographic projection:

    φn(x,y,z)=(x1−z,y1−z)=(u,v)

    u and v are the two dimensionless coordinates of the address. The denominator is 1 − 4/5 = 1/5, which is strictly positive. We can therefore divide: u = 3/5 ÷ 1/5 = 3; v = 0 ÷ 1/5 = 0. The number 3 is not a path length in centimeters.

  4. Construct the inverse, then make the return trip

    For any address (u, v) in the plane, let q be the sum of the squares of u and v. The letter q denotes this squared norm, which is dimensionless. The map Fn below returns from the plane to the sphere; it will be the inverse of φn: it reconstructs the point from its address.

    Fn(u,v)=(2u1+q,2v1+q,q−1q+1)

    For our address, q = 3 squared + 0 squared = 9 + 0 = 9. The common denominator is 1 + 9 = 10. The numerators are 2 × 3 = 6, 2 × 0 = 0, and 9 − 1 = 8. We therefore recover (6/10, 0/10, 8/10) = (3/5, 0, 4/5): exactly P.

    To return to centimeters, we multiply each component by the radius R: 5 cm × 3/5 = 3 cm; 5 cm × 0 = 0 cm; 5 cm × 4/5 = 4 cm. We recover the original physical data.

  5. Verify the whole chart, beyond the calculated point

    A round trip for P does not yet prove that the formula defines a chart. For any address in the plane, q ≥ 0, so 1 + q > 0. Adding the squares of the components of Fn gives the fraction below. Expanding its numerator shows that it equals the denominator; the image therefore belongs to M.

    4q+(q−1)2(q+1)2=1

    Its height satisfies 1 − z = 2/(1 + q) > 0, so the image is never N. Substituting the components of Fn for x, y, and z in φn recovers u and v. In the other direction, for a point in Un, the sphere equation gives q = (1 + z)/(1 − z); substituting into Fn gives back x, y, and z.

    The formulas in 2 directions are quotients of polynomials with nonzero denominators on their domains: they are continuous. Thus φn is indeed a homeomorphism from Un onto the entire real plane. The latter is an open subset of the plane. We have a chart of dimension 2.

  6. Change charts on the overlap

    Projecting from the south pole, define Us = M with S removed and φs(x, y, z) = (x/(1 + z), y/(1 + z)) = (a, b). The subscripts s mean “south”; a and b are dimensionless. Its inverse Fs(a, b) has components (2a/(1 + r), 2b/(1 + r), (1 − r)/(1 + r)), with r = a squared + b squared. The same algebraic check establishes a chart; only the sign of the height is reversed.

    The overlap Un ∩ Us is the region where the 2 charts both exist: M with both poles removed. For P, 1 + z = 9/5, so a = 3/5 ÷ 9/5 = 1/3 and b = 0. We can also convert its north address directly:

    T=φs∘Fn:(u,v)↦(uq,vq),q=u2+v2>0

    The symbol ∘ tells us to apply Fn first, then φs. Indeed, for Fn(u, v), 1 + z = 2q/(1 + q); this factor simplifies the fractions to u/q and v/q. Our address becomes (3/9, 0/9) = (1/3, 0), as in the direct calculation. Its first component is approximately 0.33, with no units; the exact fraction remains 1/3.

  7. Understand the smooth atlas and its limits

    In north coordinates, the overlap is the plane with (0, 0) removed. The origin represents S and therefore does not belong to the overlap. The rule T is a rational function whose denominator q does not vanish on this open subset: its partial derivatives exist to every order. This is what “smooth”, or C∞, means.

    For the south address, r = a squared + b squared. The inverse transition is (a, b) ↦ (a/r, b/r). Since r = 1/q for an address obtained from T, the second transition gives back (u, v); it is smooth as well. The charts are smoothly compatible: their coordinate transitions are smooth in 2 directions.

    An atlas is a family of charts whose domains cover M. Un and Us cover the entire sphere: each contains the pole that the other excludes. With this compatibility, they define its structure as a smooth manifold of dimension 2. A maximal atlas adds all compatible charts; it does not change the points on the globe.

    The north chart fails at N because its denominator vanishes, but the south chart remains defined there. We have established a reversible local description and its transitions. To calculate a length from coordinates, we will next need a metric; addresses alone do not specify one.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The sphere has 3 coordinates, so its local dimension is 3.”

    The 3 ambient coordinates are linked by the sphere equation. In a chart, 2 independent coordinates suffice, and the inverse reconstructs the point. Distinguish the number of ambient components from the local dimension.

    Review the definition of a chart and its inverse

  • “The denominator vanishes at the pole: the sphere is singular.”

    Test another chart at the same point. At N, the south chart has denominator 2 and gives a finite address. The failure of the north chart is therefore not enough to conclude that the surface has a defect.

    Handle the pole in the diagnostic exercise

  • “Changing charts moves the marker on the sphere.”

    The transition T converts one address into another by passing through the same point. Check the two inverse images or apply the inverse transition. A new address is not a movement of the globe.

    Recalculate the transition between the 2 charts

Exercise 1 · Direct application

An address at the equator

On the same globe, place the marker at the normalized point E = (1, 0, 0). Verify that it belongs to M, then calculate its north and south addresses. What are the units of these addresses?

Hint 2

For E, the height z is 0. Both denominators are therefore 1.

Compare with the solution

E belongs to M because 1 squared + 0 squared + 0 squared = 1. It is neither pole, so the 2 charts can both be used.

North chart: (1/(1 − 0), 0/(1 − 0)) = (1, 0). South chart: (1/(1 + 0), 0/(1 + 0)) = (1, 0). The addresses are dimensionless because the starting coordinates are normalized. Their equality here does not hold for all points, as P shows.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and inverse check

Two sheets, one point

Start again from the north address of P: (3, 0). Calculate its south address using T, then return to the north address using the inverse transition. Why does the result not describe a movement?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate q = u squared + v squared before converting both components.

Hint 2

In the other chart, the denominator is r = a squared + b squared; you must recalculate it.

Compare with the solution

q = 3 squared + 0 squared = 9. The south address is (3/9, 0/9) = (1/3, 0). It is defined because q > 0.

We now need r = 1/3 squared + 0 squared = 1/9 + 0 = 1/9. The inverse gives (1/3 ÷ 1/9, 0 ÷ 1/9) = (3, 0). We recover the original north address. Reusing q on the return trip would give an incorrect conversion.

T = φs ∘ Fn passes through P and then describes it in the second chart. The round trip therefore preserves the point. In the example, Fn had already reconstructed its position (3/5, 0, 4/5).

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the squared norm specific to each address.
  • Obtain the exact fraction on the outward trip, then the original address on the return trip.
  • Justify the absence of movement using the composition of the charts.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and justification

Is an address failure a singularity?

The marker now reaches N = (0, 0, 1). Someone replaces the impossible north address with (0, 0) and claims that the surface is broken. Repair the reasoning: choose a valid chart, check its inverse, then explain why the transition T cannot be applied to this new address.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The south chart excludes S; it therefore contains N.

Hint 2

The south origin is a valid address for N, but N does not belong to the overlap of the charts.

Compare with the solution

In the north formula, 1 − z = 0. The quotients 0/0 are undefined: replacing them with a 0 address would invent a result. Moreover, the north origin represents S, not N.

In the south chart, 1 + z = 2. The address is (0/2, 0/2) = (0, 0). Its inverse uses r = 0 and returns (0, 0, (1 − 0)/(1 + 0)) = N. This chart therefore provides a regular neighborhood of N.

The transition to the north chart requires r > 0. At the south origin, this condition fails because N is absent from the north domain. This is not a failure of the south chart: it remains valid here. The regularity of a point is judged by the existence of a chart around it, not by the success of every possible formula.

Check your reasoning

  • Reject all division by 0 and any arbitrary replacement address.
  • Calculate the south address and verify that it reconstructs N.
  • Distinguish the domain of a chart from that of a chart transition.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What conditions make an address into a local chart?

An open domain in the manifold, an open coordinate set of the same dimension, and a continuous bijection with a continuous inverse between them. The inverse lets you recover the point and its neighborhoods.

Recall 2Why does the sphere of our globe have local dimension 2?

Each point has a chart to an open subset of the plane with 2 independent coordinates. The 3 ambient components are linked by the sphere equation.

Recall 3How do you define a chart transition and its domain?

Return to the point using the inverse of the first chart, then apply the second chart. The domain is the image of the overlap in the first chart. Here it excludes the origin; the conversion divides the address by its squared norm.

Recall 4When do charts form a smooth atlas?

Their domains cover the manifold, and their coordinate transitions on the overlaps are smooth in 2 directions, meaning differentiable to every order.

Recall 5What does the failure of a formula at the north pole prove?

It indicates a limitation of this chart. Here another chart describes a neighborhood of the same point and its inverse reconstructs it; the failure therefore does not demonstrate any singularity of the sphere.
