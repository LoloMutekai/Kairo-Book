Level 4 · C226·M3

Compensation

Your goalConstruct the compensator of a homogeneous Poisson counting process and prove why the compensated count is a martingale with respect to information about the past.

Before you start
c168-m5 : Radon-Nikodym · c169-m5 : optional stopping under assumptions · c171-m5 : multidimensional case · c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c226-m1 : waiting times · c226-m2 : Poisson process

✿01 See the idea

A turnstile counts crossings. The counter increases in jumps, but we want to separate what comes from the expected rate from what represents a deviation from that rate. If the model predicts λ = 2 crossings per minute, waiting without a crossing already provides information: time advances while the counter stays still.

We follow a single fictional record: crossings at 0.25, 0.50, 0.75 and 2.25 min after the start. At s = 1 min, the counter reads 3; at t = 2.50 min, it reads 4. The model expects 2 and 5 crossings respectively. Subtracting the expected count therefore leaves deviations of 1 and then -1 crossing.

Compensation means subtracting a predictable accumulation from the counter. In this homogeneous model, that accumulation is a straight line with slope λ. The result can rise or fall and become negative: it describes a deviation, rather than a physical number of crossings to erase.

Everything is fictional: the times and intensity are exercise data. We assume a homogeneous Poisson process here; a real turnstile does not establish that assumption. Compensation removes neither the jumps nor the randomness. Its claim concerns the mean of the next deviation, conditional on the past.

The rule to remember

Subtracting λt removes the predictable drift from the count; the conditional expectation of the compensated increments is 0, while individual paths need not be 0.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the turnstile to the martingale property

The same turnstile is modelled by a homogeneous Poisson process with known intensity λ = 2 crossings/min. The fictional record gives N_s = 3 at s = 1 min and N_t = 4 at t = 2.50 min. Let us construct the compensator, calculate the result, then check what its probabilistic property states.

  1. Specify the information and assumptions

    N_r denotes the number of crossings from the start up to and including time r, with N initially equal to 0. r, s and t are deterministic times in minutes; r is a generic time variable. λ is a known positive constant, in crossings per minute. The values of N are integers; a jump of 1 represents a crossing.

    F_s, written 𝓕_s in the formulas, is the information provided by the entire path of the counter up to s: its natural filtration. It contains the times already observed, with no advance information about future crossings. E denotes expectation under this model; E[X | 𝓕_s] is the conditional mean of a variable X given this past. Here X is an integrable random variable, meaning that its absolute value has finite expectation.

    For every deterministic pair 0 ≤ s ≤ t, the increment N_t − N_s has a Poisson distribution with parameter λ(t − s) and is independent of 𝓕_s. We will use this independence of the future from the entire past. The expectation of this increment is λ(t − s). An empirical average of crossings alone cannot guarantee these assumptions.

    The count is mathematically dimensionless; “crossing” is the unit used to read it. λ therefore has units of inverse time. λt is read as an expected number of crossings and can be fractional, even though the observed counter remains an integer.

  2. Construct the predictable accumulation

    Ar=λr,Mr=Nr−Ar

    A_r is the expected accumulation since the start. M_r is the compensated count, measured in the same reading unit. A starts at 0, is continuous and increasing; its total variation on every bounded time interval is finite. It is predictable: its value is known without waiting for a new jump to be announced. Formally, a continuous adapted process, such as this deterministic function of time, is predictable.

    Adapted means that the value at time r is known from 𝓕_r. N is adapted by the definition of this information; M is adapted too. We call A a compensator of N when, in addition to the preceding properties, N − A is a martingale. We must therefore prove this last condition before calling a reference curve a compensator.

    An integrable martingale with respect to (𝓕_r) is an adapted process whose future mean conditional on the past equals its present value. Here the definition requires E[M_t | 𝓕_s] = M_s for every deterministic pair s ≤ t.

  3. Perform the subtractions on the record

    At time s, subtract the expected count since the start: A_s = 2 × 1 = 2 crossings; M_s = 3 − 2 = 1 crossing. The counter is above its reference at this instant.

    At time t, the same reference has continued to increase: A_t = 2 × 2.50 = 5 crossings. Subtraction gives:

    Mt=4−5=-1 passage

    This negative result means that the counter is 1 crossing below its expected accumulation. No crossing has been removed from the turnstile: we have changed the quantity we describe.

  4. Check using increments

    Δ means “final value minus initial value” over the interval from s to t. Its duration is Δt = 2.50 − 1 = 1.50 min. The counter increases by ΔN = 4 − 3 = 1 crossing. The accumulation increases by ΔA = λΔt = 2 × 1.50 = 3 crossings: we subtract this expected count over the same interval.

    ΔM=ΔN−λΔt=1−3=-2 passages

    Another method: M_s + ΔM = 1 + (-2) = -1 crossing, as in the direct calculation of M_t. We must not subtract λ alone: it is a rate, whereas λΔt is an expected count over a duration.

  5. Prove the conditional property

    M_s is known at s. The future increment is independent of 𝓕_s and has mean λ(t − s). These facts justify first separating the terms, then cancelling them:

    E[Mt∣𝓕s]=Ms+E[Nt−Ns∣𝓕s]−λ(t−s)
    E[Mt∣𝓕s]=Ms+λ(t−s)−λ(t−s)=Ms

    Integrability remains to be checked: the bars in |M_r| denote absolute value. For every finite time r, |M_r| ≤ N_r + λr, so E[|M_r|] ≤ 2λr, which is finite. We have already checked adaptedness. M is therefore a martingale with respect to this filtration, and A is indeed the stated compensator.

    Initially, M equals 0; taking expectations gives E[M_t] = 0. This unconditional mean is a weaker consequence: it does not replace the conditional equality we have proved.

  6. Interpret the mean without predicting the path

    If we consider time s, before seeing what follows, the expected counter value at t is 3 + 3 = 6 crossings. Subtracting A_t = 5 gives E[M_t | 𝓕_s] = 6 − 5 = 1 crossing, exactly M_s. Yet on our realised path, M_t = -1 crossing. The observed value is under no obligation to equal its conditional mean.

    Between crossings, N remains constant while A increases: M falls with slope −λ. At a crossing, A remains continuous and M jumps by 1. Randomness and dispersion remain; only the conditional drift is compensated.

    The conclusions hold under the model, for the deterministic times and information defined here. Replacing λ with a rate fitted to observations, or changing the information available, requires a fresh examination. We stop at this compensation over time; random measures and integration with jumps will be covered next.

Without rereading, explain why M can fall during a wait even though its conditional future mean equals its present value. Then identify the exact point where the proof uses independence.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “We must subtract the rate λ from every reading.”

    A reading contains a number of crossings; λ is a number per minute. First multiply λ by the time elapsed since the start, or by the interval duration if you are working with an increment. The check using increments must reproduce the direct calculation.

    Return to “Check using increments” in the example

  • “A martingale stays horizontal; a negative value reveals an error.”

    The graph shows a fall between crossings and jumps at crossings. The property concerns a conditional mean, rather than the slope of a realised path. M is a signed deviation and can therefore be negative.

    Review the graph of the same count after compensation

  • “A 0 mean, or a rate estimated from the record, is enough to prove compensation.”

    Return to the conditional expectation of increments for every admissible past. The last exercise shows that estimating a rate and then holding it fixed can leave a conditional drift. The model and information must be checked, even if a subtraction seems natural.

    Try the estimated rate diagnosis

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Recover the compensation by two methods

Hide the worked calculations. For the same turnstile, λ = 2 crossings/min, N_s = 3 at s = 1 min and N_t = 4 at t = 2.50 min. Calculate A_t and M_t, then ΔM between s and t. Check that the results agree and interpret the sign of M_t.

Hint 1

Multiply the rate by a time before subtracting it from the count.

Hint 2

Compare the difference between the compensated values with the count over the interval minus λ(t − s).

Compare with the solution

A_t = 2 × 2.50 = 5 crossings; M_t = 4 − 5 = -1 crossing. The counter is 1 crossing below its expected reference; this sign does not indicate a physical impossibility.

At time s, M_s = 3 − 2 = 1 crossing. By direct subtraction, ΔM = -1 − 1 = -2 crossings. Using the interval, ΔN = 1 crossing and λΔt = 3 crossings: 1 − 3 = -2 crossings. Both methods agree.

Check your reasoning

  • Form λt and λ(t − s) with consistent units.
  • Recover the same increment by both methods.
  • Interpret the negative result as a deviation from the model.

Exercise 2 · Conditional reasoning

No crossing during the next interval

Extend the same record to u = 3 min, with no crossing after t = 2.50 min. Calculate M_u and M_u − M_t. Then consider time t, before knowing about this absence: what is E[M_u | 𝓕_t] under the model? Why do the answers differ?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The counter stays at 4, but its expected accumulation continues to grow.

Hint 2

Separate the continuation actually observed from the mean over all future continuations allowed by the model.

Compare with the solution

The duration is 3 − 2.50 = 0.50 min. A_u = 2 × 3 = 6 crossings and M_u = 4 − 6 = -2 crossings. The observed increment is 0 − 2 × 0.50 = -1 crossing: M falls during this wait.

At t, before seeing what follows, the expected number of new crossings is 1. The expectation of the compensated increment is therefore 1 − 1 = 0, and E[M_u | 𝓕_t] = M_t = -1 crossing. The record with no crossing produces an increment of -1, but the other possible continuations contribute to the conditional mean. There is no contradiction.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the counter unchanged and let A advance.
  • Calculate the observed increment and the conditional mean separately.
  • Do not assume that a martingale has a constant path.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and repair

Estimating a rate does not turn it into a compensator

The model remains Poisson with true intensity λ = 2 crossings/min. At s = 1 min, we estimate a rate λ̂_s = N_s/s from N_s = 3, then hold it fixed. For r ≥ s, define C_r = (N_r − N_s) − λ̂_s(r − s). Calculate the estimated rate, C_t on our record, then E[C_t | 𝓕_s] at t = 2.50 min. Since C_s = 0, does this rule give a martingale after s? Repair it under the known model.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Conditional on 𝓕_s, the estimated rate is known; the mean of N_t − N_s remains that of the true model.

Hint 2

The required conditional mean is λ(t − s) − λ̂_s(t − s). Compare it with C_s, then put the true intensity back into the rule.

Compare with the solution

The hat denotes an estimate calculated at s: λ̂_s = 3/1 = 3 crossings/min. This rate is random before s, but known once the past has been observed. C is adapted after s and C_s = 0; adaptedness alone does not guarantee a martingale.

On our record, the expected count calculated with the estimated rate is 3 × 1.50 = 4.50 crossings; C_t = 1 − 4.50 = -3.50 crossings.

In the conditional mean, replace the future count with its mean under the true model, namely 3 crossings. This gives E[C_t | 𝓕_s] = 3 − 4.50 = -1.50 crossing. Another check: the rate error is 2 − 3 = -1 crossing/min, and multiplying it by 1.50 min gives -1.50 crossing.

This mean differs from C_s = 0 for the observed past. The event N_s = 3 has strictly positive probability under this model, so the failure is not confined to a past of probability 0. C is not a martingale. Yet, for fixed s, E[λ̂_s] = E[N_s]/s = λ, so E[C_t] = 0. This unconditional centring is not enough.

Putting λ back into the rule gives C_r = M_r − M_s for r ≥ s, whose conditional expectation at s is 0. Its observed value at t becomes -2 crossings again. In practice, if λ is unknown, this repair requires a justified model of the intensity; this exercise does not provide an estimation method that would guarantee compensation.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish the true intensity from the estimate calculated from the past.
  • Use λ, rather than λ̂_s, in the expectation of the future count.
  • Compare E[C_t | 𝓕_s] with C_s and justify the conclusion for a past of positive probability.
  • Repair the rule under the known model without promising calibration in practice.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be subtracted from the homogeneous count, and why is it predictable?

Subtract A_t = λt, with λ known and constant. This deterministic, continuous, increasing function that is initially 0 is predictable. Then check that N − A is a martingale with respect to the chosen information.

Recall 2What calculation proves the martingale property for the natural filtration?

The increment N_t − N_s is independent of the past 𝓕_s and has mean λ(t − s). Thus E[M_t | 𝓕_s] = M_s + λ(t − s) − λ(t − s) = M_s. M is adapted and integrable; these conditions complete the calculation.

Recall 3What happens to the compensated process during a wait and at a crossing?

It falls between crossings with slope −λ, then jumps by 1 at each crossing. It can become negative. The conditional mean of the increments is 0, but the observed increments are not all 0.

Recall 4Why does holding the rate N_s/s fixed fail to guarantee compensation?

Under the true model with rate λ, the conditional expectation of the increment corrected this way is (λ − N_s/s)(t − s), which is not identically 0. The estimated rate does not automatically replace the conditional intensity of the model.

Recall 5Why is the subtraction N_t − λ ill formed in our example?

N_t is read in crossings; λ in crossings per minute. Multiply λ by a duration to compare quantities with the same reading unit: λt since the start, or λ(t − s) for an increment.
