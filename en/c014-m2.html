Level 1 · C014·M2

Contrapositive

Your goalProve that an odd total requires at least one odd batch by using the contrapositive, then distinguish this proof from a converse.

Before you start
c012-m5 : counterexamples · c011-m5 : binomial · c014-m1 : direct proof

✿01 See the idea

You empty socks onto a table: one batch contains 4, the other 3. After combining them, the total is 7. You can make 3 pairs, but 1 sock is left on its own. The counts are fictional; here, a pair means a group of 2 socks, regardless of colour or style.

Now imagine that each batch had been arranged entirely in pairs. Combining the batches would simply combine all those pairs. An odd total therefore cannot come from batches that were both even. At least one batch was odd: this is the conclusion to justify.

This approach has a name: using the contrapositive. Instead of starting with an odd total, we start with batches that are both even and show that the total is even. We negate both propositions and reverse their order. This gives the same logical requirement in another form.

The conclusion remains precise: “at least one odd batch”. The diagram already shows why “both batches odd” would be too strong. It helps explain the mechanism; the proof must then work for arbitrary counts.

The rule to remember

To prove “if P, then Q”, you can prove “if not Q, then not P”. First write the exact negations; simply reversing the arrow produces a different claim.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From counting to a proof that works for all batches

Batches A and B contain 4 and 3 socks respectively. We calculate their total and the remainder after pairing, then prove the general rule: an odd total requires at least one odd batch.

  1. Set the domain and calculate the total

    x and y denote the counts of batches A and B, in socks. They are integers greater than or equal to 0. The batches are separate, and combining them neither loses nor counts any object twice: the total is therefore calculated by addition. In the case shown, x = 4 and y = 3.

    x+y=4+3=7 chaussettes

    A count is even if everything fits into groups of 2, with 0 left over. It is odd if, after making as many pairs as possible, 1 sock remains. These possibilities cover all our integer counts. The count 0 is even: it corresponds to an empty batch.

  2. Check the case by pairing

    In A, 4 ÷ 2 = 2 complete pairs. In B, set aside 1 sock: 3 − 1 = 2, then 2 ÷ 2 = 1 pair. Combining the pairs gives 2 + 1 = 3.

    These pairs use 2 × 3 = 6 socks. Subtracting the paired socks from the total gives the remainder: 7 − 6 = 1 sock. The remainder confirms that the total is odd, and the diagram provides a check by counting the objects.

    We have checked this case, but have yet to prove a universal rule. Division was used to count the pairs; the remainder must always be checked separately.

  3. Name the propositions and negate them exactly

    For arbitrary counts x and y in the same domain, P means “x + y is odd” and Q means “at least one of the counts x and y is odd”. “At least one” also allows both. P and Q are propositions that are true or false, with no physical unit.

    The symbol ¬ is read as “not” and negates the entire proposition that follows it. Thus ¬P means “the total is even”. For ¬Q, neither batch may be odd: both are therefore even. Simply saying “at least one batch is even” does not negate Q, as our starting batches show.

    P⇒Q

    The arrow ⇒ is read as “implies”, or “if… then…”. On the left is the condition; on the right, the conclusion guaranteed when that condition is true. Our goal is P ⇒ Q. Its contrapositive is ¬Q ⇒ ¬P.

  4. Why this approach proves the same implication

    We use classical logic here: every proposition is true or false. An implication fails exactly when its condition is true and its conclusion is false. P ⇒ Q therefore fails when P is true and Q is false. ¬Q ⇒ ¬P fails when Q is false and P is true: this is the same forbidden case.

    In the following table, V means true and F means false. The 4 general combinations of P and Q are shown; they cannot all occur with the socks. The implication columns match, even when P is false. In that case, P ⇒ Q does not require us to assert Q: its condition is not met.

    PQP⇒Q¬Q⇒¬PVVVVVFFFFVVVFFVV
    (P⇒Q)⇔(¬Q⇒¬P)

    The symbol ⇔ means here that the two implications have the same truth value in every case. This does not say that P and Q are equivalent. To reach our goal, we still need to prove the more convenient statement: “two even batches give an even total”.

  5. Prove the contrapositive without fixing the counts

    Start with ¬Q: the counts x and y are both even. There are therefore integers a and b greater than or equal to 0 such that x = 2a and y = 2b. Here a and b count the pairs in batches A and B; the coefficient 2 converts each number of pairs into a number of socks. The letters a and b are not fixed by the diagram and may be different.

    x+y=2a+2b=2(a+b)

    The first equality replaces the counts with their even forms. The second groups all the pairs by taking out the common factor 2. The sum a + b is still a nonnegative integer: the total can therefore be written as 2 times an integer. By definition, it is even, which means ¬P. We have proved ¬Q ⇒ ¬P.

    This form also covers an empty batch: 2 × 0 = 0 socks. No particular count was needed for the proof.

  6. Return to the goal without just reversing the arrow

    Since the contrapositive has been proved and is equivalent to the goal, P ⇒ Q is established: if the total is odd, at least one batch is odd. The case 4 + 3 = 7 illustrates this: B is odd and A is even. It therefore also refutes the stronger conclusion “both batches are odd”.

    The converse would be Q ⇒ P: “if at least one batch is odd, then the total is odd”. It does not follow from our proof. To test it, use the same scene with 3 socks in each batch: 3 + 3 = 6. The batches are odd, but combining them makes 3 pairs and leaves 0 socks. Q is true and P is false: the converse fails.

    This counterexample does not refute P ⇒ Q: its total is even, so P is false and the condition of the goal is not met. Always specify which implication you are testing.

  7. Check the direction used in the proof

    For a numerical check of the contrapositive, keep 4 socks in A and take 2 in B. The second batch contains 2 ÷ 2 = 1 pair. Adding the counts gives 4 + 2 = 6 socks.

    By another method, add the pairs: 2 + 1 = 3 pairs. Then return to the objects: 2 × 3 = 6 socks. The methods give the same even total. This check makes the grouping visible, while the letters a and b support the general proof.

    Pay attention to the starting domain: with 4 and 3, the batches are not both even. You cannot put these counts into the hypothesis of the contrapositive by claiming that y consists of complete pairs.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the formulas. State the goal, negate its conclusion precisely, then explain why grouping the pairs completes the proof. Finish by saying which arrow the case of two odd batches refutes.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The contrapositive is just the arrow reversed.”

    Q ⇒ P is the converse. The contrapositive also negates both propositions: ¬Q ⇒ ¬P. Rewrite the sentences before the symbols, then check that the forbidden case is still P true and Q false.

    Return to the propositions and the truth columns

  • “Negating “at least one odd batch” means saying “at least one even batch”.”

    Our batches of 4 and 3 socks include both an even batch and an odd batch. The two sentences could therefore be true together: they are not negations of each other. To negate Q, no batch may be odd, so both must be even in our integer domain.

    Review the batches and the statement of the contrapositive

  • “An odd total requires both batches to be odd.”

    The total 7 is already obtained with an even batch of 4 and an odd batch of 3. The proof guarantees “at least one”, but does not guarantee “both” or identify the odd batch if the batches are hidden. The 3 and 3 socks in the exercise also show what happens when those batches are combined.

    Test the converse and the strength of the conclusion

Hint 1

Combine the objects before pairing them: an unpaired sock in each batch can form a pair after the batches are combined.

Hint 2

An implication is false only if its condition is true and its conclusion is false. What is the truth value of ¬Q here?

Compare with the solution

The total is 3 + 3 = 6 socks. We make 6 ÷ 2 = 3 pairs. They use 2 × 3 = 6 socks, giving a remainder of 6 − 6 = 0 socks.

The total is even: P is false. Each batch is odd: Q is true. Q ⇒ P is therefore false, which refutes the converse. However, ¬Q is false and ¬P is true: the contrapositive ¬Q ⇒ ¬P is true in this case, although its condition is not met.

P ⇒ Q is also true in this case because P is false. Finding this value does not by itself constitute the general proof; that proof rests on the arbitrary pairs in the demonstration.

Check your reasoning

  • You find 6 socks, 3 pairs and a remainder of 0.
  • You identify the true condition and the false conclusion of the converse.
  • You do not call an even total a counterexample to a rule whose condition requires an odd total.

Exercise 3 · Reconstruction · defend the conclusion

Prove it without seeing the batches

The counts x and y are hidden, but their total is reported to be odd. Without choosing their values, write a proof by contraposition that at least one is odd. What role do a and b play? Can you identify the odd batch or assert that both are odd? Answer using the batches from the scene again.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with “if both batches were even…” and represent each by its number of pairs.

Hint 2

The same total 7 can come from A containing 4 and B containing 3, or from swapping these batches. Distinguish showing that something exists from identifying it.

Compare with the solution

The contrapositive starts with batches that are both even. Write x = 2a and y = 2b, where a and b are nonnegative integer numbers of pairs. Then x + y = 2a + 2b = 2(a + b). Since a + b is an integer, the total is even. The contrapositive is proved; logical equivalence therefore establishes the goal for all counts in our domain.

Neither a nor b represents a remainder, and they do not need to be equal. This proof applies to arbitrary counts; it does not start from a handful of observed totals.

The total 7 does not identify the odd batch: A may contain 4 and B 3, or the other way round. It does not require both batches to be odd either, since the initial case includes an even batch. The exact conclusion is “at least one odd batch”.

Check your reasoning

  • You state the hypothesis of the contrapositive before writing the even counts.
  • You explain why a + b is an integer and why that makes the total even.
  • You explicitly return from ¬Q ⇒ ¬P to P ⇒ Q.
  • You distinguish the general proof, the numerical illustration and the rejection of an overly strong conclusion.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you form the contrapositive of “if P, then Q”?

Negate the propositions and reverse their order: “if not Q, then not P”, or ¬Q ⇒ ¬P. This implication has the same truth value as P ⇒ Q.

Recall 2What is the negation of “at least one batch of socks is odd”?

No batch is odd, so both are even for nonnegative integer counts. “At least one even” would still allow an odd batch.

Recall 3Why does the proof about batches that are both even establish our goal?

Writing x = 2a and y = 2b gives x + y = 2(a + b), an even total because a + b is an integer. This proves the contrapositive of “odd total ⇒ at least one odd batch”, and therefore that implication itself.

Recall 4Which statement is the converse of our goal, and which case refutes it?

“At least one odd batch ⇒ odd total”. With 3 socks in each batch, the batches are odd but the total 6 is even. Its condition is true and its conclusion is false.

Recall 5What does the case 4 + 3 = 7 show, and what is it insufficient to prove?

It illustrates an odd total with only one odd batch and refutes the conclusion “both batches odd”. It does not by itself prove the implication for all batches; arbitrary counts of pairs support that proof.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the rule for the contrapositive by naming the condition and the conclusion, then their negations. Repeat the proof with the numbers of pairs a and b. Finish with the different roles of 4 + 3 = 7 and 3 + 3 = 6: illustrate the goal, reject an overly strong conclusion, refute the converse.
