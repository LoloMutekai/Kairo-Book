Level 4 · C263·M4

Decision cost

Your goalMove from a probability of a price rise to a choice between buying and staying out by calculating monetary consequences, costs and the decision threshold.

Before you start
c105-m5 : cost and limits · c201-m5 : costs and events · c262-m5 : robustness across multiple regimes · c261-m5 : model uncertainty · c041-m5 : transactions and windows · c263-m1 : temporal labels · c263-m2 : overlap · c263-m3 : nested temporal validation

✿01 See the idea

In a simulation on paper, you can buy a lot and then sell it at a fixed horizon, or hold no position. The model assigns a probability p = 0.60 to a price rise; the probability of a fall is its complement q = 1 − p = 0.40. You might be tempted to buy because a rise is more likely than a fall. But how much money does each outcome actually leave?

The lot earns 8 € before costs if the price rises and loses 10 € before costs if the price falls. The round trip costs 1 € on entry, 1 € on exit and 1 € in total execution shortfall: the prices obtained are less favorable than the reference prices. These costs are also paid when the prediction is correct. Staying out leaves a relative result of 0 €.

All these values and the probability are fictional, chosen for the calculation. They are neither market prices nor an observed success rate. The label is the direction of the price movement before costs at this horizon; it does not indicate a positive net result. Decision cost requires connecting this label to the consequences of the action.

Before looking at the solution, predict the sign of the average result after costs. A decision based on expected value compares averages predicted by a model; it does not predict which outcome will occur on the next purchase.

The rule to remember

To choose, compare the net consequences of the actions under the same assumptions. A probability of a price rise only becomes a buying threshold once the gains, losses, costs and alternative have been defined.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A likely price rise can still leave a negative net expected value

The gross consequences for the lot are +8 € or −10 €. The probability of a rise is 3/5, and of a fall 2/5. The total round-trip cost is deterministic and identical in each outcome. Let us calculate the expected result, then the probability threshold that makes buying preferable to staying out.

  1. Define the decision problem

    I denotes the information available when making the decision, before any future observation. p is the conditional probability of a rise given I; q = 1 − p is the probability of a fall. These outcomes are mutually exclusive and cover our entire model. p and q are unitless, between 0 and 1. A numerical model output can only be used as p if its probabilistic interpretation is defensible; a simple ranking score is not enough.

    G denotes the gross gain in the rising-price outcome and L the positive magnitude of the gross loss in the falling-price outcome, in euros for this lot and horizon. Here, G = 8 € and L = 10 €. C is the total cost, in euros per round trip. Y denotes the net monetary result of buying. E[Y | I] means the average of Y weighted by the conditional probabilities; E is the expectation operator and the bar is read as “given”.

    We start with no position, keep a fixed lot size and assume that entry and then exit are fully executed at the planned horizon. The alternative is to do nothing, with a relative result of 0 €. Utility is linear: comparing expected euros is sufficient in this model. Risk constraints, taxes, financing and an alternative return are outside the calculation. A preference for safety would require a different utility function.

  2. Build the net consequences before weighting them

    Adding the costs is necessary because the lot incurs entry and exit costs, plus the total execution shortfall: C = 1 + 1 + 1 = 3 €. Here, this last term represents the cumulative monetary difference from the reference prices; it is counted only once for the round trip.

    If the price rises, Y is G − C = 8 − 3 = 5 €. If it falls, Y is −L − C = −10 − 3 = -13 €. Fees therefore also increase the loss. These two values are the entries to weight, rather than just the words “rise” or “fall”.

  3. Weight the results by the correct probabilities

    E[Y|I]=p(G−C)+(1−p)(−L−C)

    The first contribution is 3/5 × 5 = 3 €. The second is 2/5 × (-13) = -26/5 €, or -5.20 €. Probabilities are unitless; each contribution retains the euro as its unit.

    Their sum is 3 + (-26/5) = -11/5 €, or -2.20 €. This is below the 0 € from staying out: under the stated assumptions, buying does not maximize the expected result. This number is not a predicted loss for the next trial; its outcomes remain 5 € or -13 €.

  4. Derive the threshold rather than choosing an arbitrary majority rule

    The costs are paid in every outcome of buying. Since p + q = 1, expanding gives E[Y | I] = pG − (1 − p)L − C = p(G + L) − (L + C). Costs are not multiplied only by the probability of being wrong.

    E[Y|I]>0⇔p>p⋆=L+CG+L

    p⋆, read as “p threshold”, is unitless. Division preserves the direction of the inequality because G + L = 18 € is strictly positive. Here, p⋆ = (10 + 3)/(8 + 10) = 13/18 ≈ 0.7222. We buy only above the threshold; at equality, our convention is to stay out.

    Our p = 0.60 exceeds 0.50, the threshold for a choice based solely on the majority, but remains below 0.7222. Here, both the loss being larger than the gain and the costs raise the economic threshold. This is not a universal rule: if the gains or the alternative change, the comparison must be repeated.

  5. Check the calculation using a different decomposition

    First calculate the gross expected value: 3/5 × 8 − 2/5 × 10 = 24/5 − 4 = 4/5 €, or 0.80 €. Then subtract the certain cost: 4/5 − 3 = -11/5 €, or -2.20 €. This is indeed the result obtained by weighting the two net consequences. Subtracting C again would count the same cost twice.

    Check at the boundary: with p = 13/18 and q = 5/18, the contributions are 65/18 € and -65/18 €; their sum is 0 €. At the endpoints p = 0 and p = 1, the formula gives -13 € and 5 € respectively. The sign and units remain consistent.

    If costs depend on the outcome, the executed size or the information I, replace the common C with the corresponding conditional costs. If they are already included in the results or prices used, subtracting them again is wrong. Our threshold applies to the stated model, not to every possible execution.

  6. Fix the rule before receiving the future test

    The choice of horizon, the definition of consequences, the estimate of p and the cost assumptions must use information available before the decision. Under the temporal protocol in the previous unit, a variant is chosen within the inner periods; the future period reserved for final evaluation must not be used to adjust either the threshold or the rule after its results have been observed.

    The formula calculates what follows from a set of assumptions. It validates neither the calibration of p, meaning the agreement between stated probabilities and observed frequencies in comparable cases, nor the plausibility of G, L and C. Replacing them after the fact with values favorable to the test would create a retrospective decision.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the equations. Reconstruct the two net results, explain why the total cost can be taken outside the average, then derive the threshold. Distinguish what your calculation proves from what a future evaluation would still need to establish.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A rise is more likely, so buying is profitable.”

    The probability does not say how much each outcome yields. Here, p = 0.60 exceeds 0.50, but the gross loss and the cost lead to a net expected value of -2.20 €. Reconstruct the threshold 13/18 from the consequences.

    Derive the threshold in the example

  • “I only pay the fees if my prediction is wrong.”

    The purchased lot incurs its round-trip costs in both outcomes. Write the cost in each box, or subtract C once from the gross expected value. If a price already includes an execution shortfall, do not subtract it again.

    Reread the consequences in the diagram

  • “I found the threshold that works best on the final test: it is validated.”

    The final test has then been used to choose the rule. Return to the protocol: fix the variants within the inner periods, keep a future period outside this choice and evaluate the fixed rule there. A calculated positive expected value remains a conclusion of the model.

    Review how the rule is fixed

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

An uncertain probability spanning the threshold

For this same lot, assume only that p belongs to [0.70 ; 0.80], with G, L and C unchanged. This range is a fictional sensitivity assumption, not an estimated statistical interval. Adopt the following cautious rule: allow buying only if its expected value is strictly positive for every admissible probability. Calculate the expected values at the bounds, then decide. Can the lower bound be replaced with the upper bound after seeing the final test result?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The form p(G + L) − (L + C) is increasing in p because G + L is positive.

Hint 2

To require positivity everywhere, start with the smallest admissible probability.

Compare with the solution

At p = 7/10 and q = 3/10, the contributions are 7/2 € and -39/10 €: the expected value is -2/5 €, or -0.40 €. At p = 4/5, it is 1.40 €. Check: 7/10 × 18 − 13 = -2/5 € and 4/5 × 18 − 13 = 7/5 €.

The slope of the expected value with respect to p is G + L = 18 €, so its minimum over the range occurs at the lower bound. It is negative. The threshold 13/18 lies inside the range; buying does not satisfy the stated cautious rule. Staying out, at 0 €, is chosen under this rule.

Choosing the upper bound after consulting the test would change the assumption to obtain a favorable verdict. The range and the rule must be fixed before the outer evaluation. Their empirical relevance remains to be established; this sensitivity calculation does not claim to measure real uncertainty.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain -0.40 € and 1.40 € at the bounds.
  • Justify why the lower bound is sufficient to check the cautious rule.
  • Connect staying out to the stated rule and reject a retrospective adjustment based on the test.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which consequences must be written down before using the probability of a price rise?

The net result of each action in each outcome: for buying, G − C and −L − C; for staying out, 0 €. The gains, losses and costs concern the same lot, the same horizon and the same monetary unit.

Recall 2Why is the certain round-trip cost subtracted only once from the gross expected value?

It is paid in every outcome of buying, and their probabilities sum to 1. The sum of the weighted costs is therefore C. Subtracting it again after weighting results that are already net would count it twice.

Recall 3Reconstruct the buying threshold and the assumptions behind this formula.

The expected value is p(G + L) − (L + C). If G and L are positive, buying outperforms staying out with a 0 result when p > (L + C)/(G + L). The cost is identical in each outcome, the size and horizon are fixed, the probabilities are interpretable and utility is linear.

Recall 4What does a calculated positive expected value establish, and what remains to be tested?

It establishes a preference under the stated assumptions. The probabilities, consequences, costs and fixed rule still need to be tested on a future period kept separate from the choice. It guarantees neither the next result nor real utility.

Recall 5How can you check a rule that requires a positive expected value over an entire range of probabilities?

With G, L and C fixed, the expected value is increasing in p because G + L is positive. Checking the lower bound is sufficient. A range chosen for an exercise does not constitute measured statistical uncertainty.
