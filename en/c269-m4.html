Level 4 · C269·M4

Publication bias

Your goalExplain how selection linked to results distorts a review, audit its denominator, and distinguish a calculation using all results from a sensitivity analysis for missing studies.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You are preparing a review of a quiet break before an attention task. The articles on your desk give an encouraging impression. In the register of completed studies, many less striking results are still waiting for an article. The articles on your desk and the register do not necessarily give the same picture.

Publication bias occurs when making a study available depends on its results and thereby distorts the accessible results. This filtering can happen before submission, during editorial selection, or through delays in dissemination. A careful literature search does not automatically find what has never been made accessible.

The entire set of studies below is fictional. It contains 12 completed studies on the same task: 4 report an estimated difference of +6 points in favour of the break; 8 report 0 points. Only 3 of the first group and 1 of the second group are published. To isolate the mechanism, we stipulate selection that favours positive results among studies of comparable quality. These are not frequencies measured in actual research.

Here, “favourable” describes the sign of the estimated difference. It means neither a certain result nor a conclusive statistical test. An estimated difference of 0 does not prove the absence of an effect either. Before calculating, simply predict which selection will give the higher mean.

The rule to remember

Ask which studies should have been able to appear, then whether their visibility depends on the result. More favourable publications may reflect the filter as much as the phenomenon being studied.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The quiet break: the same register, two sets of studies to read

The fictional register provides all the results: 4 studies at +6 points and 8 at 0 points; the publications include 3 and 1 of these respectively. Let us calculate a mean per study in each set, then examine what this comparison allows us to say.

  1. Define what the numbers mean and what is included

    For each study, d is the estimated difference between the mean score after the break and the mean score in the comparison condition, in points on the same scale. A positive d favours the break. A denotes the group of favourable studies, and B the group whose estimated difference is 0: d_A = 6 points and d_B = 0 points in this simplified set of studies.

    Each study has the same weight. We calculate the descriptive mean of their estimated differences, without weighting by precision. We assume that the register is complete and contains no duplicates, that scores are comparable, and that results have been transcribed correctly. We have neither participant counts nor uncertainties for each study: this calculation is not a justified estimate of the actual causal effect of the break.

  2. Reconstruct the denominators before the means

    The register contains N = 4 + 8 = 12 completed studies. The published set contains n = 3 + 1 = 4 studies. Changing the set therefore changes the denominator.

    In A, 4 − 3 = 1 study is missing; in B, 8 − 1 = 7 are missing. Their sum gives 8 unpublished studies. Count check: 4 published + 8 unpublished = 12 completed. Adding a second version of the same article would cause this check to fail when study identifiers are compared.

  3. Calculate the mean for the complete register

    The bar above d means the mean. The subscript meaning “all” refers to all completed studies in our register. Multiplying a count by a shared value reconstructs the sum of the differences; dividing by the total count then gives a mean in points.

    d¯toutes=NAdA+NBdBNA+NB

    The sum is 4 × 6 + 8 × 0 = 24 points added across studies. This total is not a person’s score. The mean is 24 ÷ 12 = 2 points per estimated study difference.

    Check: the fraction of favourable studies is 4/12 = 1/3. Since all the other differences are 0, multiplying this fraction by 6 points gives 2 points again.

  4. Recalculate after selection

    The subscript meaning “published” now refers to the set of articles. The calculation rule is the same, but its counts change. The numerator and denominator must change together.

    d¯publiées=3×6+1×04=9/2 points

    The visible sum is 18 points; dividing it by 4 gives exactly 9/2 points, or 4.5 points. The difference between the means is 4.5 − 2 = 2.5 points. It measures the distortion in this constructed set of studies, rather than an actual improvement in attention.

    Composition check: favourable studies now account for 3/4 = 3/4, compared with 1/3 in the register. The mean increases because their weight increases. Yet each individual difference has stayed the same.

  5. Audit the filter while keeping the questions distinct

    The ratio q_A/q_B is 6: in this set, the published fraction is 6 times as large among favourable results. This describes our fictional selection. It is neither a success rate for the break nor a general estimate of editorial practices.

    In this model with constant differences within each group, if the same strictly positive fraction were published in A and B, it would cancel out of the numerator and denominator: the mean would be preserved. The effect of the filter therefore depends on what it favours. Outside this construction, different publication rates can also accompany other differences between studies; the reasons for non-publication must be examined before attributing a cause.

  6. Check using the studies left out

    The unpublished studies contain 1 difference at +6 points and 7 at 0 points. Their sum is 6 points. The total sum can be reconstructed using another partition: 18 visible + 6 missing = 24 points, then 24/12 = 2 points.

    Another check: a mean must stay between the smallest and largest differences included. 2 and 4.5 points are indeed between 0 and 6 points. But this check of bounds is not enough: the complete and selected means both pass it.

  7. Limit the conclusion to the information available

    Here we know the unpublished results thanks to the complete fictional register; we can therefore calculate the exact distortion of this descriptive mean. A review using only articles generally knows neither all the missing studies nor their results. It cannot automatically subtract 2.5 points, and must not replace an absent result with 0.

    For this same set of studies, a traceable review would match register identifiers to articles, check which studies are completed and eligible, then request the missing results. It would record unanswered requests and reasons for non-publication. A protocol announced before the results helps identify what was planned; its presence alone does not guarantee complete dissemination.

    If some results remain unknown, stating assumptions and examining several possible values is a sensitivity analysis. The final exercise does this without inventing the missing values. Favourable selection can exaggerate a mean without making every article false; the absence of an article does not turn a study into evidence of ineffectiveness either.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations and draw the register, then its published part. Explain why their means differ even though no individual result changes. Then say what information you would lack if the register gave only the number of completed studies.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Most articles are favourable, so most studies are too.”

    The favourable fraction is 3/4 among articles, but 1/3 among completed studies. Return to the register and compare the published fractions within each group. A search restricted to articles can find all of a subset that has already been selected.

    Recount the groups in the register diagram

  • “An absent study means a 0 effect; I adjust the mean on that basis.”

    An absent result and an estimated difference of 0 provide different information. Here, the register even identifies an unpublished favourable study. When this information is hidden, you must state assumptions and test their influence, as in the final exercise.

    Examine the sensitivity interval for the set of studies

  • “Favourable selection proves that the break is useless and that the articles are lying.”

    The calculation shows a distorted composition, even when individual results have been transcribed correctly. The complete mean of 2 points remains a mean of estimates; without their uncertainties and an examination of the methods, it does not establish the actual effect. Keep the audit of the filter separate from judgements about each study.

    Review the assumptions and conclusion of the example

Exercise 1 · Identify, then calculate

Which denominator answers which question?

In the same fictional register, 3 of the 4 favourable studies are published, compared with 1 of the 8 studies with a difference of 0. Calculate each published fraction and their ratio. Explain why the favourable proportion among articles does not answer the first question, despite the numerical coincidence.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For a published fraction among favourable studies, the denominator must contain all completed favourable studies.

Hint 2

To compare the dissemination fractions, divide the first ratio by the second; do not just divide the article counts.

Compare with the solution

Published fraction in A: 3/4 = 3/4 = 75 %. In B: 1/8 = 1/8 = 12.5 %. The ratio is (3/4)/(1/8) = 6. Multiplying 1/8 by 6 does indeed give 3/4 again.

The favourable proportion among publications is 3/4 = 3/4. Its denominator counts all articles, whereas the denominator of q_A counts all favourable studies. Here, these denominators are equal; that does not make the questions interchangeable. These calculations describe availability in this set of studies, without measuring the probability that the break works.

Check your reasoning

  • I name what each denominator contains before dividing.
  • I obtain 75 % and 12.5 %, then a dimensionless ratio of 6.
  • I distinguish dissemination of a study from the actual result of the intervention.

Combine the articles with the register results

Hint 1

Find the sum of the differences in the unpublished set before dividing.

Hint 2

Each mean must be weighted by its number of studies: 4/12 for the articles, 8/12 for the missing studies.

Compare with the solution

The missing sum is 1 × 6 + 7 × 0 = 6 points. The missing mean is 6/8 = 3/4 points, or 0.75 points.

The weights are 1/3 and 2/3, which sum to 1. The complete mean is (1/3) × (9/2) + (2/3) × (3/4) = 3/2 + 1/2 = 2 points.

Check using sums: (18 + 6)/12 = 24/12 = 2 points. Giving the two sets equal weight would give equal weight to groups of different sizes. Our counting unit remains the study, without claiming that their precisions are identical.

  • I link the weights to the study counts and recover 2 points using the sums.
  • I distinguish this descriptive mean from a synthesis weighted by precision, which would require other data.

Exercise 3 · Independent critique and sensitivity analysis

The register no longer reveals the absent results

Return to the same 4 publications and their sum of 18 points. This time, the register attests only to 12 eligible completed studies, without giving the results of the 8 unpublished studies. To explore uncertainty, suppose that each missing difference is between -6 and +6 points. This range is an assumption of the exercise, with no empirical validation. Bound the complete mean. Do the articles determine its sign? What evidence would you request before concluding?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Call the unknown mean of the missing differences m, in points. The complete mean is (18 + 8 × m)/12.

Hint 2

The coefficient of m is positive: the smallest complete mean uses the lower bound for every missing difference, and the largest uses the upper bound.

Compare with the solution

m is unknown, even though the number of missing studies is known. Under the assumed range, their sum runs from 8 × (-6) = -48 to 8 × 6 = 48 points. These are scenarios, rather than recovered results.

18+(-48)12≤d¯toutes≤18+4812

The complete sum can range from -30 to 66 points. The mean can therefore range from -5/2 to 11/2 points, or from -2.5 to 5.5 points. The interval contains 0: the publications do not fix the sign of the complete mean under these assumptions.

This is not a confidence interval: no coverage probability has been calculated. These bounds are conditional on the study counts, equal weighting of studies, and the assumed limits. Without a defensible bound, these articles and the missing study count alone provide no guaranteed finite bounds.

I would request the results, planned measurements, and reports for the studies identified as completed, together with documented reasons for non-publication. I would also check eligibility and duplicates in the register. In the meantime, I would write that the published mean of 4.5 points does not determine the complete mean. I would not replace m with 0 for convenience.

Check your reasoning

  • I write the contribution of the unknown studies before choosing values for the sensitivity analysis.
  • I recover the bounds of -2.5 and 5.5 points and recognise that they are hypothetical.
  • I distinguish sensitivity bounds, statistical uncertainty, and the actual causal effect.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What mechanism should you look for behind a very favourable set of articles?

Visibility that depends on results: some studies become accessible more easily. You must link the articles to the full set of eligible studies, then examine the reasons for absences; a majority of articles does not necessarily represent a majority of studies.

Recall 2How do you distinguish the favourable proportion among articles from the published fraction among favourable studies?

The first divides favourable articles by all articles; the second divides published favourable studies by all completed favourable studies. Their denominators answer different questions, even when they have the same value.

Recall 3Why can you not replace an unpublished result with a difference of 0?

An unknown result can be positive, 0, or negative. The number of missing studies does not give their values. You must seek these results or state sensitivity assumptions, without presenting them as observations.

Recall 4What do the bounds obtained by bounding the missing effects demonstrate?

The possible values of the mean under these bounds, these study counts, and this weighting rule. They provide no coverage probability, no recovered results, and no proof of the actual causal effect. Their validity depends on the assumptions made.

Recall 5What records would allow another person to check the audit of the filter?

Identifiers linking the register, completed studies, and articles; eligibility criteria; recovered results; reasons for absences; and unanswered requests. A protocol announced before the results helps the audit; it does not by itself guarantee a complete set of studies.

Without rereading, reconstruct the sequence: completed studies → selection depending on the result → visible articles → changed mean. Then recover the means of 2 and 4.5 points from the counts. Finish with what the register proves, what remains unknown without it, and the evidence you would request. What you understand
A synthesis depends on what it includes. The fictional register distinguishes favourable results, visible results, and missing results. A list of planned runs plays an analogous role in auditing a technical report; it does not constitute a literature review.
