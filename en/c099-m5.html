Level 2 · C099·M5

Probability calibration

Your goalCompare a predicted probability with an observed frequency, calculate a calibration gap by group, and define what this check allows us to conclude.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional greenhouse, a device predicts the probability of a water leak during the next hour. A prediction of 20 % does not promise a leak in this particular case. It makes a claim about frequency: among many comparable situations receiving this prediction, about 20 % should be followed by a leak.

We recorded the predictions before the outcome, then checked for leaks using the same inspection rule. Group A contains 30 situations with a predicted probability of 20 %, including 9 with a leak. Group B contains 10 situations with a predicted probability of 80 %, including 5 with a leak. Everything is invented for the calculation; these are not readings from the real device.

The first diagram places the predictions and the outcomes side by side. A underestimates the observed frequency; B overestimates it. The second plots these same pairs. On the diagonal, prediction and frequency match. Here, a point above the diagonal indicates underestimation; a point below it indicates overestimation.

Calibration means this agreement between probabilities and frequencies at each prediction level. Ranking asks a different question: do situations receiving the highest score have more leaks? A transformation can preserve their order while changing their probabilities. The threshold serves another purpose: choosing when to intervene.

The rule to remember

Group comparable predictions, count the outcomes, then compare within each group. A correct overall average can hide errors that cancel each other out.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A correct average, predictions to correct

Let us audit the 40 fictional greenhouse situations. The model was fixed before this batch. Groups A and B are defined by their exact predictions, without adjusting their boundaries after seeing the leaks.

  1. Define the event and the meaning of the symbols

    A situation consists of a forecast followed by an inspection at the end of the forecast hour. Y equals 1 if the inspection confirms that a leak occurred during that hour, and 0 otherwise. A leak missed by the inspection would therefore distort our reference. P denotes the probability predicted before the outcome; p is a possible value of P, between 0 and 1, with no unit.

    The calibration ideal is written below. The symbol Pr denotes a probability; the vertical bar means “among the situations where”. The formula says: among situations assigned the prediction p, the probability of a leak is p. It does not say whether a particular individual situation will have a leak.

    Pr(Y=1|P=p)=p

    In this finite batch, we observe only frequencies. We assume that the situations come from the same usage context, without repeating the same incident across several rows. Extrapolation would also require the future to resemble this context and outcomes to be observed without selecting only easy cases. Exact equality in a small batch does not establish the general property.

  2. Relate each count to its group

    The subscripts a and b identify groups A and B. For each group, n denotes its number of situations; k counts those with a leak; p is the prediction shared by its situations; f is the observed frequency, obtained by dividing k by n. The subscripts link each symbol to its group. N is the total number of situations. Group sizes are counts; probabilities, frequencies and weights have no unit.

    fa=kana=930=0.30

    A has 30 % leaks for a predicted 20 %. B has 5 / 10 = 0.50, or 50 %, for a predicted 80 %. The denominator is always the group receiving the prediction being studied, never just the set of leaks.

  3. Measure disagreement without letting opposite signs cancel

    Let d be the absolute gap for a group, with subscript a or b. The bars indicate absolute value: we keep the size of the gap even if its direction changes. We describe the direction separately.

    da=|fa−pa|;db=|fb−pb|

    A: 0.30 − 0.20 = 0.10, or 10 percentage points of underestimation. B: 0.80 − 0.50 = 0.30, or 30 points of overestimation. These are gaps observed in this batch, not error rates known with certainty throughout the greenhouse.

  4. Give each situation equal weight

    N = 30 + 10 = 40 situations. The weight of A is 30 / 40 = 3/4; that of B is 10 / 40 = 1/4. These shares of situations sum to 1. Define E as the mean absolute calibration gap for these groups. It has no unit, and expressing it as a percentage uses percentage points.

    E=naNda+nbNdb

    A contributes (3/4) × (1/10) = 3/40; B contributes (1/4) × (3/10) = 3/40. So E = 3/40 + 3/40 = 3/20, or 0.15, equivalent to 15 percentage points. An unweighted average would give a small group as much importance as the large one.

  5. Check using expected counts

    If we take the predictions seriously, the expected numbers of leaks are, for A, 30 × 0.20 = 6 and, for B, 10 × 0.80 = 8. An expected count is a predicted average, not a promise for this batch. Adding expectations does not require the leaks to be independent.

    The count discrepancies are |9 − 6| = 3 and |5 − 8| = 3. Their sum is 6; divided by 40, it gives 3/20 = E. This check recovers the weighted calculation without using the weights.

    But the expectations total 6 + 8 = 14 leaks, and the observations total 9 + 5 = 14. The average prediction is 14 / 40 = 0.35; the overall frequency is 14 / 40 = 0.35. Their agreement hides underestimation in A and overestimation in B.

  6. Define the limits of the audit and prepare a correction

    These gaps indicate a descriptive disagreement. A finite batch fluctuates: the gaps alone do not quantify how certain we can be of poor calibration. Keep the group sizes visible. If predictions varied within an interval, we would compare its frequency with the average prediction in the group, rather than the midpoint of the interval. Groups that are too broad can still hide internal disagreements.

    Recalibration transforms predictions to align them more closely with frequencies. Here, a small table could replace the prediction for A with 0.30 and that for B with 0.50. Agreement would be exact on the batch used to build the table; this result holds by construction. To evaluate it, freeze the table, then use other situations held out from training both the model and the table. No result for this evaluation batch is provided.

    B would keep the higher prediction, both before and after this transformation. Threshold decisions and their false positives must therefore be examined separately. We stop at this audit and its evaluation protocol, without learning a general fitting method here.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain how the expected counts can be correct in total and wrong in each group. Then reconstruct E from the counts, and say why the corrected table must be judged on other situations.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The score is between 0 and 1, so it is a reliable probability.”

    The interval allows a probabilistic interpretation but does not validate it. In the greenhouse, all predictions are in this interval, yet they do not match their groups’ frequencies. Compare predictions and outcomes on held out cases.

    Review the pairs in the reliability diagram

  • “The average prediction equals the overall frequency: calibration is proved.”

    The expected total and the observed total both equal 14, but the group discrepancies cancel each other out. Take absolute values before aggregating, then retain the limits associated with the finite batch.

    Repeat the check using expected counts

  • “After replacing the predictions with the batch frequencies, I have proved an improvement.”

    You fitted a table and evaluated it on the same outcomes. Its agreement is automatic in these groups. Freeze the table and reserve a batch independent of its construction; also check the threshold decisions and the definition of the label.

    Prepare the evaluation in the last exercise

Exercise 2 · Application · audit a calculation

Two misleading averages

Two arguments are proposed: “The expected and observed totals are equal, so everything is calibrated” and “The average gap is (0.10 + 0.30) / 2”. Refute the first using the groups, then correct the second calculation by giving each situation equal weight.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The 30 situations in A and the 10 in B give the groups different weights.

Hint 2

Also repeat the check using absolute count discrepancies; cancellation of signed differences must not erase the gaps.

Compare with the solution

Both totals equal 14, but A has 3 more leaks than expected and B has 3 fewer. Overall equality therefore does not imply equality within each group.

The proposed calculation gives 0.20. It computes an average that gives equal weight to each group. For our purpose, E = (30 / 40) × 0.10 + (10 / 40) × 0.30 = 0.15, or 15 percentage points. Check: (3 + 3) / 40 = 3/20.

Check your reasoning

  • The cancellation is explained using both count discrepancies.
  • The weights 3/4 and 1/4 replace equal weights.
  • The result 0.15 is recovered by another route.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · protocol and decision

A table that is perfect on its own batch

The manager replaces the predictions for A with 0.30 and those for B with 0.50, then announces: “Calibration is fixed; there will be no more unnecessary interventions.” The rule is to intervene if the probability is greater than or equal to 0.50. Examine this statement on our batch, then propose a check on other situations.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The new prediction for B exactly reaches the inclusive threshold. The observed labels do not change.

Hint 2

Separate the batch used to fit the table from the batch that will be used to judge it.

Compare with the solution

On this batch, the new predictions equal the frequencies by construction: the calibration gap by group is 0. A remains below the threshold; B remains selected because 0.50 equals 0.50. All 10 situations in B would trigger an intervention; only 5 have a leak. The others, 10 − 5 = 5, are false positives. The ranking of the groups and the decisions are unchanged despite the new scale.

Freeze the model, the table, the groups, the time horizon and the inspection rule. Then evaluate predictions and outcomes on a separate batch that was not used to train the model or fit the table, showing the size of each group. Recalculate the gaps and false positives under the chosen rule. If this batch is then used to modify the table, it no longer remains an independent final evaluation.

Our fictional batch does not provide this evaluation. Rare outcomes, missing inspections or a change in the greenhouse context can alter the conclusion. A well calibrated probability still allows outcomes without leaks among the interventions; choosing the threshold requires knowing the cost of both types of error.

Check your reasoning

  • Agreement constructed on the fitting batch is distinguished from validation.
  • The inclusive threshold is applied and 5 false positives are recovered.
  • A separate batch and a stable labeling rule are required, without claiming an unmeasured gain.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What should a prediction of 20 % mean in the greenhouse?

Among many comparable situations assigned this probability, about 20 % should be followed by a leak within the defined hour. The prediction does not determine the individual outcome.

Recall 2How can you reconstruct the mean calibration gap for our groups?

For each group, divide the leaks by all its situations; compare this frequency with the prediction; take the absolute value; multiply by the group’s share of situations, then add. Keep the group sizes visible and use the same groups.

Recall 3Why is equality of the expected and observed totals not enough?

Underestimation in A and overestimation in B can cancel each other out. Calibration must be examined at the level of the predictions, not only after pooling them.

Recall 4Does better calibration eliminate false positives at a given threshold?

No. Calibration compares predictions and frequencies. Intervening in cases whose probability of a leak remains below 1 can still produce false positives. The threshold and error costs must be examined separately.

Recall 5What is missing after replacing each prediction with its group’s frequency?

An evaluation on other situations held out from fitting the model and the table. Agreement on the batch used to choose these values is automatic; it does not demonstrate their future validity.
