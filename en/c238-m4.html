Level 4 · C238·M4

Memory and roughness

Your goalRelate the sign of the covariance of two adjacent increments to the Hurst exponent, then read roughness from the scaling of the standard deviation, using the same time grid and three values of H.

Before you start
c235-m5 : divergence · c232-m5 : short-time limits · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c238-m1 : fractional covariance · c238-m2 : Hurst exponent · c238-m3 : simulation

✿01 See the idea

A fictional dashboard tracks the logarithm of volatility minute by minute. To keep the calculations exact, we model it as fractional Brownian motion Bh with Hurst exponent H, as in the previous units of this chapter. Nothing is measured here: the series, the unit u and the values of H are invented for the exercise.

The first question concerns memory. Take two consecutive segments, one lasting 9 min and the other 16 min. Does knowing that the series rose during the first change what we expect from the second? For H = 0.50, no: the two increments are uncorrelated. For H > 0.50, the rise tends to continue. For H < 0.50, it tends to be followed by a reversal. This sign of dependence between two adjacent increments is what we call memory here.

The second question concerns roughness. It compares the same increment at two time step scales, rather than two adjacent segments. When we move from a time step of 16 min to a time step of 1 min, the typical variation does not decrease in proportion to the duration: the smaller H is, the more slowly it decreases, and the more irregular the path remains at the fine scale.

The same parameter H links both questions in this model, but they examine two distinct objects: the sign of a covariance between two increments, and a scaling law for a single increment. Confusing them is the most common mistake. This unit stops here: simulation is covered in C238·M3 and the limitations of Ito calculus in C238·M5.

Assumptions: a centered Gaussian process with covariance given by the formula in C238·M1, and H strictly between 0 and 1. Outside this model, nothing guarantees that memory and roughness vary together.

The rule to remember

Memory: the covariance C of two adjacent increments has the sign of H − 0.50. Roughness: multiplying the time step by k multiplies the standard deviation by k to the power H, so apparent speed, the standard deviation divided by duration, increases more as the time step decreases when H is smaller.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Three values of H on the grid 0, 9, 25 minutes

A fictional indicator of log volatility follows centered fractional Brownian motion, with a fictional unit u. At times 0, 9 and 25 minutes, calculate the covariance C of the two adjacent increments and then ρ², for H = 0.25, 0.50 and 0.75. Then compare the standard deviation of an increment over 16 minutes with that over 1 minute.

  1. Set out the model, symbols and assumptions

    Times are expressed in minutes, with origin 0 min. Bh(t) is fractional Brownian motion with Hurst exponent H, where 0 < H < 1 and Bh(0) = 0. We assume a centered Gaussian process with covariance given by the formula in C238·M1, where s and t are two times:

    Cov(BH(s),BH(t))=12(s2H+t2H−|t−s|2H)

    By convention, every variance is expressed in u²: a time step of one minute has variance 1 u², and the exponent 2H applies only to the number of minutes. The two adjacent segments run from 0 to a minutes, then from a to a + b minutes, with a = 9 and b = 16. Their increments are I1 = Bh(a) − Bh(0) and I2 = Bh(a + b) − Bh(a). V1 and V2 are their variances, C is their covariance and ρ² = C² / (V1 V2) is a dimensionless number.

    This choice of a and b is not based on market considerations: the square roots 3, 4 and 5 of 9, 16 and 25 are integers, which keeps all the calculations below exact.

  2. Derive V₁, V₂ and C

    With s = t, the covariance gives Var(Bh(t)) = t to the power 2H. For s < t, expanding the variance of a difference gives:

    Var(BH(t)−BH(s))=(t−s)2H

    Thus V1 = a to the power 2H and V2 = b to the power 2H. Since Bh(0) = 0, I1 = Bh(a) and C = Cov(Bh(a), Bh(a + b)) − Var(Bh(a)). The covariance formula gives Cov(Bh(a), Bh(a + b)) = half of [a to the power 2H + (a + b) to the power 2H − b to the power 2H]; we still need to subtract Var(Bh(a)) = a to the power 2H, giving:

    C=Cov(I1,I2)=12((a+b)2H−a2H−b2H)

    Interpretation: if 2H exceeds 1, the power function is superadditive, (a + b) to the power 2H exceeds a to the power 2H + b to the power 2H, and C is positive. If 2H equals 1, equality gives C = 0. If 2H is less than 1, C is negative.

  3. First case, H = 0.50: the control case with no memory

    Here 2H = 1, so V1 = 9 u², V2 = 16 u² and the power of 25 equals 25. The sum 9 + 16 equals 25; the difference 25 − 25 equals 0.

    C=12(25−9−16)=02=0u²

    The two increments are uncorrelated; since they are Gaussian, they are independent. This case serves as a reference: any nonzero covariance in the following cases comes from H rather than the grid.

  4. Second case, H = 0.25: the rough path

    Here 2H = 0.50, so taking this power means taking a square root. The roots are V1 = 3 u², V2 = 4 u² and 5 for the total duration. Step by step: 3 + 4 = 7; 5 − 7 = -2; -2 ÷ 2 = -1.

    C=12(5−3−4)=-22=-1u²

    C is negative: a rising segment tends to be followed by a reversal. Adjacent increments are antipersistent and are not independent.

  5. Third case, H = 0.75: the persistent path

    Here 2H = 1.50, a power of three halves: x to the power three halves equals x times its square root. Thus a to the power three halves = 9 × 3 = 27; b to the power three halves = 16 × 4 = 64; (a + b) to the power three halves = 25 × 5 = 125. Then 27 + 64 = 91 and 125 − 91 = 34.

    C=12(125−27−64)=342=17u²

    C is positive: a rising segment tends to be followed by a continuation of the rise. V1 = 27 u² and V2 = 64 u².

  6. Check using the variance of the total and ρ²

    First check, using another approach: the total I1 + I2 = Bh(a + b) − Bh(0) has variance (a + b) to the power 2H, which also equals V1 + V2 + 2C. For H = 0.75:

    V1+V2+2×C=27+64+2×17=125

    For H = 0.25:

    V1+V2+2×C=3+4+2×(-1)=5

    The two totals do give 125 and 5. Second check: the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality requires C² ≤ V1 V2, so ρ² stays between 0 and 1. We calculate:

    ρ2=112≈0.083
    ρ2=2891728≈0.167

    ρ² is the fraction of the variance of I2 explained by the best linear prediction from I1. It is 0 for H = 0.50. These are properties of the fictional model: a single observed pair of segments would not be enough to estimate H.

  7. Move on to roughness: the same increment at two time steps

    Compare the time step of 1 min with the time step of b = 16 min. The variance over 1 min equals 1 u² for every H, because 1 raised to any power remains 1. Over 16 min, b to the power 2H equals 4 u² for H = 0.25, 16 u² for H = 0.50 and 64 u² for H = 0.75.

    The standard deviations, obtained by taking square roots, are 2 u, 4 u and 8 u (their squares give 4, 16 and 64 again). Apparent speed is the standard deviation divided by duration, in u per minute. Moving from the time step of 16 min to the time step of 1 min multiplies it by 16 ÷ 2 = 8 for H = 0.25, by 16 ÷ 4 = 4 for H = 0.50 and by 16 ÷ 8 = 2 for H = 0.75.

    The smaller H is, the more apparent speed increases as we refine the time step: this is roughness. It increases for every value of H below 1, indicating a path with no defined slope at the fine scale.

  8. Interpret the result within its limits

    On the same grid, C equals -1, 0 and 17 u² for H = 0.25, 0.50 and 0.75; ρ² equals 0.083, 0 and 0.167; apparent speed increases by factors of 8, 4 and 2. Memory and roughness vary together here because the model ties both to H.

    For H > 0.50, correlations between increments of one minute are known to decay so slowly with lag that their sum diverges: this is long memory. We have not proved it here. None of this proves anything about a real series: estimating H from data is a separate task, which this unit does not cover.

Explain it in your own words

Why does a smaller H give both a negative covariance between adjacent segments and a greater increase in apparent speed? Do these two facts come from the same calculation or from two different calculations, and what would happen if the process were not Gaussian?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A small H means there is no memory.”

    For H = 0.25, the covariance on the grid equals -1 u² and ρ² equals 0.083: dependence exists, but its sign is negative. Only H = 0.50 gives 0 covariance, and therefore independent increments under this model.

    Revisit the three cases in the example

  • “Memory and roughness are the same property, so a single measurement is enough.”

    Memory is read from a covariance between two adjacent increments; roughness is read from the variance of an increment at two time steps. In the exact fractional model, H controls both; outside this model, nothing forces them to agree.

    Reread the two questions and the rule

  • “A covariance of 17 is strong because the number is large.”

    C is in u² and changes with the durations: on the grid scaled by 4, it takes different values. Compare the dimensionless ρ²: it equals 0.167 for H = 0.75, far from 1.

    Move on to the scaling exercises

Exercise 1 · Explained application

All durations are multiplied by 4

Take H = 0.25 again. The two segments now last 36 min and 64 min, for a total of 100 min. Calculate the covariance C of their increments, then the factor by which it has changed compared with the example. The values are fictional.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The square roots of 36, 64 and 100 are integers: calculate them first.

Hint 2

Apply the same formula as in the example: half of the quantity obtained by subtracting the other two square roots from the square root of the total duration.

Compare with the solution

The roots are 6, 8 and 10. Then 6 + 8 = 14, 10 − 14 = -4, and -4 ÷ 2 = -2 u².

The factor equals -2 ÷ (-1) = 2. This follows because all durations are multiplied by 4, so the power 2H = 0.50 multiplies C by 4 to the power 0.50, that is, by 2. The sign remains negative.

Check your reasoning

  • The three roots are calculated exactly before adding.
  • The sign of C is preserved and discussed.
  • The factor is linked to the exponent 2H rather than H alone.

Exercise 2 · Guided reasoning

Infer H from two variances

Two fictional series A and B have variance 1 u² over a time step of one minute. Over a time step of 16 minutes, A has variance 64 u² and B has variance 4 u². From H = 0.25, 0.50 and 0.75, assign a value to each series, say which is rougher and give the expected sign of the covariance of two adjacent increments.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Reread the variance over 16 min for each of the three values in the example.

Hint 2

The sign of C is read from H compared with 0.50, rather than from the variance alone.

Compare with the solution

A variance of 64 u² over 16 min corresponds to H = 0.75: this is A. A variance of 4 u² corresponds to H = 0.25: this is B.

B is rougher: its apparent speed increases by a factor of 8 as the time step decreases, compared with 2 for A. For A, H exceeds 0.50 and C is positive (it equals 17 u² on the grid in the example); for B, C is negative (-1 u²).

Limitation: we chose from three candidates and assumed the model was exact. Without this assumption, two variances do not determine H.

Check your reasoning

  • The two variances are compared at the same time step.
  • Roughness is justified using apparent speed.
  • The sign of C comes from H relative to 0.50, and the limitation of the identification is stated.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and checking

Does correlation change with scale?

Keep H = 0.25 and the grid scaled by 4 from the previous exercise, with segments lasting 36 min and 64 min. Calculate ρ² and compare it with the value in the example. What can you conclude about using raw covariance to compare two grids?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The variances V₁ and V₂ are the roots 6 and 8, in u².

Hint 2

ρ² is C² divided by the product V₁ V₂; use C from the previous exercise.

Compare with the solution

The product of the variances equals 6 × 8 = 48 and C² = 4. Thus ρ² = 4 ÷ 48 = 0.083, matching the value 0.083 in the example.

When all durations are multiplied by the same factor k, C, V1 and V2 are each multiplied by k to the power 2H. C² and V1 V2 are therefore multiplied by the same factor k to the power 4H, and their quotient ρ² does not change: it depends only on H and the ratio of the two durations. Raw covariance depends on scale; ρ² therefore allows the comparison.

Check your reasoning

  • C² and V₁ V₂ are used with their exact values.
  • The equality of the two values of ρ² is stated and then explained.
  • The conclusion distinguishes a raw quantity from a dimensionless quantity.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the covariance of two adjacent increments measure, and what is its sign for different H?

Their linear dependence. It is positive for H > 0.50, 0 for H = 0.50 and negative for H < 0.50, under the centered fractional model.

Recall 2What formula gives C for two adjacent segments with durations a and b?

Half of [(a + b) to the power 2H minus a to the power 2H minus b to the power 2H].

Recall 3How does the standard deviation of an increment change when the time step is multiplied by k?

It is multiplied by k to the power H. The smaller H is, the more apparent speed increases as we refine the time step.

Recall 4Why does a small H not mean an absence of memory?

The covariance of two adjacent increments is then negative, hence nonzero: the dependence is antipersistent. Only H = 0.50 gives independent increments.

Recall 5Why compare ρ² rather than C across two time grids?

C is in u² and scales with the durations. ρ² is dimensionless and stays the same when all durations are multiplied by the same factor.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Set out centered Gaussian Bₕ with its covariance; derive V₁, V₂ and C for two adjacent segments; read memory from the sign of C, which follows H − 0.50, and normalize it using ρ² before any comparison; read roughness from the increase in apparent speed as the time step decreases; check using the variance of the total; retain the model assumption and draw no conclusions about a real series.

What you understand
