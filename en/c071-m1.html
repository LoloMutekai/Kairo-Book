Level 2 · C071·M1

Paths and multiplicative propagation

Your goalCalculate the probability of completing a path through relays and justify what each factor in the product means.

Before you start
c070-m5 : minimum spanning tree · c029-m5 : Bayes · c030-m5 : Poisson · c063-m5 : synthetic dataset and external validity

✿01 See the idea

In a workshop, a radio alert starts at A and must reach hangar D by passing through B on the roof, then C in the yard. These 2 intermediate relays must receive the alert before retransmitting it. If it stops along the way, D does not receive it along this path.

A, B, C and D are the vertices of the graph; an arrow is a directed link, usable in the direction shown. The path A → B → C → D is the sequence of links we follow. It has no return link or backup route. A sends the alert with certainty; the model allows 1 attempt per link, with no retry after a failure.

At each step, only a fraction of the possibilities that have reached that point continues. For A → B, this fraction is p = 4/5; for B → C, q = 3/4; for C → D, r = 1/2. These are probabilities with no units. The parameters and the entire scene are fictional: they come from no radio measurements.

We first assume that link successes are independent: knowing whether the other links succeed does not change the probability that this link succeeds. The fraction that passes through the last relay applies to what has reached C. It does not apply to all the alerts sent at the start. This change in the reference group explains the multiplication.

The rule to remember

Along a path, multiply the probability of reaching the current vertex by the probability of passing through the next link, conditional on the path already completed successfully.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Getting the alert to the hangar

A has sent the alert. The independent links A → B, B → C and C → D succeed with respective probabilities 4/5, 3/4 and 1/2. A failed link stops this transmission. What is the probability of reaching D along this path?

  1. Define the event of interest

    E means “link A → B succeeds”, F means “B → C succeeds” and G means “C → D succeeds”. We can specify the potential outcome of each link in advance, even if the alert ultimately does not reach it. Reaching D requires E, F and G together. The intersection E ∩ F ∩ G denotes exactly this joint success.

    P denotes a probability. The vertical bar in P(F | E) is read as “given E”: we restrict attention to the possibilities where E has succeeded. s(X) denotes the probability of reaching vertex X from A; here X is one of the names A, B, C or D. All these probabilities have no units and lie between 0 and 1. Since A has sent the alert, s(A) = 1.

  2. Why the product appears

    By the definition of conditioning, the probability that E succeeds and then F succeeds is P(E) multiplied by the fraction of E cases in which F succeeds. We apply the same reasoning to G among the cases where E and F succeed. Here, these conditioning events have strictly positive probabilities.

    P(E∩F∩G)=P(E)P(F|E)P(G|E∩F)

    This equality does not assume independence. In our independent model, P(F | E) = P(F) = q and P(G | E ∩ F) = P(G) = r; p = P(E). We can therefore use the individual link probabilities in the product p × q × r. Without independence, we would need the correct conditional probabilities; marginal probabilities, given without conditions on the other links, would not be enough.

  3. Go from the workshop to the roof

    The first link lets a fraction p of the certain starting event pass through. We multiply s(A) by p: 1 × 4/5 = 4/5. Thus s(B) = 4/5, or 0.80 with no units. This number already describes the path from A.

  4. Go from the roof to the yard

    The fraction q applies to the cases where B has received the alert. We calculate s(C) = s(B) × q = 4/5 × 3/4. Multiplying the numerators counts the favorable possibilities; multiplying the denominators counts the total possibilities.

    s(C)=4×35×4=1220=3/5

    We obtain 0.60 with no units. This is not q = 0.75: q described success on the next link, whereas s(C) also includes the preceding link.

  5. Reach the hangar

    Among the possibilities that have reached C, the fraction r continues to D. The rule stays the same: probability of next arrival = probability of current arrival × probability of success on the next link, conditional on the path completed so far.

    s(D)=s(C)r=3/5×1/2=3/10=0.30

    The result 0.30, with no units, describes the probability under this model. It does not guarantee that this particular alert will arrive. We have neither repeated real trials nor estimated the reliability of any equipment.

  6. Check by counting another way

    Let us construct an exact implementation of the fictional model. The draw for A → B has 5 equally likely outcomes, including 4 favorable ones; the draw for B → C has 4, including 3 favorable ones; the draw for C → D has 2, including 1 favorable outcome. An outcome is a possible result of the draw; equally likely means having the same probability. The draws are independent, so all their combinations are equally likely.

    There are 5 × 4 × 2 = 40 combinations. After the first filter, 4 × 4 × 2 = 32 remain possible for arrival at B; after the next, 4 × 3 × 2 = 24 for C. Finally, 4 × 3 × 1 = 12 reach D. We recover 12/40 = 3/10. The other 28 interrupt the path; 12 + 28 = 40.

    These combinations form a mathematical sample space, rather than 40 observed transmissions. Also check the ordering 0 ≤ 3/10 ≤ 3/5 ≤ 4/5 ≤ 1: adding a success condition cannot increase the probability of completing the entire path successfully. A certain link leaves the product unchanged; an impossible link makes it 0.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain which group of possibilities q applies to, then which group r applies to. Reconstruct the product and state when you can use the marginal link probabilities.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I add the link probabilities to find the probability of the path.”

    Every link must succeed. The sum 0.80 + 0.75 + 0.50 gives 2.05, above 1: this is not a probability. Each link filters the possibilities that have already arrived; the product gives 3/10. Review the definition of the joint event.

    Review the intersection and the conditional product

  • “The last link succeeds with probability r, so D is reached with probability r.”

    r concerns link C → D. To attempt it, we must first reach C, which happens with probability 3/5. Multiply this fraction by r; the diagram distinguishes the link from arrival starting at A.

    Compare the link labels and the probabilities of arrival

  • “Any label between 0 and 1 can be used in a product of probabilities.”

    We need to know what the label measures. Marginal probabilities of dependent events cannot replace the conditional probabilities along the path. A confidence score is not a transmission probability merely because of its numerical range, either. The last exercise shows the product of marginal probabilities failing.

    Study the variant where link successes depend on one another

Exercise 1 · Application · limiting cases

Repair or cut the last link

In the same independent chain, keep p and q. Calculate s(D) if C → D becomes certain, r = 1, then if it is impossible, r = 0. Is a perfect last link enough to guarantee arrival at the hangar?

Hint 1

Start again from s(C), then apply the factor for the last link.

Hint 2

A local repair does not repair the preceding links.

Compare with the solution

With r = 1, s(D) = 3/5 × 1 = 3/5, or 0.60 with no units. The possibilities lost before C are still lost, so arrival is not guaranteed.

With r = 0, s(D) = 3/5 × 0 = 0. All the possibilities that would have reached C are stopped at the next link.

  • Obtain 3/5 and 0, explaining each result.
  • Distinguish a certain link from a certain path.

Exercise 2 · Inverse calculation · check

Find the factor for the central relay

Still in the same independent scene, p = 4/5 and r = 1/2 stay fixed. A different configuration of the central relay must give s(D) = 1/5. What value of q works? Check that it is admissible, then work forwards along the path again.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write s(D) = p × q × r and group the known factors p × r.

Hint 2

Divide the target by this nonzero product. A probability must stay between 0 and 1.

Compare with the solution

The known factors give p × r = 4/5 × 1/2 = 2/5. The equation becomes 1/5 = 2/5 × q; we therefore need q = 1/5 ÷ 2/5 = 1/2, or 0.50 with no units. Here, dividing removes the effect of the factors already known.

The value lies in [0 ; 1]. Check forwards: s(B) = 4/5, s(C) = 4/5 × 1/2 = 2/5, then s(D) = 2/5 × 1/2 = 1/5. We recover the target exactly. Setting q = 1 would give at most 2/5: a higher target would be impossible with p and r unchanged.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify dividing by 2/5, which is nonzero.
  • Find q = 1/2 and check that it lies in [0 ; 1].
  • Recalculate the successive probabilities of arrival up to the target.

Exercise 3 · Reasoning · assumptions

The same marginal probabilities, a different result

The same links now share a common electrical state. Only their marginal probabilities P(E) = 4/5, P(F) = 3/4, P(G) = 1/2 are given. In addition, every success of G implies that F succeeds, and every success of F implies that E succeeds. A has still sent the alert. What is the probability of reaching D? Is the product of marginal probabilities still justified?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

If G occurs, what do you already know about F and E?

Hint 2

Compare the event “E and F and G” with the event G alone. The given numbers are no longer the conditional probabilities of the first example.

Compare with the solution

The events are nested: G is a subset of F, which is itself a subset of E. Success on G therefore means success on every link. Thus P(E ∩ F ∩ G) = P(G) = 1/2, or 0.50 with no units.

The product of marginal probabilities would still give 3/10, which is different. The links are not independent: learning that a link has succeeded gives information about the electrical state and the other links. The formula using conditional probabilities remains valid, but the marginal probabilities q and r can no longer replace them. Without the nesting information or other information about dependence, the marginal probabilities alone would not determine the probability of the path.

Check your reasoning

  • Explain why the intersection equals G before calculating.
  • Obtain 1/2 and distinguish it from 3/10.
  • Identify the unjustified substitution of marginal probabilities for conditional probabilities.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What event represents the alert arriving at D along our path?

The joint success E ∩ F ∩ G of links A → B, B → C and C → D. A has already sent the alert, with no other route or retry.

Recall 2What product remains valid without independence, and what do its factors mean?

P(E) × P(F | E) × P(G | E ∩ F): succeeding on the first link, then on the next among those successes, then on the last among the preceding successes. The conditioning events must have positive probabilities for these conditional probabilities to be defined.

Recall 3How can we check 3/10 without simply repeating the same product of fractions?

In our sample space of independent draws, count 40 equally likely combinations and 12 in which every link succeeds, then calculate 12/40 = 3/10. Also check that the probability of arrival decreases or stays constant at each link. These numbers are not observations.

Recall 4Why are link probabilities given separately not always enough?

Using their product here requires independence, or replacing them with the appropriate conditional probabilities. With the same marginal probabilities but G a subset of F, which is a subset of E, the probability of the path is 1/2 instead of 3/10.

Recall 5What does the exact calculation for our path fail to demonstrate?

It demonstrates neither the reliability of a real radio, nor the outcome of a particular alert, nor the probability of a cascade with several paths. The model and its parameters need separate justification.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the formulas, reconstruct the chain of reasoning: define joint success; start from a source that has sent the alert; apply each conditional probability to the fraction that has already arrived; explain when marginal probabilities can replace it; check using outcomes and bounds; distinguish exact calculation from fit to reality.
