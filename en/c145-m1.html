Level 2 · C145·M1

Endogeneity

Your goalExplain how unobserved demand distorts the slope of sales against extra staffing hours, then distinguish the causal model error from the fitted residual.

Before you start
c074-m5 : identification assumptions · c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c101-m5 : rolling-origin forecasting

✿01 See the idea

A bakery schedules more extra staffing hours when it expects more customers. Mornings with more extra staff also end with more pastries sold. How much comes from the extra staffing, and how much would have come from the customers anyway?

Extra staffing can help serve customers. But demand also affects sales and the staffing schedule. If you compare all the mornings directly, the hours therefore carry information about demand. This link with a cause left out of the regression makes the hours endogenous here.

The scene and all its values are fictional. We construct a small population of morning types A, B and C with equal probabilities. The manager’s records contain only hours X and sales Y; to understand the trap, we also reveal a contribution U from demand, which would normally be unobserved. U is a deviation in sales, not a number of customers: its negative value does not mean negative demand.

Read the arrows first: they state the assumptions of this story; a correlation does not reveal them. We want the effect of adding an extra staffing hour while keeping demand the same. Instruments and the other methods in the chapter will follow this diagnosis.

The rule to remember

A slope that compares mornings with different demand can absorb part of the effect of demand. We need to examine what the structural error contains, even when the numerical fit is correct.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A correctly calculated slope, a wrongly attributed effect

Take the 3 equally likely types in the diagram. The chosen causal model is Y = α + βX + U, with α = 10 items and β = 2 items per hour. We will fit a line using only columns X and Y, then measure what it wrongly attributes to extra staffing.

  1. State what changes and what stays fixed

    X is the duration of extra staffing during a morning, in hours; Y is the number of pastries sold that same morning, in items. α is the baseline of the model, in items; β is the effect of an added hour at fixed demand, in items per hour. Here U contains the omitted contribution of demand to sales, in items. Lowercase xᵢ, yᵢ and uᵢ denote the values for type i, where i ranges over A, B and C.

    Y=α+βX+U

    The equation is an assumption built into the construction: if we add an hour while holding U fixed, Y increases by 2 items. We assume the same effect over the range studied, enough stock to serve customers and no other omitted cause. This deliberately simple model does not claim to describe every real bakery.

    Data check: A gives 10 + 2 × 1 + (-2) = 10 items; B gives 10 + 2 × 2 + 1 = 15 items; C gives 10 + 2 × 3 + 1 = 17 items.

  2. Specify the exogeneity condition that fails

    E denotes the mean under the probabilities of this population; E(U | X = x) is the mean of U among mornings with x extra staffing hours. The mean exogeneity condition requires this mean to be 0 for each value of X. Here E(U) = (-2 + 1 + 1)/3 = 0 item, but E(U | X = 1 h) = -2 items. Overall centring is therefore insufficient.

    E(U|X)=0

    In this linear model, a nonzero covariance between X and U also shows that this condition fails and shifts the slope. Cov(X,U) measures their joint variation around their means. The equality Cov(X,U) = 0 alone would be a weaker condition than a 0 conditional mean; neither expression by itself replaces the causal assumptions of the story.

  3. Centre the values to compare variations

    The bar indicates the mean. Since each type has probability 1/3, the population mean is also the mean of the rows: x̄ = (1 + 2 + 3)/3 = 6/3 = 2 h. Similarly, ȳ = (10 + 15 + 17)/3 = 42/3 = 14 items.

    Write dᵢ = xᵢ − x̄, in hours, and vᵢ = yᵢ − ȳ, in items. The deviations of X are (-1, 0, 1) h; those of Y are (-4, 1, 3) items. Each list sums to 0: centring removes the mean level.

  4. Calculate the least squares slope

    The fitted line is ŷᵢ = a + bxᵢ: the hat denotes sales predicted by this line; a is its intercept, in items, and b its slope, in items per hour. Least squares chooses a and b to minimise the sum of squared residuals yᵢ − ŷᵢ. With an intercept and nonzero variation in X, the slope formula uses Sxx, the sum of squared dᵢ values, and Sxᵧ, the sum of products dᵢvᵢ; Σ means adding over types A, B and C.

    b=ΣdiviΣdi2;a=y¯−bx¯
    Sxx=(-1)2+02+12=1+0+1=2 heures au carré

    Sxᵧ = (-1) × (-4) + 0 × 1 + 1 × 3 = 4 + 0 + 3 = 7 h·items. Type B does contribute to the means; its 0 deviation dᵢ only makes its centred products 0.

    The slope is b = 7/2 = 7/2 = 3.5 items per hour. Then a = 14 − 3.5 × 2 = 14 − 7 = 7 items. The quotient has consistent units: h·items divided by hours squared gives items per hour.

  5. See how demand enters the slope

    The mean of U is 0 in this population. Centring the structural equation gives vᵢ = βdᵢ + uᵢ. Multiply by dᵢ, then add: Sxᵧ = βSxx + Sxᵤ, where Sxᵤ = Σdᵢuᵢ is the sum of products of deviations in hours and demand contributions.

    b=β+SxuSxx

    Here Sxᵤ = (-1) × (-2) + 0 × 1 + 1 × 1 = 2 + 0 + 1 = 3 h·items. The contamination of the slope is therefore 3/2 = 3/2 = 1.5 item per hour. The positive sign comes from the higher demand that accompanies more extra staffing in this population.

  6. Move from sums to population moments

    Var(X) is the mean of the squared deviations of X, in hours squared. Cov(X,U) is the mean of the products of deviations of X and U, in h·items. Here E(U) is 0, so the uᵢ values are already centred. Dividing the sums by 3 gives Var(X) = 2/3 = 2/3 hours squared and Cov(X,U) = 3/3 = 1 h·item. We are describing the entire constructed population: this is not a variance estimator with a sample correction.

    b=Cov(X,Y)Var(X)=β+Cov(X,U)Var(X)

    The same divisor cancels in the ratio: 1 ÷ (2/3) = 3/2 item per hour. We recover b = 2 + 1.5 = 3.5 items per hour. The difference is already present in the population slope; it does not arise solely from chance in a small sample. Here the word bias denotes this departure from the target in the population, without calculating the expectation of an estimator over repeated samples.

  7. Check the result and its scope

    The line must pass through the means: 7 + 3.5 × 2 = 7 + 7 = 14 items. The structural check recovers the numerator from the effect and demand: 2 × 2 + 3 = 7 h·items, matching the direct calculation of centred products.

    Interpretation check: 3.5 does differ from 2 items per hour. If Sxᵤ were 0, with positive Sxx, the additional term would disappear from this identity. In our data it is nonzero. A correct calculation can therefore answer the question about association without answering the question about intervention.

  8. Separate the structural error from the fitted residual

    U is defined by the causal model; here it contains the omitted demand. The fitted residual eᵢ = yᵢ − a − bxᵢ is what remains after choosing the line. These quantities have the same unit, the item, but different definitions. The fit has already absorbed part of U into b and a.

    With an intercept, the least squares equations impose Σeᵢ = 0 and Σdᵢeᵢ = 0. The residual exercise will verify this for our line, even though Cov(X,U) = 1 h·item. This calculation check therefore does not test the exogeneity of the structural error.

Explain it in your own words

Close the calculation. Describe how demand influences scheduling, then reconstruct the term added to β. Next explain why replacing U with the residuals of the line would make the problem disappear on paper without justifying the causal effect.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “An extra hour gives the observed slope, so that is the effect of extra staffing.”

    The slope 3.5 also compares different demand levels. In our construction, the effect at fixed demand is 2 items per hour and the contribution of demand to the slope is 1.5 item per hour. Start again with the arrows, then Sₓᵤ/Sₓₓ, to state what is held fixed.

    Reconstruct the slope decomposition

  • “The residuals are uncorrelated with hours: I have proved exogeneity.”

    Fitting with an intercept imposes this property on the residuals. It does not concern U, the omitted contribution of demand. Calculate the residuals of our line, then compare their 0 centred product sum with the nonzero Cov(X,U).

    Check the residuals

  • “More days or better forecasts will necessarily remove the bias.”

    Repeating the same types in the same proportions preserves the population slope 3.5. Greater statistical precision around this association is insufficient to recover β. For a causal interpretation, we need to justify why variation in hours is separate from the omitted causes of sales.

    Return to the scheduling mechanism

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Compare at identical demand

In the same fictional population, compare only B and C. Calculate the difference in sales divided by the difference in hours. Why does this result recover β here? State what supports this conclusion in our story.

Hint 1

Look at column U: does its value differ between B and C?

Hint 2

Subtract the structural equations for C and B. The baseline cancels, and so does the demand contribution.

Compare with the solution

The difference in sales is 17 − 15 = 2 items; the difference in hours is 3 − 2 = 1 h. The ratio is 2/1 = 2 items per hour, matching β.

Both demand contributions are 1 item. Subtraction therefore eliminates α and U, leaving β times the difference in hours. This conclusion rests on the given causal model and the absence of any other cause that varies between these types. In a study, labelling them “same demand” would not establish these assumptions; here U is revealed by construction.

  • I cite the equal values of U and the additive model as reasons for the simplification.
  • I distinguish this proof within the example from a justification using real data.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and diagnosis

A check that does not settle causality

Return to the line fitted to all the data, a = 7 items, b = 3.5 items per hour. Calculate the residuals of types A, B and C, then their sum and Σdᵢeᵢ. Does a 0 sum of products justify saying that Cov(X,U) is 0?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Use eᵢ = yᵢ − a − bxᵢ, or the equivalent form eᵢ = vᵢ − bdᵢ.

Hint 2

The product with dᵢ is 0 for B. Keep the fractions for A and C before adding them.

Compare with the solution

A: e = -4 − (-7/2) = -1/2 = -0.5 item. B: d = 0 so e = 1 − 0 = 1 item. C: e = 3 − 7/2 = -1/2 = -0.5 item.

Sum: (-1/2) + 1 = 1/2, then 1/2 + (-1/2) = 0 item. Centred products: (-1) × (-1/2) = 1/2 h·item; 0 × 1 = 0 h·item; 1 × (-1/2) = -1/2 h·item. Their sum is 0 h·item.

These equalities verify the equations for the fitted line. They say nothing similar about the structural contribution U, whose covariance with X is 1 h·item. Confusing e and U would turn a property of the calculation into an unjustified causal assumption.

Check your reasoning

  • I recover the exact residuals and check both sums.
  • I distinguish the unit of the residuals from that of their products with hours.
  • I name U and e separately to answer the final question.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and argument

More rows in the records

The manager adds mornings that reproduce exactly the values and proportions of A, B and C. Only X and Y are kept. The manager claims: “With more rows and accurate forecasts, the slope will necessarily end up measuring the effect of extra staffing.” Respond by explaining what happens to the ratio of sums and to the scheduling problem. What evidence would you need to seek to target a causal effect?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

If each type is repeated the same number of times, how do the means and the two sums defining b change?

Hint 2

Do an hour added because of demand and an hour added independently of demand provide equivalent comparisons?

Compare with the solution

The means remain 2 h and 14 items. The sums Sxᵧ and Sxx are multiplied by the same number of repetitions: their ratio remains 7/2 = 3.5 items per hour. It does not automatically become 2 items per hour. The covariances of this population and the contamination 1.5 item per hour remain the same.

Demand still guides scheduling and sales. A successful forecast under this same arrangement can use this association; it does not necessarily describe what would happen if hours changed while demand stayed fixed.

We would need to justify a comparison in which variation in hours is separate from demand and the other causes of sales, for example random assignment of extra staffing under a suitable protocol. Simply saying “we have more rows” or “we added controls” does not demonstrate this separation. The identification method and its assumptions need their own evidence.

Check your reasoning

  • I justify the stability of the slope using the common factor in the sums.
  • I link the bias to the way extra staffing is assigned.
  • I describe evidence to seek without claiming it exists in the records.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why are extra staffing hours endogenous in the fictional bakery?

Demand contributes to sales through U and also guides scheduling. Hours X are therefore linked to a cause of sales left in the structural error; here Cov(X,U) is nonzero.

Recall 2What decomposition separates the observed slope from the effect at fixed demand?

For Y = α + βX + U, with an intercept and positive Var(X), b = β + Cov(X,U)/Var(X). In our population, 3.5 = 2 + 1.5 items per hour. The second term measures the contamination of the slope.

Recall 3Why is an overall mean of 0 for U insufficient?

Mean exogeneity requires E(U | X) = 0 for each value of X. Here E(U) = 0 item, but for type A the conditional mean is -2 items. The overall mean hides the link with hours.

Recall 4Why do residuals uncorrelated with hours fail to prove exogeneity?

Least squares with an intercept imposes this property on the fitted residuals e. U denotes the structural error of the causal model; part of U may have been absorbed into the coefficients. Our residuals have centred products that sum to 0 despite Cov(X,U) = 1 h·item.

Recall 5What does a precise slope or a good forecast still leave to establish?

The effect of an intervention on hours at fixed demand. We need to justify the separation between variation in extra staffing and the omitted causes of sales; repeating the same associations does not provide this evidence.
