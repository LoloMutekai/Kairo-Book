Level 2 · C061·M3

MAP

Your goalChoose a hypothesis by maximum a posteriori estimation, justify the role of the prior, and explain why the choice can differ from maximum likelihood estimation.

Before you start
c060-m5 : approximations and hypotheses · c059-m6 : deterministic discrete Bellman · c061-m1 : likelihood · c061-m2 : MLE

✿01 See the idea

A label reader has just recognized 2 labels, then rejected 1. What recognition rate should we assign to it? A model offers only hypotheses A, B, and C: recognition probabilities of 1/4, 1/2, and 3/4, respectively, on each trial. This small set is a working assumption, rather than a list of all possible reliability values.

Before these trials, we give more weight to B: its initial probability is 2/3, compared with 1/6 for A and 1/6 for C. This distribution, fixed before seeing the sequence, is called the prior. It represents the information we have chosen to use before these new observations.

Likelihood asks which hypothesis makes the observed sequence most probable. Maximum a posteriori estimation, abbreviated MAP, asks which hypothesis is most probable after combining likelihood and prior. A hypothesis can therefore explain the readings alone better and lose after this combination.

The entire scenario and all its values are fictional. We are not evaluating any real device. The aim is to choose among the stated hypotheses, while distinguishing this choice, its associated probability, and evidence of reliability.

The rule to remember

For each hypothesis, multiply its likelihood by its prior; choose a maximum of these products. A shared positive normalizer does not change their ranking.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Assigning a reliability value to the same label reader

Sequence D is “recognized, recognized, rejected,” in that order: 2 recognitions and 1 rejection over 3 trials. We compare only Θ = { 1/4 ; 1/2 ; 3/4 }. The prior masses of A, B, and C are 1/6, 2/3, and 1/6. Let us calculate the MAP estimate, then check its interpretation.

  1. Specify what varies and what the model assumes

    θ, the Greek letter theta, denotes the unknown probability of recognition. Θ, uppercase theta, is the set of values allowed by our model. The letters A, B, and C name the hypotheses θ = 1/4, θ = 1/2, and θ = 3/4. The probability of rejection is 1 − θ.

    D is the sequence actually observed. We assume the trials are conditionally independent given θ: for a fixed θ, the outcome of a trial does not change the probabilities of the others. We also assume the same probability θ on each trial and no selection of only favorable readings. Without these assumptions, multiplying the same probabilities would not be justified.

    For i equal to A, B, or C, θᵢ is the hypothesis value, πᵢ its prior mass, Lᵢ its likelihood, and sᵢ its score πᵢLᵢ. All these quantities are dimensionless; the trials are counts. The masses πᵢ are nonnegative and their sum is 1. They are fixed before D, without reusing D to construct them.

  2. Calculate the likelihoods of the sequence

    Each recognition contributes a factor θ, and the rejection contributes a factor 1 − θ. L(θ ; D), read as “likelihood of θ for D,” is therefore the product below. We vary θ while keeping D fixed.

    L(θ;D)=θ2(1−θ)

    A: square 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16, rejection probability 3/4, so L_A = 1/16 × 3/4 = 3/64. B: square 1/4, rejection probability 1/2, so L_B = 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8. C: square 9/16, rejection probability 1/4, so L_C = 9/16 × 1/4 = 9/64.

    We are studying an ordered sequence, rather than all possible orders with the same counts: no counting factor is needed here. Among the values in Θ, the maximum likelihood estimate, MLE, is C, because 9/64 > 1/8 > 3/64. This conclusion is limited to Θ; it does not claim to solve the optimization over all possible probabilities.

  3. Include the prior through Bayes’ rule

    In this finite set, P(θᵢ | D) denotes the probability of hypothesis i after observing D. The vertical bar is read as “given.” Bayes’ rule divides the product πᵢLᵢ by the sum of the products for all hypotheses. Call this sum Z; here it is strictly positive. The function π(θ) assigns each value in Θ its prior mass; π(θᵢ) = πᵢ.

    P(θi|D)=πiLiZ;Z=πALA+πBLB+πCLC

    Let us calculate the numerators: s_A = 1/6 × 3/64 = 1/128 ; s_B = 2/3 × 1/8 = 1/12 ; s_C = 1/6 × 9/64 = 3/128. Multiplying by the prior favors an initially more plausible hypothesis when likelihoods are equal. The product is not an average of the θ values.

  4. Choose the maximum without normalizing

    Dividing all scores by the same positive Z preserves their inequalities. The symbol argmax denotes the set of parameter values that attain the maximum; a hat marks an estimate, and the subscript MAP indicates the rule used.

    θ^MAP∈argmaxθ∈ΘP(θ|D)=argmaxθ∈ΘL(θ;D)π(θ)

    The score of B, 1/12, exceeds that of C, 3/128, and that of A, 1/128. The MAP estimate is therefore θ̂_MAP = 1/2, or 0.50, dimensionless. MLE and MAP differ here because they optimize different objectives.

    On a finite set with positive Z, a maximum exists. Equal scores could yield several MAP values: we would need to state the tie or specify a rule for breaking it, without inventing a preference.

  5. Check the choice and its associated probability

    The sum of the first scores is 35/384; adding the last gives Z = 11/96. Dividing each score by Z gives P(A | D) = 3/44, P(B | D) = 8/11 (approximately 0.7273, dimensionless), and P(C | D) = 9/44. Their sum is 1: the normalization check passes, and B remains maximal.

    Also compare B directly with C: the data alone favor C by the ratio L_C/L_B = 9/8; the prior favors B by the ratio π_B/π_C = 4. The score ratio s_B/s_C = 32/9 exceeds 1, confirming the choice without using Z.

    θ̂_MAP = 1/2 is the recognition probability assigned to the reader by the chosen model. P(B | D) = 8/11 is the probability of hypothesis B in this model after D. These numbers answer different questions. Neither is a reliability value measured on a new trial.

  6. Understand what the maximum summarizes

    The MAP estimate selects a mode, meaning a value where the distribution attains its maximum. It does not calculate the mean of the possible θ values and does not, by itself, retain all the uncertainty. The model still assigns positive mass to A and C. Choosing B does not remove these possibilities.

    The prior and the assumptions about the trials remain open to question in a real application. The choice can change if we modify the prior or the set Θ. An exact result for the stated calculation does not prove that this model correctly represents the reader.

  7. Connect the choice to an optimization objective

    When L and π are strictly positive, the natural logarithm ln is strictly increasing: maximizing Lπ is equivalent to maximizing ln L + ln π. By changing the sign, we can minimize the sum −ln L − ln π. The second term penalizes values to which the prior assigns less mass; this explains the role of the prior in the objective.

    ln(L×π)=lnL+lnπ

    A 0 prior mass gives a 0 score: we do not take its ordinary logarithm. If all scores are 0, Z = 0 and this update is undefined. With a positive uniform prior, its common factor does not change the maximum: we recover the MLE on the same set Θ.

    For a continuous parameter, MAP maximizes a posterior density in a specified parameterization, rather than the probability of an exact value. We must then check that this density attains a maximum. Here, we have stayed with a finite set: masses and densities are not interchangeable.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the scores. Explain how C can win on likelihood and lose on MAP, then reconstruct the product to compare. Finally, distinguish the chosen value from the probability of the chosen hypothesis.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The greatest likelihood must give the MAP estimate.”

    You need to compare the products with the prior. Here C explains D better, but B has enough initial weight to obtain the greatest product. Revisit the step that chooses the maximum and justify why Z cancels out.

    Recalculate the scores, then choose the maximum

  • “The score 1/12 is the probability of B after the trials.”

    This score is only a numerator. To obtain a posterior probability, divide by the sum of all scores: you get 8/11. The MAP value of the parameter remains 1/2. Revisit the check that distinguishes these quantities.

    Distinguish score, probability, and parameter value

  • “By assigning a 0 prior, I let the data decide.”

    A 0 mass excludes the hypothesis: its likelihood is multiplied by 0. A positive uniform prior, by contrast, lets the likelihood ranking decide. The exercises compare these choices while keeping the data the same.

    Compare the uniform prior and exclusion

Exercise 1 · Understanding the weights

How much must the prior compensate?

Keep the initial data and hypotheses. To compare only B and C, calculate L_C/L_B, then π_B/π_C, and deduce s_B/s_C. What inequality on the prior ratio is sufficient for B to beat C?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write s_B/s_C = (π_B/π_C) × (L_B/L_C). The likelihood ratio requested in the question is the reciprocal of L_B/L_C.

Hint 2

B beats C when its score ratio exceeds 1. Isolate the ratio π_B/π_C in this inequality.

Compare with the solution

L_C/L_B = 9/64 ÷ 1/8 = 9/8. The prior gives π_B/π_C = 2/3 ÷ 1/6 = 4. Therefore s_B/s_C = 4 ÷ 9/8 = 32/9 > 1.

The condition is π_B/π_C > L_C/L_B, meaning here a prior ratio strictly greater than 9/8. At equality, B and C would have the same score. You must still check A before declaring a maximum over all of Θ; the example did this.

Check your reasoning

  • You keep the numerator and denominator of each ratio in the correct order.
  • You obtain 32/9 and justify the threshold 9/8.
  • You distinguish a comparison between B and C from the maximum over all hypotheses.

Exercise 2 · Recalculating on paper

Remove the initial preference

In the same scenario, replace the prior masses with the same mass 1/3 for A, B, and C. Recalculate the scores, choose the MAP estimate, and compare it with the MLE on Θ. Do you need to calculate Z to choose?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A strictly positive common factor preserves inequalities.

Hint 2

Multiply 1/3 by each of the likelihoods already calculated, in turn.

Compare with the solution

The scores become s_A = 1/3 × 3/64 = 1/64, s_B = 1/3 × 1/8 = 1/24, and s_C = 1/3 × 9/64 = 3/64. The greatest is the score of C.

The MAP estimate is therefore 3/4, as is the MLE on Θ. The preference for B has disappeared: uniform priors do not alter the likelihood ranking. Calculating Z is still unnecessary for choosing, since it would be the same positive denominator for all hypotheses.

Check your reasoning

  • You present the scores of all hypotheses.
  • You give the parameter value 3/4 and name C.
  • You explain the common factor and do not claim that MAP and MLE coincide for all priors.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

A hypothesis excluded before the trials

Start again with the same readings. We now propose π_B = 0, π_A = π_C = 1/2. Someone says: “If B explains the data well, its probability can become positive again.” Respond by calculating the scores and the MAP estimate; also calculate the posterior probability of C. What must change to allow B to be considered?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A positive likelihood does not change a product whose other factor is 0.

Hint 2

Add the scores of A and C to obtain the new normalizer, then divide the score of C by this total.

Compare with the solution

The scores are s_A = 3/128, s_B = 0, and s_C = 9/128. The MAP choice is C, giving θ̂_MAP = 3/4. The new normalizer is 3/32; P(C | D) = 9/128 ÷ 3/32 = 3/4.

B retains a 0 posterior probability because its score is 0. To make B possible in the model, you must revise the prior and assign it strictly positive mass; you must state this revision, without presenting it as the result of the original multiplication.

The chosen parameter value and the probability of C are numerically equal in this variant. They remain different quantities: a recognition parameter in one case, a hypothesis probability in the other. This coincidence does not provide a general rule.

Check your reasoning

  • You calculate the 0 score of B and justify why the data do not make it positive.
  • You obtain 3/4 with a positive normalizer.
  • You distinguish a revision of the prior from an update under the stated prior.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What product should you compare to calculate the MAP estimate?

For each hypothesis, the likelihood of the data multiplied by the prior mass. The maximum selects a parameter value; a common positive normalizer preserves this choice.

Recall 2Why can you choose the MAP estimate without calculating the normalized probabilities?

They all share the same strictly positive denominator Z. Dividing by it preserves the inequalities between scores. Without normalization, however, the scores are not yet posterior probabilities.

Recall 3When does MAP recover the MLE ranking on the same set?

With a strictly positive uniform prior, its common factor does not change the likelihood ranking. A nonuniform prior can shift the maximum; ties can also produce several maximizers.

Recall 4What do 1/2 and 8/11 distinguish in the initial example?

1/2 is the MAP value of the reader’s recognition probability. 8/11 is the probability of hypothesis B after the sequence. The MAP estimate is a mode; it does not summarize the entire distribution.

Recall 5What happens to a hypothesis with 0 prior mass during this update?

Its mass remains 0 as long as the likelihood is finite and the normalizer is positive: its score contains a 0 factor. Making it possible requires explicitly revising the prior.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the example, reconstruct this sequence: state the hypotheses and prior; calculate the likelihood of the same sequence under each hypothesis; multiply; choose a maximum; explain why the normalizer does not change this choice. Then recall why this mode is neither a mean nor the probability of the selected hypothesis.
