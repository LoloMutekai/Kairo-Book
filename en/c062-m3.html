Level 2 · C062·M3

Brier score and reliability by group

Your goalCalculate a binary Brier score, compare probabilities and frequencies by group, and identify what an overall average can hide.

Before you start
c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c056-m5 : effect size · c009-m5 : reading notes · c062-m1 : measurement, score and probability · c062-m2 : missing value and error

✿01 See the idea

A delivery service reports, before each departure, the probability of arriving after the promised delivery window. For 4 deliveries, it reports 0.25; for 4 others, 0.75. In each group, 2 deliveries end up late. Did the higher forecasts describe a group that was actually at greater risk?

All the data in this scene are fictional. We call the group with a forecast of 0.25 A, and the group with a forecast of 0.75 B. Lateness is defined in the same way for all deliveries. Its outcome is 1 if it occurred and 0 otherwise; no outcome is missing from the initial dataset.

The Brier score measures the difference between each forecast probability and the observed outcome: square this difference, then take the average. A confident forecast contradicted by the outcome incurs a greater loss than a cautious forecast. The smaller this score, the closer the forecasts are to the outcomes according to this calculation.

Reliability by group asks another question: among deliveries given the same forecast probability, what proportion were actually late? We compare this frequency with the forecast. Here, the frequency is 0.50 in both A and B. The score summarizes the individual differences; the diagram of the groups shows where forecasts differ from frequencies.

The rule to remember

Keep the original probabilities, calculate the squared differences for each delivery, then compare the average forecast with the frequency in each group, displaying its size.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Evaluate the forecasts before changing them

Keep the 8 original forecasts and the outcomes from the first diagram. Calculate the binary Brier score and the reliability of each group, then check whether the overall averages tell the whole story.

  1. Specify the event, timing and data

    i denotes a delivery; n = 8 is the total number of deliveries. pᵢ is its probability of being late, forecast before departure, between 0 and 1. yᵢ is the indicator observed after the delivery window closes: 1 for late, 0 for on time. pᵢ and yᵢ have no physical unit; the counts refer to deliveries.

    hlwjix wdhcfr vlfrpb ldawwb dcxdai iycejb klyf ihkncr czzbac tznfvp paeutq ctuuay qkmljh rghl The symbols r_A and r_B count the observed late deliveries.

    Each delivery counts once and has the same weight. The forecasts remain those recorded before the outcome. The descriptive calculation does not assume that deliveries are independent; to generalize to future deliveries, we would need to examine, among other things, their dependence, the stability of the situation, and how representative this dataset is.

  2. Calculate the difference for each delivery, then square it

    bᵢ denotes the loss for a delivery and B the average of these losses, called the Brier score. The symbol Σ means to add the terms for all deliveries. Squaring makes each contribution nonnegative: errors with opposite signs no longer cancel out.

    bi=(pi−yi)2;B=1n∑i=1nbi

    For a late delivery in A: 1/4 − 1 = -3/4, then (-3/4) × (-3/4) = 9/16 = 0.5625. For an on-time delivery: 1/4 − 0 = 1/4, whose square is 1/16 = 0.0625.

    All these losses, and therefore B, are unitless. Under this binary convention, they lie between 0 and 1. B equals 0 if all probabilities match their outcomes. We count only the event “late”, without adding a second loss for its complement.

  3. Add up the losses in each group

    In A, the 2 late deliveries contribute 2 × 9/16 = 9/8. The 2 on-time deliveries contribute 2 × 1/16 = 1/8. Their sum S_A is 5/4; the group Brier score is B_A = 5/4 ÷ 4 = 5/16.

    In B, a late delivery gives 3/4 − 1 = -1/4, so its square is 1/16. An on-time delivery gives a square of 9/16. The contributions are 2 × 1/16 = 1/8 and 2 × 9/16 = 9/8. Hence S_B = 5/4 and B_B = 5/4 ÷ 4 = 5/16.

    S_A and S_B denote sums of losses; B_A and B_B denote averages. Here, the groups have the same average: the small and large losses have simply exchanged roles.

  4. Take the average over all deliveries and check it

    The total sum S = S_A + S_B is 5/4 + 5/4 = 5/2. Divide it by the number of deliveries n = 4 + 4 = 8, since each delivery must have the same weight.

    B=5/4+5/44+4=5/16=0.3125

    Check by arranging the terms differently: there are 4 losses of 1/16 and 4 of 9/16. Their sum divided by 8 again gives 5/16. The result does lie between 0 and 1.

    hbxtod dftljx ptln Taking a simple average of B_A and B_B is a general rule only when the group sizes are equal; different sizes require these weights. In our dataset, because the group averages are equal, changing the weights would not reveal a weighting error.

  5. Read the reliability in each group

    g denotes a group, A or B; n_g is its size. f_g is its observed frequency of late deliveries, meaning the number of outcomes equal to 1 divided by n_g. p̄_g, the p with a bar, denotes the average of the forecast probabilities in this group. The notation i ∈ g means that we add only the deliveries in the group.

    fg=∑i∈gying;p¯g=∑i∈gping

    The probabilities are identical within each of our groups: p̄_A = p_A and p̄_B = p_B. A gives f_A = 2/4 = 0.50, for a forecast of 0.25. The difference f_A − p̄_A is 0.25: the point is above the diagonal. B gives f_B = 2/4 = 0.50, for a forecast of 0.75. The difference is -0.25: the point is below it.

    The diagonal represents f_g = p̄_g. Being above it indicates more late deliveries than the average forecast, and being below it indicates fewer. When forecasts take many values, we can define probability bins before observing the outcomes; we then plot their average forecast, rather than arbitrarily using the midpoint of each bin. An empty bin provides no frequency.

  6. Detect differences that cancel out overall

    The sum of the forecast probabilities is 4 × 1/4 + 4 × 3/4 = 1 + 3 = 4. The average forecast probability is therefore 4/8 = 0.50. The overall observed frequency is 4/8 = 0.50. These averages agree, while the group differences 0.25 and -0.25 cancel each other out.

    Replacing all forecasts with their average before scoring them would change the forecast being evaluated. The constant rule p = 0.50 would have a loss of 1/4 for each delivery, giving a Brier score of 0.2500. This result differs from the original Brier score 0.3125. Squaring comes before averaging; an average forecast does not preserve the original losses.

    The Brier score therefore measures more than a calibration defect: even a constant forecast equal to the observed frequency has a positive score here. Individual outcomes still vary around this forecast. Comparing methods requires the same cases, the same event, and the same scoring convention.

  7. State exactly what the dataset allows us to conclude

    We have established a Brier score of 5/16 for this fictional dataset, and opposing descriptive differences in its groups. With only 4 deliveries per point, a new observation can shift a frequency substantially. An observed difference is insufficient to demonstrate a lasting defect; observed equality does not prove future calibration either.

    Displaying the group sizes is part of the diagnostic process. We must also keep unavailable outcomes marked as such and state the scope actually evaluated. If a dataset is used to adjust probabilities, judging that adjustment on the same dataset would give an overly favorable evaluation; other outcomes must be set aside to evaluate it. We are not fitting any model here.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Why does agreement between the overall averages fail to guarantee reliability within the groups? Then explain why the Brier score is calculated before replacing the forecasts with an average.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The average forecast matches the overall frequency: everything is calibrated.”

    Compare A and B separately. Their differences 0.25 and -0.25 cancel out in the overall average. Display the average forecast, frequency and size for each group; even agreement within these groups does not guarantee agreement for every possible subpopulation.

    Reread the points and the diagonal in the diagram

  • “I average the probabilities first; the Brier score will be identical.”

    The constant rule would have a score of 0.2500, while the original forecasts give 0.3125 on the same outcomes. Recalculate the individual differences, their squares, then their average; changing the order evaluates a different rule.

    Revisit the squares and the overall check

  • “The frequency observed in a group is its true future probability.”

    The quotient describes the available outcomes, with their sample size. Additional deliveries, a different situation or a selection of outcomes can shift it. Keep the frequency as an estimate to be examined; an outcome that is still unknown remains unknown.

Exercise 1 · Application · two possible outcomes

A high forecast contradicted by the outcome

For a delivery in group B with a forecast of 0.75, calculate the loss if it is on time, then if it is late. Which outcome incurs the greater loss for this forecast? Justify your answer without converting the probability into a binary decision.

Hint 1

On time means y = 0; late means y = 1.

Hint 2

Calculate p − y in each case, then multiply this difference by itself.

Compare with the solution

On time: (3/4 − 0) squared is 9/16 = 0.5625. Late: 3/4 − 1 = -1/4, whose square is 1/16 = 0.0625.

An on-time outcome contradicts this high forecast more strongly; it receives the greater loss. The negative sign of the other difference does not make its loss negative, since we square it. A high probability does not promise that every delivery will be late.

Check your reasoning

  • Encode both outcomes without reversing them.
  • Give 9/16 and 1/16, with no physical unit.
  • Connect the greater loss to the outcome least expected by the forecast.

A reference rule without groups

On the same 8 deliveries, compare the original forecasts with a rule that would always have forecast p = 0.50 before departure. Work out the Brier score for each rule and their difference. Can we conclude that the constant rule will be better on new deliveries?

Hint 1

The constant rule has the same loss for y = 0 and for y = 1.

Hint 2

For the original forecasts, keep the probabilities for each group. Then subtract the two scores in the stated order.

Compare with the solution

For the constant rule, each difference has an absolute value of 1/2, so each square is 1/4. The average is also 1/4 = 0.2500. The original forecasts have a sum of losses of 5/2 over 8 deliveries, giving 5/16 = 0.3125.

Original Brier score minus reference Brier score: 5/16 − 1/4 = 1/16 = 0.0625. The positive difference means that the constant rule has the smaller Brier score on these fictional cases. This calculation is neither a success rate nor a gain measured in a real service.

The constant rule matches the overall observed frequency here. This does not demonstrate its future superiority: we have only a small dataset constructed for the exercise. If the rule had been chosen after examining the outcomes, it would in particular need to be evaluated on other deliveries.

Check your reasoning

  • Compare the two rules on exactly the same outcomes.
  • Obtain the difference 1/16 and interpret its sign correctly.
  • Limit the conclusion to the fictional dataset; state the need for a separate evaluation to judge a rule chosen using these outcomes.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer · unavailable outcome

One more delivery, with no outcome yet

An additional delivery had a forecast of 0.75, but its outcome is unavailable. Someone adds it with y = 0 and publishes the Brier score for the 9 deliveries. Explain the problem, then calculate the two possible scores depending on its future outcome. What can be published now?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start again from the sum 5/2 of the known losses, before dividing it by 8.

Hint 2

Add either the loss 1/16 for a late delivery or the loss 9/16 for an on-time delivery, then divide by 9.

Compare with the solution

Encoding y = 0 asserts an observed on-time outcome. A missing outcome does not provide this observation. We therefore cannot calculate a single realized score for the 9 deliveries.

If lateness is confirmed, the sum will become 5/2 + 1/16 = 41/16, then B = 41/16 ÷ 9 = 41/144 ≈ 0.2847. If it is ruled out, the sum will become 5/2 + 9/16 = 49/16, then B = 49/144 ≈ 0.3403.

The final score will be one of these values if no other case changes. These are conditional possibilities, rather than two actual measurements. We can already publish the Brier score 5/16 for the 8 known outcomes, declaring 1 unavailable outcome among 9 deliveries. This describes the observed subset; if missing outcomes mainly concern late deliveries, it will not necessarily represent the whole dataset.

Check your reasoning

  • Reject treating a missing outcome as an on-time delivery.
  • Keep the sum of known losses before adding the new contribution.
  • Obtain 41/144 and 49/144 using the new sample size.
  • Distinguish the observed score on 8 cases from the possibilities on 9 cases, with the limitation caused by which outcomes are observed.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you reconstruct the binary Brier score, and what do its symbols mean?

For each delivery i, keep its probability pᵢ forecast before the outcome yᵢ, encoded as 0 or 1. Calculate (pᵢ − yᵢ) squared, add these losses, and divide by the number n of observed deliveries. B is unitless, and a smaller score is preferable on the same cases.

Recall 2What information is needed to place a group on the reliability diagram?

Its size, the average of its forecast probabilities, and the frequency of outcomes equal to 1. The horizontal coordinate of the point is the average forecast and its vertical coordinate is the frequency. The diagonal represents equality between them; an empty bin provides no frequency.

Recall 3Why can the overall averages agree while the groups lie away from the diagonal?

An underestimate in one group and an overestimate in another can cancel out. In the dataset, the differences 0.25 and -0.25 cancel out with equal group sizes. We must examine the groups and their sizes, as well as the total.

Recall 4Does equality between forecast and frequency in each group prove future calibration?

No. It describes a particular sample and grouping. Group sizes, the selection of outcomes, their dependence, and changes in the situation limit generalization. A method fitted to these outcomes requires a separate evaluation.

Recall 5What happens to the Brier score when an outcome remains unavailable?

The corresponding loss remains unknown. We can calculate the score for the observed cases while declaring the sample size and missing outcomes, or present explicitly conditional possibilities. Entering 0 would invent a negative observation.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking, reconstruct the delivery protocol: event and time of the forecast; observed binary outcome; difference then square; sum and correct denominator; average forecast, frequency and size for each group. Then explain how differences cancel out overall and what a missing outcome prevents us from concluding.
