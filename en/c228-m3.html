Level 4 · C228·M3

Jump decomposition

Your goalReconstruct a jump process by separating drift, large jumps and compensated small jumps, then check that changing the threshold leaves the path unchanged.

Before you start
c227-m5 : Ito-Tanaka · c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c173-m5 : completeness · c228-m1 : characteristic function · c228-m2 : Levy-Khintchine

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a fictional market quote whose cumulative change we track in points. Between events, it rises steadily. Small announcements make it rise in steps of 0.25 points; major announcements make it fall in steps of -2 points. We want to account for these movements without calculating a contract price.

To read the path, we can separate the steps using a size threshold. “Large” means large in absolute value, so our negative jump is large. The threshold classifies events; it does not remove their effect.

The small steps can contribute an average increase. To isolate their fluctuation around that mean, we subtract the expected contribution. This is compensation. The mean removed must then be added to the deterministic part; otherwise, we would no longer be describing the same quote.

All parameters, counts and paths on this page are fictional. The chosen model has a finite number of jumps on every bounded interval. It lets us calculate exactly what the general Levy-Ito decomposition also does when there are infinitely many small jumps.

The rule to remember

Set the threshold, compensate the small jumps and adjust the drift together. A centered fluctuation can be negative even when all its steps are positive.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Accounting for a quote over 2 hours

The raw continuous slope is d = 1 point/h. Small jumps have size a = 0.25 points and intensity λₚ = 4 h⁻¹; large jumps have size b = -2 points and intensity λ_g = 1 h⁻¹. Over T = 2 h, the observed counts are Nₚ(T) = 6 and N_g(T) = 1. Set c = 1 point. No Brownian term is added in this example.

  1. From the Levy triplet to a path

    Xt denotes the cumulative change in points, with X initially equal to 0. Time t is in hours. A Levy process has independent increments whose distribution depends only on the duration; it is continuous in probability. We use its version with paths that are right continuous and have left limits. A jump at time s is ΔXs = Xs − Xs-, where Xs- is the limit just before s.

    The Levy-Ito decomposition gives a path interpretation of the triplet from the previous unit. For the positive threshold c, γ_c is the drift under this convention, in points/h. Wt is a standard Brownian motion with mean 0 and variance t; its unit is the square root of an hour. The coefficient σ is nonnegative, is measured in points per square root of an hour, and measures its continuous contribution. Jᶜ collects jumps with absolute size greater than c; Mᶜ collects compensated small jumps. The time subscript indicates the time; the superscript c on J and M indicates the threshold. All terms below are in points.

    Xt=γct+σWt+Jtc+Mtc

    The Brownian motion and the jump measure are independent. Our quote model will use σ = 0. This separation describes the sources of movement; it does not yet establish anything about financial replication.

  2. Define what is counted and what is subtracted

    For a set A of sizes bounded away from 0, N((0, t] × A) counts the times s up to t at which ΔXs belongs to A. N is the Poisson random measure of jumps. The Levy measure ν gives their intensity: ν(A) is the mean number of events per hour, and tν(A) is the expected count. ν does not give the probability of an individual size; its total mass can be infinite.

    The size x is in points; s is a time in hours, and ds is an element of duration. The lower cutoff ε is in points. The notation N(ds, dx) sums events at time s with size x. The compensator ds ν(dx) gives their infinitesimal mean; the compensated measure is Ñ(ds, dx) = N(ds, dx) − ds ν(dx). Here, the symbol ∫ denotes a sum weighted by the sizes, or its limit. For large jumps:

    Jtc=∫(0,t]×{|x|>c}xN(ds,dx)

    There are almost surely finitely many large jumps over any bounded time interval. For small jumps, first introduce a positive lower cutoff ε:

    Mtε,c=∫(0,t]×{ε<|x|≤c}x[N(ds,dx)−dsν(dx)]

    For fixed ε, this is exactly the observed sum of the selected sizes minus t times their mean contribution per hour. The words “small” and “large” refer to this threshold convention, rather than two universal distributions.

  3. Understand why compensation makes the limit possible

    The Levy measure has no mass at 0 and satisfies the following integrability condition over all real sizes. This notation normalizes the sizes by c to compare dimensionless quantities. It implies that the integral of squared sizes over the small sizes is finite, but the integral of |x| need not be.

    ∫min(1,(xc)2)ν(dx)<∞

    For 0 < η < ε ≤ c, η is a second lower cutoff, in points. The isometry for compensated Poisson sums gives the mean square of their difference. E denotes expectation, the mean under the model:

    E[(Mtη,c−Mtε,c)2]=t∫η<|x|≤εx2ν(dx)

    On a bounded band of sizes bounded away from 0, the number of jumps has a Poisson distribution. The variance of their sum is the mean number of jumps multiplied by the mean squared size. Subtracting the compensator centers the sum without changing this variance, giving the preceding identity.

    The right side tends to 0 as the cutoffs decrease to 0. The compensated sums therefore have a mean square limit, called Mᶜ. This term is a centered martingale: its future increment has conditional mean 0 given the past. It can nevertheless be nonzero on a path.

    The existence of this limit does not justify a raw sum of all small jumps. That sum is absolutely summable when the integral of |x| over these sizes is finite; this stronger condition is not automatic. The Brownian component σW remains a separate term in the decomposition. Our finite model needs no delicate limiting argument.

  4. Specify the finite model completely

    Np(t) and N_g(t), where p labels small jumps and g labels large jumps, are independent homogeneous Poisson processes with respective intensities λp and λ_g. The counts are dimensionless; λ denotes the intensity of the count considered, in h⁻¹. Each count over a duration t has expectation and variance λt. The intensities are constant; the sizes a and b are deterministic. Our raw model is therefore:

    Xt=dt+aNp(t)+bNg(t)

    The Levy measure is ν = λp δa + λ_g δᵦ. δa assigns unit mass to size a and no mass to any other size; δᵦ does the same for b. Its total mass is 5 h⁻¹, so the activity is finite. The expected counts over T are 8 small jumps and 2 large jumps, giving 10 events on average; these do not determine the observed counts.

    Since 0 < |a| ≤ c < |b|, only family a is compensated. Adding and subtracting aλpt in the raw formula requires γ_c = d + aλp. The mean rate of the small steps is 0.25 × 4 = 1 point/h. The drift in the compensated form is therefore 1 + 1 = 2 points/h. It differs from the raw slope d between events.

  5. Calculate the fluctuation of the small steps

    The expected count is λpT = 4 × 2 = 8. The path has only 6 such events. The raw sum is aNp(T) = 0.25 × 6 = 1.50 points. Its theoretical mean is aλpT = 1 × 2 = 2 points.

    MTc=a(Np(T)−λpT)=1.50−2=-0.50 point

    The negative result means there were fewer increases than expected. It does not turn any positive small step into a negative jump: compensation adds a downward slope between events without changing their sizes.

  6. Assemble the terms and check against the raw sum

    The large jump is not compensated: Jᶜ at T is bN_g(T) = (-2) × 1 = -2 points. The deterministic part is γ_cT = 2 × 2 = 4 points. The Brownian term is 0 points in this model.

    XT=4+(-0.50)+(-2)=1.50 point

    Independent check: in the raw model, dT = 2 points; add 1.50 points, then -2 points. This gives 2 + 1.50 + (-2) = 1.50 points. The results agree because the added drift and the subtracted mean cancel, rather than because the observed counts equal their means.

  7. Distinguish path, mean and variance

    In this example with bounded sizes, all the moments used exist. E[Mᶜ at T] = 0, but E[Jᶜ at T] = bλ_gT = -4 points. The total mean rate is γ_c + bλ_g = 2 + (-2) = 0 points/h. Thus E[X at T] = 0 points; the observed path nevertheless ends at 1.50 points.

    Var denotes variance, in squared points. Compensation does not change the variance of a sum. Independence of the counts allows their variances to be added:

    Var(XT)=a2λpT+b2λgT

    The small jump term gives 1/16 × 8 = 0.50 points²; the large jump term gives 4 × 2 = 8 points². Total: 8.50 points². A negative jump contributes positively to the variance. For a general Levy process, having finitely many large jumps does not guarantee their integrability: a finite total mean or variance cannot always be written down.

  8. Prepare to change the threshold

    If a threshold c′ greater than c brings new sizes into the compensated term, their sum loses its mean. The same mean per hour must be added to γ_c. For fixed positive thresholds, the band between them is bounded away from 0 and bounded above, so the following integral is finite:

    γc′=γc+∫c<|x|≤c′xν(dx)

    The prime marks the new convention, rather than a derivative. The integral is signed: compensating an additional negative family reduces the drift. In the final exercise, c′ = 3 points will include our family b. The decomposition will change while every value of X remains identical.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations. Explain why γ_c differs from d, why the compensated small jump term is negative, and where its removed mean reappears. Then reconstruct the two ways of accounting for the same path, with their units.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Compensation makes the small steps disappear.”

    The jump sizes stay the same. A continuous mean trend is subtracted. Even without any small event, the compensated term can be negative: it measures the difference from what was expected.

    Test a period with no small announcement

  • “The drift γ_c must be the mean of X per hour.”

    Large jumps remain uncompensated under this convention and can have a nonzero mean. In our example, their mean is added to γ_c to obtain the total mean rate. The existence of this mean must also be checked.

    Review mean, path and variance

  • “I move the threshold while keeping the same drift.”

    A newly compensated family loses its mean in the jump term. The drift must gain that same signed mean. Compare the complete sums before and after; the small and large components alone are not invariant.

    Recalculate the contributions with the larger threshold

Exercise 1 · Direct application

No small announcement

Keep T = 2 h, the intensities and c = 1 point, but suppose Nₚ(T) = 0. Calculate only Mᶜ at T. Why is it nonzero even though no small jump occurred?

Hint 1

The expected count remains λₚT; it does not become 0 when the observed count is 0.

Hint 2

Subtract the mean contribution of 2 points from the raw sum of 0.

Compare with the solution

The observed sum is 0 points. The model mean remains aλpT = 2 points. The compensated term is therefore 0 − 2 = -2 points.

Between small announcements, the compensation decreases at the rate aλp = 1 point/h. This continuous movement reflects the shortfall in announcements relative to the mean. A centered martingale does not have an identically 0 path.

  • Distinguish a model mean from an observed count.

A mean counted twice

Hint 1

γ_c already includes the mean of the small steps.

Hint 2

You can keep γ_c and compensate the small jumps, or keep the small jumps uncompensated and return to d.

Compare with the solution

Their sum gives 4 + 1.50 + (-2) = 3.50 points. The correct result is 1.50 points. The excess is 3.50 − 1.50 = 2 points, exactly aλpT.

First fix: subtract 2 points from the small jump term while keeping γ_cT. Second fix: use dT = 2 points with both raw sums. Either way, the result is 1.50 points. Changing just one term without stating the convention causes the double counting.

  • Give both corrected accounts of the contributions and their units.
  • Explain why the same path cannot have two totals.

Exercise 3 · Change of convention

The large jump becomes small at the new threshold

On the original path, replace c = 1 point with c′ = 3 points. Calculate γ at the new threshold, the compensated contribution of family b, and the total X at T. What happens to the uncompensated sum of large jumps? Justify the sign of the drift change before calculating.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The new threshold includes b, whose absolute size is 2 points. The mean of this family is negative.

Hint 2

Calculate b(N_g(T) − λ_gT) and add bλ_g to the old drift. Do not alter any event.

Compare with the solution

Family b enters the compensation. Its mean rate is bλ_g = -2 points/h, so γ at the new threshold is 2 + (-2) = 0 points/h. It decreases because the newly transferred mean is negative.

The expected large jump count was 2. Its raw sum is -2 points, and its mean is -4 points. The new compensated contribution is -2 − (-4) = 2 points: there were fewer decreases than expected.

This model has no sizes greater than c′, so J at the new threshold is 0 points. The combined small jump terms give -0.50 + 2 = 1.50 points; the cumulative drift is 0 points. Total: 1.50 points, identical to 1.50 points. The equality holds for every path by adding and subtracting the same mean, rather than only for our counts.

Check your reasoning

  • Predict a decrease in the drift and obtain 0 points/h.
  • Compensate family b as well, without changing its jumps.
  • Justify the disappearance of the large jump term using the sizes in the model.
  • Recover the total and explain its algebraic invariance.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the difference between N((0, t] × A) and tν(A)?

The first is the realized count of jumps whose size belongs to A; the second is its expectation when A is bounded away from 0. ν(A) is an intensity, in events per hour, rather than a size probability.

Recall 2What does compensation subtract for a family with size a and intensity λ?

It replaces aN(t) with a(N(t) − λt). The compensator aλt is a mean contribution in points. The centered term keeps the same jumps and can be negative on a path.

Recall 3Why compensate before letting the lower cutoff tend to 0?

The integral of squared sizes near 0 is finite for a Levy measure. It controls the mean square of the differences between compensated sums, and hence their mean square convergence. Absolute summability of the raw sum requires integrability of the absolute sizes, which is not guaranteed.

Recall 4What must change when the threshold brings new jumps into the compensation?

Add their signed mean per hour to the drift: the integral of xν(dx) over the newly included band. The components change together and their sum remains the same process.

Recall 5Is a drift γ_c enough to give E[Xₜ]/t?

The mean per hour of the uncompensated large jumps must also be added, if it exists. The compensated small jump term and the Brownian motion are centered. A total mean is neither the observed slope between events nor the value of a path.
