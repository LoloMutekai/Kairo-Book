Level 3 · C222·M5

Replication

Your goalDistinguish recalculation from replication, compare effects directly with their uncertainty, then state a conclusion whose scope remains justified.

Before you start
c074-m5 : identification assumptions · c145-m5 : policy evaluation · c073-m5 : interaction and repeated measures · c081-m5 : experimental ethics · c222-m1 : protocol · c222-m2 : repeated measures · c222-m3 : interaction · c222-m4 : attrition

✿01 See the idea

A team asks whether a silent break of 5 min before a visual attention task shortens reaction time. Its study A finds a mean reduction of 20 ms. Another team repeats the protocol with new people: its study B finds 7.5 ms. Must the first number be reproduced exactly for this to count as a replication?

Replication reexamines the same claim with new observations. A direct replication aims to preserve the elements of the protocol that define this claim: target population, break, comparison, measurement and analysis. Unavoidable deviations are documented. A conceptual replication changes a way of testing the mechanism; its scope depends on what has changed.

Rerunning the calculation on the file from A checks its computational reproducibility: can its result be reproduced? This does not provide a new study. Even new trials with the same people do not automatically constitute an independent sample: learning and individual characteristics can link the results.

The entire scenario, sample sizes, results and uncertainty model are fictional. We are learning to compare evidence, without recommending a health practice or reporting an observed benefit. Replication is not a binary stamp of approval: we need to examine what was retested, the size of the effects and the precision of their comparison.

The rule to remember

New observations and the same claim; direct comparison of effects and their uncertainty; a conclusion limited to the protocol actually repeated.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Another team retests the break

In each study, 16 adults each take part in both conditions, a silent break of 5 min and neutral reading of the same duration, in separate sessions. A and B recruit different people from the same target population. The sum of individual reductions is 320 ms for A and 120 ms for B. The standard deviation of individual differences is assumed known and equal to σ = 20 ms in each of the populations studied: this is a fictional assumption, not an estimate derived from these sums.

  1. Define the claim before seeing B

    For a person i, dᵢ is their mean reaction time during reading minus their mean time after the break, in ms, for correct responses according to a fixed rule. A positive dᵢ means a faster response after the break. The target effect δ is the mean of these differences in the population; δ̂, with a hat, is its estimate in the sample. The letters A and B identify the studies and have no unit.

    The order of conditions is randomized and balanced; the sessions are far enough apart to assume no carryover effect. Equipment, instructions, handling of extreme times, primary outcome, error checks and exclusions follow a plan written before the results. Otherwise, responding faster while making more errors could change the meaning of the claim. Each person’s observations are summarized by 1 difference: the many trials do not become that many participants.

    People are assumed independent within each study, the studies independent of each other, and individual differences normally distributed, meaning that they follow a bell-shaped distribution. Their means are then normally distributed with known variance σ²/n. Variance is a squared measure of dispersion; the standard error is the standard deviation of the estimate. These assumptions simplify the comparison; they are not established by the table of sums.

  2. Calculate the effects in the same direction

    For j equal to A or B, nⱼ is the sample size, Sⱼ the sum of the dᵢ in ms and δ̂ⱼ their mean in ms. Dividing by the number of people makes the studies comparable on the same quantity. wzcomc vbgerq noox

    A: 320 ÷ 16 = 20 ms. B: 120 ÷ 16 = 15/2 ms, or 7.5 ms. The estimates point in the same direction, but B is smaller. Reverse check: 16 × 20 = 320 and 16 × 7.5 = 120 ms.

  3. Give each estimate an uncertainty

    vⱼ = σ²/nⱼ is the variance of δ̂ⱼ, in ms². The standard error sⱼ = σ/√nⱼ is in ms. Since 4 × 4 = 16, we obtain s_A = s_B = 20 ÷ 4 = 5 ms. Check: their square is 25 ms², as is 400 ÷ 16.

    vj=σ2nj;sj=σnj

    The fictional plan uses c = 2, with no unit, as a two-sided threshold in standard errors. For each study, the band δ̂ⱼ ± c sⱼ has a half-width of 10 ms: A gives [10 ; 30] ms and B [-2.5 ; 17.5] ms. At the chosen threshold, A is distinguishable from the null effect 0 ms and B is not. This last sentence does not mean that B demonstrates an absence of effect.

    These bands are confidence intervals under our normal model with known σ. Their coverage comes from this model; the threshold is neither a probability that the break works nor a universal rule. If σ were estimated, or if people shared an unmodeled dependence, the uncertainty and the calibration of the threshold would need to be recalculated.

  4. Compare the studies directly

    D = δ̂_B − δ̂_A is the estimated difference between the effects, in ms. It equals 7.5 − 20 = -12.5 ms. Assuming the studies are independent, its variance V adds the variances of the estimates: 25 + 25 = 50 ms². Here we are comparing studies, not conditions within a person.

    D=δ^B−δ^A;V=vB+vA

    Why add after a subtraction? The sign changes the direction of the error from A, not its variance. If the studies were linked, we would also need to subtract 2 times their covariance, a quantity describing how their errors vary together. Reusing people or data would therefore invalidate this addition without further justification.

  5. Assess the difference without confusing the tests

    Under the assumption of equal population effects, D is centered on 0 and its variance is V. The ratio D/√V measures the difference in standard errors. To avoid calculating a numerical square root, we use its square Q = D²/V, with no unit. The two-sided rule |D| > c√V is exactly equivalent to Q > c² since V is positive. Q is not a p-value.

    Q=D2V=625/450=25/8=3.125<c2=4

    The squared difference is 156.25 ms². Check using another expression: the bound c²V is 4 × 50 = 200 ms²; 156.25 remains below it. The effects are not distinguishable at the specified comparison threshold. Comparing only “A significant” and “B not significant” would have answered a different question.

  6. Give a verdict that stands up to objections

    Conclusion: B provides new observations following the stated protocol. Its estimate keeps the direction of A but is smaller; it is not distinguishable from the null effect at the chosen threshold. The direct comparison does not show a difference between the effects at that same threshold. This result demonstrates neither their equality nor a replication that definitively confirms the claim.

    Also suppose a practical equivalence margin ε = 5 ms, chosen before B: the effects would be judged close enough if their population difference remained between −ε and +ε. The observed center D = -12.5 ms is already outside this margin: D² = 156.25 exceeds ε² = 25 ms². We therefore cannot show a confidence interval centered on D entirely within the margin. This does not prove that the true difference exceeds ε either. An absence of a detected difference does not establish equivalence.

    The strength of the replication also depends on recruitment, attrition, measurement reliability and the availability of all planned results. A shared bias can recur. A divergence calls for checking methods and testing explanations identified as new; it does not justify deleting B or rewriting its primary outcome after the fact. Here we are neither synthesizing all studies nor providing a general demonstration of the mechanism.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking, separate the questions “Does recalculation reproduce A?”, “Is B distinguishable from the null effect?” and “Do the effects of A and B differ?”. Then explain why our result does not prove their equivalence.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The same program gives the same number: the study has been replicated.”

    Ask where the observations come from. The same file and a fixed seed can make a calculation reproducible. Replication of the empirical claim requires new relevant observations; changing the file name does not create them.

    Review the recalculation and new study branches

  • “A is significant and B is not: their effects must be different.”

    Each individual test compares its effect with the null reference. To compare A with B, calculate D and its variance V; under the assumptions of the example, Q = 3.125 remains below 4. The labels from individual tests do not replace this comparison.

    Revisit the difference between studies and its uncertainty

  • “No detected difference means the effects are identical.”

    An imprecise comparison can miss a large difference. To support practical equivalence, state a margin before the results and check that an appropriate measure of uncertainty about the difference stays within that margin. The verdict must retain the limitations of the model and protocol.

Exercise 1 · Evidence diagnosis

What was repeated?

In the break scenario, one team recalculates the file from A; another recruits new people and keeps the protocol; a final team replaces the visual task with an auditory task to examine the same proposed mechanism. Classify the approaches and state what must be documented before interpreting their results.

Hint 1

Start by distinguishing new observations from new operations on the same observations.

Hint 2

Then ask whether the operational measurement and protocol are preserved.

Compare with the solution

Recalculation checks the computational reproducibility of A; it adds no observations. Independent recruitment with the protocol preserved aims at a direct replication. The auditory task aims at a conceptual replication: it changes the test of the mechanism and requires an explanation of why it does test the general claim.

Document the population, any people shared between studies, measurements, exclusions, analysis plan and protocol deviations. For the auditory task, state the prediction and its link with the mechanism before looking at the results. A change of task is not by itself evidence of generalization.

  • Justify the classification by the origin of the observations and by what is preserved or modified.
  • Name the information needed for interpretation, without automatically declaring success.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and interpretation

The same estimated effect with better precision

In a fictional variant of B, independent recruitment planned from the start yields 64 people and a sum of 480 ms. A stays unchanged; σ remains known and equal to 20 ms. Calculate the effect of this variant, its variance, the variance of the difference from A, then Q. Why can the verdict change while the estimated effects stay the same?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Recalculate the mean, then σ²/n. The sample size of A does not change.

Hint 2

Use the D² already calculated, but replace the denominator V with the new sum of variances.

Compare with the solution

The mean is 480 ÷ 64 = 7.5 ms, as in B. Its variance is 400 ÷ 64 = 25/4 ms², or 6.25 ms². The comparison keeps the variance of A: V = 25 + 6.25 = 31.25 ms².

D remains -12.5 ms and D² remains 156.25 ms². Q is 156.25 ÷ 31.25 = 5, greater than 4. Check: c²V = 125 ms², less than D². This variant therefore shows a difference at the specified threshold under the model; it does not show that the break has no effect.

The same difference becomes more discernible when uncertainty decreases. This is a planned sample size, not an addition of people decided by monitoring the threshold. Stopping or extending the study based on results would require an appropriate statistical plan.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 6.25 then 31.25 ms² while retaining the contribution from A.
  • Compare Q = 5 with 4 and justify the difference from the example.
  • Separate the difference between studies, the effect of the break and practical equivalence.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and reasoned decision

A replication that contradicts the initial estimate

Another fictional variant, never combined with the previous ones: B involves 16 new people, but the sum of the differences is -160 ms. The protocol and model are still stated to be identical. Calculate the effect and Q for its comparison with A. Then write a cautious verdict and a methodological review to request before a new study.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Keep the direction reading minus break: a negative mean here means slower responses after the break.

Hint 2

The variance V remains 50 ms². Statistical disagreement alone does not distinguish between an initial error, bias, chance or variation in context.

Compare with the solution

The effect is -160 ÷ 16 = -10 ms. D = -10 − 20 = -30 ms; D² = 900 ms². Q = 900 ÷ 50 = 18, greater than 4. This variant diverges from A according to the planned rule and its estimate points in the opposite direction.

Possible verdict: “This new study provides a result that disagrees with A under the stated model; it does not allow the initial effect to be generalized without examining this divergence.” It demonstrates neither that A was fraudulent nor that a break can never help.

Examine measurements and their calibration, response errors, session order, exclusions, dropouts, the independence of people and adherence to the plan. Keep discordant results in the overall assessment. If an explanation linked to context emerges after reading the results, present it as a hypothesis to test on further observations, with a written plan and a study authorized according to its ethical requirements.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the negative effect, D and Q = 18 with their respective units.
  • State a divergence conditional on the model without attributing a definite cause.
  • Propose a documented review and a testable next step, without selecting only favorable studies.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does a replication provide that recalculating the file from A does not?

New observations that reexamine the claim. Recalculation checks the computational reproducibility of the existing result; it does not by itself retest the claim on new observations.

Recall 2What must be specified to distinguish direct from conceptual replication?

What is preserved in the population, manipulation, measurement and analysis. Direct replication aims at the same relevant protocol; conceptual replication changes a way of testing the mechanism and must justify the scope of that change.

Recall 3Why are the labels significant and not significant insufficient to compare A and B?

They concern separate comparisons with the null effect. Comparing the effects requires their difference D and its uncertainty; with independent studies, the variance of this difference adds the variances of the estimates.

Recall 4What assumption permits V = v_B + v_A? What happens to it when people are shared between studies?

Independence of the estimation errors, which is sufficient to make their covariance vanish. Shared people can link them; this covariance must then be justified and taken into account before using the simplified formula.

Recall 5Why does failing to detect a difference not establish practical equivalence?

The comparison may lack precision. Equivalence requires a relevant margin chosen in advance and a procedure showing that uncertainty about the difference stays within this margin; reading or an assisted answer does not prove mastery of this reasoning.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without support: state the claim being retested and the origin of the new observations; distinguish what the protocol preserves or changes. Then recover the effects, their difference and its uncertainty, and explain why an undetected difference and equivalence are not the same. Finish with a verdict that retains the assumptions, discordant results and limits on generalization.
