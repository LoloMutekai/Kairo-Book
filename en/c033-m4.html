Level 1 · C033·M4

Second derivative

Your goalDifferentiate a slope, give its unit, and explain how a temperature can fall while its slope increases.

Before you start
c031-m5 : asymptotes · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c033-m1 : derivative · c033-m2 : function composition · c033-m3 : exp and log

✿01 See the idea

You place a hot cup in a room at a constant temperature. It cools quickly at first, then more slowly. The curve keeps falling, but its tangents become less steep: the slope is negative and approaches 0.

The first derivative describes the change in temperature. The second derivative describes the change in this slope. A slope that goes from -6 to -3 °C/min increases, even though it remains negative. The cup continues to cool; the cooling slows down.

All the parameters in this scene are fictional. We study a smooth model with no added heat, no change in room temperature, and a constant cooling rate coefficient. A real cup does not necessarily meet these assumptions. No experimental readings are presented.

The diagrams separate the ideas before the calculation. In the first, the curve is qualitative and the axes have no scale markings. In the second, each row represents a different quantity: their numbers cannot be compared directly because they have different units.

The rule to remember

Read the temperature, then its slope, then the change in that slope. A positive second derivative means that the slope increases; it does not by itself tell you whether the temperature increases or decreases.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Why does the cup cool more slowly?

The model is T(t) = b + A exp(−kt). We take b = 20 °C, A = 60 °C, and k = 0.1 per minute. Let us calculate T, T′, and T″ at t = 0 min, then check what the results mean.

  1. Define the symbols and assumptions

    t is the elapsed time in minutes, positive or 0. T(t) is the temperature of the cup in degrees Celsius. b is the constant room temperature; A is the initial excess T(0) − b. The rate coefficient k is positive and constant, in inverse minutes: k = 1 / (10 min) = 0.1 per minute. The product kt is therefore dimensionless, as the argument of the exponential function exp must be.

    The prime mark in T′ indicates differentiation with respect to t. T′ is expressed in °C/min. T″, read “T double prime”, denotes the derivative of T′ and is expressed in °C/min/min: degrees per minute, per additional minute. It is neither the square of T′ nor a second of clock time; “second” refers to the order of differentiation.

    The exponential formula has successive derivatives. We work with strictly positive t and extend these derivatives to the initial instant; the same result follows from the right-hand derivative at the start. Our model includes no stirring, no change in room temperature, and no variation in the rate coefficient k.

  2. Differentiate for the first time

    The constant b disappears on differentiation. To differentiate exp(−kt), we use the chain rule for composition: setting u(t) = −kt gives the derivative u′(t) = −k. We therefore multiply the exponential by −k. The coefficient A remains constant.

    T′(t)=−kAexp(−kt)

    The excess A exp(−kt) is positive. Since −k is negative, T′ is negative: the model does describe cooling. The unit comes from multiplying a rate coefficient in inverse minutes by a difference in degrees.

  3. Differentiate the slope itself

    In T′, the coefficient −kA is constant. The same chain rule supplies another factor of −k. The product of the negative signs is positive; the rate coefficients also multiply. This second factor of k must be kept.

    T″(t)=(−kA)(−k)exp(−kt)=k2Aexp(−kt)

    T″ is therefore positive: the slope increases. On an interval where this second derivative is positive, we also say that the curve is convex, or concave up. Here this means that the falling curve flattens out, since T′ remains negative. The sign of T″ cannot replace the sign of T′ when deciding whether the temperature rises.

  4. Substitute the data at the initial instant

    At t = 0 min, the exponent is −0.1 × 0 = 0, with no unit, and exp(0) = 1. The exponential factor therefore requires no approximation.

    Temperature: T(0) = 20 + 60 × 1 = 80 °C. Slope: T′(0) = -0.1 × 60 × 1 = -6 °C/min. The second derivative requires the square of the rate coefficient: 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01 per minute squared.

    T″(0)=1102×60×1=3/5=0.6 °C/min/min

    The fraction 3/5 is exact, and its decimal form 0.6 is also exact here. This unit describes the instantaneous change in a slope expressed in °C/min. It does not mean that the temperature increases by 0.6 °C.

  5. Check the calculation using another relation

    Since T(t) − b = A exp(−kt), we recover T′(t) = −k[T(t) − b]. Differentiating this relation with b and k constant gives T″(t) = −kT′(t). We can therefore check the second derivative from the slope, without recalculating the square of the rate coefficient.

    At the start: -0.1 × (-6) = 0.6 °C/min/min. This is the same result. Unit check: inverse minutes × °C/min = °C/min/min. Sign check: temperature above b, negative slope, positive second derivative.

    Boundary check: if the cup were exactly at temperature b, the excess would be 0 °C. In the same model, T′ and T″ would then both be 0. A positive temperature therefore does not determine the sign of its change: the difference from b is what matters.

  6. State precisely what the result tells us

    The temperature falls while its slope increases. Here “increases” refers to a negative quantity approaching 0. T″ measures this change in slope, rather than an amount of heat or a cooling percentage.

    A derivative gives instantaneous information. The result does not let us predict the temperature a minute later simply by adding T″ to T. Nor does it mean that the slope will increase by exactly 0.6 °C/min over the whole of the next minute: T″ itself varies in this model. Local approximations belong to the next unit.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the formulas, explain why the slope can increase while the temperature falls. Then recover the factors of −k from the successive differentiations, together with their units.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “T″ is positive, so the cup is warming up.”

    Whether T increases or decreases depends on T′. Here T′ is negative: the cup cools. A positive T″ indicates that this negative slope becomes less negative. Read the three quantities at the same instant again in the second diagram.

    Distinguish temperature, slope, and change in slope

  • “To differentiate again, I simply copy the factor of −k.”

    The factor of −k already present remains; differentiating the exponential adds another. Their product gives k × k, with a positive sign. Apply the chain rule again, keeping the constant coefficient separate.

    Revisit the second differentiation and its check

  • “T″ = 0.6 means 0.6 °C more after a minute.”

    The unit is °C/min/min: this is a change in slope, and it is instantaneous. It cannot be added directly to a temperature. The exercise on changing the time unit makes each division by time visible.

    Check the units with a clock in seconds

Exercise 1 · Interpretation

What does each number represent?

At the start, the model gives 80, -6, and 0.6. Match each number to its symbol and unit. Is the cup cooling? Is its slope increasing? Justify your answer without simply saying “it speeds up”.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First ask yourself which quantity describes a state and which describes its change.

Hint 2

Whether the temperature increases or decreases is given by T′; whether the slope increases or decreases is given by T″.

Compare with the solution

T(0) = 80 °C is the temperature. T′(0) = -6 °C/min is its slope, which is negative: the cup cools. T″(0) = 0.6 °C/min/min is the change in this slope, which is positive: the slope increases.

Since the slope is negative while increasing, its value approaches 0: the cooling slows down locally. A positive temperature and a negative change coexist without contradiction.

Check your reasoning

  • Match each number to T, T′, or T″ and write its unit.
  • Infer cooling from T′ and an increasing slope from T″.
  • Name what is slowing down, without predicting a rise in temperature.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and checking

The same cup, later

The model reaches T = 50 °C, with b and k unchanged. Without finding the corresponding time, calculate the slope and the second derivative. Compare the slope with its initial value and explain why the difference alone does not represent a second derivative.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate T − b. The relation T′ = −k(T − b) avoids having to find the time.

Hint 2

Check T″ using −kT′. Then ask whether the difference between the slopes has a time interval in its denominator.

Compare with the solution

The excess temperature is 50 − 20 = 30 °C. The slope is therefore -0.1 × 30 = -3 °C/min. The second derivative is 0.01 × 30 = 0.3 °C/min/min. Checking with -0.1 × (-3) gives the same result.

The slope goes from -6 to -3 °C/min: it increases by 3 °C/min while remaining negative. The cup therefore continues to cool, more slowly than at the start. T″ remains positive but is smaller than at the start.

The difference 3 °C/min is not a second derivative: it is not divided by any time interval. Even if divided by the time between these instants, it would give an average rate of change of the slope, rather than automatically giving its instantaneous value at either endpoint.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the difference from room temperature rather than T alone.
  • Obtain -3 °C/min and 0.3 °C/min/min with a justified method.
  • Explain the increase in slope and distinguish a difference, an average rate, and an instantaneous derivative.

Exercise 3 · Transfer through composition

Change the clock without changing the cup

We describe the same cooling with time r in seconds. Set c = 60 s/min and U(r) = T(r/c), where U is the temperature as a function of this new clock. At the start, someone claims U″ = 0.01 °C/s/s after dividing T″ by c. Check this method, calculate U′ and U″ exactly, and give the units.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Composition with r/c introduces a factor of 1/c at each differentiation.

Hint 2

The second differentiation therefore introduces a second factor of 1/c. Keep the exact fraction for U″.

Compare with the solution

The time t in minutes is r/c. The derivative of r/c with respect to r is the constant 1/c. The chain rule gives U′(r) = T′(r/c)/c, then U″(r) = T″(r/c)/(c × c). The conversion applies at each differentiation because we again differentiate with respect to time in seconds.

At the start, U′(0) = -6 / 60 = -0.1 °C/s. Next, c × c = 3600 s/min × s/min; U″(0) = 3/5 / 3600 = 1/6000 °C/s/s. This fraction is exact. Dividing only once would give 0.01, a value incompatible with differentiating twice using the new clock.

Reverse check: multiplying the slope back by 60 recovers -6 °C/min; multiplying the second derivative back by 3600 recovers 0.6 °C/min/min. The numbers change with the clock, but the cup still cools and its slope still becomes less negative.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify the conversion factors using composition and the chain rule, without guessing them.
  • Find -0.1 °C/s and 1/6000 °C/s/s, with the correct sign.
  • Return to the original units to check the results.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does T″ describe, and what is its unit if T is a temperature and t is time in minutes?

T″ is the derivative of the slope T′ with respect to the same time variable. Its unit is °C/min/min: a change in degrees per minute, per minute.

Recall 2How can the cup cool while its second derivative is positive?

T′ remains negative: the temperature falls. A positive T″ indicates that this slope increases, so it becomes less negative. The cooling slows down.

Recall 3In T(t) = b + A exp(−kt), with b, A, and k constant, what factor appears each time the exponential is differentiated?

The chain rule introduces −k each time. Thus T′ = −kA exp(−kt), then T″ = k × k × A exp(−kt). The positive sign comes from multiplying the negative factors.

Recall 4What other relation can be used to check T″ in the cup model?

T′ = −k(T − b), so T″ = −kT′ when b and k are constant. This recovers the result and its unit without starting again from the exponential.

Recall 5Why does changing from minutes to seconds divide the second derivative by the square of the conversion factor?

Composition with the new clock introduces an inverse factor at each differentiation. For U(r) = T(r/c), we get U′ = T′/c, then U″ = T″/(c × c), at the corresponding instants.
