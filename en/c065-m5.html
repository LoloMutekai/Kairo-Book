Level 1 · C065·M5

Prediction interval

Your goalConstruct an interval for the time of a future journey and explain why it is wider than the interval for the mean journey time.

Before you start
c040-m5 : least squares · c056-m5 : effect size · c037-m5 : controlled cleaning · c065-m1 : regression · c065-m2 : correlation · c065-m3 : residuals · c065-m4 : coefficient uncertainty

✿01 See the idea

A courier is planning her next journey. The distance will be 4.5 km; the line fitted to her previous journeys predicts 19 min. Can she rely on this time to the nearest minute? Even if the mean journey time were known exactly, small random effects would make this particular journey time vary.

We need to separate two questions. The confidence interval for the mean brackets the unknown mean journey time at this distance. The prediction interval targets the time of a new individual journey. It accounts for uncertainty about the line and for the variation specific to the journey that has yet to take place.

The diagrams compare these two targets at the same distance and the same nominal level of 95 %. In the chosen model, the next journey requires the wider margin. The centre stays the same: changing the quantity to be covered changes the uncertainty without shifting the central prediction.

The scene and all its journey times are fictional. We assume the same mode of transport and comparable conditions; we are measuring no real performance. The nominal rate is a property that a procedure aims to achieve under assumptions, rather than an observed success rate.

The rule to remember

For a future observation, add the variability of the new random error to the uncertainty in the estimated mean; then take the square root and multiply by the coverage factor.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Bracket the time of the next 4.5 km journey

The 8 fictional pairs (distance in km; time in min) are (1 ; 8), (2 ; 14), (3 ; 18), (4 ; 20), (5 ; 22), (6 ; 24), (7 ; 24), (8 ; 22). We use their least squares fit again, then construct a two-sided interval at the nominal level of 95 % for a new journey of 4.5 km.

  1. State what the model allows

    x is the distance, in km; Y is the random journey time, in min. The model is Y = a + bx + ε. a is an unknown fixed time, in min; b is an unknown slope, in min/km; ε is a random deviation, in min. We assume a linear mean a + bx, distances known without error, and independent deviations with a mean of 0 and the same strictly positive variance σ², in min². The Greek letter σ is the standard deviation, in min.

    To use Student’s t distribution exactly here before numerical rounding, we also assume that the deviations follow a normal distribution, the bell-shaped distribution. The new deviation is independent of those for the journeys used in the fit and follows the same distribution. These assumptions are given for the exercise; the few points do not prove them. Journeys affected by the same incident, for example, would not provide independent deviations.

    A and B are the least squares estimates of a and b. The central journey time ŷ(x) = A + Bx estimates the mean at x; the hat marks an estimate. x⋆ denotes the distance of the next journey. Our target is its unknown journey time Y⋆, rather than just the mean a + bx⋆.

  2. Recover the centre from the fit

    n counts the journeys. x̄ and ȳ are the means of the distances and journey times. The sums are 36 km and 152 min, so x̄ = 36 / 8 = 4.5 km and ȳ = 152 / 8 = 19 min. Sxx is the sum of the squared distances from x̄, equal to 42 km²; Sxy is the sum of the products of the deviations from x̄ and ȳ, equal to 84 km·min. The sums include all the pairs.

    We reuse the fitting rules: B = Sxy / Sxx = 84 / 42 = 2 min/km. Then A = ȳ − Bx̄ = 19 − 9 = 10 min. The intercept is a parameter of the line; we do not infer an actual journey time at 0 distance from it.

    y^(x⋆)=A+Bx⋆=10+2×4.5=19 min

    The product Bx⋆ is 9 min. Adding this time to A gives the centre 19 min; this calculation alone gives no margin.

  3. Estimate the spread around the line

    A residual is the observed journey time minus the fitted journey time at the same x. We denote the sum of their squares by SSE: here it is 48 min². s² estimates the variance σ² of the random errors. Since the fit estimated the 2 coefficients A and B, the degrees of freedom, denoted by ν, are n − 2 = 6. They count the freedom remaining to estimate the spread.

    s2=SSEn−2=486=8 min²

    We divide a sum of squares by its degrees of freedom to estimate the variance; we do not use SSE itself as the variance of a journey time. Check: 8 × 6 = 48 min².

  4. Calculate the uncertainty in the mean at this distance

    We use h to denote the dimensionless factor that measures the uncertainty in the estimated mean at x⋆. It depends on the number of journeys and on how far x⋆ is from x̄. The estimated variance of this error in the mean is s²h.

    h=1n+(x⋆−x¯)2Sxx

    Here x⋆ = x̄ = 4.5 km: the difference is 0 km, its square is 0 km² and its quotient by Sxx is 0. This leaves h = 1 / 8 + 0 = 1/8. The variance of the error in the mean is therefore 8 × 1/8 = 1 min².

    The quotient of two quantities in km² is dimensionless. If x⋆ moves further from x̄, the squared term increases: the line is known less precisely at that location.

  5. Add the future journey’s random error before taking the square root

    The error Y⋆ − ŷ(x⋆) combines the new deviation ε⋆ and the error in estimating the mean. The new deviation is independent of the data that produced the line: the variances add. We estimate the total variance by s² + s²h = s²(1 + h). The term 1 therefore corresponds to the new random error, rather than an additional coefficient to fit.

    Epréd=s2(1+h)=9=3 min

    Epred denotes the prediction standard error, meaning the estimate of the standard deviation of the prediction error. Numerically, 1 + 1/8 = 9/8, then 8 × 9/8 = 9 min². Its square root is 3 min, because 3 × 3 = 9. For the mean alone, Emoy = √(s²h) = 1 min, because 1 × 1 = 1.

    Check using another expression: 1 + 8 = 9 min². The added contribution is indeed 8 min². The prediction standard error exceeds that for the mean; the square root of a sum is not the sum of the square roots.

  6. Choose the coverage factor

    We set the nominal level at 95 % before observing the future journey. α is the dimensionless proportion left outside the interval: α = 1 − 0.95 = 0.05. q is the positive factor from Student’s t distribution with ν = 6 degrees of freedom that leaves α / 2 in each tail. This distribution accounts for estimating the variance from the sample.

    For these settings, q ≈ 2.447. This value is supplied rounded; you are not asked to calculate the quantile by hand. The theoretical interval uses the unrounded quantile. The following operations use exactly the fraction 2447 / 1000, then the bounds are rounded for reading; they therefore remain approximations to the theoretical interval.

    Under the stated assumptions, the ratio of the prediction error to Epred follows Student’s t distribution with ν degrees of freedom. Taking the factor q from this distribution lets us construct the central interval. An arbitrarily chosen multiplier would not give the same nominal level.

  7. Form the margin and the two bounds

    I=[y^(x⋆)−qEpréd;y^(x⋆)+qEpréd]

    I denotes the prediction interval for this journey. The margin is qEpred = 2.447 × 3 = 7.341 min. We subtract this margin from the centre for the lower bound: 19 − 7.341 = 11.659 min. We add it for the upper bound: 19 + 7.341 = 26.341 min.

    At the chosen display precision, I ≈ [11.66 ; 26.34] min. The confidence interval for the mean would instead be 19 ± 2.447 min, or approximately [16.55 ; 21.45] min. This second margin is qEmoy: it omits the variation in the next journey because it targets a different quantity.

  8. Check the calculation and limit the interpretation

    Check symmetry using the bounds before display rounding: their sum is 38 min and their midpoint is 19 min, matching the centre. Their difference is 14.682 min, or 2 times the margin 7.341 min. The prediction margin is 3 times that for the mean in this case.

    The nominal level of 95 % means that, if we repeated sampling and then constructing the interval with a new journey at this distance, the theoretical procedure would cover this new journey time in this proportion, under the model. This does not guarantee coverage of this particular journey; nor does it say that 95 % of the points already used must fall within this interval.

    The distances used in the fit range from 1 to 8 km; 4.5 km stays within this range. This check does not prove the assumptions. Another mode of transport, a variance that changes with distance or dependent random errors can make the interval poorly calibrated. A narrow interval proves no causal relationship.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the formulas. Reconstruct the two contributions to the error for the next journey, explain the term added before taking the square root and recover the unit of the margin. Then say which quantity the small interval in the diagram addresses.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The margin for the mean is enough for the next journey.”

    It covers the uncertainty in the estimated mean. For an individual journey, add the variance of the new random error before taking the square root. Recover the origin of the term 1 in s²(1 + h).

    Return to the step “Add the future journey’s random error before taking the square root”

  • “I multiply the variance directly by the coverage factor.”

    The variance is 9 min². Its square root is 3 min; this is what q multiplies. A margin in min² cannot be added to a time in min. Also check the midpoint and width of the interval.

    Review the square root, units and checks on the bounds

  • “At the 95 % level, my next journey time is guaranteed to fall within the interval.”

    The level concerns a repeated procedure under the model. An observation can fall outside the interval; a change in conditions can also invalidate the model. Read the last exercise before drawing conclusions from a single journey.

    Examine the new observation and the change in conditions

Exercise 1 · Distinguish the targets

Which interval goes with the delivery?

The courier asks for a range for the time of her next 4.5 km journey. She is offered either [16.55 ; 21.45] min or [11.66 ; 26.34] min. Choose and explain what the other proposal covers.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Ask whether the unknown quantity is a mean or an individual outcome.

Hint 2

The journey that has yet to take place will bring its own random error.

Compare with the solution

For a future individual journey time, choose [11.66 ; 26.34] min. The margin includes the new random error and uncertainty in the estimated mean. The centre is 19 min in both cases.

The shorter interval [16.55 ; 21.45] min targets the unknown mean at this distance. It does not become an interval for the next journey just because its centre seems right.

Check your reasoning

  • Choose the interval for an individual journey and name its target.
  • Identify the mean as the target of the other interval.
  • Explain the contribution of the new random error, beyond simply choosing the longer segment.

Exercise 2 · Reconstruct the calculation

A student’s work has omitted a contribution

At the same distance, a student’s work uses s² = 8 min², h = 1/8 and q ≈ 2.447, then reports 19 ± 2.447 min for the next journey. Correct the calculation from the total variance through to the bounds and give an independent check.

Hint 1

Start by writing s²h + s² and checking its unit.

Hint 2

Take the square root before calculating q times the standard error.

Compare with the solution

The student has only calculated the margin for the mean. For the future journey, the total variance is 8 × 1/8 + 8 = 1 + 8 = 9 min². Its square root is 3 min, rather than 1 min.

The margin becomes 2.447 × 3 = 7.341 min. The bounds before display rounding are 11.659 and 26.341 min, giving approximately [11.66 ; 26.34] min.

One possible check: the midpoint of the bounds is 19 min and the width 14.682 min is 2 times the margin. Check the mechanism: the variance added compared with the mean is 8 min², exactly s².

Check your reasoning

  • Restore the term for the new random error and justify adding it.
  • Convert from min² to min by taking a square root.
  • Calculate both bounds and perform a check that does more than repeat their subtraction.

Exercise 3 · Interpret and limit

After the journey, then beyond the range

The new 4.5 km journey eventually takes 27 min. Is this single outcome outside the interval enough to refute the nominal level of 95 %? The courier then wants to reuse the same interval for 15 km, using another mode of transport. Which conclusions are justified?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare the journey time with the upper bound before interpreting the nominal level.

Hint 2

Distinguish an observation outside the interval from a coverage check over a series. Then reread the distance range and the assumption of comparable conditions.

Compare with the solution

27 min exceeds 26.34 min. Its difference from the centre is 8 min and exceeds the margin 7.341 min. The interval therefore did not cover this journey time. A procedure at the nominal level of 95 % allows outcomes outside the interval; this single case does not measure its coverage and is not enough to refute it. It calls for checking the conditions and, to assess calibration, a set of predictions made before their observations.

15 km exceeds the fitting range [1 ; 8] km: this is extrapolation. Even keeping the coefficients, we would need to recalculate the centre and h. The change in mode of transport also calls into question the line and the distribution of the random errors. Reusing the old bounds addresses none of these difficulties; even recalculated bounds would not prove the model’s validity outside its conditions.

Check your reasoning

  • State explicitly that the interval did not cover the observed journey time.
  • Separate this finding from measuring coverage over repeated trials.
  • Name the extrapolation, the calculations to repeat and the change in assumptions.
  • Do not treat rounding, reading or an assisted answer as validation of the model or as an acquired skill.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which quantity does a prediction interval target here?

The time of a new individual journey at a given distance. The confidence interval for the mean targets the unknown mean journey time at this distance.

Recall 2Where does the term 1 added in s²(1 + h) come from?

From the new random error of the journey: its variance is estimated by s². The error in the estimated mean contributes s²h. Independence of the new random error and the fitting data allows the variances to be added.

Recall 3How do you go from an error variance to a margin in minutes?

Take the square root to obtain the standard error in min, then multiply it by the dimensionless factor q. The margin is added to and subtracted from the centre; it is not the total width.

Recall 4What does the nominal level of 95 % mean, and what does it not guarantee?

Under the model, the theoretical procedure covers a new journey time in this proportion when sampling and prediction are repeated. It does not guarantee coverage of this particular journey and does not measure an actual coverage rate from this example alone.

Recall 5Which assumptions must you check before using Student’s t formula?

A linear mean, distances known without error, independent normal deviations with a common positive variance, and an independent new journey following the same model. Extrapolation or a change in conditions requires a new justification.
