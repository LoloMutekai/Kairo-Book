Level 2 · C060·M5

Approximations and assumptions

Your goalCalculate a normal approximation to a probability, check its error in a simple model, and name the assumptions that justify the calculation.

Before you start
c035-m5 : finite covariance and conditioning · c058-m5 : change of variables · c046-m5 : approximation error · c060-m1 : transformations · c060-m2 : conditioning random variables · c060-m3 : generating functions · c060-m4 : limit distributions

✿01 See the idea

A packing workshop fills bags. In our fictional model, each bag contains either 98 g or 102 g, with equal probability. A batch of 16 bags is prepared: what is the probability that its mean mass is between 99 g and 101 g, including the endpoints?

Independent deviations can offset one another in a mean. The spread of batch means can therefore be smaller than the spread of individual bag masses. A normal bell curve then provides a convenient approximation to the distribution of the means, even though the bags themselves do not follow a normal distribution.

The bell curve is smooth; the mean masses in this model change in steps. Replacing their distribution with a bell curve can therefore shift probability near the endpoints. The diagram illustrates the question; it does not prove accuracy.

All the data and the chosen tolerance are fictional. We assume independent fills with the same distribution, and masses known without weighing error. We will compare the approximate calculation with exact counting made possible by this very simple model. No readings from a real workshop are presented.

The rule to remember

State the assumptions, calculate the scale of the mean, use the sign ≈ for the approximate distribution, then compare its error with the required accuracy.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Is the bell curve sufficient for the batch of bags?

Find the probability of the closed interval [99 g ; 101 g] for the mean of 16 bags. An approximation will be considered sufficient if its absolute probability error does not exceed 0.01, with no unit. This tolerance is a choice made for the exercise.

  1. Name the objects before approximating

    Xᵢ is the random mass of bag i, in grams; i runs from 1 to n, the number of bags. A bar above X denotes the batch mean. E denotes the expected value, meaning the mean predicted by the model; Var denotes the variance, a mean of squared deviations from the expected value.

    X¯n=X1+⋯+Xnn

    The dots stand for the intermediate masses: add all the masses, then divide by the number of bags. μ, in grams, denotes the expected mass of a bag; σ, in grams, is its strictly positive standard deviation. The variance of a bag is the square of σ, in grams squared.

    The variables Xᵢ are independent: knowing one mass gives no information about the others in the model. They have the same distribution: each equals 98 g or 102 g with probability 1/2. Their variance is finite. These assumptions come before the calculation; a large number of rows does not prove them.

  2. Calculate the centre and scale exactly

    The masses are symmetric: μ = (98 + 102)/2 = 100 g. Each deviation from the centre has absolute value (102 − 98)/2 = 2 g. The variance is therefore 2 × 2 = 4 g squared. This requires no normal approximation.

    E(X¯n)=μ;Var(X¯n)=σ2n

    The expected value of a sum is the sum of the expected values. Independence also allows the variances to be added; dividing the sum by n divides its variance by the square of n. Hence Var(mean) = 4/16 = 1/4 g squared.

    The standard deviation of the mean is σ/√n = 2/4 = 0.50 g, since 4 × 4 = 16. Check: 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25 g squared. These moment identities remain exact even when the normal shape is a poor approximation.

  3. Standardize and recall what the limit establishes

    Zn is the mean with μ subtracted, divided by its standard deviation. This division compares a deviation in grams with a scale in grams: Zn has no unit. Standardization alone does not make the distribution normal.

    Zn=X¯n−μσ/n

    G denotes a variable with a standard normal distribution, with expected value 0 and variance 1. Φ(t) = Pr(G ≤ t) is its cumulative distribution function; t is a threshold with no unit and Pr denotes a probability. The central limit theorem from the previous unit states that, under our assumptions, the distribution of Zn approaches that of G as n tends to infinity.

    For a fixed positive a with no unit, this gives Pr(−a ≤ Zn ≤ a) → Φ(a) − Φ(−a). The arrow expresses a limit of probabilities. Here it provides neither a universally sufficient sample size nor an error bound for n = 16.

    Our half-width is 1 g = 2 × 0.50 g. The standardized endpoints are therefore −2 and 2. This transformation allows a standard normal area to be used for the same event.

  4. Calculate the approximation and separate its rounding error

    Pr(99≤X¯n≤101)≈Φ(2)−Φ(−2)

    The endpoints in this formula are expressed in grams. On the right, the area under the density of G between −2 and 2 is evaluated numerically. It equals 0.95 when rounded to the nearest hundredth: it is a probability, with no unit. An explicit margin will allow a decision despite this rounding.

    Rounding to the nearest hundredth can change the displayed value by at most 0.005. The normal area used is therefore at most 0.955. This rounding in the calculation must be separated from the difference caused by replacing the true distribution with a normal distribution. Writing 0.95 with more decimal places does not reduce this second error.

  5. Obtain an exact reference by counting

    K is the number of bags with mass 102 g in the batch. The others have mass 98 g. The mean is therefore 98 + 4K/16, in grams. The requested closed interval is equivalent to 4 ≤ K ≤ 12; for example, K = 4 reaches exactly the endpoint 99 g.

    An elementary outcome specifies which mass is drawn for each bag. The 65536 outcomes are equally likely: 2 independent possibilities repeated 16 times. K therefore follows the binomial distribution. The number of outcomes with exactly k heavy bags is the binomial coefficient C(n,k), meaning the number of ways to choose k positions from n; here k is an integer count.

    For the lower tail K < 4, the coefficients are C(16,0) = 1, C(16,1) = 16, C(16,2) = 16 × 15/2 = 120 and C(16,3) = 120 × 14/3 = 560. Their sum is 697 outcomes.

    Swapping heavy and light bags gives the same number of outcomes in the upper tail K > 12. The tails are disjoint: together they contain 1394 outcomes. Subtract them from all the outcomes to obtain the exact probability. In the following formula, p with the label “exact” denotes this probability.

    pexact=1−139465536=6414265536

    The exact fraction is 32071/32768; its decimal value is approximately 0.9787. An independent check directly adds the coefficients from K = 4 to K = 12. Here “exact” means exact for the stated filling model, without guaranteeing that this model describes a real workshop.

  6. Decide using the required tolerance

    Let pn denote the exact probability and pᴳ the area from the normal approximation. The absolute error we seek is |pn − pᴳ|, with no unit. We know that pᴳ ≤ 0.955 and that pn ≈ 0.9787. Even in the most favourable case for the normal distribution, the difference is therefore at least pn − 0.955 = 19439/819200 ≈ 0.0237.

    This bound exceeds 0.01: the normal approximation used does not meet our tolerance. An equivalent check: 32071/32768 is greater than 0.965, which is the maximum normal area allowed by rounding plus the tolerance. The failure therefore does not come solely from the displayed decimal places.

    We have assessed a specific event, for a specific sample size and an approximation without adjustment of the discrete endpoints. This conclusion does not rule out every normal approximation. Exact counting is possible here; elsewhere, a suitable error bound or another justified reference is needed before promising accuracy.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results. Explain why the centre and variance of the mean are exact, while its probability of lying in the interval is obtained from a normal distribution only by approximation. Where did you use independence, and which check allowed you to reject the required accuracy?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I have standardized, so my variable follows an exactly normal distribution.”

    Standardization changes the centre and scale. The mean remains discrete in our model. Find the sign ≈ in the step from the probability to the normal area, then compare with exact counting.

    Review standardization, then counting

  • “A large enough sample size removes the need to check the assumptions.”

    Sample size creates neither independence nor finite variance. Check how the bags are produced. Repeating the same weighing in several cells does not create that many independent fills; the last exercise tests this case.

    Examine the copied weighings

  • “The difference comes from rounding: I will ask for more decimal places.”

    Even after allowing 0.005 for rounding, the difference remains greater than 0.0237, and therefore greater than our tolerance 0.01. The approximate distribution must be reconsidered. The calculations use the exact fraction before any decimal value is read.

    Review the decision using the tolerance

Exercise 1 · Application

Increase the batch size

The same model now provides 64 independent bags. Calculate the standard deviation of their mean and the interval centred on μ whose half-width is 2 times this standard deviation. Can the previous tolerance be guaranteed from this calculation alone?

Hint 1

Calculate the square root of 64, then divide the standard deviation of a bag by this square root.

Hint 2

The endpoints are the centre minus and plus the new half-width. Does the limit theorem give a numerical error for this sample size?

Compare with the solution

Since 8 × 8 = 64, the standard deviation is 2/8 = 0.25 g. The half-width is 2 × 0.25 = 0.50 g. The interval is [99.50 g ; 100.50 g].

The standardized endpoints remain −2 and 2, so the same normal area applies approximately. Accuracy at a finite sample size still needs to be checked: the exact reduction in standard deviation does not by itself provide a bound on the approximation error.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 0.25 g and the endpoints 99.50 g and 100.50 g.
  • Justify dividing by the square root of the sample size.
  • State that the accuracy guarantee still requires proof.

Exercise 2 · Explained check

Assess a rounded value

For the initial batch, someone reports 0.95 and says that the probability error is at most 0.01. You know the exact fraction 32071/32768. Use the rounding margin 0.005 to decide without assuming that the normal area equals its displayed value exactly.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The normal area can exceed its rounded value; allow it this entire margin.

Hint 2

Compare the exact probability with 0.95 + 0.005 + 0.01.

Compare with the solution

The normal area is at most 0.95 + 0.005 = 0.955. If the error did not exceed 0.01, the exact probability would be at most 0.965. However, 32071/32768 ≈ 0.9787 is greater.

The error is therefore at least 32071/32768 − 0.955 = 19439/819200 ≈ 0.0237, exceeding 0.01. The claim is rejected for this event and this model. Rounded decimals help us read the values; the fractions and inequalities determine the decision.

Check your reasoning

  • Add the rounding margin before rejecting the claim.
  • Compare probabilities with no unit.
  • Draw a conclusion about the approximation examined, using the given tolerance.

Exercise 3 · Transfer of assumptions

The same bag in every cell

The sheet has 16 cells, but only one bag was weighed and its mass was then copied into every cell, without weighing error. Call the mass of this bag Y; it follows the same distribution as before. What is the mean on the sheet? Is its variance 0.25 g squared? What is its probability of lying in [99 g ; 101 g]?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Replace each mass in the sum with Y, then divide by the number of cells.

Hint 2

Can the mean take a value other than 98 g and 102 g?

Compare with the solution

All the masses are Y. Their sum is nY and their mean is Y. Its variance therefore remains 4 g squared, that of a bag, rather than 0.25 g squared. The copies are perfectly dependent.

This mean equals only 98 g or 102 g. Both values lie outside the requested interval: its probability is 0. Using the reduced standard deviation or the same normal approximation would be unjustified. The number of cells does not replace the number of independent fills.

Check your reasoning

  • Simplify the mean to Y before discussing a limit distribution.
  • Keep the variance of a bag.
  • Obtain a probability of 0 and identify the lost independence.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which assumptions of the central limit theorem did you use for the bags?

Independent masses with the same distribution, a finite expected value, and a finite, strictly positive variance. The accuracy obtained at a finite sample size needs an additional check.

Recall 2Why divide the standard deviation of a bag by the square root of the sample size?

Independence allows the variances in the sum to be added. Dividing this sum by n divides its variance by the square of n; the standard deviation of the mean is therefore σ/√n. It is expressed in grams.

Recall 3What does the limit theorem not promise here?

It gives neither an exactly normal distribution at a finite sample size, nor a universally sufficient sample size, nor a numerical error for our interval. Standardizing is not enough to make a variable normal.

Recall 4Which errors must you distinguish when reading an approximate probability?

The rounding error in the calculated value and the error due to replacing the distribution with a normal distribution. Increasing the number of decimal places does not fix this replacement. Whether the model fits the real world remains a separate question.

Recall 5Why do copies of a weighing not reduce the spread of the mean?

The mean of identical copies of Y remains Y. Its variance remains that of the initial weighing. The reduction factor derived from independence does not apply.
