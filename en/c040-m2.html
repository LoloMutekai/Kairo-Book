Level 1 · C040·M2

Norm

Your goalCalculate the length of a vector from the dot product, check its unit, and explain what changes when the displacement is reversed or described differently.

Before you start
c039-m5 : changes of basis · c040-m1 : dot product

✿01 See the idea

A cart must reach a mark painted on the floor, 3 m east and 4 m north of its starting point. Would a taut cable between the starting point and the mark be as long as the route that follows the horizontal edge first, then the vertical edge? Draw both before calculating.

The vector v describes the displacement from the starting point to the mark: it retains its direction and which way it points. Its norm, written ‖v‖ with double bars, is the length of the direct segment here. It is a number accompanied by a unit. This number no longer tells us which way to go.

The scene and its values are fictional. The floor is flat, east and north are perpendicular directions, and the coordinates are measured in the same unit, the metre. We are finding a geometric length; we do not model obstacles, time, or the energy used by the cart.

In the second drawing, imagine the same displacement in reverse. The way it points changes, but the cable needed keeps its length. This distinction between a vector and its size will be our simplest check.

The rule to remember

For the Euclidean norm, take the nonnegative square root of the dot product of the vector with itself. Check the basis and units before adding squared coordinates.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

How much cable is needed between the starting point and the mark?

The components of displacement v are x = 3 m east and y = 4 m north. Let us calculate its Euclidean norm, then check the result while distinguishing a length from its square.

  1. Relate length to the dot product

    x and y denote the components of v in the east and north directions. An orthonormal basis uses perpendicular directions and basis vectors of unit length. Here, each unit represents 1 m. In this basis, the dot product ⟨v,v⟩ is the sum of the squared components; its physical unit is m².

    ‖v‖=⟨v,v⟩=x2+y2

    The symbol √ denotes the unique nonnegative square root: the number whose square is the quantity under the root sign. Nonnegative means positive or 0. The relation to the length of the segment is the right triangle relation, since the two directions are perpendicular. The coordinate formula requires this basis; the definition using the dot product remains meaningful in any basis, provided that product is calculated correctly.

  2. Square each component

    The dot product compares corresponding components. With the same vector on both sides, we therefore multiply x by x and y by y. We get 3 × 3 = 9 m² and 4 × 4 = 16 m². The products have the same unit, so we can add them.

    ⟨v,v⟩=9+16=25 m²

    This sum is the square of the norm. At this stage, we do not yet have a length in metres.

  3. Return to a length

    The square root converts square metres back to metres. Let us find a positive number whose square is 25: 5 × 5 = 25. The negative value would also give the same square, but it cannot be the nonnegative square root.

    ‖v‖=25 m=5 m

    The taut cable between the marks therefore measures 5 m in our model. This does not tell us whether the cart can follow that segment in a real workshop.

  4. Check the square and the expected size

    First check: the square of the result, 25 m², does give 25 m². Second check: the direct segment must be at least as long as the larger component, 4 m, and at most as long as the route along the edges.

    That route measures 3 + 4 = 7 m. We do have 4 ≤ 5 ≤ 7, in metres. The detour here adds 7 − 5 = 2 m. Answering 7 m would have measured the bent route; answering 25 m would have omitted the square root and changed the unit without justification.

  5. Recognize what makes a norm

    A norm is a rule for size that satisfies the following properties. u and w denote vectors in the same space, using the same unit; λ denotes a real number with no unit. The 0 vector, written o here, represents no displacement.

    ‖u‖≥0;‖u‖=0⇔u=o

    The size is positive or 0, and only the 0 vector has 0 size. In our formula, a sum of squares is 0 only if every component is 0.

    ‖λu‖=|λ|‖u‖

    |λ| is the absolute value of λ, its distance from 0. Scaling multiplies the norm by this absolute value. The squared components acquire a factor of λ squared; the square root of that factor is |λ|. A negative factor also reverses the way the vector points, without making its norm negative.

    ‖u+w‖≤‖u‖+‖w‖

    This is the triangle inequality: the size of the total displacement does not exceed the sum of the sizes of the two successive displacements. The grey route illustrates it with u east and w north: 5 m ≤ 7 m. This numerical case illustrates the general property of the Euclidean norm; it is not a proof for all vectors.

    The sum of squares alone is not a norm: when the vector doubles, this sum is multiplied by 2 × 2, whereas a norm must only double. The square root ensures the correct scaling. Other rules for size can satisfy the properties of a norm; this page studies the Euclidean norm derived from the dot product.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The norm is 25 m: I added the squares.”

    The sum is 25 m²: it measures the square of the norm. Take its nonnegative square root to recover 5 m, then square that length to check.

    Review the step from square metres to metres

  • “On the return, the coordinates are negative, so the norm is negative too.”

    The signs tell us which way the displacement points. Squaring removes those signs, and the chosen square root is positive or 0. The return has the same norm 5 m. Check this equality on the opposite arrows.

    Compare the displacement with its opposite

  • “I can take the square root of the sum of all the squared coordinates, whatever the basis.”

    This coordinate formula requires an orthonormal basis for the chosen dot product. A command that represents a longer step must be counted using its actual length. Reconstruct the components in metres before calculating, as in the final exercise.

    Handle the change of unit on the controller

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The factor acts on each component. For the length, take its absolute value.

Hint 2

Compare the squares of the norms using the factor λ squared.

Compare with the solution

The displacement becomes (-2 × 3 ; -2 × 4) m = (-6 ; -8) m. The way it points is reversed and its dimensions are enlarged by a factor of 2.

Using the norm rule: |-2| × 5 = 2 × 5 = 10 m. The factor used for the length is positive.

Using the components: (-6) squared is 36 m² and (-8) squared is 64 m²; the sum is 100 m². The square root is 10 m, whose square is 100 m². The two methods agree.

The dot product of the vector with itself goes from 25 m² to 100 m², with a factor of λ squared. A norm must scale by |λ|: here, it goes from 5 m to 10 m. The square of the norm therefore does not have the correct scaling rule to be a norm itself.

Check your reasoning

  • Multiply both components by the signed factor.
  • Obtain 10 m in two ways and compare them.
  • Explain the difference between the factor for a norm and the factor for its square.

Hint 1

One copy of b does not represent the same length as one copy of c.

Compare with the solution

The coefficients are indeed α = 3/2 = 3/2 = 1.5 and β = 4/1 = 4. They are counts of steps. Reconstructing gives 1.5 × 2 = 3 m east and 4 × 1 = 4 m north: the displacement is unchanged.

The naive sum of the squared coefficients is 9/4 + 16 = 73/4, with no unit. This is not the square of the length in metres. The directions of B are perpendicular, but b has a length of 2 m, instead of the unit 1 m used before.

‖v‖2=(2α)2+(1β)2 en m²

Restoring the step lengths gives 9 + 16 = 25 m², hence 5 m. Check: changing the description of the same segment cannot change its length. The naive sum 73/4 differs from 25 by 27/4 when we compare their numerical values; attaching the unit m² to it would be precisely the mistake of ignoring the step scale.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you obtain the Euclidean norm from the dot product? What happens to the units?

Take the nonnegative square root of ⟨v,v⟩. For components in metres, this product is in m² and the norm in m. In an orthonormal basis, the product is the sum of the squared components.

Recall 2What is lost when displacement v is replaced by its norm?

The norm gives a size that is positive or 0, without the direction or which way the vector points. Thus v and its opposite have the same norm 5 m in the scene, although they describe different displacements.

Recall 3What factor multiplies the norm of λv? Why is an absolute value needed?

The norm is multiplied by |λ|, where λ is real and has no unit. A negative factor reverses the way the vector points, but its size remains positive or 0. The sum of squares acquires a factor of λ squared; its square root acquires a factor of |λ|.

Recall 4What relation connects the norm of a total displacement to the norms of its stages?

‖u + w‖ ≤ ‖u‖ + ‖w‖, for two displacements of the same kind. In the scene, the direct segment measures 5 m and the route along the edges 7 m. The norms therefore do not always add with equality.

Recall 5What should you check before taking the square root of the sum of squared coordinates?

The basis must be orthonormal for the chosen dot product, and the components must use the same unit. If the coordinates count steps of different sizes, reconstruct the physical components before applying this formula.

What you understand
The Euclidean norm assigns a size to a vector. A 0 norm corresponds to the 0 vector; it provides no direction. The geometric scene helps explain the calculation without assigning a physical unit to the components of a vector used in computing.
