Level 1 · C040·M4

Gram-Schmidt

Your goalConstruct orthogonal directions from two displacements, normalize them, and check that they still allow the same displacements.

Before you start
c039-m5 : changes of basis · c040-m1 : dot product · c040-m2 : norm · c040-m3 : orthogonality

✿01 See the idea

On a sheet of paper, a small cart receives movement commands. Command a sends it 3 cm east and 4 cm north. Command b sends it 6 cm east and 3 cm north. The arrows start at the same point to compare the commands, rather than to show a sequence of movements. All these data are fictional.

The commands point in different directions, but they are not perpendicular. Imagine decomposing b into two movements: a slide along the direction of a, followed by a sideways displacement that reaches the same endpoint. The sideways displacement is what b contributes beyond the direction already available.

Gram-Schmidt constructs exactly this sideways displacement. It keeps the first arrow, removes from the next arrow its component parallel to the first, and repeats if the list continues. The result must allow the original commands to be reconstructed: we change the reference directions, while preserving the possible displacements.

In this unit, we work through this pair of commands in full. An orthogonal basis requires nonzero vectors that are pairwise perpendicular and span the space being studied. If their norms also equal 1, the basis is orthonormal. Making the arrows perpendicular and giving them unit norm are separate operations.

The rule to remember

Keep the first direction; subtract the parallel component of the next; check that the residual is orthogonal and nonzero; normalize if an orthonormal basis is needed.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Two perpendicular directions for the same commands

We start with a = (3 ; 4) cm and b = (6 ; 3) cm. Let us construct an orthogonal basis (u, v), then an orthonormal basis (q, s). The arrows represent unrestricted displacements: the coefficients in a combination can be real and negative. We impose no mechanical constraints on the cart here.

  1. Set the coordinate system, units, and assumptions

    The plane is real and Euclidean. The east and north axes are perpendicular, with the same unit of length. For vectors w and z, the subscripts E and N denote their east and north components; the dot · denotes the dot product. We use the following rule:

    w·z=wEzE+wNzN

    The notation ‖w‖ denotes the norm; its square equals w·w. For two displacements in cm, the dot product is expressed in cm². We keep u = a, which is nonzero. We then seek v, the residual of b after removing its component along u. To obtain a basis of the plane from this pair, a and b must be linearly independent: neither can be a multiple of the other. The calculated residual will let us check this.

  2. Determine what to remove instead of guessing a coefficient

    Let α, the letter alpha, denote the as yet unknown coefficient to use in the subtraction. Set p = αu and v = b − p. p is parallel to u. We choose α so that v is perpendicular to u, meaning that their dot product must be 0:

    u·v=u·(b−αu)=u·b−α(u·u)=0
    α=u·bu·u;p=αu;v=b−p

    u·u is strictly positive because u is nonzero. The cm² units cancel in the division: α is dimensionless, while p and v remain displacements in cm. This decomposition defines the orthogonal projection p of b onto the line of multiples of u. The denominator is the squared norm, rather than the norm.

  3. Calculate the coefficient using the components

    Start with the denominator: u·u = 3 × 3 + 4 × 4 = 9 + 16 = 25 cm². Its positive square root is ‖u‖ = 5 cm, since 5 × 5 = 25.

    Next calculate the numerator: u·b = 3 × 6 + 4 × 3 = 18 + 12 = 30 cm². It measures the component of b in the direction of u, taking the length of u into account.

    α=3025=6/5=1.2

    The coefficient is greater than 1: here the projection extends beyond the tip of a. A projection is onto the entire line, rather than just the segment drawn by a.

  4. Subtract the parallel component, component by component

    The projection is p = (6/5 × 3 ; 6/5 × 4) cm = (18/5 ; 24/5) cm, or (3.6 ; 4.8) cm. These are the components of the arrow along a, rather than those of the residual.

    v=(6−18/5;3−24/5)=(12/5;-9/5) cm

    In decimal form for readability, v = (2.4 ; -1.8) cm. Its north component is negative: after moving up to the projection, we must move back down to reach the tip of b. We subtract the whole of p, rather than the same number from both components.

  5. Check the right angle and the preserved displacements

    The drawing suggests a right angle; the dot product proves it in this exact model. u·v = 3 × (12/5) + 4 × (-9/5) = 36/5 + (-36/5) = 0 cm². The residual v is nonzero, so (u, v) is an orthogonal pair of independent directions.

    Also check the reconstruction: east, 18/5 + 12/5 = 6 cm; north, 24/5 + (-9/5) = 3 cm. We recover b = p + v = αu + v and a = u.

    Why are the possible displacements the same? u = a and v = b − αa can be constructed from the original commands. Conversely, a = u and b = αu + v can be reconstructed from the new ones. Their linear combinations, meaning sums of real multiples of the arrows, therefore span the same plane. Perpendicularity alone would not establish this preservation.

  6. Normalize after making the directions orthogonal

    The squared norm of v is its dot product with itself:

    v·v=(12/5)2+(-9/5)2=144/25+81/25=9 cm²

    Its positive square root is 3 cm. Both norms are nonzero, so we can define q = u/‖u‖ and s = v/‖v‖.

    q=(3/5;4/5);s=(4/5;-3/5)

    The components of q and s are dimensionless: we divided cm by cm. These are unit directions; the displacement lengths will now be carried by their coefficients. In decimal form, q = (0.6 ; 0.8) and s = (0.8 ; -0.6).

    Norm check: q·q = (9 + 16)/25 = 1; s·s = 9/(3 × 3) = 1. Orthogonality check: q·s = (u·v)/(‖u‖‖v‖) = 0. The pair (q, s) is an orthonormal basis. Normalizing a and b directly would have preserved their oblique angle.

  7. Perform another check and read the new coordinates

    Since p and v are perpendicular, their squared norms must add together. Adding the squares of the components of p gives 324/25 + 576/25 = 36 cm². For b, we find 36 + 9 = 45 cm². The check does give 36 + 9 = 45 cm². This is the Pythagorean identity applied to the decomposition.

    In the orthonormal basis, let t and h denote the coefficients of b along q and s. Since q and s are unit vectors and orthogonal, the dot product isolates each coefficient: t = b·q = (u·b)/‖u‖ = 6 cm; h = b·s = (v·v)/‖v‖ = 3 cm. The last equality uses b = p + v and p·v = 0.

    a=(5 cm)q;b=(6 cm)q+(3 cm)s

    Check using the components: 6 × 3/5 + 3 × 4/5 = 6 cm, and 6 × 4/5 + 3 × (-3/5) = 3 cm. α = 6/5 multiplied a vector with norm 5 cm; t = 6 cm multiplies a unit direction. Their values and units differ.

    The chosen order matters: starting with b would generally give different directions while preserving the same plane. For a longer list, subtract from each new vector its projections onto all the orthogonal directions already obtained. A 0 residual adds no direction and cannot be normalized. Here the calculations are exact; on a computer, rounding requires precautions that this unit does not develop.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations. Derive the coefficient α from the condition u·v = 0, explain the difference between α and t, then reconstruct b from the new directions. Which check prevents you from normalizing a 0 residual?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I divide each command by its length: they will be perpendicular.”

    Dividing by a positive norm changes the length while preserving the direction. The product a·b equals 30 cm² and does not become 0 when divided by the norms. First subtract the parallel component, then normalize the residual.

    Review the residual that completes the projection

  • “The denominator of the coefficient is the length of u.”

    In αu, u already carries its length. The condition u·(b − αu) = 0 requires division by u·u, the squared norm of u. With the unit direction q, we instead use t = b·q, which is a length. Work through the derivation again and compare the units.

    Derive the denominator from orthogonality

  • “If the residual is 0, I divide it anyway to finish the basis.”

    The 0 vector has no direction and its norm equals 0: division is impossible. The new vector was already a combination of the previous ones. Keep the directions obtained; do not claim to have added a direction to the plane.

Exercise 1 · Guided application

The same projection from a unit direction

The cart keeps the commands from the example. Start from q = (3/5 ; 4/5), which is dimensionless, and calculate t = b·q. Reconstruct p = tq and compare it with αu. Why are t and α not interchangeable?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate the product b·q using the components; the norm of q equals 1.

Hint 2

q = u/‖u‖. A coefficient multiplying q must therefore carry the length that u already carried.

Compare with the solution

t = 6 × 3/5 + 3 × 4/5 = 6 cm. Multiply q by this length: p = (6 × 3/5 ; 6 × 4/5) cm = (18/5 ; 24/5) cm. This is indeed the projection from the example.

The coefficient α = 6/5 is dimensionless and multiplies u, whose norm is 5 cm. t = α‖u‖ = 6 cm multiplies q, whose norm is 1 and dimensionless. Replacing α with t in front of u would change the displacement and the units.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the dot product and give t in cm.
  • Recover both components of p.
  • Explain t = α‖u‖ while distinguishing their units.

Exercise 2 · Transfer within the same scene

Execute the commands in sequence using the new basis

The cart executes a followed by b. Its total displacement is c = a + b. Calculate its east–north coordinates, then its coefficients along q and s. Check each coefficient with a dot product and reconstruct c. The basis (q, s) remains the one from the example.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

You already know a = (5 cm)q and b = (6 cm)q + (3 cm)s.

Hint 2

In an orthonormal basis, c·q and c·s isolate the coefficients because q·q = s·s = 1 and q·s = 0.

Compare with the solution

In the east–north coordinate system, c = (3 + 6 ; 4 + 3) cm = (9 ; 7) cm. Adding the expressions in the new basis, the coefficient along q becomes 5 + 6 = 11 cm; the one along s remains 3 cm.

Independent calculation: c·q = 9 × 3/5 + 7 × 4/5 = 11 cm; c·s = 9 × 4/5 + 7 × (-3/5) = 3 cm. The results confirm the addition of the coefficients.

Reconstruction: 11 × 3/5 + 3 × 4/5 = 9 cm east; 11 × 4/5 + 3 × (-3/5) = 7 cm north. The coordinates change when the basis changes, but the total displacement c is identical.

Check your reasoning

  • Find c = (9 ; 7) cm.
  • Justify the coefficients 11 cm and 3 cm by addition and then by dot products.
  • Reconstruct the east–north components and state what is preserved.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and repair

A command that adds no direction

In another configuration of the same cart, b is replaced by b′ = 2a = (6 ; 8) cm. Keeping u = a, derive the projection coefficient, then the residual. Can an orthonormal basis of the plane be constructed from these commands alone? State exactly where to stop.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate u·b′, or use b′ = 2u directly.

Hint 2

Before normalizing the residual, check whether a nonzero displacement remains.

Compare with the solution

The product u·b′ equals 3 × 6 + 4 × 8 = 50 cm². The new coefficient is 50/25 = 2, which is dimensionless. The projection is therefore 2u = b′.

The components of the residual are 6 − 2 × 3 = 0 cm and 8 − 2 × 4 = 0 cm. Its norm is 0; the calculation stops before division by this norm.

We can keep q as a unit direction for the line of the commands. We cannot obtain a basis of the plane from this collinear pair: it spans only a line. Choosing a new independent command would add information that was not present. The 0 residual reveals this limitation; it does not justify choosing an arbitrary vector while claiming to preserve the same space.

Check your reasoning

  • Derive the coefficient 2 and the 0 residual.
  • Explicitly refuse to divide by a 0 norm.
  • Distinguish a basis of the spanned line from a basis of the plane.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does Gram-Schmidt construct, and what must it preserve?

Orthogonal directions, optionally then made unit directions, that span the same space as the starting vectors. Remove the components along the directions already constructed.

Recall 2How can you derive the coefficient to subtract without reciting the formula?

Write v = b − αu and impose u·v = 0. This gives α = (u·b)/(u·u) for nonzero u. The projection is αu; the residual is what remains after subtracting it.

Recall 3Why is normalizing the initial vectors insufficient?

Dividing by a positive norm preserves the directions and their angle. Subtracting the projections creates orthogonality; subsequently dividing by each nonzero norm gives unit directions.

Recall 4Which checks verify the new pair in the example?

Check u·v = 0, that v is nonzero, then b = αu + v and a = u. The reverse relations u = a and v = b − αa establish that the spanned space is preserved. After normalization, also check the unit norms.

Recall 5What does a 0 residual mean, and what should you do?

The vector being processed already belongs to the space spanned by the previous directions. It adds no direction. Do not divide by its 0 norm; keep only the nonzero directions obtained.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the example, reconstruct the sequence on paper: choice of u; condition determining α; projection p; residual v; checks; normalization into q and s. Explain in one sentence why the possible displacements are preserved, then where the sequence stops for a collinear command. Finish by deriving the units of α, q, and the displacement coefficients.
