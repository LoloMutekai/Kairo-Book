Level 4 · C232·M3

Large deviations

Your goalCalculate a binomial probability, bound it on the exponential scale, and justify precisely what its asymptotic rate allows us to claim.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

An imaginary urn contains 1 red ball and 1 blue ball. You draw a ball uniformly at random, note its colour, then replace it and mix the balls before drawing again. The model assumes independent draws. The probability of red is p = 1/2 on each draw; these data are chosen for the lesson and do not come from real observations.

In a sequence of 8 draws, the mean number of red outcomes is 4. We want the probability of obtaining at least 6, a proportion q = 3/4. For a longer sequence, we will keep this proportion as the threshold: the difference q − p = 1/4 stays fixed. Keeping only the count 6 would change the question.

Ordinary fluctuations of the proportion narrow around p on the scale of the inverse square root of the sample size. Here, we instead ask for a difference in proportion that does not narrow. Large deviations describe the cost of this event in the logarithm of its probability as the sample size grows.

We will first count the colour sequences exactly. Exponential weighting will then give a bound valid for a finite sample size. Finally, a lower bound will show why its exponent is correct in the limit. At our small sample size, the event is still fairly frequent: the word “large” describes the scale of the deviation, rather than an already huge number of draws.

The rule to remember

Fix the proportion threshold and the model; distinguish the exact probability, a bound for a finite sample size, and the rate in the logarithm as the sample size tends to infinity.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

At least 6 red outcomes in 8 draws

Keep the imaginary urn, p = 1/2, and the threshold q = 3/4. We consider sample sizes n that are multiples of 4, so that nq is an integer. The worked sequence has n = 8. All probabilities and logarithms below have no physical unit.

  1. Define the event and the symbols

    For the draw indexed by j, Xⱼ equals 1 if the ball is red, and 0 otherwise. The Xⱼ are independent and have the same distribution, with P(Xⱼ = 1) = p = 1/2. n is the number of draws; Sn counts the red outcomes; Yn is their proportion. Counts are expressed in numbers of draws, while proportions are dimensionless.

    Sn=Σj=1nXj;Yn=Snn;An={Yn≥q}

    Σ means to add the Xⱼ over the indicated indices. P denotes probability under our urn model, and E denotes expectation, a weighted average under this distribution. exp is the exponential function and ln is the natural logarithm, its inverse. For n = 8, the event An is Sn ≥ 6. The mean of Sn is np = 4, while the mean of Yn is p. The distribution of Sn is called binomial: it counts the red outcomes of these independent draws.

  2. Count exactly before approximating

    Each ordered sequence of 8 colours has the same probability. There are 2 to the power 8 = 256 sequences. The notation C(n,k) counts the ways to choose k positions out of n; it does more than count proportions.

    For exactly 6 red outcomes, choose the positions of the 2 blue outcomes: 8 × 7 / 2 = 28 choices. We divide by 2 because the order in which the blue positions are chosen does not change the sequence. There are then 8 sequences with 7 red outcomes, and 1 sequence with 8 red outcomes.

    P(A8)=28+8+1256=37256≈0.145

    The fraction 37/256 is an exact probability under the stated assumptions. The decimal is rounded and dimensionless. We will keep this fraction to check the bound we obtain later.

  3. Give more weight to sequences with many red outcomes

    Take a strictly positive, dimensionless parameter t. On An, exp(tSn) is at least exp(tnq). The contribution of this event to the expectation therefore gives E[exp(tSn)] ≥ exp(tnq)P(An). Dividing by the positive weight exp(tnq) gives an upper bound on the probability: this is Markov’s inequality applied to the exponential weight.

    Independence allows us to multiply the expectations of the weights for the individual draws. For each draw, E[exp(tXⱼ)] = (1 + exp(t)) / 2. Define Λ(t) as the logarithm of this expectation; Λ is dimensionless.

    Λ(t)=ln(1+exp(t)2)
    P(An)≤exp(−tnq)(1+exp(t)2)n=exp(−n[tq−Λ(t)])

    This calculation gives an inequality for every admissible sample size. It does not yet require any limit at infinity. Without independence, raising the expectation for a single draw to the power n would not be justified.

  4. Choose the exponent and define the rate

    To make the bound as small as possible, maximise tq − Λ(t) over t ≥ 0. The supremum, written sup, is the smallest value that bounds all values of this expression from above. Here it is attained, so we are looking for a maximum. Its value is the rate I(q) for this upper tail.

    I(q)=supt≥0{tq−Λ(t)};Λ′(t)=exp(t)1+exp(t)

    The prime denotes the derivative with respect to t. The second derivative of Λ is exp(t) divided by (1 + exp(t)) to the power 2 and is strictly positive; tq − Λ(t) is therefore strictly concave. Its derivative vanishes when exp(t) = q / (1 − q). Since q = 3/4 exceeds p, the optimal parameter t* = ln(3) is positive. The star marks this optimal choice.

    I(q)=qln(2q)+(1−q)ln(2(1−q))=34ln(3)−ln(2)

    The first expression is obtained by replacing t with ln(q / (1 − q)) in tq − Λ(t), then combining the logarithms. The last expression is its exact value for our threshold. I(q) is a dimensionless logarithmic cost per draw; it is not a probability.

  5. Evaluate the upper bound without rounding logarithms

    For t* = ln(3), the weight exp(t*) is 3 and the expected weight for a single draw is (1 + 3) / 2 = 2. For 8 independent draws, its expectation is 256. On the event of at least 6 red outcomes, the weight is at least 3 to the power 6 = 729. This gives the following division, which is entirely exact.

    P(A8)≤B8=2836=256729≈0.351

    Bn now denotes the upper bound exp(−nI(q)). Check: 37/256 is less than 256/729. The bound is approximately 0.351, compared with 0.145 for the probability, both dimensionless. The upper bound therefore does not establish equality between them.

    Over a block of 4 draws, exp(−4I(q)) = 16 / 27 = 16/27, which is less than 1. For every n that is a multiple of 4, Bn = (16/27) to the power (n / 4). This gives exponential decay and confirms I(q) > 0.

  6. Prove that the bound has the correct exponent

    An upper bound alone could decay too slowly. To construct a lower bound, temporarily assign an auxiliary distribution Q to the same colour sequences. Under Q, the draws remain independent but the probability of red is q. This distribution is a tool for the proof; the probability we seek is still the one under the original urn distribution P.

    Set k = nq, an integer. For any sequence with exactly k red outcomes, the ratio of its weight under P to its weight under Q is (1/2) to the power n, divided by q to the power k and (1 − q) to the power (n − k). Taking its logarithm gives −nI(q). This ratio is therefore Bn for all these sequences, and summing their weights gives P(Sn = k) = Bn Q(Sn = k).

    Here, a probability mass means the probability of a specific value of the count. Under Q, the mass at k = nq is maximal among the n + 1 possible values of Sn. To check this, take j as a possible number of red outcomes: the ratio of the mass at j + 1 to the mass at j is (n − j)q / ((j + 1)(1 − q)). It decreases with j, exceeds 1 when moving from k − 1 to k, and becomes less than 1 when moving from k to k + 1. The maximal mass is at least 1 / (n + 1), since all the masses sum to 1.

    Bnn+1≤P(Sn=nq)≤P(An)≤Bn

    For our sequence, n + 1 = 9: the lower bound is 256/729 / 9 = 256/6561. Independent check by counting: 256/6561 ≤ 37/256 ≤ 256/729. The rounded values are respectively 0.039, 0.145, and 0.351, all dimensionless.

  7. Interpret the limit without claiming asymptotic equivalence

    Apply −ln to the preceding bounds, then divide by n. The minus sign reverses the inequalities. The difference between the resulting bounds is ln(n + 1) / n, which tends to 0.

    I(q)≤−lnP(An)n≤I(q)+ln(n+1)n
    limn→∞,n∈4ℕ−lnP(An)n=I(q)

    ℕ denotes the natural numbers; here, the limit is taken along their positive multiples of 4, as stated earlier. We can also write P(An) = exp(−nI(q) + o(n)): o(n) denotes a remainder whose ratio to n tends to 0. This is a large deviations result for this binomial tail with a fixed threshold.

    This does not assert that P(An) / Bn tends to 1. A polynomial factor can remain and disappear after taking the logarithm and dividing by n. As with Laplace’s method, the dominant scale and relative accuracy are separate questions. The result obtained here rests on the stated distribution and independence; it does not establish any frequency for a real urn.

Explain it in your own words

Start from the urn and justify, in order, the counting, the power of an expectation, the choice of parameter, and the lower bound. Which step establishes a logarithmic limit rather than just an upper bound?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The exponential bound is the probability of the event.”

    Counting gives 37/256, whereas the bound is 256/729. Keep the ≤ sign. To obtain the logarithmic limit, we also need the lower bound; even this limit does not give a ratio tending to 1.

    Review the counting and the logarithmic bounds

  • “If I lengthen the sequence, I keep the threshold of 6 red outcomes.”

    The large deviation studied keeps the proportion q = 3/4. At n = 12, the threshold becomes 9 red outcomes. In contrast, the fixed count 6 eventually falls below the mean np as n grows.

    Return to the question with a fixed proportion

  • “Each draw has the correct probability, so I can multiply the expectations.”

    Identical marginal probabilities do not prove independence. If all colours copy the same first draw, the event keeps probability 1/2 for every sample size: exponential decay with a positive rate disappears.

    Examine the sequence of copied colours

Exercise 1 · Application with justification

Recover the bound from the weight

Hide the worked steps. For n = 8 and q = 3/4, take the weight 3 to the power Sₙ. Calculate its expectation, its minimum on Aₙ, and then the bound. Is the number obtained the exact probability?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

On each draw, the weight is 1 or 3, each with the same probability 1/2.

Hint 2

On Aₙ, Sₙ is at least 6. Compare the weight with 3 to the power 6.

Compare with the solution

On each draw, the expectation is (1 + 3) / 2 = 2. Independence then gives 2 to the power 8 = 256 for the expectation of the product. On An, the weight is at least 3 to the power 6 = 729.

The total expectation is therefore at least 729P(An), giving P(An) ≤ 256 / 729 = 256/729. Exact counting gives 37/256, which is strictly smaller. This operation bounds a probability; it does not replace the bound with an equality.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify the power using independence.
  • Divide the expectation by the minimum weight on the event.
  • Keep an inequality and distinguish 37/256 from 256/729.

Exercise 2 · Transfer with the same proportion threshold

Lengthen the sequence of urn draws

Move to n = 12 while keeping q = 3/4. Calculate the new threshold, the bound Bₙ, and then the exact probability by counting sequences. Compare the ratio of the bounds with what you could claim about the ratio of the probabilities.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The threshold is nq. For the bound, keep t* = ln(3).

Hint 2

Count the blue positions: there can be from 0 to 3. The sequences remain equally likely.

Compare with the solution

The threshold is 12 × 3/4 = 9 red outcomes. The expectation of the weight is 2 to the power 12 = 4096; its minimum on the event is 3 to the power 9 = 19683. The bound is therefore 4096/19683, approximately 0.208, dimensionless.

With 3 blue outcomes, the number of sequences is 12 × 11 × 10 / (3 × 2 × 1) = 220. With 2 blue outcomes, it is 12 × 11 / 2 = 66. Add 12 sequences with 1 blue outcome and 1 sequence with no blue outcomes: 220 + 66 + 12 + 1 = 299 favourable sequences.

The exact probability is 299 / 4096 = 299/4096, approximately 0.073, dimensionless; it is indeed below 4096/19683. The bound has been multiplied by 16/27 because the sample size increased by 4. The formula for the bounds does not give this same ratio for the exact probabilities: their nonexponential factors still matter.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep q fixed and find 9, rather than keeping 6.
  • Obtain 4096/19683 as an upper bound.
  • Count the 299 sequences, then divide by 4096.
  • Apply the factor 16/27 only to the bounds.

Exercise 3 · Counterexample and diagnosis

All colours copied from the same draw

The setup now makes 1 draw from the same urn, then copies its colour to every position in the sequence. Each Xⱼ therefore equals the same variable Z. The marginal probability of red remains p = 1/2. For q = 3/4, calculate P(Aₙ) and its logarithmic rate, then identify the line of reasoning that fails.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The proportion Yₙ equals Z: it takes only the values 0 and 1.

Hint 2

Compare the expectation of the product with the power of the expectation of a single factor.

Compare with the solution

If Z = 1, all positions are red and An occurs; if Z = 0, An does not occur. Thus P(An) = 1/2 for every n. The rate −ln(1/2) / n tends to 0. The fixed deviation does not become rare as the sample size grows.

Independence fails: E[exp(tSn)] equals (1 + exp(tn)) / 2, whereas the proof for the independent urn used ((1 + exp(t)) / 2) to the power n. Markov’s inequality remains valid, but this factorisation is no longer justified. Correct marginal distributions are not enough to transfer the rate I(q) from the first model.

Check your reasoning

  • Find the constant probability 1/2 and the limiting rate 0.
  • Identify the factorisation of expectations as the invalid step.
  • Retain Markov’s inequality, which does not require independence.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What stays fixed in our large deviation as n increases?

The distribution of the draws and the threshold proportion q = 3/4, hence the difference q − p = 1/4. The required number of red outcomes grows with n; it does not stay equal to 6.

Recall 2Why introduce exp(tSₙ), and where does independence enter?

The weight increases with Sₙ for positive t; its minimum on the event gives the Markov upper bound. Independence then allows us to write the expectation of the product as the product of the expectations.

Recall 3How do we choose t and interpret I(q) in our model?

Maximise tq − Λ(t). The condition Λ′(t*) = q gives t* = ln(3), then I(q) = (3/4)ln(3) − ln(2). This rate is a dimensionless logarithmic cost per draw, distinct from a probability.

Recall 4Why is the upper bound alone insufficient to establish the logarithmic limit?

It gives only one side of the bounds. The lower bound Bₙ / (n + 1) gives the other; after applying −ln and dividing by n, the difference ln(n + 1) / n tends to 0.

Recall 5Does the limit −ln P(Aₙ) / n = I(q) imply P(Aₙ) / Bₙ → 1?

No. It fixes the dominant exponent. A polynomial factor can persist while disappearing in the logarithm divided by n. Our calculation at n = 8 already distinguishes 37/256 from 256/729.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the formulas, reconstruct the sequence: proportion far from its mean → exponential weight → upper bound and optimal choice of parameter → rate → lower bound under an auxiliary distribution → logarithmic bounds. Then explain why this limit leaves relative accuracy unresolved, and name the assumption that the copied colours violate.
