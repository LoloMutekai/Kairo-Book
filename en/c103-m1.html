Level 3 · C103·M1

Initialization

Your goalChoose the weight variance of a dense layer so that the signal maintains a stable variance at the start of training, then justify this choice, its assumptions and its limits.

Before you start
c102-m5 : finite difference check · c099-m5 : probability calibration · c067-m5 : pipeline

✿01 See the idea

A dense network is a stack of layers. Each layer mixes its inputs using weights, then passes the result to the next layer. Before the first training step, these weights are randomly drawn numbers: this is initialization. It is more than a tuning detail. It sets the scale of the signal passing through the stack, and therefore also the scale of the gradients that training will receive at the start.

Think of a chain of amplifiers. If each one amplifies a little too much, the signal explodes after a few stages; if each one attenuates a little too much, it dies away. At the start, a classifier with enormous outputs reports extreme probabilities without having learned anything, which is the opposite of the calibration seen earlier. The idea is therefore to set the amplification of each layer around 1.

Two points help avoid a bad start. First, equal weights, including 0 weights, make the neurons in a layer interchangeable: randomness serves to differentiate them. Second, this randomness must be reproducible: in a pipeline, set and record a seed so that exactly the same draw can be repeated.

Our only example is fictional: 3 dense layers of the same width 100, inputs with variance 1, no physical units and no activation function. Activations are covered in the next unit of the chapter. These values describe a mathematical exercise; they are neither a measurement nor a recipe validated on data.

The rule to remember

A linear dense layer multiplies the signal variance by n × σ², where n is the number of inputs and σ² is the variance of each weight. Choosing σ² equal to 1 / n aims for a gain equal to 1; draw the weights randomly, with 0 mean and independently, never all equal.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Three layers of width 100: two ways to draw the weights

A fictional stack has 3 linear dense layers, each with 100 inputs. The inputs have variance v equal to 1. Compare the output variance when the weights have variance σ² = 1, then when it equals 1 / n.

  1. Define the symbols and assumptions

    An output z of the layer is the sum of n products, with no bias or activation:

    z=∑i=1nwixi

    Here x denotes an input, w a weight, and n the number of inputs to the layer. σ² is the common variance of the weights, v the variance of the inputs and V that of z. Everything is unitless in this exercise; with an input expressed in a unit u, v and V would be in u² and σ² would remain unitless.

    Assumptions: the weights are drawn independently of one another, with 0 mean; they are independent of the inputs; the inputs have 0 mean. The inputs do not need to be independent of one another. Without these assumptions, the following rule must be reconsidered.

  2. Calculate the variance of an output

    Take a term w x. Since w and x are independent, the mean of (w x)² is the mean of w² multiplied by that of x², giving σ² × v. The term has 0 mean, so its variance is σ² v.

    For two different terms, the product of the two weights has 0 mean because the weights are independent and have 0 mean: the cross terms disappear. The variance of the sum is the sum of the variances of its n terms:

    V=n×σ2×v

  3. Move to a stack: the gains multiply

    Let g = n σ² be the variance gain of a layer. The output has 0 mean, so the mean of its square is V. The weights of the next layer are drawn independently of this output: the same rule applies, with V replacing v. After L layers:

    VL=gL×v

    This result describes the variance at the time of the draw, for a linear stack. It says nothing about subsequent training or the effect of activations.

  4. First draw: σ² = 1

    The gain is 100 × 1 = 100. The successive variances are therefore 1 × 100 = 100, then 100 × 100 = 10000, then 10000 × 100 = 1000000.

    For the standard deviation, which returns to the scale of the inputs, take the square root: the square root of the gain is 10, since 10 × 10 = 100. After 3 layers, the standard deviation is multiplied by 10 × 10 × 10 = 1000. An output of this size would saturate a classifier from the start.

  5. Second draw: σ² = 1 / n

    σ2=1n=0.01

    The gain becomes 100 × 0.01 = 1. The variances remain 1, 1 and then 1: the stack does not distort the scale at the start. The standard deviation of the weights is the square root of 0.01, giving 0.1, since 0.1 × 0.1 = 0.01.

    For layers of the same width, the Glorot rule σ² = 2 / (n + m) gives this choice again, with m the number of outputs: 100 + 100 = 200, and 2 / 200 = 0.01. Cases with unequal widths are the subject of the last exercise.

  6. Check using limiting cases and enumeration

    If σ² were 0, the gain would be 100 × 0 = 0 and the output variance 0 × 1 = 0: the signal dies away, and all neurons in a layer are identical. At the other extreme, the first draw shows the explosion.

    A reading pitfall: drawing weights with a standard deviation equal to 1 / n instead of a variance. The variance would then be (1 / n)² = 0.0001, the gain 0.01 and the variance after 3 layers 0.000001: the signal dies away.

    To cross-check the rule without reusing it, calculs.py enumerates all cases of a tiny network where each weight is plus or minus the same amplitude and each input is plus or minus 1, then compares the mean of the squares with n σ² v. In a real network, a finite difference check of the gradient also verifies that the resulting scale is the one the code calculates.

Explain it in your own words

Why do layer gains multiply instead of adding up? And what assumption about the weights must be stated for the cross terms to disappear?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Setting everything to 0 is the most neutral choice.”

    With σ² equal to 0, the gain is 0: the output is 0, and equal weights produce identical neurons, which receive the same inputs and, in a symmetric network, the same gradient. Randomness with 0 mean serves to break this symmetry.

    Reread the intuition about symmetry

  • “Small weights are always more cautious.”

    A gain below 1 accumulates from one layer to the next: with a standard deviation set to 1 / n, the variance falls to 0.000001 after 3 layers. Cautious means close to 1, rather than as small as possible.

    Revisit the check using limiting cases

  • “σ = 1 / n and σ² = 1 / n are the same rule.”

    σ² is a variance, and σ is its standard deviation. For n = 100, σ² = 0.01 corresponds to σ = 0.1; taking σ = 0.01 gives σ² = 0.0001. Always check whether the formula refers to a variance or a standard deviation.

    Redo the exercise on input width

Exercise 1 · Explained application

A wider layer

A fictional layer receives n = 400 inputs with variance 1. What weight variance σ² keeps its gain equal to 1? Also give the corresponding standard deviation.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write the gain n × σ² = 1 and isolate σ².

Hint 2

The standard deviation is the square root of the variance: find a number whose square equals σ².

Compare with the solution

The gain is n × σ². Setting it to 1 gives σ² = 1 / 400 = 0.0025. Check: 400 × 0.0025 = 1.

The standard deviation is 0.05, since 0.05 × 0.05 = 0.0025. With σ² = 1, the gain would be 400 and the output standard deviation would be multiplied by 20 at each layer. The wider the layer, the smaller σ² must be.

Check your reasoning

  • Solve for a gain equal to 1, without reusing the value from the lesson.
  • Distinguish variance from standard deviation, with a check by squaring.
  • State that σ² decreases as n increases.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis

A colleague’s choice

A colleague sets σ² = 0.02 for layers of width 100, with an input variance of 1, in a linear stack. Calculate the gain, the variance after 3 layers and then after 6 layers, and say whether you find the choice suitable. All values are fictional.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate the gain n × σ², then multiply it as many times as there are layers.

Hint 2

For 6 layers, reuse the result after 3 layers: 6 layers are two stacks of 3 layers.

Compare with the solution

Gain: 100 × 0.02 = 2. Variances: 1 × 2 = 2, then 4, then 8 after 3 layers. After 6 layers: 8 × 8 = 64, since the input variance is 1.

The gain is greater than 1: the variance doubles at each layer and the growth is geometric. This choice is unsuitable for a linear stack. It could be considered with certain activations, the subject of the next unit, but that is outside our case here.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the gain before calculating the stack.
  • Show the repeated multiplication of the gains.
  • Draw a conclusion about the gain relative to 1, recalling the linear stack assumption.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

A layer that narrows

A fictional layer goes from n = 100 inputs to m = 50 outputs. The forward gain is n σ²; in backpropagation, the gradient received by an input sums m terms, so the backward gain is m σ². Compare σ² = 1 / n and σ² = 2 / (n + m).

σ2=2n+m
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For each choice of σ², calculate the forward gain n σ² and the backward gain m σ².

Hint 2

Compare each gain with 1: which of the two choices is perfect in one direction and imperfect in the other?

Compare with the solution

With σ² = 1 / n = 0.01: forward gain 1, backward gain 50 × 0.01 = 0.5. The forward variance is preserved, and the backward variance is divided by 2.

With σ² = 2 / (100 + 50) = 2 / 150 = 1/75: forward gain 100 × 1/75 = 4/3 and backward gain 50 × 1/75 = 2/3. Check: their ratio is 2, that is, n / m.

The compromise is perfect in neither direction when the widths differ: it distributes the deviation instead of concentrating it on one side. For equal widths, it gives 1 / n again. These results are gains at the time of the draw, rather than a guarantee about training.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate both gains for each choice.
  • Explain why the backward gain depends on m.
  • State that the compromise does not set both gains to 1 if n and m differ.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the variance gain of a linear dense layer with n inputs whose weights have variance σ²?

It is n × σ²: the output variance is n × σ² × v, with v the variance of the inputs.

Recall 2What assumptions justify V = n σ² v?

Weights with 0 mean and the same variance, drawn independently of one another and of the inputs; inputs with 0 mean; no bias or activation.

Recall 3Why avoid equal weights, including 0 weights?

The neurons in a layer would remain interchangeable. A random draw with 0 mean differentiates them; 0 also makes the output 0.

Recall 4In σ² = 1 / n, which quantity is equal to 1 / n?

The variance of the weights. The standard deviation is its square root; setting it equal to 1 / n would give a variance that is far too small.

Recall 5What does σ² = 2 / (n + m) make a compromise between when n and m differ?

The forward gain n σ² and the backward gain m σ²: neither is exactly equal to 1, but their deviation is distributed between the two directions.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define n, σ², v and V; state the assumptions of 0 means and independence; calculate the variance of an output n σ² v; multiply the gains from layer to layer; choose σ² to aim for a gain equal to 1; distinguish variance from standard deviation; check using limiting cases and enumeration; set and record the seed for the draw.
