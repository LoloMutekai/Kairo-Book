Level 2 · C064·M6

Bounds, logit and comparison with an EMA

Your goalCompare a direct update, a logit update and an exponential moving average for the same fictional fill level, stating what each guarantees.

✿01 See the idea

A tank has a fill gauge: empty corresponds to 0, full to 1. Before a new reading, you estimate its fill level at 0.10 of its capacity. The gauge reports 0.80. The values are fictional; the true fill level is not given. You need to combine this information without confusing an admissible value with an accurate one.

One rule moves the estimate directly towards the reading. If it produces a value outside the bounds, you can move it back to the boundary: this is clamping, or clipping. It fixes the displayed value, but does not reconstruct the uncertainty that accompanies it.

Another rule changes scale before combining. The logit stretches fill levels near empty or full onto an unbounded line. Its inverse then maps any finite value strictly between empty and full. The midpoint on this stretched line generally does not correspond to the midpoint of the fill levels.

The exponential moving average, abbreviated EMA, keeps a fixed weight for each new reading. Kalman filtering calculates this weight from the uncertainties in the model. Here we will compare their operations; without a true reference value, we cannot identify the best estimate.

The rule to remember

State the scale and assumptions before comparing numbers: clamping an output, transforming a model and fixing a weight are distinct choices.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The gauge reports a much higher fill level

At the same fictional instant, compare the methods, starting each time from the prediction p⁻ = 0.10 and the reading z = 0.80. The direct comparison uses P⁻ = R = 0.01 in squared fractions of capacity. The EMA uses α = 0.50. The logit variant will have its own uncertainty model.

  1. Define the quantities being compared

    p denotes the unknown true fraction of capacity in [0, 1]; p⁻ is its predicted value, z the reading and p⁺ an estimate after the update. The signs − and + mean before and after the reading. These fractions have no physical unit: 1 represents the entire capacity, rather than an amount in litres.

    P⁻ is the error variance assigned to the prediction; R is the variance of the measurement noise. Their unit is the squared fraction of capacity. K is the dimensionless gain. We use the direct observation again, with coefficient H = 1 and independent errors with mean 0. The Gaussian formulas on the direct scale are an approximation here: a Gaussian distribution with positive variance always extends beyond the physical interval.

    For the EMA, m is the stored value before the reading and m⁺ the value afterwards; here m = p⁻. The dimensionless α is the fixed weight for the new observation. All methods receive the same reading; none is given the true fill level.

  2. Calculate the direct update

    K=P−P−+R;p+=p−+K(z−p−)

    Adding the variances gives 0.01 + 0.01 = 0.02; dividing the prediction variance by this sum gives K = 1/2 = 0.50. The reading exceeds the prediction by 0.80 − 0.10 = 0.70 of capacity. We retain 0.50 × 0.70 = 0.35, then add this change: 0.10 + 0.35 = 0.45 of capacity.

    Check: the result is between 0.10 and 0.80. Indeed, p⁺ = (1 − K)p⁻ + Kz is an average with positive weights whose sum is 1. Here, clamping changes nothing. The updated variance in the direct model is (1 − 0.50) × 0.01 = 0.005 in squared fractions of capacity. This is not a measured actual error.

  3. Calculate the EMA and explain the equality

    m+=(1−α)m+αz

    The retained part is 0.50 × 0.10 = 0.05; the new part is 0.50 × 0.80 = 0.40. Their sum is 9/20 = 0.45 of capacity. This is exactly the direct update: we chose α = K and m = p⁻, on the same scale.

    For the Kalman mean to reproduce an entire EMA sequence, we also need the same initialization, the same observations, a transition that preserves the mean (F = 1) and the same fixed gain at every step. In general, K changes with P⁻ and R; the EMA with fixed α does not, and its recurrence alone does not propagate a variance. Equality at this step therefore does not prove that the methods are identical in general.

  4. Understand what clamping fixes

    b(a)=max(0,min(1,a))

    a denotes a calculated candidate and b its clamped value. To test the same display, suppose another setting produces a candidate of 1.10 of capacity. This is not the result of the previous data: a convex average of our inputs strictly inside the bounds cannot produce it. Taking the minimum with 1 brings it down to 1, then taking the maximum with 0 keeps this value.

    We have removed 0.10 from the value. We have neither learned the true fill level nor recalculated an error distribution. If the program keeps the variance unchanged, its being positive is not enough to make it consistent with this new value. Clamping a mean is not equivalent to correctly conditioning a distribution to remain in the interval; even a mean inside the interval can accompany a Gaussian distribution that extends beyond it.

  5. Introduce an unbounded coordinate

    For a fill level p strictly inside the bounds, its odds o are the dimensionless ratio p/(1 − p). The logit s is the natural logarithm ln of these odds. ln and exp are inverse functions: exp(ln(o)) = o for positive o. The function σ, called the sigmoid, maps the real coordinate s back to the fill level.

    s=ln(p1−p);σ(s)=exp(s)1+exp(s)

    As p tends to 0, s tends to negative infinity; as p tends to 1, s tends to positive infinity. For finite s, exp(s) is strictly positive and less than 1 + exp(s), so 0 < σ(s) < 1. The inverse mapping therefore never reaches the exact bounds in real arithmetic. Rounding in a program requires its own checks.

    The exact logit is undefined at the bounds. One possible numerical convention replaces the input with min(1 − ε, max(ε, p)), with ε strictly between 0 and 1/2. If we choose the fictional value ε = 0.01, empty becomes 0.01 and full becomes 0.99. This modifies the input and must be stated and justified according to the reading. A truly empty tank does not suddenly become partly full: an exact observation at a bound may require another model.

  6. Combine in logit and map back exactly

    Let s⁻ denote the predicted logit and u the logit of the reading. For this variant, we assume a linear model with direct observation of s and independent Gaussian errors with mean 0 on this scale. Its predicted variance Ps⁻ and measurement variance Rs each equal 1 in squared logit coordinates. Their values are chosen for the exercise, separately from the direct variances. Their equality gives Ks = 0.50 and Ps⁺ = 0.50 in squared logit coordinates.

    The odds are o⁻ = 0.10/(1 − 0.10) = 1/9, then o_z = 0.80/(1 − 0.80) = 4. Thus s⁻ = ln(1/9) and u = ln(4). No approximate logarithm is needed.

    s+=ln(19)+ln(4)2=ln(49)=ln(23)

    Why a square root? The sum of the logarithms is the logarithm of the product; dividing this logarithm by 2 takes the positive square root of that product. Exact check: 2/3 × 2/3 = 4/9. The updated odds are therefore 2/3.

    p+L=σ(s+)=231+23=25=0.40

    L indicates the logit variant. The denominator is 5/3; the inverse mapping gives 0.40 of capacity, between our inputs. Inverse check: 2/5/(1 − 2/5) gives 2/3 again. The direct EMA is higher by 0.05 of capacity: the same weight, applied in a different space.

  7. Keep uncertainty on the correct scale

    Ps⁺ describes dispersion in squared logit coordinates. It is not a fill variance to copy alongside the mapped value 0.40. σ(s⁺) is the inverse transformation of the central logit estimate; it is generally not the mean of the transformed distribution, because transforming a mean and averaging transformed values are different operations.

    Similarly, additive Gaussian noise on the direct reading generally remains neither additive nor Gaussian after the logit. Our variant assumes noise on the transformed scale; it does not follow automatically from the direct model. The bounds are respected by construction, but the quality of the model and its variances still needs checking.

    A real comparison of the methods would require an independently obtained fill reference, the same times and initializations, and settings chosen before a comparison period kept separate from tuning. We would then compare the errors and behaviour near the bounds. Here, the calculated gap is a difference between outputs, rather than a gain in accuracy.

  8. Also check the meaning of the transition

    The random walk in the model preserves the predicted mean: p⁻ = previous p for F = 1; process noise can nevertheless increase its variance. A damped transition multiplies this mean by a factor F between 0 and 1, pulling it towards empty on the direct scale. F is dimensionless; we are discussing the mean here, rather than a validated physical flow rate.

    Applying the same factor to s gives s⁻ = F previous s. This pulls s towards 0, whose inverse is 0.50 of capacity, rather than towards empty. With F = 1/2, the last exercise calculates this difference. The choice of scale therefore also changes the meaning of the dynamics, as well as the update.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Reconstruct the direct result, then the value mapped back from logit using the odds. Explain why their difference does not identify a winner and why a logit variance cannot be labelled as a fill variance.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The value is within the bounds, so its uncertainty is correct.”

    Clamping only handles the value. Identify the distribution and the scale of its variance; a clamped mean and an unchanged variance do not constitute a correct probabilistic update through this operation alone.

    Review “Understand what clamping fixes”

  • “Same weight, same answer: the logit is just another display.”

    The weight acts before the return to the fill scale. Recalculating the odds gives 0.40 instead of 0.45. You need to transform the inputs, combine their logits, then map back, and state the noise model on the transformed scale.

    Review “Combine in logit and map back exactly”

  • “Kalman filtering and the EMA give the same result here, so they are always equivalent.”

    The equality uses the same starting point, the same scale and α = K. Change only the reading variance: the Kalman gain changes, while the EMA keeps its fixed weight. The exercise with the noisier gauge checks this.

    Review the exercise “The gauge becomes less reliable”

The gauge becomes less reliable

Restart from the beginning of the example, without continuing from its update: p⁻ = 0.10, z = 0.80, P⁻ = 0.01. The reading variance becomes R = 4 × 0.01 = 0.04 in squared fractions of capacity. Calculate K and the direct update. Compare with the EMA keeping α = 0.50. Can we identify the more accurate output?

Hint 1

The innovation is unchanged. First recalculate the denominator P⁻ + R.

Hint 2

Apply the new gain to the gap, then restart from the same initial state for the EMA.

Compare with the solution

The sum of the variances is 0.01 + 0.04 = 0.05. K = 0.01/0.05 = 1/5 = 0.20. The change is 0.20 × 0.70 = 0.14; the direct update gives 0.10 + 0.14 = 0.24 of capacity.

The EMA keeps 0.45, because its inputs and its fixed α are unchanged. The Kalman model gives less weight to the reading when R increases with P⁻ fixed. Both results are between the prediction and the reading. Since the true fill level is unknown, this test explains a response of the methods without proving which estimates it best.

Exercise 3 · Reasoned transfer

Towards empty or towards the midpoint?

The tank is estimated to be at 0.10. Without a new reading, compare only the mean predicted by p⁻ = Fp and the mean obtained by s⁻ = F logit(p), then mapped back through σ. Take F = 1/2 and ignore variance calculations here. The starting odds are 1/9. Why do these transitions not describe the same damping?

Hint 1

On the direct scale, multiply the fill level by F. On the logit scale, multiplying a logarithm by 1/2 amounts to taking the logarithm of a square root.

Hint 2

Find the positive square root of 1/9; then transform the resulting odds o into o/(1 + o).

Compare with the solution

Direct prediction: 1/2 × 1/10 = 1/20 = 0.05 of capacity. It moves closer to empty.

s−=ln(19)2=ln(13);σ(s−)=1343=14

Square root check: 1/3 × 1/3 = 1/9. Mapping back gives 0.25 of capacity, which moves closer to the midpoint 0.50. Indeed, damping s pulls it towards 0 and σ(0) = 1/2. ✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does clamping an estimate guarantee, and what still needs checking?

It guarantees a value within the chosen interval. It reconstructs neither the distribution nor its variance; an admissible output does not prove correct uncertainty or an accurate estimate.

Recall 2How do you map a fill level onto an unbounded line and then back?

For 0 < p < 1, form the odds o = p/(1 − p), then s = ln(o). Map back through σ(s) = exp(s)/(1 + exp(s)). For finite s, the result is strictly inside the bounds in real arithmetic. The bounds require a convention or another model.

Recall 3Why do logit variance and the transformed mean require separate interpretations?

The variance applies to the squared logit coordinate, rather than to the fill level. Mapping a logit mean back through σ(s⁺) generally does not give the mean after transformation. Additive Gaussian noise on one scale does not automatically retain that form on the other.

Recall 4Under what conditions does the Kalman mean follow the same sequence as an EMA with a fixed weight?

The same scale, the same observations, the same initialization, a mean transition F = 1 and a gain K equal to the fixed weight α at every step. Without these conditions, equality at a single step is not enough; the EMA alone does not propagate the Kalman variance.

Recall 5Why do our different outputs fail to prove any gain in accuracy?

The true fill level is not supplied. We need an independent reference, comparable conditions and initializations, settings fixed before the evaluation period and a stated error criterion. Respecting a bound or having a smaller variance is not enough.
