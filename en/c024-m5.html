Level 1 · C024·M5

Measurement quality

Your goalDistinguish the spread of repeated readings from their agreement with a reference, then limit what a correction allows you to conclude.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

Before weighing the flour for a recipe, you check your kitchen scale. You place, remove, then replace the same reference mass, whose value is 200 g. The scale displays 208 g, 210 g, and 212 g in succession. These readings are close together. Yet they are all above the reference: agreement among readings is not enough to establish agreement with what you are trying to measure.

Precision describes how close repeated measurements are under specified conditions. Here, the mass, the operator, and the tare setting stay the same, and you wait for the reading to stabilize. Trueness concerns the agreement of the mean with a reference. Accuracy concerns how close a result is to the value you are trying to find; a small spread does not guarantee it. These words describe aspects of quality, without forming a single score here.

A measurement error is a difference from the reference; uncertainty expresses the doubt that remains about the result. A known error in this exercise is therefore not a calculated uncertainty. The unit covers only a local check: it develops neither a confidence interval nor a complete calibration procedure.

All scale readings and mass values are fictional. The reference values are assumed to be exact for the calculations; a real reference also has uncertainty. The aim is to compare information, without claiming to characterize a real scale.

The rule to remember

Look separately at where the center lies, how much the readings vary, and which reference you can compare them with. A correction must then undergo a separate check.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Check the scale before making the recipe

The reference mass is r = 200 g. The readings, labeled a, b, and c in their order, are 208 g, 210 g, and 212 g. There are n = 3 readings, all retained. Let us calculate their center, their differences from r, and then the effect of a common correction.

  1. Define what is measured and how

    The quantity is the mass placed on the pan, expressed in grams, symbol g. r is the reference value; a, b, and c are the scale readings. All these quantities have the same unit. n counts the readings: it is a number without a physical unit. The mass stays constant, the pan is horizontal, the tare setting is retained, and readings are taken after stabilization.

    The display increment is h = 2 g: adjacent possible readings differ by h. This resolution does not guarantee an error smaller than h. Keep the unit, the reference used, and the reading conditions alongside the values. Without this information, an exact calculation can summarize a poorly defined measurement.

  2. Calculate the center while keeping the same question

    The mean m gives the arithmetic center of the readings. Adding the masses combines the readings; dividing by their count amounts to sharing the total equally. Dividing by n preserves the unit g. The partial sum is 208 + 210 = 418 g, then 418 + 212 = 630 g.

    m=a+b+cn=6303=210 g

    The center is 210 g, rather than the reference 200 g. Averaging only these readings introduces no information that could recover the reference. Writing 210.00 g would add digits to the presentation, without providing evidence of quality.

  3. Compare with the reference and retain the sign

    For any reading x, the signed difference e is x − r, in g. A positive sign means that the scale reads above the reference; a negative sign means below it. For readings a, b, and c: 208 − 200 = 8 g; 210 − 200 = 10 g; 212 − 200 = 12 g.

    Call the observed mean offset d. It is the difference between the center and the reference, rather than the spread around the center.

    d=m−r=210−200=10 g

    Check by another route: 8 + 10 = 18 g; adding 12 g gives 30 g. Dividing by 3 gives 10 g, the same as m − r. This offset in a short series suggests a shift to investigate; it proves neither its cause nor a constant bias across all weighings.

  4. Measure the observed spread

    Let E denote the range: the largest reading minus the smallest. Here the readings are already ordered, so E = c − a. It expresses the observed width of the group, in g.

    E=c−a=212−208=4 g

    The range is 4 g while the mean offset is 10 g: these numbers answer different questions. This range describes only the 3 readings; it does not bound the error of a future weighing. We cannot declare precision to be “sufficient” without specifying the intended use and its requirements.

  5. Try a local additive correction

    Let us test the hypothesis that the same offset d can be subtracted from the readings, under these conditions and near this mass. For a reading x, x′ = x − d denotes the corrected value; the prime is a label, rather than a derivative. The correction to add is therefore −d, because our readings are too high.

    We obtain a′ = 208 − 10 = 198 g; b′ = 210 − 10 = 200 g; c′ = 212 − 10 = 202 g. Their sum is calculated as 198 + 200 = 398 g, then 398 + 202 = 600 g. Their mean is 600 ÷ 3 = 200 g.

    The corrected differences from the reference are -2 g, 0 g, and 2 g, respectively. The mean difference is 0 on the data used to choose d: this follows from the construction and does not constitute independent validation. The raw readings and the correction rule must remain distinct.

  6. Check what the correction preserves

    The corrected range E′ is 202 − 198 = 4 g. We can check this without using the numbers: subtracting d from both extremes makes it cancel in their difference.

    E′=(c−d)−(a−d)=c−a=E

    The center has been brought back to the reference for this series; the observed spread is unchanged. The correction adds no new measurement and does not remove the doubt about d. Repeating a measurement can better document its variability; repeating the same setting fault does not make it disappear.

  7. Prepare a test that could contradict the rule

    Before using d for every mass in the recipe, a separate check is needed: a reference that was not used to calculate d, repetitions, and the same documented conditions. The final exercise provides such a test weighing. A failure is enough to reject the proposed generalization; a single success would not demonstrate quality across the full range of the scale.

    In this fictional check, we set a tolerance T = 2 g in advance: the corrected result is acceptable for the test weighing if its distance from the reference does not exceed T. This is a rule for the exercise, rather than a metrological norm or an uncertainty deduced from the display increment.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Why must the corrected mean equal the reference used to choose d? Then explain, using the drawing or the subtraction of the extremes, why this success does not bring the readings closer together.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The readings are close together, so the mass is correct.”

    Compare the center 210 g with the reference 200 g, then examine the range 4 g. Closeness among readings tells us about spread; it does not replace comparison with a reference.

    Recalculate the center, offset, and range separately

  • “With this display increment, the error cannot exceed 2 g.”

    The increment describes the available readings. Here, the reading 210 g is consistent with the stated increment while exceeding the reference by 10 g. Check the unit, conditions, and reference instead of inferring accuracy from the displayed number.

    Review the distinction between reading and reference

  • “I have subtracted the mean offset: the scale is validated across its whole range.”

    The return of the center to the reference is imposed by the choice of d for this series. Retain the residual spread and test the correction on another mass before extending its scope.

    Test the generalization in the final exercise

Exercise 1 · Identification and calculation

Which difference answers which question?

For the same reference mass 200 g, the readings are still 208 g, 210 g, and 212 g. Calculate the range and the signed error of the middle reading. Can we claim that the resolution 2 g bounds this error?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The range compares two readings; the error compares a reading with the reference.

Hint 2

Subtract the smallest reading from the largest, then subtract the reference from the middle reading.

Compare with the solution

Range: 212 − 208 = 4 g. Error of the middle reading: 210 − 200 = +10 g. The sign means that the reading is too high.

The increment 2 g does not bound this error: we observe a larger difference here. We have calculated a spread among readings and a disagreement with a reference; these are two different comparisons.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 4 g for the range and +10 g for the error.
  • Name the two terms compared in each subtraction.
  • Reject treating the display increment as a guarantee of accuracy.

Exercise 2 · Application with justification

The center returns, the differences remain

Without rereading the example, recover the offset d of the readings 208 g, 210 g, and 212 g relative to 200 g. Apply the common correction, then check the mean and the range. A classmate announces: “Every reading has become exact.” Respond using the corrected differences.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate d as the mean minus the reference; readings that are too high require subtracting d.

Hint 2

Compare each corrected result with the reference, then compare the corrected extremes with each other.

Compare with the solution

The mean is (208 + 210 + 212) ÷ 3 = 210 g. So d = 210 − 200 = 10 g. Subtracting d gives 198 g, 200 g, and 202 g.

Their sum is 600 g and their mean is 200 g. Their range remains 4 g. The correction shifts the values together: it cannot make them coincide.

The differences from the reference are -2 g, 0 g, and +2 g. Only the middle reading coincides with the reference here. A 0 mean difference does not mean that every difference is 0; moreover, these same readings were used to choose d.

Check your reasoning

  • Reconstruct d = 10 g and subtract this offset, using the correct sign.
  • Check the mean 200 g and the range 4 g separately.
  • Refute the statement using the corrected differences and point out that the data were reused.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

The same correction on another mass

Still before making the recipe, you place another reference mass on the scale, with a value of 300 g, which was not used to calculate d. The same scale displays 314 g. Apply the previously chosen correction d = 10 g, then test the tolerance T = 2 g. Can we accept this test weighing and generalize the correction to all masses? What should be examined next?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Do not recalculate d from this new reference: the aim is to test the rule that has already been fixed.

Hint 2

After subtracting d, compare the result with 300 g. The tolerance applies to the distance from this reference.

Compare with the solution

The corrected result is 314 − 10 = 304 g. Its difference from the new reference is 304 − 300 = +4 g. The distance is therefore 4 g, exceeding T = 2 g by 2 g. This test weighing fails the stated rule.

We therefore reject the claim that the correction is validated for all masses. This failure alone cannot distinguish between a fault that depends on the load, a change in conditions, or a fluctuation in the reading. Recalculating d from this weighing and then presenting it as its own validation would remove the test.

Next, repeat the checks, verify the tare setting and the stability of the conditions, retain the raw readings, and compare several references within the useful range. Real use must also take the quality of the references into account. This calculation decides whether this weighing passes the fictional rule; by itself, it does not characterize the entire scale.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the d that was already fixed and obtain 304 g, then +4 g.
  • Compare the distance with T and state the failure without shifting the tolerance.
  • Limit the conclusion to the observed test and propose a check of the conditions with repeated readings.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can readings that are close together still be offset?

They can cluster around a center far from the reference. Precision concerns the spread of repeated readings; trueness concerns the agreement of their mean with a reference.

Recall 2What does the display increment tell us, and what remains to be checked?

The increment separates adjacent possible readings. It does not bound the actual error: the unit, conditions, and agreement with a suitable reference must be checked.

Recall 3Why does subtracting the same offset preserve the range?

The offset is subtracted from both extremes, so it cancels in their difference. The center shifts, while the distances between readings stay the same.

Recall 4Why is the observed difference from the reference not the whole uncertainty?

The difference describes a disagreement that can be calculated using the chosen reference. Uncertainty expresses the remaining doubt about the result, including doubt related to the conditions and the reference; it is not calculated in this unit.

Recall 5What check avoids validating a correction through its own construction?

Test the previously chosen rule on separate reference measurements, with a requirement set before seeing the results. Agreement on the data that determined the rule is not enough to generalize it.
