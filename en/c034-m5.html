Level 1 · C034·M5

Average value

Your goalFind the constant flow rate that would fill a tank with the same volume over the same duration, then distinguish this average from an average of readings.

Before you start
c033-m5 : linear approximation · c034-m1 : Riemann sums · c034-m2 : integral · c034-m3 : fundamental theorem of calculus · c034-m4 : substitution

✿01 See the idea

A pump fills a tank. Its flow rate increases over time: it delivers little water at the start and more at the end. We observe only the filling between minute 1 and minute 4 after the pump starts. What constant flow rate would have delivered exactly the same volume during this window?

Imagine flattening the region under the flow rate curve without changing its area or moving the endpoints. The resulting rectangle has the same width as the observation window. Its height is the average flow rate we want: an equivalent height, not an extra volume.

In this fictional scene, the flow rate rises from 3 L/min to 18 L/min. We will find a constant height of 9 L/min over 3 min: it gives the same volume of 27 L. The diagrams use the same scales to allow this comparison.

We assume the tank is large enough, with no leaks or water flowing out, and that the flow rate model is known over the entire window. These numbers are used for calculation; they are not readings from a real pump. Before continuing, explain why an average flow rate must still be expressed in L/min.

The rule to remember

First accumulate over the entire window, then divide by its duration: the average is the constant height that preserves this accumulation.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Delivering the same volume at a constant flow rate

The pump follows a quadratic model: a base flow rate α = 2 L/min plus the square of time multiplied by β = 1 litre per minute cubed. Time t is measured from when the pump starts. We want the average flow rate between a = 1 min and b = 4 min, excluding the water delivered before a.

  1. Define the quantities and the window

    t is time, in minutes; q(t) is the flow rate at that instant, in litres per minute. α is a base flow rate; β multiplies the square of time, so its unit is the litre divided by the minute cubed. The product of β and the square of t is therefore in litres per minute. The exponents indicate powers.

    q(t)=α+βt2

    a and b are the time endpoints, with a < b. The duration D = b − a is 4 − 1 = 3 min. V will denote the volume received during this window alone, in litres. The bar above q in q̄ denotes the average value, in L/min.

    The model is continuous on the closed interval [a, b], so it is integrable; this is enough here to define its average. More generally, an integrable function on an interval of strictly positive length has an average value. We make no assumption that q is constant.

  2. Define the average by what it preserves

    The integral adds up the small volumes q(t) dt: dt represents a short duration, in minutes. It gives V. A constant flow rate q̄ over D would give q̄D; requiring the same volume imposes q̄D = V. Since D is positive, we can divide by D.

    V=∫abq(t) dt;q¯=Vb−a

    This is the definition of the average value of a function on an interval. In a Riemann sum, each flow rate is multiplied by the duration of its time slice; after division by D, the weights are the fractions of the total duration. A handful of readings without these durations does not automatically give this average.

    If a = b, the duration is 0 and this quotient is undefined. We then assign no average using this formula.

  3. Find an antiderivative and check it

    An antiderivative Q of q is a function whose derivative is q. Its unit is the litre: differentiating Q with respect to time gives L/min. Choose the antiderivative below; the additive constant can be 0, since it would cancel in Q(b) − Q(a).

    Q(t)=αt+βt33;Q′(t)=α+βt2=q(t)

    The prime indicates the derivative. The factor 3 from differentiating the cube cancels the divisor 3. This checks the antiderivative before we use it; we do not choose Q because its expression resembles q.

  4. Calculate the volume by subtracting endpoint values

    The fundamental theorem of calculus, studied earlier, lets us replace the integral with Q(b) − Q(a). Calculate with t measured in minutes and the given coefficients: at b, the linear term is 2 × 4 = 8 L; the cube is 64 minutes cubed, and its contribution is 64/3 L. So Q(b) = 88/3 L.

    At a, the linear term is 2 × 1 = 2 L; the cube is 1 minute cubed, and its contribution is 1/3 L. So Q(a) = 7/3 L. We must subtract what came before the window, even if the stopwatch started earlier.

    V=Q(b)−Q(a)=643+8−(2+13)=27 L

    The fractions have been kept exact: Q(b) = 88/3 L and Q(a) = 7/3 L. Only their difference is the volume for our window.

  5. Divide by the duration and check the rectangle

    q¯=273=9 L/min

    We divide litres by minutes to obtain a flow rate again. The reverse check gives 9 L/min × 3 min = 27 L: the rectangle does produce the required volume. Dividing by b would have confused an instant with a duration.

    Another check: the square of t increases between the positive endpoints a and b. The flow rate therefore stays between 3 and 18 L/min; the average 9 L/min does lie within this interval. These bounds are necessary, but they do not suffice to prove the exact result.

  6. Test the endpoint shortcut

    The arithmetic mean of just the initial and final flow rates would be (3 + 18)/2 = 21/2 L/min, or 10.50 L/min. It would produce 63/2 L, or 31.50 L, over 3 min. This volume exceeds our 27 L: the shortcut fails.

    The average of the endpoint values is exact for a flow rate that is an affine function of time, whose graph is a line segment. Our flow rate is quadratic: the segment joining its endpoints lies above the curve between them. It therefore does not give the same filling.

    The average flow rate describes the total accumulation, not how the filling unfolds. A pump with a truly constant flow rate of 9 L/min would deliver the same total over this window, but different intermediate volumes.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The integral is 27, so the average flow rate is 27 L/min.”

    The integral gives a volume of 27 L. Spread this volume over 3 min: the average is 9 L/min. Check the product average flow rate × duration before concluding.

    Review the definition and the rectangle check

  • “I divide by the final time, since that is when the filling ends.”

    The window starts at 1 min. Its length is 4 − 1 = 3 min. Draw the endpoints, then identify the width; a final time alone does not measure the observed duration.

    Find the width shared by both regions

  • “An average always means adding up the visible values and dividing by their number.”

    Each portion of time contributes to the volume. For windows with different durations, the averages must be weighted by their durations. Using only the endpoint flow rates gives 10.50 L/min here instead of 9 L/min.

Compare with the solution

At c = 2 min, the square of c is 4 minutes squared and its cube is 8 minutes cubed. The linear term is 4 L and the cubic term is 8/3 L. Thus Q(c) = 20/3 L.

The volume received is Q(c) − Q(a) = 20/3 − 7/3 = 13/3 L. The duration is 2 − 1 = 1 min. The average is therefore 13/3 L/min, or approximately 4.33 L/min.

The flow rate increases with time over our window of positive times: restricting the observation to its start should give an average below 9 L/min. This is indeed the case. This reasonableness check accompanies the exact calculation without replacing it.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · justify the weights

Two averages do not always carry the same weight

Split the window at c = 2 min. On [a, c], the average is 13/3 L/min over 1 min. On [c, b], it is 34/3 L/min over 2 min. Without integrating the entire curve again, find the overall average. Someone proposes the arithmetic mean of these two flow rates: use the volumes to respond to this objection.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A local average multiplied by its duration gives the volume for its window.

Hint 2

Add the volumes, then divide by the sum of the durations. The two windows do not have the same length.

Compare with the solution

First window: (13/3 L/min) × 1 min = 13/3 L. Second: (34/3 L/min) × 2 min = 68/3 L. These volumes cover consecutive windows, so we can add them.

The total volume is 13/3 + 68/3 = 27 L. The total duration is 1 + 2 = 3 min. Their quotient gives 9 L/min, as in the full example.

The duration weights are 1/3 and 2/3, with no unit; their sum is 1. The second average counts more because it represents a longer window. The proposed arithmetic mean would give 47/6 L/min, or approximately 7.83 L/min: it artificially assigns the same weight to the two durations.

Optional local check: Q(b) − Q(c) = 88/3 − 20/3 = 68/3 L; dividing by 2 min does give 34/3 L/min again. An unweighted average of the averages would work if the durations were equal.

Check your reasoning

  • Reconstruct each volume before combining the windows.
  • Obtain 27 L and 9 L/min.
  • Relate the weights 1/3 and 2/3 to the durations, then refute the unweighted average.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What equality defines the average flow rate over a window?

The average flow rate multiplied by the duration must give the volume received: q̄(b − a) = ∫ from a to b q(t) dt. We therefore divide the integral by b − a, with a < b and q integrable.

Recall 2Why do the integral of the flow rate and its average have different units?

The flow rate in L/min multiplied by a duration gives litres: the integral is a volume. Dividing by the duration of the window then gives L/min again for the average.

Recall 3Why are the initial and final flow rates alone not enough here?

They ignore the shape of the curve between the endpoints. Their average is exact for a flow rate that is an affine function of time; for our quadratic flow rate it gives 10.50 L/min, whereas the integral gives 9 L/min.

Recall 4How do you combine averages from windows with different durations?

Multiply each average by its duration to recover a volume. Add the volumes and divide by the total duration. The weights are the fractions of the total duration; they have no unit and their sum is 1.

Recall 5Do two flow rates with the same average give the same filling at every instant?

They give the same total volume over the same window. They can produce different intermediate volumes. The average does not preserve all the variations in flow rate.
