Level 4 · C233·M4

Intensity control

Your goalConstruct the jump term of an HJB equation, choose an intensity under constraints and check the local minimum without claiming unproved global optimality.

Before you start
c227-m5 : Ito–Tanaka · c059-m6 : deterministic discrete Bellman equation · c167-m5 : initial and boundary value problem · c184-m5 : Longstaff-Schwartz · c233-m1 : costs, constraints and admissibility · c233-m2 : HJB · c233-m3 : drift control

✿01 See the idea

A workstation in a workshop is waiting for a job to be completed. You can increase the effort to make completion more frequent, but you do not set its time. Once the job is finished, the workstation stays idle: no new arrival is planned in this scene.

Intensity describes an instantaneous frequency conditional on what is already known. Setting it to 2 per hour does not promise 2 completions over the next hour: here, the job can only be completed once. It adjusts the chance of finishing within a very short interval while the job is still pending.

Speeding up is useful only if the state reached after completion reduces future cost. We therefore compare the future cost while waiting with the future cost after the jump. We then need to balance this difference against the cost of effort.

All data are fictional. Credits are an accounting unit of cost invented for this exercise, with no real price. The future values supplied belong to a candidate to be examined, rather than a complete solution that has already been proved. The diagrams show the local calculation we will reconstruct.

The rule to remember

Multiply the difference “after the jump minus before the jump” by the intensity; add the cost of effort; minimize while respecting the bounds.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Choosing effort while the workstation waits

At the time being studied, the candidate has value v(t, 1) = 6 credits while waiting and v(t, 0) = 2 credits once idle. The waiting cost is c = 5 credits per hour. Effort costs κ × λ × λ/2 credits per hour with κ = 2 credit-hours; intensity λ must stay between 0 and 3 per hour. We will calculate only the control that minimizes the local part of the HJB equation for these values.

  1. Define the model before choosing a control

    t is the current time and T the end of the study, both in hours. X denotes the state, with no unit: 1 means waiting, 0 means idle. A completion takes X from 1 to 0. The idle workstation is absorbing: its state no longer changes and effort there is 0.

    The control λ, in events per hour, is nonnegative, bounded by λmax = 3 per hour and chosen using information available before any event occurs. It uses no knowledge of the future event time. We assume that this jump mechanism is valid and that the state is observed without error.

    δt is a positive duration that tends to 0. Conditional on the past and the workstation still waiting, the probability of a completion during δt is λδt + o(δt). The remainder o(δt), dimensionless like the probability, becomes negligible relative to δt in the limit. The product λδt is therefore a local approximation; it is not an exact probability for an arbitrary interval.

    The total cost adds up the waiting cost c(X) and the effort cost κ × λ × λ/2 until T, then adds the terminal cost g(X at T). Here c(1) = 5 credits per hour and c(0) = 0. g is a fixed function, in credits. V(t, x) is the smallest expected total cost among admissible controls; v(t, x) is a candidate for V. The expectation is over random completions.

    For the idle state, g(0) = 2 credits and no running cost is added. The waiting-state values of v are supplied to isolate the minimization, without claiming that they solve the problem over the entire time interval.

  2. Recover the jump term from an average

    During δt, the future cost remains that of waiting if nothing happens; it becomes that of the idle workstation if completion occurs. At a fixed time, the first-order average is therefore (1 − λδt)v(t, 1) + λδt v(t, 0). Subtracting v(t, 1), then dividing by δt, gives the jump term.

    Δv=v(t,0)−v(t,1)

    Δv, in credits, denotes the difference in value after and before the jump; Δ here is the symbol for a difference. Numerically: 2 − 6 = -4 credits. The corresponding rate is λΔv, in credits per hour. Its negative sign represents a reduction in expected future cost when completion becomes more likely.

    The jump is a full state change: we evaluate v in the other state. We do not replace this finite difference with a derivative with respect to x. Such an approximation would require a different model.

  3. Insert this difference into the HJB equation

    The passage of time adds ∂tv, the derivative of v with respect to t at a fixed state, in credits per hour. Set H(λ) = c + κ × λ × λ/2 + λΔv. H is the local cost rate that depends on the control; min means its smallest value among admissible intensities. For a candidate sufficiently differentiable in time, the Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman equation in the waiting state takes the following form.

    0=∂tv(t,1)+min0≤λ≤λmax[c+κλ22+λΔv]

    At T, the candidate must also agree with g. These conditions are part of the global problem; our table of values at a single time does not verify them. The minimization below is exact for the supplied H. The next unit will address the conditions that allow us to move from a candidate to a globally optimal control.

  4. Find the minimum, then respect the bounds

    κ is strictly positive. The slope of H with respect to λ is κλ + Δv; its second derivative is κ, which is positive. The slope is 0 at λlibre = −Δv/κ. This unconstrained intensity must then be brought into the admissible interval.

    λ⋆=min(λmax,max(0,−Δvκ))

    The star denotes the local minimizer of H. The inner max rules out a negative intensity; the outer min applies the upper limit. In our scene: λlibre = (6 − 2)/2 = 4/2 = 2 per hour. This value lies between 0 and 3 per hour: λ⋆ = 2 per hour.

    The waiting cost c and the time derivative do not depend on λ at this instant: they do not change the local minimizer. They are nevertheless needed in the HJB equation and influence future values in the full problem.

  5. Calculate the contributions with their units

    Effort: λ⋆ × λ⋆ = 2 × 2 = 4 in units of squared hourly frequency. Multiplying by κ and then dividing by 2 gives a cost of 2 × 4/2 = 8/2 = 4 credits per hour. The credit-hours unit of κ does turn a squared frequency into a cost rate.

    Jump: 2 × (-4) = -8 credits per hour. The effort plus jump part is 4 + (-8) = -4 credits per hour. Adding waiting gives H(λ⋆) = 5 + (-4) = 1 credit per hour.

    Check at the bounds: with no effort, H(0) = 5 credits per hour. At the upper limit, effort costs 2 × 3 × 3/2 = 9 credits per hour, the jump contributes 3 × (-4) = -12 credits per hour and H(3) = 5 + 9 + (-12) = 2 credits per hour. The interior choice gives a lower value than either bound.

  6. Check another way and clarify the scope

    Comparing only a few controls would not prove the minimum over the entire interval. Completing the square gives a proof independent of setting the slope to 0:

    H(λ)=κ2(λ+Δvκ)2+c−Δv22κ

    The square is nonnegative; it is 0 at our admissible λ⋆. This identity also explains why we choose the nearest bound when λlibre lies outside the interval. Each term remains a cost rate, even if a continuation contribution is negative.

    Check using a fictional small time step: take δt = 1/100 h, that is, 0.01 h. To first order, completion has weight 2 × 1/100 = 0.02 and waiting has weight 0.98, both dimensionless. The expected change in value due to the jump alone is 0.02 × (-4) = -0.08 credit. Waiting and effort contribute 0.05 + 0.04 = 0.09 credit. The local balance is 0.01 credit, equal to H(λ⋆)δt.

    These fractions are exact first-order calculations, rather than exact probabilities over a finite duration. The time variation of v and the neglected remainder are still missing from this balance: it demonstrates neither the full HJB equation nor any actual saving. An answer constructed after a hint remains assisted; rereading the solution does not establish that any level has been achieved.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “An intensity of 2 per hour is a probability of 2.”

    An intensity has units of frequency. Multiply it by a short duration to obtain a dimensionless first-order weight; explicitly retain the neglected remainder. An absorbing workstation can have at most one completion.

    Review what intensity controls

  • “I multiply the intensity by only the value after the jump.”

    Also subtract the value of the state being left. The branch weights must sum to unity: expanding their average gives λ[v after − v before]. Forgetting the subtracted term can reverse the choice.

    Reconstruct the average and its sign

  • “The slope is 0: the control must be allowed and globally optimal.”

    First check the bounds, as in the last exercise. The resulting control minimizes only H for the supplied candidate; the equation, terminal condition and verification conditions over the entire horizon still need to be addressed.

    Test controls at the bounds

The jump becomes less beneficial

At the same fictional workstation, replace only the candidate value once idle with 5 credits. The waiting value remains 6 credits. Predict the direction of the change in control, then calculate the admissible minimum. This is a new assumption about future cost, rather than an observed update.

Hint 1

Compare the reduction in future cost 6 − 5 with that in the example.

Hint 2

The unconstrained control is the reduction in future cost divided by κ; then apply the bounds.

Compare with the solution

The benefit of the jump falls to 6 − 5 = 1 credit. Paying for high effort becomes less worthwhile. The unconstrained control is 1/2 = 1/2 per hour, that is, 0.50 event per hour. It is admissible and therefore minimizes H.

We need the difference in values: using only the value 5 would assign more effort to a jump that has become less useful. The result applies to the supplied values; their global consistency remains a separate question.

Exercise 3 · Transfer under constraints

Reach the upper limit or use no effort

Still using the same model, examine these variants separately: a waiting value of 10 credits and an idle value of 2 credits; then a waiting value of 6 credits and an idle value of 7 credits. κ and the upper limit are unchanged. Give the unconstrained control, then the admissible control in each case; also justify the sign of the slope at the upper bound in the first variant.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A minimizer outside the interval tells you which bound to choose; it does not authorize exceeding that bound.

Hint 2

At the upper bound, the slope is κλmax minus the benefit of the jump.

Compare with the solution

First variant: the benefit is 10 − 2 = 8 credits. The unconstrained control is 8/2 = 4 per hour, above the upper limit of 3 per hour. The admissible control is therefore 3 per hour. At this bound, the slope is 2 × 3 − 8 = -2 credits: H is still decreasing as λ increases, but going further is prohibited.

Second variant: the benefit is 6 − 7 = -1 credit. The unconstrained control would be -1/2 per hour, that is, -0.50 per hour. A negative intensity is not admissible; the control becomes 0. The jump now increases future cost, and effort itself has a cost: the model chooses not to cause the event.

This is still a minimization for hypothetical future costs. To conclude that a policy is optimal for the entire problem, these values would still need to satisfy the conditions stated in the example.

  • Find the upper bound in the first variant and the lower bound in the second.
  • Justify the first choice using the slope of -2 credits and the constraint.
  • Refuse to turn a negative unconstrained control into a physical frequency.
  • Name the global proof that is still missing.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does an intensity mean while the workstation is still waiting?

A conditional frequency: intensity multiplied by a very short duration gives the completion probability to first order. It sets neither a date nor a certain number of events.

Recall 2Why does the jump term contain a difference in values?

The average combines the branch that stays with the one that changes state. Subtracting the current value leaves the intensity multiplied by the value after the jump minus the value before it.

Recall 3How do you choose the intensity with a quadratic cost and bounds?

Divide the negative of the difference in values by κ, then bring the result between the 0 bound and the upper limit. Convexity justifies the local minimum for the supplied values.

Recall 4Which units allow effort and jump contributions to be added?

Intensity is per hour and the difference in values is in credits: their product is in credits per hour. κ is in credit-hours, so κ times the squared intensity is also in credits per hour.

Recall 5What does the calculated minimum still fail to demonstrate?

It does not verify the value function over the entire horizon, the terminal condition or the conditions for global verification. Reading or an assisted answer also does not demonstrate independent recall.
