Level 3 · C075·M1

Baseline and declared hypothesis

Your goalFix a baseline and a measurable hypothesis before the comparison, then give a verdict that follows the announced criterion.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

In a workshop, the time needed to prepare an order is predicted from its number of items. The usual rule allows 2 min for setup, then 3 min per item. A candidate rule keeps the same setup time but allows 2 min per item. Predicting a shorter time does not make the workshop work faster: we are comparing prediction accuracy.

The reference rule, called the baseline in the title, is the rule against which the proposal is compared. Here, it is the usual rule, labelled R. It makes the question “better than what?” concrete. The candidate, labelled C, is not better just because of its name: we must observe its errors on the same orders.

Before seeing the times, the workshop writes down its hypothesis: the candidate must reduce the mean absolute error by at least 1 min on the held-out batch. An absolute error is the distance between the predicted time and the observed time; we take its magnitude, regardless of its direction. The mean gives each order the same weight.

The entire scene is fictitious: the rules, orders, times and threshold. The threshold represents the minimum improvement considered useful for this exercise; it does not come from any real measurement. The rules are assumed to have already been chosen through separate preliminary work. The held-out batch is used only to evaluate them, without adjusting them again.

The rule to remember

Before the results, write down the baseline, the candidate, the batch, the metric, the favourable direction and the threshold. After the results, apply exactly this contract and also retain an unfavourable verdict.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A more accurate prediction that misses the announced objective

The batch contains N = 4 complete orders, A to D, with 1, 2, 3 and 4 items. Let us compare the predictions from R and C with the fictitious times in the diagram. The item count is available before preparation; the observed time is never used to produce the prediction for that same order.

  1. Write down what could be contradicted

    The advance record identifies the exact formulas for R and C, the batch A to D, the definition of time in minutes, the mean absolute error, the equal weighting and the threshold δ = 1 min. Measurement starts when preparation begins and ends when the order is ready. All orders are complete; none will be removed after its result has been read.

    Declared operational hypothesis: “On this held-out batch, C will reduce the mean absolute error by at least δ compared with R.” It also specifies its failure condition: a reduction below δ does not meet the objective. Equality is included in “at least”.

    This hypothesis concerns a finite batch here. By itself, it is not a statistical test about all future orders. To preserve the sequence of events in real work, keep a dated version of the rules, the plan and the batch identification before consulting the results.

  2. Define the symbols and their units

    i denotes an order among A, B, C and D. nᵢ is its number of items; yᵢ is its observed time in minutes. Rᵢ and Cᵢ are the times predicted by the corresponding rules, also in minutes. a = 2 min is the fixed time; bᴿ = 3 min per item and bᶜ = 2 min per item are the coefficients already fixed.

    Ri=a+bRni;Ci=a+bCni

    N = 4 is the number of orders evaluated, a count with no physical unit. Lᴿ and Lᶜ will be the mean absolute errors of R and C, in minutes. Δ = Lᴿ − Lᶜ will be the reduction in error, in minutes; δ will be the minimum threshold, in the same unit. A positive reduction favours C; a reduction of 0 means that the means are equal.

    The superscript R or C identifies the rule; it is not a power. We assume that the reference times are comparable and correctly recorded. The predictions are evaluated without changing how the workshop is organised: this comparison measures no causal effect of a new organisation.

  3. Produce the predictions without looking at the actual time

    For R, multiplying the number of items by bᴿ gives the variable part, then adding a gives the predicted time. Order A: 3 × 1 = 3 min, then 2 + 3 = 5 min. Order B: 3 × 2 = 6 min, then 2 + 6 = 8 min.

    Order C: 3 × 3 = 9 min, then 2 + 9 = 11 min. Order D: 3 × 4 = 12 min, then 2 + 12 = 14 min. The sequence of predictions from R is therefore (5 ; 8 ; 11 ; 14) min, in order from A to D.

    For C, keep a and use bᶜ. The variable parts are 2 × 1 = 2, 2 × 2 = 4, 2 × 3 = 6 and 2 × 4 = 8 min. Adding 2 min to each gives (4 ; 6 ; 8 ; 10) min. None of these operations uses yᵢ.

  4. Measure the distances from the observed time

    The bars |…| denote the absolute value: the distance from 0 of the number between them. For an order, |Rᵢ − yᵢ| or |Cᵢ − yᵢ| measures the discrepancy in minutes. A prediction that is too short and one that is too long therefore do not cancel out in the mean.

    For R: A gives |5 − 4| = 1 min; B, |8 − 7| = 1 min; C, |11 − 9| = 2 min; D, |14 − 12| = 2 min.

    For C: A gives |4 − 4| = 0 min; B, |6 − 7| = 1 min; C, |8 − 9| = 1 min; D, |10 − 12| = 2 min. For the negative discrepancies from C, remove the sign after subtraction; do not retain a negative error.

    Local check: on D, R exceeds the time by 2 min and C underestimates it by 2 min. The predictions differ, but their distances from the observed time are equal.

  5. Add, then divide by the same number of orders

    For any rule M, where M represents R or C, Lᴹ is the sum of the distances from its predictions Mᵢ to the times yᵢ, divided by N. The notation ∑ means that we add over all orders in the batch:

    LM=∑i|Mi−yi|N

    R: orders A and B contribute 1 + 1 = 2 min of error; C and D contribute 2 + 2 = 4 min. Total: 2 + 4 = 6 min. Divide by 4 to obtain Lᴿ = 6/4 = 1.50 min per order.

    C: the same groupings give 0 + 1 = 1 min and 1 + 2 = 3 min. Total: 1 + 3 = 4 min. Thus Lᶜ = 4/4 = 1.00 min per order. The same denominator here ensures that each of the same orders has the same weight.

  6. Compare with the declared threshold without moving it

    Δ=LR−LC=1.50−1.00=0.50 min

    Subtracting in this direction makes a reduction in error positive. The declared criterion is Δ ≥ δ. Here 0.50 min is less than 1 min: there is a reduction on this batch, but the announced objective is not met. The margin Δ − δ = -0.50 min is negative.

    Keep the results and the initial threshold. Replacing δ with 0.50 min afterwards would make the criterion easier to satisfy, but would answer a new question. This new question can prepare another comparison, declared before new results.

  7. Check by another method and limit the verdict to what is shown

    Start again from the orders rather than the means. C reduces the error by 1 min on A, by 0 min on B, by 1 min on C and by 0 min on D. The total reduction in error is 6 − 4 = 2 min; its mean is 2/4 = 0.50 min. This gives exactly Δ again. It is prediction error avoided, not working time saved.

    Full verdict: “On the fictitious batch A to D, the mean errors are 1.50 min for R and 1.00 min for C. The reduction of 0.50 min remains below the advance objective of 1 min.” Nothing justifies replacing this verdict with “no progress” or “general improvement demonstrated”.

    The calculation is descriptive: it has no accompanying sampling model that could quantify uncertainty about future orders. Even meeting the threshold on this batch would demonstrate neither general reliability nor a right to modify a real system.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Reconstruct the declaration made before the results, the predictions, the distances, the means and the verdict. Why can a positive reduction in error leave the operational hypothesis unsatisfied? Why does this reduction not measure faster work?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The candidate is better because it predicts a shorter time.”

    A predicted time is a prediction. Measure its distance from the observed time. On D, R and C are wrong by the same amount in opposite directions; predicting less is therefore not enough.

    Review the distances and their check on D

  • “We will choose the baseline and the threshold once we have seen the results.”

    First write down the exact usual rule, the candidate, the batch, the metric and the threshold. A later change to this contract must be identified as a new question; it does not validate the initial declaration.

    Review the sequence of the declaration

  • “A lower mean error is enough to prove the hypothesis and replace the baseline.”

    Compare the reduction with the advance threshold. In this example it remains insufficient. Then distinguish the finding on the batch, the evidence about future cases and the decision to change the system: this calculation does not conflate them.

    Review the verdict and its scope

Compare with the solution

A sufficient declaration for the exercise is: “On the held-out batch A to D, C, with 2 min fixed and 2 min per item, will reduce the mean absolute error by at least 1 min compared with R, with 2 min fixed and 3 min per item. All orders will have the same weight.”

Fixing the rules, the batch identification, the definition of time, the metric and the threshold before the results prevents silently reformulating the objective to obtain a favourable verdict. If Δ is below 1 min, the criterion is not satisfied; if it is equal to it, the criterion is satisfied on this batch.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the baseline for in the workshop?

It gives meaning to “better than what”. Here R is the exact usual rule, with its parameters fixed; a baseline is not chosen after the results to favour C.

Recall 2What must be announced to make the hypothesis falsifiable on the batch?

Rules R and C, the held-out batch, the definition of the metric, the weights, the favourable direction and the threshold. The criterion Δ ≥ δ must be written before the results; Δ < δ is its failure condition.

Recall 3Why is Δ defined as Lᴿ − Lᶜ?

A smaller error is preferable: subtracting the error of C from that of R makes the reduction positive. Δ retains the unit of the mean errors, here the minute.

Recall 4Why does the workshop miss its objective despite the observed reduction?

The reduction is 0.50 min, below the declared minimum of 1 min. We retain both the favourable result on the batch and the failure to meet the initial criterion.

Recall 5What does the calculation on this page not prove?

It proves neither a general improvement on future orders, nor a faster workshop, nor a right to replace the rule. It compares prediction errors on a finite fictitious batch.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without support: why R is the baseline; what must be fixed before the times are revealed; how to go from predictions to distances and then to means; why a positive Δ can remain below δ; which sentence reports the result and its limits.
