Level 1 · C014·M1

Direct proof

Your goalProve that the sum of 2 even numbers is even by starting from the assumptions and justifying each equality.

Before you start
c012-m5 : counterexamples · c011-m5 : binomial

✿01 See the idea

You have socks already arranged in pairs on trays A and B. You bring the trays together without breaking up the pairs. Even before counting the socks again, you can see why the total can still be arranged in pairs: each original pair has stayed complete.

In our fictional scene, A holds 3 pairs and B holds 2. Each pair contains 2 socks. The batches are separate: no sock is counted in both A and B. As the batches are combined, no socks are added or removed. These conditions explain why we add the counts.

The drawing makes the grouping visible for these quantities. A proof must go further: it must explain why the same reasoning works for any allowed counts. We will therefore leave the numbers of pairs unspecified and call them a and b.

A direct proof starts from what is assumed to be true, the assumptions, and proceeds with explicit reasons to what we want to establish, the conclusion. Here, we assume that the starting counts are even; we must establish that their sum is even.

The rule to remember

Start from the assumptions, express them using a definition, transform the expressions through justified equalities, then recognize the conclusion.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From particular trays to a proof for all counts

Let us calculate the total of 3 pairs and 2 pairs, then write a justification that remains valid for arbitrary numbers of pairs. The numerical batch sizes on this page are fictional.

  1. Specify the domain, symbols, and goal

    The domain is the set of nonnegative integers: the counts allow 0 and the positive integers. Such a number is even if it can be written as 2 times a nonnegative integer. Being even therefore means being able to form complete pairs with no remainder.

    x and y denote the numbers of socks in batches A and B. The assumptions are "x and y are even." They provide nonnegative integers a and b such that x = 2a and y = 2b. In the scene, a and b count pairs; the factor 2 corresponds to 2 socks per pair.

    s denotes the total number of socks: s = x + y. The conclusion to establish is "s is even." The letters denote counts, with no physical unit of length or mass. We keep the words pairs and socks to specify what each number counts.

    x=2a;y=2b;s=x+y

  2. Count the socks in each batch

    For the particular calculation, choose a = 3 pairs and b = 2 pairs. Multiplying each pair count by 2 gives its sock count: x = 2 × 3 = 6 socks; y = 2 × 2 = 4 socks.

    We then add quantities of the same kind, since the batches are separate and combining them preserves every object: s = 6 + 4 = 10 socks.

  3. Count again in pairs to check this case

    Keeping the pairs complete, we first count 3 + 2 = 5 pairs. They contain 2 × 5 = 10 socks. Counting by batch and counting by pair give the same total.

    Check in reverse: 10 ÷ 2 = 5 pairs, with no remainder. This calculation checks our drawing and operations for these inputs. It is not enough to establish the claim for all even nonnegative integers.

    6+4=10=2×(3+2)

  4. Start again from the assumptions without choosing a and b

    Now take arbitrary even x and y in the domain. The word arbitrary means that we impose no particular values on them. The definition of even gives us x = 2a and y = 2b for nonnegative integers a and b, which may be different.

    In the sum s = x + y, replace x and y with these expressions. This substitution is allowed because a quantity can be replaced with an equal quantity.

    s=x+y=2a+2b

  5. Factor out the common factor

    The distributive property says that multiplying a sum is equivalent to multiplying each term and then adding. We use it in the direction that groups the terms: the factor 2 is common to the terms 2a and 2b. Factoring here means rewriting this sum as a product.

    The parentheses group the numbers of pairs. This corresponds exactly to bringing the trays together without breaking up their pairs. This equality does not require the batches to have the same size.

    2a+2b=2(a+b)

  6. Justify the final step and conclude

    The sum of nonnegative integers is still a nonnegative integer: combining whole counts creates neither a fraction of an object nor a negative quantity. So a + b is a nonnegative integer. This is the integer that allows us to recognize s as even; here we call it the integer witness to the evenness of s.

    We have obtained s = 2(a + b), or 2 times this integer. The definition of even applies: s is even. At no point did we assume that s was even in order to prove it.

    The proof covers all choices in the domain because a and b remained arbitrary in the symbolic steps. The distributive property and integer addition remain valid when either batch is empty. The scope of the proof is exactly the sum of 2 even numbers in this domain.

Hide the calculation and proof. Explain what allows each step: definition of even, substitution, grouping, resulting integer, conclusion. What sentence would be missing if you stopped at s = 2(a + b)?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I found an even total in the drawing, so it is always true.”

    The drawing checks the chosen values. Start again with arbitrary a and b and justify the grouping: no finite list of trials can, by itself, justify this step to all counts.

    Revisit the move from a particular calculation to a proof

  • “I start by assuming that the sum is even.”

    That would assume the conclusion. Write down what is given: x and y are even. Express each assumption separately using the definition, then calculate x + y until you recognize the required definition.

    Review the assumptions and the goal

  • “We still need to prove that a + b is even.”

    The definition requires the number multiplied by 2 to be an integer. a + b is an integer because a and b are integers; its own parity is not required. Confusing these roles would add an unnecessary condition.

    Try repairing the incomplete justification

Change the batch sizes

This time, the same trays hold 4 pairs and 3 pairs. Calculate the numbers of socks in each batch, then the total. Check by counting the combined pairs first. Does this calculation by itself constitute the general proof?

Hint 1

Each pair contains 2 socks: start with the separate batches.

Hint 2

Compare 2 × 4 + 2 × 3 with 2 × (4 + 3).

Compare with the solution

The batches contain 2 × 4 = 8 socks and 2 × 3 = 6 socks. The total is therefore 8 + 6 = 14 socks.

Grouping gives 4 + 3 = 7 pairs, or 2 × 7 = 14 socks. The counts agree; we have checked another case. For the general proof, we must keep the letters arbitrary and justify the transformations of their expressions.

Check your reasoning

  • Give 8 and 6 socks, then 14 socks in total.
  • Obtain the same total from 7 pairs and specify what each number counts.
  • Distinguish checking a case from proving the claim for the whole domain.

Exercise 2 · Proof repair

The right form, the wrong condition

Someone writes: "x = 2a and y = 2b for nonnegative integers a and b. So x + y = 2(a + b). To conclude, we must assume that a + b is even." Keep the correct steps and replace the final sentence with a sufficient justification. Also explain why starting with "I assume x + y is even" would not repair anything.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In the definition of even, what property must the number multiplied by 2 have?

Hint 2

Distinguish what is given about x and y from what you must establish about x + y.

Compare with the solution

The grouping is correct by the distributive property. The final sentence should become: a + b is a nonnegative integer, since a and b are nonnegative integers; therefore x + y is 2 times a nonnegative integer and is even by definition.

Assuming that a + b is even adds an unnecessary condition without justifying that condition. Assuming that x + y is even at the start would directly use the desired conclusion: the reasoning would be circular. We have only the evenness of x and of y to start from.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the factorization and name the distributive property.
  • State that a + b is a nonnegative integer and connect this property to the definition of even.
  • Explain the circular reasoning created by assuming the conclusion.

Exercise 3 · Independent proof writing and a boundary case

A proof that includes the empty tray

Without looking at the example, write a direct proof for all even nonnegative integer counts x and y. State your assumptions, define a and b, and justify each equality. Only then check the case of 5 pairs on A and 0 pairs on B. Explain why this check does not add an exception to the proof.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Express "x is even" and "y is even" separately using the definition, without stating the parity of their sum.

Hint 2

After grouping, state explicitly which integer allows you to apply the definition. A count of 0 is allowed.

Compare with the solution

Let x and y be arbitrary even nonnegative integers. There exist nonnegative integers a and b with x = 2a and y = 2b, by the definition of even. Substituting and then factoring gives the following equality.

x+y=2a+2b=2(a+b)

Since a + b is a nonnegative integer, the sum x + y is even by definition. No particular choice of a and b was needed: this is what makes the proof general.

For the requested check, a = 5 and b = 0. B contains 2 × 0 = 0 socks. The combined batches give 5 + 0 = 5 pairs, or 2 × 5 = 10 socks. The count 0 is indeed even because 0 = 2 × 0. The addition and distributive property used in the proof already included it.

Check your reasoning

  • State the domain, assumptions, and conclusion without confusing their roles.
  • Define a and b and justify substitution, factorization, and the fact that a + b is an integer.
  • Give the check for the empty tray with its counting units and recognize that it is already covered.
  • Mark the attempt as assisted if you consulted a hint or the solution; try again later without support.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Where does a direct proof start, and where must it lead?

It starts from the stated assumptions and establishes the conclusion through justified steps. Here, the starting counts are assumed to be even; the evenness of their sum must be established.

Recall 2What does the assumption "x is even" allow us to write in our domain?

x = 2a for a nonnegative integer a. In the scene, x counts socks and a counts pairs; the value of a does not need to be fixed.

Recall 3Why does s = 2(a + b) allow us to conclude? What justification must be added?

a + b is a nonnegative integer because a and b are nonnegative integers. s is therefore 2 times a nonnegative integer and is even by definition. a + b does not itself need to be even.

Recall 4What is missing from the calculation 6 + 4 = 10 alone for it to be a general proof?

It fixes the counts. The general proof keeps a and b arbitrary and justifies x + y = 2a + 2b = 2(a + b), then the fact that a + b is an integer. The calculation checks a case and helps show how the reasoning works.

Recall 5Why is the empty tray already included in the proof?

0 is an allowed count and 0 = 2 × 0: it is even. The sum a + b remains a nonnegative integer and the distributive property remains valid if b = 0.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the proof without rereading: state the allowed counts and the assumptions; express evenness for each batch using the definition; substitute into the sum; factor out 2; name the resulting integer and conclude by definition. Then explain what the drawing checks and what makes the proof general.
