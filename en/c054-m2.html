Level 1 · C054·M2

Mean value theorem

Your goalRelate a change over an interval to a derivative at an interior point, then bound that change while checking the assumptions.

Before you start
c033-m5 : affine approximation · c014-m5 : invariants · c054-m1 : extrema

✿01 See the idea

A small cart moves along a marked rail. At 1 s, it is at the 1 m mark; at 3 s, it is at the 9 m mark. Its position has changed by 8 m in 2 s: its average velocity is 4 m/s. This does not mean that it maintained this velocity throughout the journey.

On the position–time graph, joining the starting and ending positions gives a line called a secant line. Its slope is the average velocity. The tangent line gives the velocity at an instant. In our smooth model, a tangent line at an interior point must be parallel to the secant line: at that instant, instantaneous velocity and average velocity coincide.

The mean value theorem guarantees this instant under specific assumptions. It generally does not give its location. It also lets us bound a displacement: if velocity stays within a bound throughout the interval, displacement cannot exceed that bound multiplied by the elapsed time.

The scene, positions and law of motion are fictional. We study an exact function, with no measurement noise or friction to estimate. The mathematical conclusion does not validate the motion of a real cart.

The rule to remember

Check continuity on the closed interval and differentiability in its interior; the slope of a secant line is then a derivative somewhere between the endpoints. A derivative bound that holds everywhere gives a bound on the change.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the displacement of the cart to an interior instant

Position is f(t) = k × t × t, where t is time in seconds and k = 1 m per second squared. We consider a = 1 s and b = 3 s. We will calculate the slope of the secant line, find an instant with that velocity, then check the displacement against bounds.

  1. Define the objects before calculating

    f assigns a position in metres to a time t. a and b are the starting and ending instants, with a < b. The closed interval [a, b] includes its endpoints; the open interval (a, b) excludes them. The derivative f′(t) is the signed instantaneous velocity, in m/s. The prime symbol indicates a derivative, not a new instant.

    The coefficient k carries the required units: multiplying k by a time squared gives metres. The differentiation rule for a square gives:

    f(t)=kt2;f′(t)=2kt

  2. Check the assumptions and state the theorem

    The function must be continuous on [a, b]: in particular, its value at each endpoint must agree with the limit of nearby values from within the interval. It must be differentiable at every point of (a, b): there must be no interior corner where the derivative fails to exist. The chosen polynomial meets these conditions. The theorem does not require a continuous derivative or a derivative at the endpoints.

    Under these assumptions, there is at least one instant c strictly between a and b such that:

    f′(c)=f(b)−f(a)b−a

    c is expressed in seconds. The quotient, which we will call q, is in m/s. The theorem asserts existence; it promises neither a unique instant nor the midpoint of the interval. The change f(b) − f(a) is signed: it is displacement, generally not the total distance travelled.

  3. Calculate the change and elapsed time in the same direction

    At the start: a × a = 1 × 1 = 1 seconds squared, so f(a) = 1 × 1 = 1 m. At the end: b × b = 3 × 3 = 9 seconds squared, so f(b) = 1 × 9 = 9 m.

    Subtract the starting value from the ending value for both quantities: Δf = f(b) − f(a) = 9 − 1 = 8 m and Δt = b − a = 3 − 1 = 2 s. Here the Greek letter Δ means a difference between the endpoints. The elapsed time is strictly positive, so division is allowed.

  4. Divide to obtain the slope of the secant line

    Dividing displacement by elapsed time expresses the average change over 1 second. This measures the slope of the secant line AB, even if the cart accelerates.

    q=ΔfΔt=82=4 m/s

    Immediate check: q × Δt = 4 × 2 = 8 m. This gives exactly the displacement we calculated, with a unit of length.

  5. Find c and check its role

    We seek f′(c) = q. Since f′(c) = 2kc and 2k = 2 m per second squared, divide the average velocity by this coefficient:

    c=q2k=42=2 s

    The instant is indeed in the interior: 1 s < 2 s < 3 s. Its velocity is f′(c) = 2 × 2 = 4 m/s, equal to q. Its position is f(c) = 1 × 4 = 4 m. Do not confuse this result in metres with velocity in m/s.

    Another check uses b squared minus a squared = (b − a)(b + a). Dividing by b − a gives q = k(b + a) = 1 × (3 + 1) = 4 m/s. Here, c = (a + b)/2: the midpoint comes from this quadratic law, not from the theorem in general.

  6. Bound the change without finding c

    Throughout our interval of positive times, f′(t) = 2t stays between L = 2 m/s and M = 6 m/s: multiplying a ≤ t ≤ b by the positive coefficient 2 preserves the order. L and M are bounds on velocity. Since c is in the interior, its velocity satisfies these bounds. Multiplying by the positive quantity b − a gives:

    L(b−a)≤f(b)−f(a)≤M(b−a)

    Here: 2 × 2 = 4 m and 6 × 2 = 12 m; the exact calculation satisfies 4 m ≤ 8 m ≤ 12 m. The upper bound is not an exact value of the displacement.

    When only the magnitude of velocity is bounded, the absolute value | | removes the sign. If |f′(t)| ≤ M throughout the interior, with M ≥ 0, the consequence is:

    |f(b)−f(a)|≤M|b−a|

    This inequality concerns the difference between the endpoint positions, even if the motion changes direction. It still requires the assumptions of the theorem.

  7. Understand why a parallel tangent exists

    Subtract the secant line from the position function. The position on this line is ℓ(t) = f(a) + q(t − a). ℓ is an affine function in metres, with slope q. The residual r(t) = f(t) − ℓ(t), also in metres, is the vertical difference between the curve and this line. By construction, r(a) = r(b) = 0 m.

    If r is identically 0, its derivative is 0 at every interior point: every interior instant works. Otherwise, r takes a positive or negative value. A continuous function on a closed bounded interval attains its maximum and minimum. Here a positive maximum or a negative minimum must occur in the interior, since the endpoint values are 0 m.

    At this interior extremum c, the derivative r′(c) is 0, as we saw with extrema. Since r′(c) = f′(c) − q, we obtain f′(c) = q. This argument is Rolle’s theorem applied after subtracting the secant line; it explains the role of continuity and differentiability without assuming that velocity is constant.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The instant c is always at the midpoint.”

    Here, the difference of squares identity lets us find the midpoint. The general statement guarantees only at least one interior point. For another law of motion of the same cart, we would have to solve f′(c) = q or leave c unknown; several points might work.

    Reread the existence of c and its calculation for the chosen law of motion

  • “I multiply the starting velocity by the elapsed time: the result is exact.”

    The starting velocity is 2 m/s. Multiplying it by 2 s gives 4 m, whereas the exact displacement is 8 m. A derivative at one point provides a local approximation; the equality in the theorem uses a derivative at the interior point c, which is generally unknown. A bound requires control throughout the interval.

    Compare the exact displacement with its bounds

  • “The curve is continuous, so the theorem applies.”

    A derivative must also exist at every interior point. A corner can leave position continuous while making the derivative fail to exist. The last exercise gives precisely this variant of the journey of the cart: we calculate the slope of the secant line, then see why no tangent line has that slope.

    Examine the journey with an instantaneous change of direction

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Shorten the time window for the same journey

Keep f(t) = k × t × t with k = 1 m per second squared. Now consider [1 s, 2 s]. Calculate the elapsed time, displacement, average velocity and an interior instant when instantaneous velocity coincides with this average. Check the assumptions and the unit of each result.

Hint 1

Evaluate position at the new endpoints before taking a difference.

Hint 2

Once you have the average velocity, solve 2kc = q.

Compare with the solution

The polynomial is continuous on the closed interval and differentiable in its interior. The new endpoint is 2 s; its square is 4 seconds squared, so its position is 4 m. The initial position remains 1 m.

Elapsed time: 2 − 1 = 1 s. Displacement: 4 − 1 = 3 m. The quotient is 3/1 = 3 m/s.

Solve 2c = 3: c = 3/2 s, or 1.50 s. This instant is strictly between 1 s and 2 s. Check: f′(c) = 2 × (3/2) = 3 m/s. This recovers the average velocity; this check does not require confusing velocity with position.

  • Subtract the endpoints in the same direction for position and time.
  • Give c in seconds and check that it is in the interior.

Exercise 2 · Bounding

A bound without knowing the final position

A smaller time window within the initial journey lasts 1/4 s, or 0.25 s, and lies entirely within [1 s, 3 s]. Use only the bound |f′(t)| ≤ 6 m/s, which holds everywhere in this window. What maximum difference between positions can you guarantee? Can you say that this difference will be exactly equal to the bound?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Call the endpoints of the smaller window α and β. They inherit the assumptions of the initial journey.

Hint 2

Apply |f(β) − f(α)| ≤ M|β − α|; the units m/s and s must give metres.

Compare with the solution

α and β are the starting and ending instants of the smaller window, in seconds, with α < β. The continuity and differentiability of f still hold on this window. We have |β − α| = 1/4 s and M = 6 m/s.

The exact product is 6 × (1/4) = 3/2 m, or 1.50 m. Thus |f(β) − f(α)| ≤ 1.50 m. This guarantee does not locate α and β or calculate their positions: it provides a common bound for all permitted smaller windows.

An inequality does not become an equality because the data are exact. To find the precise displacement, we would need to know the endpoints of the window and evaluate f, or have other sufficient information.

Check your reasoning

  • Use a bound that holds throughout the interior of the smaller window.
  • Keep the ≤ sign and give the result in metres.
  • Distinguish a guarantee from a calculation of the exact displacement.

Exercise 3 · Checking assumptions

The cart turns around at an ideal corner

Replace only the law of motion of the same cart by g(t) = u|t − τ|, with u = 1 m/s and τ = 2 s. Keep [1 s, 3 s]. This mathematical variant idealizes an instantaneous change of direction; it does not describe the mechanics of a real turnaround. Calculate the signed average velocity. Does the theorem guarantee an instant with velocity equal to this average? Justify your answer by examining the corner.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

τ is the instant of the turnaround. The endpoints are the same time distance from τ.

Hint 2

Before τ, |t − τ| = τ − t; after τ, |t − τ| = t − τ. Compare the derivatives of the two branches.

Compare with the solution

The time from the start to τ is 2 − 1 = 1 s; the time from τ to the end is 3 − 2 = 1 s. The positions are therefore g(a) = 1 m and g(b) = 1 m. The displacement is 0 m in 2 s: the signed average velocity is 0 m/s. The cart has moved, even though its initial and final positions coincide.

g is continuous on the closed interval. For t < τ, its derivative is −u = -1 m/s; for t > τ, it is u = 1 m/s. At τ, these one-sided slopes differ: g is not differentiable at this interior point.

The theorem therefore does not apply. No existing derivative equals 0 m/s; the corner has no tangent line with a defined slope. The missing assumption is indeed necessary for this general guarantee, and a secant slope alone does not restore it.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the signed displacement without confusing it with distance travelled.
  • Identify precisely the failure of differentiability at τ.
  • Explain why neither the branches nor the corner provides the required derivative.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which assumptions should you check before applying the mean value theorem?

For a < b, f must be continuous on [a, b] and differentiable at every point of (a, b). The derivative need not be continuous or exist at the endpoints.

Recall 2What exactly does the theorem relate, and what do we know about the point c?

There is at least one c in (a, b) such that f′(c) = (f(b) − f(a))/(b − a). The derivative at c equals the slope of the secant line. Existence guarantees neither uniqueness nor the midpoint of the interval.

Recall 3How do you turn a bound on |f′| into a bound on a change?

Under the assumptions of the theorem, if |f′(t)| ≤ M everywhere in the interior, with M ≥ 0, then |f(b) − f(a)| ≤ M|b − a|. In the scene, a velocity in m/s multiplied by an elapsed time in s gives a difference in m.

Recall 4Why does the derivative at the start not necessarily give the exact displacement?

It describes the local change at the start. The theorem uses a derivative at an interior point that depends on the journey and endpoints. For the initial quadratic motion of the cart, multiplying the starting velocity by the elapsed time gives 4 m, whereas the exact displacement is 8 m.

Recall 5Why can a continuous position function with a corner make the conclusion fail?

The derivative may fail to exist at the corner. In the ideal turnaround, the secant slope is 0 m/s, but the only existing derivatives are -1 or 1 m/s. Continuity alone therefore does not guarantee a parallel tangent.
