ROC and PR

Your goalConstruct the ROC and precision–recall curves for the same ranking, then choose a threshold using an explicit cost of errors.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

In a workshop, a device ranks parts by how likely they seem to be defective. Parts with scores at or above a threshold will be inspected. A high threshold selects few parts; lowering it adds inspections. Some reveal a defect; others use workshop resources to inspect a part that meets specifications.

We keep a single fictional batch of 6 parts, with 2 defective parts and 4 parts that meet specifications. A reference inspection has already established their status. The score is used only to rank them: it is expressed in points, rather than as a probability. The data and costs on this page are fictional, with no measurements from a real system.

For each threshold, the ROC curve plots the proportion of parts that meet specifications but are wrongly sent for inspection on the horizontal axis, and the proportion of defective parts found, called recall, on the vertical axis. The PR curve, short for precision–recall, plots recall on the horizontal axis and precision on the vertical axis: among the parts sent for inspection, how many are actually defective?

The diagrams show the same decisions in two coordinate systems. Look at the change from threshold 8 to threshold 7: a defective part is added, with no additional part that meets specifications. Recall and precision both increase. Precision therefore does not necessarily decrease every time the threshold is lowered. All these proportions have no units and lie between 0 and 1.

The rule to remember

Sweep through the thresholds of a fixed ranking, recount the errors, then name the denominator for each axis. The curve describes the trade-offs; the cost of errors guides the choice of point.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From a labeled batch to a defensible threshold

The ranking in descending order is A: 9 points, defective; B: 8 points, meets specifications; C: 7 points, defective; D: 6 points, meets specifications; E: 5 points, meets specifications; F: 4 points, meets specifications. The rule is “inspect if score ≥ threshold”. Missing a defective part has a fictional cost of 4 cost units; wrongly inspecting a part that meets specifications costs 1 cost unit. Correct decisions have no cost in this model.

  1. Fix the labels and denominators

    s denotes the score of a part, and t the threshold, both in points. Here, “positive” means “defective” for the reference label and “sent for inspection” for the decision. VP counts defective parts sent for inspection, or true positives; FN counts defective parts missed, or false negatives; FP counts parts that meet specifications but are sent for inspection, or false positives; VN counts parts that meet specifications and are left out, or true negatives. These four numbers are counts of parts.

    P = 2 is the actual number of defective parts; N = 4 is the number of parts that meet specifications. At every threshold, VP + FN = P and FP + VN = N. The proportion of defective parts in the batch, called prevalence, is P/(P + N) = 2/6 = 1/3.

    We keep the same device, the same batch, and reference labels established independently of the score. There are no tied scores. If scores are tied, all parts with the same score must be added together. No model is retrained during the sweep.

  2. Review the ratios before plotting

    R denotes recall; U the false positive rate; V precision. Each ratio divides a count of parts by another count, so it has no units. We have P > 0 and N > 0. V also requires VP + FP > 0.

    R=VPP;U=FPN;V=VPVP+FP

    An ROC point has coordinates (U ; R), and a PR point (R ; V). Recall stays the same; it changes axis. U uses all parts that meet specifications as its reference, while V uses only the parts sent for inspection. This difference explains why ROC alone provides little information about the proportion of unnecessary inspections when defects are rare.

  3. Calculate a point in full: threshold 8

    At t = 8 points, A and B are sent for inspection: A gives VP = 1, B gives FP = 1. C is left out: FN = 2 − 1 = 1. D, E, and F meet specifications and are left out: VN = 4 − 1 = 3. Check: each part belongs to exactly one category.

    R = 1/2 = 0.50 ; U = 1/4 = 0.25 ; V = 1/(1 + 1) = 0.50. ROC therefore plots (0.25 ; 0.50), and PR (0.50 ; 0.50). The decimal values are proportions with no units.

    At t = 7, C is added: VP = 2, while FP stays at 1. We obtain R = 1, U = 1/4, V = 2/3 = 2/3 ≈ 0.667. The movement is vertical in ROC and to the right and upward in PR.

  4. Sweep through all distinct states

    Start above the highest score, then lower t to each observed score. The columns of the following table give the threshold t, the counts VP and FP, then R, U, and V. The letters are defined above; each fraction remains exact.

    To reconstruct the row t = 6, add D: FP becomes 2, so U = 2/4 = 1/2. The 2 defective parts are among 4 parts sent for inspection: V = 2/4 = 1/2. With E, U = 3/4 = 3/4 and V = 2/5 = 2/5. With F, everything is included: R = U = 1, V = 1/3.

    At threshold 10, no part is sent for inspection: ROC does have the point (0 ; 0), but V would require 0/0. This quotient is undefined. Some plots add a PR point by convention; we omit it rather than assign it an observed precision.

  5. Summarize the ranking with the ROC area, then check it

    The area under ROC, denoted A, has no units. Our step-shaped curve has a strip of width 1/4 at height 1/2, then a strip of width 3/4 at height 1. The vertical segments have 0 width.

    A=1/4×1/2+3/4×1=1/8+3/4=7/8

    Another way to check: compare each defective part with each part that meets specifications. A ranks ahead of all 4 parts that meet specifications; C ranks ahead of 3, because B is above C. This gives 7 correctly ordered pairs out of 2 × 4 = 8 pairs: 7/8 = 7/8 = 0.875, the same area. Here, this equivalence assumes there are no ties.

    This area summarizes the order of scores on this batch without choosing a threshold. It does not establish that the score is a correct probability. Calibration belongs to the next unit. We also distinguish this ROC area from an area under PR: the coordinate systems and conventions for joining points differ.

  6. Choose using cost, then limit the conclusion

    c_FP = 1 is the cost of a false alarm; c_FN = 4 is the cost of a missed defect. Their unit is the cost unit per affected part. L is the total cost on the batch. Costs add up, and this scenario imposes no limit on inspection capacity.

    L=cFPFP+cFNFN

    At t = 9, FP = 0 and FN = 1: L = 1 × 0 + 4 × 1 = 4 cost units. At t = 8, B adds 1 unit without finding C: L = 5. At t = 7, C is added and FN becomes 0: L = 1 cost unit.

    Let us check all states: at thresholds 10, 9, 8, 7, 6, 5, 4, the costs are respectively 8, 4, 5, 1, 2, 3, 4 cost units. The minimum is achieved by selecting A, B, C. Any t such that 6 < t ≤ 7 produces this selection; the number 7 is not a unique optimum among real-valued thresholds.

    The choice is defensible only for these costs and this fictional batch. In practice, setting a threshold on validation data and then evaluating it on a separate test set avoids presenting the selected minimum as a new performance result. A credible labeling reference and a representative population are also needed: the curves alone do not guarantee them.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Precision is the complement of the false positive rate.”

    At t = 8, U = 0.25 while V = 0.50. U divides false alarms by all parts that meet specifications; V divides defects found by the parts sent for inspection. Write down the group counted by each denominator before comparing.

    Review the ratios and the point at the intermediate threshold

  • “Lowering the threshold always lowers precision.”

    From t = 8 to t = 7, C is defective: the number of defects found increases, with no additional false alarm. Precision rises from 1/2 to 2/3. Recall does not decrease, but precision can rise or fall depending on what is added.

    Compare the movements in the two coordinate systems

  • “A large ROC area automatically gives the right threshold.”

    All thresholds in the example belong to the same ranking, with area 7/8, but their costs differ. Compare FP and FN using the stated costs. An area under PR also depends on the convention for joining points; it cannot replace this decision.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What are the axes of ROC and PR?

ROC: false positive rate FP/N on the horizontal axis, recall VP/P on the vertical axis. PR: recall on the horizontal axis, precision VP/(VP + FP) on the vertical axis. All these proportions have no units.

Recall 2How do you construct the curves from the batch without changing the model?

Fix the scores and reference labels. Start above the highest score, then lower the threshold to each distinct score. Add the parts that cross the threshold, recount VP and FP, then calculate the axis values. Equal scores are included together.

Recall 3Why can lowering the threshold increase precision?

If a defective part is added without an additional part that meets specifications, the proportion of defects among the parts sent for inspection increases. In this batch, adding C raises precision from 1/2 to 2/3. Recall does not decrease during the sweep.

Recall 4What is precision if no part is sent for inspection?

It is undefined: VP and FP are both 0, so the quotient has a 0 denominator. A point added to a PR plot by convention is not an observed precision.

Recall 5What does the ROC area summarize, and what remains to be decided?

It summarizes the order of scores; with no ties, it equals the proportion of defective–nondefective pairs that are correctly ordered. Here 7/8 = 7/8. It chooses neither a cost nor a threshold and does not establish the quality of the scores as probabilities.
