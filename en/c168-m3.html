Level 3 · C168·M3

Independence

Your goalVerify independence under a given probability measure, move from events to observable information, and distinguish pairwise independence from mutual independence.

Before you start
c164-m5 : Fubini-Tonelli · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c165-m5 : duality · c168-m1 : measurable variables · c168-m2 : conditional expectation

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a box of 4 tickets, each with a mark on the left and a mark on the right. Each mark is 0 or 1. The pairs (0, 0), (0, 1), (1, 0) and (1, 1) each appear once. You draw a single ticket, with every ticket equally likely to be chosen. The box and its probabilities are fictional and fixed for this exercise.

Before you look, the left mark is 1 on 2 tickets out of 4. If you are told that the right mark is 1, the tickets in the right column of the diagram remain: the same proportion have 1 on the left. This information therefore leaves the probability of the event “the left mark is 1” unchanged. This illustrates independence.

Additional information can nevertheless change everything. Let C be the event “the marks differ”. Knowing only the right mark, or only whether C occurs, tells you nothing about the left mark. Knowing both determines it. We will express this distinction without confusing pairwise independence with independence of the whole family.

This lesson concerns the probability law of the draw, rather than a causal link. The marks and their codes have no physical unit; counts are expressed in tickets and probabilities have no unit. A proportion in this box describes the stated model exactly, rather than a frequency measured in a real experiment.

The rule to remember

Always specify the reference probability measure and the events involved. Independence is verified by a factorization; it cannot be inferred from variable names or an impression of separation.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

One box, several levels of information

Under the stated uniform law, let A mean “the left mark is 1”, B mean “the right mark is 1” and C mean “the marks differ”. Let us check A and B, extend this check to the variables, then examine the family A, B, C.

  1. Fix the space and the law before calculating

    Ω is the set of pairs written on the tickets. An outcome ω is the ticket drawn. Here the σ-algebra ℱ is the set of all subsets of Ω: every selection of tickets is a measurable event. P is the uniform probability measure; for E in ℱ, P(E) is its number of tickets divided by 4. This rule depends on equal probabilities, rather than merely on the presence of the pairs.

    Each ticket has probability mass 1 ÷ 4 = 1/4. The sum of the masses is 4 × 1/4 = 1. The model is therefore normalized. X(ω) reads the left mark and Y(ω) reads the right mark. X and Y are measurable variables on (Ω, ℱ, P).

  2. Count the marginals and the intersection

    A contains (1, 0) and (1, 1), giving 2 tickets. B contains (0, 1) and (1, 1), giving 2 tickets. The symbol ∩ means “and”: A ∩ B contains only (1, 1), giving 1 ticket.

    Dividing each favorable count by the same total count gives P(A) = 2/4 = 1/2, P(B) = 2/4 = 1/2 and P(A ∩ B) = 1/4 = 1/4. These probabilities have no unit. Here “marginals” means the probabilities of A and B considered separately.

  3. Compare with the product, then check by conditioning

    By definition, two events A and B are independent under P if the probability of their intersection is the product of their probabilities. We therefore do not multiply to guess the intersection: we compare its count with what independence would require.

    P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B)

    Here, 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, exactly equal to 1/4. A and B are therefore independent. Since P(B) = 1/2 is strictly positive, the definition of conditioning on B gives another interpretation:

    P(A|B)=P(A∩B)P(B)=1/41/2=1/2

    This recovers P(A). Directly in the column Y = 1, 1 ticket out of 2 is in A: the same answer. Symmetry follows from the product formula; the quotient requires the conditioning event to have positive probability. If P(B) = 0, A ∩ B has probability 0 because it is a subset of B: the product equality remains true, but this quotient is undefined.

  4. Specify what “X and Y are independent” means

    An equality for a pair of events is generally insufficient to cover all the information carried by two variables. Two sub-σ-algebras 𝒢 and ℋ of ℱ, meaning two collections of observable events, are independent if P(E ∩ D) = P(E)P(D) for every E in 𝒢 and every D in ℋ.

    For a real variable X, σ(X) is the σ-algebra of events whose occurrence can be determined from X; it is generated by the events {X ∈ U}, where U is a Borel subset of the real line. The Borel sets form the σ-algebra generated by open intervals. X and Y are independent when σ(X) and σ(Y) are independent. This is equivalent to testing {X ∈ U} and {Y ∈ V} for all Borel sets U and V.

    Here the variables take only the values 0 and 1. Each of their values has probability 1/2. For each choice of x, y in this set of codes, {X = x, Y = y} contains exactly one ticket: its probability 1/4 is indeed the product 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. The lowercase letters x and y denote possible values. Taking unions of the required cells recovers the equality for every selection of values. This proves independence of X and Y, and hence of the σ-algebras σ(X) and σ(Y), in this finite model.

  5. Connect independence to conditional expectation

    For an event A, its indicator, denoted 𝟙_A, equals 1 on A and 0 elsewhere. It is integrable. The symbol 𝔼 denotes expectation under P; “p.s.” means almost surely, that is, outside an event of probability 0. Event A is independent of a σ-algebra 𝒢 if and only if:

    𝔼[𝟙A|𝒢]=P(A) p.s.

    Why? The constant P(A) is 𝒢-measurable. For every D in 𝒢, its integral over D is P(A)P(D). The integral of 𝟙_A over D is P(A ∩ D). Equality of these integrals for every D is exactly the definition of conditional expectation, and exactly independence of A from 𝒢. Uniqueness of conditional expectation is understood almost surely.

    In our box, take 𝒢 = σ(Y). On B, the expectation of 𝟙_A is 1/2. On Bᶜ, it is 1/2, because 1 ticket out of 2 has left mark 1. The symbol ᶜ denotes the complement in Ω. This expectation therefore equals the constant P(A) = 1/2 everywhere: a check using the information from Y, rather than just one cell.

  6. Check each pair involving C

    C contains (0, 1) and (1, 0): P(C) = 2/4 = 1/2. A ∩ C contains only (1, 0), and B ∩ C contains only (0, 1). Hence P(A ∩ C) = 1/4 = 1/4 and P(B ∩ C) = 1/4 = 1/4.

    The corresponding products are P(A)P(C) = 1/4 and P(B)P(C) = 1/4. Each intersection indeed has the value of its product. Together with the pair A, B already checked, this means A, B, C are pairwise independent. This expression guarantees only the tests involving distinct pairs.

  7. Test the whole family and explain the failure

    A family (Eᵢ) indexed by a set I is mutually independent if, for every nonempty finite subset J of I, the probability of the intersection of the Eᵢ for i in J equals the product of their probabilities. I names the available indices; J chooses those to combine. For A, B, C, we therefore need the equalities for the pairs and the equality for their joint intersection.

    P(A∩B∩C)≟P(A)P(B)P(C)

    A ∩ B contains only (1, 1), which does not satisfy C. The joint intersection is empty: P(A ∩ B ∩ C) = 0/4 = 0. The product would be 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8. The exact difference is 1/8: the family is not mutually independent.

    Check using the combined information: B ∩ C forces the ticket (0, 1), so P(A | B ∩ C) = 0 ÷ 1/4 = 0, whereas P(A) = 1/2. The indicator of C, called Z, is entirely determined by X and Y: it equals 1 when they differ. X, Y, Z are also pairwise independent, but not mutually independent. For each binary pair, taking complements of the events where the variables equal 1 gives all the other cells, as in the step on σ(X) and σ(Y).

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Draw the tickets again and explain which information leaves the probability of A unchanged, which combination of information changes it, and why both findings are compatible. Then restate the criterion using σ-algebras.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Independent events cannot occur together.”

    Mutual exclusivity means an empty intersection. Here A and B are independent and occur together on (1, 1). Conversely, A and Aᶜ are mutually exclusive but not independent: their intersection has probability 0, whereas P(A)P(Aᶜ) is 1/4. Distinguish what the intersection contains from the factorization of its probability.

    Count the intersection again and compare with the product

  • “As soon as the conditional quotient cannot be used, independence no longer makes sense.”

    The product definition involves no division. An event of probability 0 is independent of every event, because their intersection is a subset of that event. The quotient P(A ∩ B)/P(B) can be used only if P(B) is strictly positive; do not assign it any value by dividing by 0.

    Reread the condition for checking with a quotient

  • “I have checked every pair, so I can multiply across the whole family.”

    The constraint “the marks differ” escapes tests of pairs alone. Reconstruct A ∩ B ∩ C: its probability 0 differs from the product 1/8. For a family, mutual independence requires every finite subfamily; for variables, it concerns all the corresponding measurable information.

    Revisit the test of the whole family

Exercise 2 · Proof · σ-algebras and conditional expectation

Which equality must be tested for a whole collection of information?

Someone checks only P(A | B) = P(A), then claims that A is independent of any information about the ticket. Explain what this calculation actually allows them to conclude. In our box, reconstruct σ(Y) and the conditional expectation of 𝟙_A given σ(Y). Is A also independent of the complete information σ(X, Y), the σ-algebra generated jointly by X and Y?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Y takes only the values 0 and 1: its observable events are unions of its two level sets.

Hint 2

The complete information determines whether A occurs. Compare the conditional expectation with the constant P(A).

Compare with the solution

Since P(B) is positive, the quotient verifies precisely the independence of A and B. By taking complements, it also gives independence of A from σ(B), the σ-algebra generated by B. It does not check any other arbitrary information.

Here σ(Y) = {∅, Ω, B, Bᶜ}, where ∅ is the empty event. On B, 𝔼[𝟙_A | σ(Y)] equals 1/2; on Bᶜ, it equals 1/2. It is therefore the constant P(A) = 1/2, and A is independent of σ(Y). The empty and certain events also satisfy the factorization.

However, σ(X, Y) distinguishes every ticket, so it equals ℱ. Since A is measurable with respect to it, 𝔼[𝟙_A | σ(X, Y)] = 𝟙_A: it equals 0 or 1, never the constant 1/2. Another check: A belongs to this σ-algebra and P(A ∩ A) = 1/2, whereas P(A)P(A) = 1/4. A is therefore not independent of the complete information.

Check your reasoning

  • Name the σ-algebra tested and reject extending the conclusion to a richer σ-algebra without proof.
  • Recover all the events in σ(Y) and both values of the conditional expectation.
  • Show precisely why knowing the whole ticket reveals A.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which definition of independence remains valid when an event has probability 0?

Under the stated law P, P(A ∩ B) = P(A)P(B). If P(B) is positive, this is equivalent to P(A | B) = P(A). If P(B) is 0, the product remains defined, but this quotient does not.

Recall 2How do we move from two events to two independent random variables?

The σ-algebras σ(X) and σ(Y) must be independent: every event observable from X must satisfy the product factorization with every event observable from Y. For real variables, test all events {X ∈ U} and {Y ∈ V} with U and V Borel sets.

Recall 3Which conditional expectation characterizes independence of an event A and a σ-algebra 𝒢? Why?

𝔼[𝟙_A | 𝒢] = P(A) almost surely. For every D in 𝒢, the integral of the constant over D is P(A)P(D), and that of the indicator is P(A ∩ D). The equality required by conditioning is exactly the factorization.

Recall 4Why are the pairwise tests in the box insufficient for the whole family?

Each pair among A, B and C has an intersection with probability 1/4, equal to the product of its marginals. But the joint intersection is empty: 0 differs from the product 1/8. Mutual independence requires factorization for every nonempty finite subfamily.
