Level 2 · C137·M3

Optimal projection

Your goalChoose a projection line using variance and prove that it minimizes the mean of the squared distances.

Before you start
c048-m5 : positivity · c050-m5 : conditioning · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c036-m5 : annotated graphs · c137-m1 : centering · c137-m2 : covariance

✿01 See the idea

A camera tracks a small moving object on a table. For this fictional calculation, we keep 4 positions, described by their displacement from the mean center in centimeters. We want to replace each position with its projection onto the same line through this center. Which orientation loses the least geometric precision?

Imagine a ruler placed among the points. Each point slides to the ruler along the shortest path, perpendicular to it. The position it reaches is its orthogonal projection. The distance traveled is the reconstruction error. A line that follows the elongation of the point cloud leaves short paths across it.

Principal component analysis, or PCA, here chooses the line on which the projected positions remain most spread out. This retained variance and the error are not competing objectives: they share a total fixed by the point cloud. We will establish this equality, then check the orientation using the eigenvalues of the covariance matrix.

The positions and results are invented for the exercise; they are not camera readings. Both coordinates have the same unit and the same weight in the distance. We do not change their scales. The line to be sought, the metric and the centering are specified before calculation; optimality is relative to these choices.

The rule to remember

With centered points and a direction of unit norm, maximizing projected variance is equivalent to minimizing squared reconstruction error. The best direction is associated with the largest eigenvalue of the covariance matrix.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Compressing the moving object’s positions onto a line

The centered positions are A = (2, 11), B = (10, 5), C = (−2, −11) and D = (−10, −5), in cm. All have the same weight. Let us calculate a projection and its error, then prove that the chosen axis is optimal for the whole point cloud.

  1. Set out the data, units and problem

    Each position is a real column vector xᵢ with 2 components; i runs through the positions from A to D. In the table below, each row gives the name, the horizontal coordinate, then the vertical coordinate, both in cm. The number of positions is n = 4.

    [A211B105C−2−11D−10−5]

    The horizontal sum is 2 + 10 − 2 − 10 = 0 cm; the vertical sum vanishes in the same way. The mean is therefore the 0 vector. We seek a line through this center, using Euclidean distances: the squared distance is obtained by adding the squared coordinate differences. No assumption of a normal distribution or probabilistic independence is needed for this finite point cloud.

  2. Recover the covariance of the point cloud

    C is the empirical covariance matrix, defined here with divisor n. Its entries are in cm². The symbol ᵀ transposes a column vector into a row vector; xᵢxᵢᵀ forms an array of coordinate products. The sum runs over the positions.

    C=1n∑i=1nxixiᵀ

    Opposite points give the same products. After grouping terms and dividing by n, the horizontal variance is (4 + 100)/2 = 52 cm², and the vertical variance is (121 + 25)/2 = 73 cm². The cross covariance is (22 + 50)/2 = 36 cm²; here 22 = 2 × 11 and 50 = 10 × 5.

    C=[52363673] cm²

    Each square comes from the corresponding coordinate: 2 × 2 = 4, 10 × 10 = 100, 11 × 11 = 121, 5 × 5 = 25. We now reuse the centering and covariance from the previous units to choose a direction.

  3. Project A and check the foot of the perpendicular

    Let us try u = (3/5, 4/5)ᵀ, which is dimensionless. Its squared norm is 9/25 + 16/25 = 1: it is a unit direction. For any position, zᵢ is the signed coordinate along u, in cm. The vector x̂ᵢ, marked with a hat, is the reconstructed position; rᵢ is its residual, also in cm.

    zi=uᵀxi;x^i=ziu;ri=xi−x^i

    For A, the dot product gives za = 3/5 × 2 + 4/5 × 11 = 6/5 + 44/5 = 10 cm. Next, multiply this coordinate by u: x̂a = (6, 8)ᵀ cm. The residual is (-4, 3)ᵀ cm.

    Geometric check: -4 × 3/5 + 3 × 4/5 = 0 cm. The residual is indeed perpendicular to u. Its squared norm is 16 + 9 = 25 cm². The squared norm of the original position is 4 + 121 = 125 cm²; the squared score is 100 cm². This confirms 125 = 100 + 25.

  4. Connect retained variance and mean error

    The scores of A, B, C and D are respectively 10, 10, −10 and −10 cm; the calculation for B will be revisited in the exercises. Their mean is 0 because the point cloud is centered. V(u) denotes their variance with the same divisor n; E(u) is the mean of the squared norms of the residuals; T is the total spread, the mean of the squared norms of the positions. V, E and T are in cm².

    V(u)=1n∑i=1nzi2=uᵀCu
    T=1n∑i=1n‖xi‖2;E(u)=1n∑i=1n‖ri‖2

    The double bars denote the Euclidean norm. The Pythagorean theorem applies to each position: its squared norm is the squared score plus the squared norm of the residual. Taking the mean gives T = V(u) + E(u). The total is also the trace of C, meaning the sum of its diagonal entries: T = 52 + 73 = 125 cm².

    Here, all scores have the same square 100 cm², so V(u) = 100 cm² and E(u) = 125 − 100 = 25 cm². For any other unit direction w, the same reasoning gives E(w) = T − V(w). T stays fixed: maximizing V exactly minimizes E.

  5. Identify the eigendirections of C

    An eigenvalue λ and its eigendirection satisfy Cu = λu: applying C changes only the length of the vector, not the line it lies on. For our direction, we can check the multiplication component by component.

    Cu=[52×3/5+36×4/536×3/5+73×4/5]=100[3/54/5]

    Thus λ = 100 cm². The direction v = (−4/5, 3/5)ᵀ is a unit direction perpendicular to u: their dot product is 0. The same matrix multiplication gives Cv = 25v, with 25 cm² as the other eigenvalue. The directions u and v therefore form an orthonormal basis of the plane: any vector can be decomposed in this basis without changing lengths.

    Cv=[−52×4/5+36×3/5−36×4/5+73×3/5]=25[−4/53/5]

  6. Prove that no direction does better

    Let w be any unit direction. Write w = αu + βv. The coefficients α and β are real and dimensionless. Since u and v are orthonormal, α squared + β squared = 1. The cross term vanishes in wᵀCw because u and v are perpendicular eigendirections of C.

    V(w)=100α2+25β2=25+75α2≤100 cm²

    The square of α lies between 0 and 1, which gives this bound. Equality requires β = 0 and α = ±1 because the eigenvalues are distinct. The only best orientations are therefore u and −u, which describe the same line. The minimum error is 125 − 100 = 25 cm².

    This reasoning explains the general rule: in an orthonormal basis of eigendirections of a covariance matrix, the variance along a unit direction is a weighted average of its eigenvalues, with nonnegative weights summing to unity. It cannot exceed the largest eigenvalue. If several largest eigenvalues are equal, an optimal direction may not be unique.

  7. Check against another line and state the limits of the conclusion

    On the horizontal axis w = (1, 0)ᵀ, the score is simply the horizontal coordinate. V(w) = 52 cm²; E(w) = 125 − 52 = 73 cm². The slanted direction therefore retains more spread and leaves less error than this alternative. The preceding bound, rather than this comparison alone, proves optimality among all lines.

    The error being measured is a mean of squared perpendicular distances. It is neither a distance in cm nor a vertical error imposed by regression. The optimum concerns the supplied positions and the stated centering and metric. It does not guarantee better prediction, information useful for every task, or performance on new positions. Here we choose only a line; SVD and the choice of the number of components belong to the following units.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why we require a unit norm, why the Pythagorean theorem connects variance and error, and then why the largest eigenvalue gives a bound for every direction. Recovering only the numerical result is not enough.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I choose the coordinate with the largest variance.”

    The coordinate variances compare only the axes already drawn. Cross covariance allows a slanted direction to retain more spread. You need to examine the eigendirections, then justify the bound over all unit directions.

    Revisit the eigendirections and the bound

  • “I can double u to double the quality of the axis.”

    Doubling u does not change its line, but it artificially multiplies the scores if you apply the formula reserved for a unit direction. Normalize the direction or use projection onto a nonunit vector, as in the last exercise. Without a norm constraint, the variance criterion can grow without bound.

    Fix the calculation with a nonunit direction

  • “A small projected variance means a small error.”

    It is the residual that needs to be small. The total stays fixed: a small retained variance leaves more error, since E = T − V. Compare the shares of the bars, then distinguish cm for a distance from cm² for its squared contribution.

    Review the split between retention and loss

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Why does B reach the same projection as A?

Keep u = (3/5, 4/5)ᵀ and calculate the score, projection and residual of B = (10, 5)ᵀ cm. Check that the residual is orthogonal and explain how two different positions can give the same score.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with uᵀB, then multiply the score by u.

Hint 2

Subtract the projection from B. Then compare this residual with that of A.

Compare with the solution

The score is 3/5 × 10 + 4/5 × 5 = 6 + 4 = 10 cm. The projection is therefore (6, 8)ᵀ cm, as for A.

The residual is (10 − 6, 5 − 8)ᵀ = (4, −3)ᵀ cm. Its dot product with u is 4 × 3/5 − 3 × 4/5 = 0 cm and its squared norm is 16 + 9 = 25 cm².

A and B differ in the direction perpendicular to the line. That component is discarded: the same score therefore cannot tell us which of the two positions was present. PCA can lose a real distinction even when it is optimal for the chosen criterion.

  • Obtain 10 cm as the score, then the projection vector in cm.
  • Calculate a perpendicular residual with squared norm 25 cm².
  • Explain the loss of information using positions A and B.

Exercise 2 · Comparison on paper

Is the best coordinate axis enough?

Before calculating, choose which of the horizontal and vertical axes retains more variance. Give the mean squared error for each, then compare them with the line along u. Explain why this comparison alone does not prove the optimality of u.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The diagonal entries of C give the variances retained by the coordinate axes.

Hint 2

For each axis, subtract its retained variance from the same total T.

Compare with the solution

The vertical axis retains 73 cm², more than the 52 cm² retained by the horizontal axis. Its error is T − 73 = 125 − 73 = 52 cm². The horizontal error is 73 cm².

The line along u retains 100 cm² and its error is 25 cm²: it does better than both coordinate axes. The cross term 36 cm² is nonzero, so the orientation of the line matters.

Comparing a few directions gives candidates. To rule out all the others, we need the bound V(w) ≤ 100 cm², obtained by decomposing any unit direction in the basis u, v.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

Same line, different length, opposite direction

A colleague replaces u with d = (3, 4)ᵀ, which is dimensionless, and proposes x̂ₐ = (dᵀxₐ)d. Identify the flaw, reconstruct the correct projection of A using d, then explain what would change if −u were used instead of u.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The norm of d is 5, not 1. Look for a coefficient t such that xₐ − td is perpendicular to d.

Hint 2

Write dᵀ(xₐ − td) = 0, then solve for t.

Compare with the solution

d is not a unit vector: dᵀd = 3 × 3 + 4 × 4 = 25. The product dᵀxa is 3 × 2 + 4 × 11 = 50 cm. The proposed formula omits division by dᵀd.

t=dᵀxAdᵀd=5025=2 cm

The perpendicularity equation gives this coefficient t, in cm. The position td is (6, 8)ᵀ cm; we recover H. The vector d is 5u: a different length has changed neither the line nor the correct projection.

With −u, the score of A becomes −10 cm, but the projection is (−10)(−u) = 10u. The scores change sign; their squares and the variance stay the same. The projections and the error do not change; choosing a direction along the axis is a convention.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify the missing norm factor without confusing d with a unit direction.
  • Recover 2 cm and then (6, 8)ᵀ cm using the perpendicularity equation.
  • Explain separately the change in the score’s sign and the invariance of the projection.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Optimal for what, and under which constraints?

For the mean of the squared Euclidean distances between the centered points and their projections onto a line through the center. The weights and scales are fixed; the direction has unit norm.

Recall 2How do you calculate the score and reconstruction from a unit direction u?

The score is zᵢ = uᵀxᵢ, then the reconstruction is x̂ᵢ = zᵢu. The residual xᵢ − x̂ᵢ is perpendicular to u. Here the score, reconstruction and residual are in cm.

Recall 3Why does maximizing projected variance minimize the error?

For each point, the Pythagorean theorem gives the squared norm of the position as the sum of the squared score and the squared norm of the residual. Taking the mean: T = V + E. The total T is fixed; increasing V decreases E by the same amount.

Recall 4Which proof rules out all the other lines for our point cloud?

Any unit direction w can be decomposed as αu + βv with α squared + β squared = 1. Its variance is 25 + 75 times α squared, at most 100 cm². Equality requires the line along u.

Recall 5Why do u and −u give the same projection?

The score changes sign at the same time as the direction. Their product does not change. The squared scores, variance and error are therefore identical.

Choosing a line and projecting onto it are two distinct operations. Once the line is fixed, orthogonal reconstruction leaves a minimal residual for each point; PCA also chooses the common orientation that minimizes the mean error.
