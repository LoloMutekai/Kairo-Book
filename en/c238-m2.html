Hurst exponent

Your goalRecover the Hurst exponent from a change in the scale of increments, distinguish H from the variance exponent and check the limits of this identification.

Before you start
c235-m5 : divergence · c232-m5 : short-time limits · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c238-m1 : fractional covariance

✿01 See the idea

A sensor tracks pressure fluctuations in a small chamber. You compare pressure differences between times separated by 1 s, then by 16 s. You are not asking whether the pressure rises: you are asking how the spread of these differences changes as their duration increases.

In our fictional example, the standard deviation of the differences is 3 Pa over the short duration and 6 Pa over the long duration. The duration is multiplied by 16, but the spread only by 2. The Hurst exponent H is the exponent that relates these factors in a scaling model.

An increment is the difference between an ending value and a starting value. A standard deviation measures spread, in the unit of those values: here the pascal, written Pa. It is neither the signed difference for a particular path nor a bound that every difference must satisfy.

We will assume a fractional Brownian motion model for the centered pressure fluctuation. The given spreads are exact theoretical quantities invented for the calculation, rather than statistics recorded by a real sensor. Agreement between these spreads would not be enough to establish this model from observations.

The rule to remember

Compare increments over different durations: H is the power applied to the duration ratio to obtain the standard deviation ratio; the variance uses a power twice as large. The amplitude and the exponent play distinct roles.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Which exponent relates the sensor durations?

In the fictional model, s(1 s) = 3 Pa and s(16 s) = 6 Pa. Let us recover H, then check it using the variances. The notation s(δ) denotes the standard deviation of an increment of duration δ, rather than the pressure at time δ.

  1. Set up a model with consistent units

    X(t) is the pressure fluctuation around a constant reference, in Pa; t is time in seconds. Write X(t) = σ B_H(t/τ). The reference time τ is 1 s; σ > 0, in Pa, is the standard deviation of increments of duration τ. B_H is the standard fractional Brownian motion from the prerequisite: a centered Gaussian process with stationary increments and unit variance at unit time. H is its dimensionless exponent, with 0 < H < 1.

    Centered means that the mean is 0. Gaussian means that every finite linear combination of its values follows a normal distribution. Stationary here means that the distribution of an increment depends on its duration, rather than its starting time. This does not assert that the increments are independent.

    Define Δ_δX(t) = X(t + δ) − X(t), with δ > 0 in seconds. The ratio δ/τ is dimensionless: a noninteger power thus applies to a number, without depending on the choice between seconds and other units of time.

  2. Go from covariance to the scaling law

    The fractional covariance from the prerequisite gives the variance v(δ) = Var[Δ_δX(t)] below. Var denotes the mean squared deviation from the mean, in Pa². Since the increment is centered, this is also the mean of its square here. Taking its positive square root gives s(δ), in Pa.

    v(δ)=σ2(δτ)2H;s(δ)=σ(δτ)H

    The self-similarity of fractional Brownian motion also states that B_H(au) and a^H B_H(u), for a > 0, have the same joint distributions at any finite set of times u. a and u are dimensionless. This is equality in distribution: enlarging a randomly drawn path does not make it pointwise identical to itself. Our calculation uses only the consequence for spreads.

  3. Eliminate the amplitude by division

    Write δ_c and δ_l for the short and long durations. Set r = δ_l/δ_c and q = s(δ_l)/s(δ_c), both dimensionless. The same σ and the same τ appear in the numerator and denominator: they cancel out. This leaves q = r^H.

    q=s(δl)s(δc)=(δlδc)H=rH

    Calculate the ratios: r = 16 s / 1 s = 16; q = 6 Pa / 3 Pa = 2. We must therefore solve 16^H = 2. Multiplying all pressures by a positive constant factor would change σ, but leave q and H unchanged.

  4. Identify H without rounding a logarithm

    We recognize 2 × 2 = 4, then 4 × 4 = 16. In other words, 16 = 2^4. The required power is therefore H = 1/4, or 0.25, with no unit: raising 16 to this power does give 2.

    16H=24H=2⇒4H=1⇒H=14

    Why is the solution unique? The base r is strictly greater than 1: its power increases strictly with the exponent. For arbitrary ratios, the same equation gives H = ln(q)/ln(r). ln is the natural logarithm; the rule ln(r^H) = H ln(r) explains this division. We need q > 0, r > 0 and r different from 1. No logarithm of a pressure with a unit is involved.

  5. Check using squares and the Brownian case

    Calculate the variances independently from the standard deviations: 3 × 3 = 9 Pa²; 6 × 6 = 36 Pa². Their ratio is 36 / 9 = 4. The scaling law predicts r^(2H), and 2H = 1/2. The square root of 16 is indeed 4, since 4 × 4 = 16.

    Take care with the factor: the slope of ln(v) against the logarithm of duration is 0.50, whereas H is 0.25. For a graph, we can plot ln[v(δ)/v(τ)] against ln(δ/τ), so that the logarithms apply to dimensionless ratios.

    Compare this with ordinary Brownian motion, H = 1/2, at the same amplitude σ. For the same duration factor 16, its standard deviation would be multiplied by 4 and would be 12 Pa. Our 6 Pa distinguishes these models. A smaller H describes slower growth in spread as duration increases; it does not say that the signed fluctuation will be positive, or that the actual pressure must stay within this standard deviation.

  6. Define the limits of what the calculation establishes

    We have identified H under the stated scaling law, using exact theoretical spreads. Finite data would provide only estimates of s(δ), hence an estimated exponent, sensitive to the chosen durations and measurement noise. Checking a scaling law is also insufficient to demonstrate Gaussianity or the full covariance of fractional Brownian motion.

    This unit stops at the exponent and its identification. Dependencies between increments and the regularity of paths will be studied in the unit “memory and roughness”; a simulation procedure belongs to the next unit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The variance has an exponent of 0.50, so H is 0.50.”

    The standard deviation is the square root of the variance. The variance has exponent 2H: divide its logarithmic slope by 2. Revisit the ratios 36/9 and 6/3 to check the difference.

    Revisit the variance check

  • “A large standard deviation necessarily means a large H.”

    A single standard deviation combines amplitude σ and exponent H. Compare different durations using the same pressure unit and constant calibration. In the ratio, the factor σ cancels out; it is the variation across scales that identifies H under the model.

    Revisit the elimination of the amplitude

  • “These spreads prove that the sensor follows fractional Brownian motion.”

    They are consistent with a scaling law in the fictional example. They prove neither the joint distributions nor the stability of the exponent at other durations. In the last exercise, an additional duration specifically requires you to check this stability.

    Examine the additional duration

Exercise 1 · Reading an exponent

The exponent hidden in the variances

For the same chamber, you are given only v(1 s) = 9 Pa² and v(16 s) = 36 Pa², under the exact assumptions of the example. Recover H starting from the variance ratio, then check using the standard deviations.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate the duration and variance ratios, then write r^(2H).

Hint 2

The square root of 16 is 4: this identifies 2H before identifying H.

Compare with the solution

The ratios are r = 16 and v_longue/v_courte = 4. Thus 16^(2H) = 4 = 16^1/2. Since the base is greater than 1, the exponents are equal: 2H = 1/2, then H = 1/4.

Check: the positive square roots of the variances are 3 Pa and 6 Pa. Their ratio is 2, and raising it to the power 4 gives 16 again. We recover the same H, without confusing Pa and Pa².

Check your reasoning

  • Form dimensionless ratios.
  • Identify the exponent 2H first and explain why it is divided by 2.
  • Recover the standard deviations using their squares.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and independent check

Extend the duration further

Assume that the same H and the same amplitude remain valid up to 256 s. Predict the standard deviation and variance at this duration. Check by starting directly from 1 s, then by starting again from 16 s. State what this extrapolation assumes.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

From the short duration, the time ratio is 256. Find its root of degree 4.

Hint 2

From the long duration, the time ratio is again 16: the spread factor is therefore the same as in the example.

Compare with the solution

Direct route: 256 = 4^4, so the spread is multiplied by 4. The predicted standard deviation is 3 × 4 = 12 Pa. Its variance is 12 × 12 = 144 Pa².

Route in stages: 256 / 16 = 16. The spread factor is 2, giving 6 × 2 = 12 Pa, identical to the direct result. This check uses the composition of changes of scale.

The extrapolation assumes the same power law, the same H and the same σ over this entire range of durations. The initial data alone do not guarantee this extension for a real sensor.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 12 Pa and 144 Pa² while distinguishing their units.
  • Show the direct and composed routes.
  • State the stability of the model that makes the extrapolation possible.

Exercise 3 · Discussing a proof

Does a new spread contradict the model?

A fictional data sheet now states that the theoretical spreads are exactly 3 Pa at 1 s, 6 Pa at 16 s and 18 Pa at 256 s, with constant calibration. Can a single H and a single amplitude be retained for these exact data? Then explain why the conclusion should be more cautious if these numbers were estimates from a sample.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The changes from the short duration to the long one, then from the long one to the new one, have the same time factor.

Hint 2

Compare 6/3 and 18/6. Under this law, the same time factor must give the same standard deviation factor.

Compare with the solution

The time factors are both 16. Yet the spread factors are 2 and 3. They differ: a law s(δ) = σ(δ/τ)^H with the same σ and H cannot satisfy all these exact equalities. The initial data require H = 1/4; they predict 12 Pa at the new duration, which is 6 Pa less than the stated value.

We can write a local exponent ln(3)/ln(16) for the second change; it differs from 1/4 because 3 differs from 2. This does not repair a model that requires a common exponent.

If these numbers were estimates, their disagreement could come from their sampling uncertainty or from measurement noise. These uncertainties would need to be quantified and other durations checked before drawing a conclusion. The exact calculation here refutes the theoretical data sheet; by itself, it does not provide a statistical rejection procedure for real measurements.

Check your reasoning

  • Compare identical time factors and different spread factors.
  • Explain why changing only σ does not restore the ratios.
  • Distinguish a contradiction between exact equalities from a disagreement between uncertain estimates.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does H measure in our pressure example?

The dimensionless exponent that relates the duration and standard deviation factors of increments under the scaling law: s(aδ)/s(δ) = a^H. It is neither the amplitude σ nor the sign of the next increment; a is a positive duration factor.

Recall 2What is the difference between the logarithmic slope of the standard deviation and that of the variance?

The first is H, the second 2H, because the variance is the square of the standard deviation. We take logarithms of dimensionless ratios. A variance slope of 0.50 gives H = 0.25 here.

Recall 3Why does the standard deviation ratio allow H to be separated from σ?

The same factor σ multiplies the standard deviations at the different durations: division eliminates it. This leaves the duration ratio raised to the power H. This assumes the same model and constant calibration.

Recall 4How can we recover H when the ratios are not easily recognizable powers?

If r is the positive duration ratio, different from 1, and q is the positive standard deviation ratio, H = ln(q)/ln(r). Both ratios are dimensionless. This formula identifies an exponent under the assumed law; it does not prove that law.

Recall 5Does a ratio consistent with H demonstrate fractional Brownian motion?

No. A ratio describes only a relation between spreads at the chosen durations. The joint distributions, Gaussianity, covariance and stability at other scales require their own checks. Empirical estimates add uncertainty that is absent from our exact example.

Finish by explaining what would change with estimated spreads.
