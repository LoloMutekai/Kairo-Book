Level 4 · C232·M2

Steepest descent

Your goalTurn an oscillatory integral into an integral with a decaying integrand by choosing a contour through a saddle point, then justify that its value is preserved.

Before you start
c231-m5 : geodesics · c228-m5 : incompleteness · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c174-m5 : conditions for validity · c232-m1 : Laplace’s method

✿01 See the idea

On a fictional signal processing test bench, a window gives a high weight to the centre of a recording and a low weight to its edges. To look for an oscillation, we add these weights with rotating orientations. Two contributions of equal size can then cancel. The height of the window alone does not tell us what response will remain.

Laplace’s method in the previous unit identifies a dominant region for a real exponential. With a complex exponential, we must also track the phase, meaning the angle of each contribution. Steepest descent seeks a path on which this angle stays constant and the size decreases as we move away from the dominant point.

We will extend the real variable into the complex plane and move the integration path. The physical signal stays the same as at the start: the new path is a calculation tool. The saddle point will be the point where the complex derivative of the exponent vanishes; the modulus will decrease horizontally away from it and increase vertically.

The settings are fictional and all variables have been made dimensionless. The scene provides neither a performance measurement nor a validated model of a device. Before the equations, read the arrows: the whole contour is traversed from left to right; decay is described from its centre towards each end.

The rule to remember

Locate a saddle point, choose the directions of constant phase along which the modulus decreases, then prove that the initial contour can be deformed onto this path. A local choice never removes the need for a global check.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The oscillatory response of a Gaussian window

The test bench combines a Gaussian window with a complex oscillation. With a = 2 and ε = 1/4 = 0.25, let us calculate its response Iε(a). We will choose the contour, justify its shift and check the sign of the result using an independent argument.

  1. Define the response and all its quantities

    x is the real coordinate of the recording, from −∞ to +∞; ℝ denotes this real axis. ε, which is strictly positive, sets the width of the window; a is real and positive in our calculation. The angular frequency of the oscillation is a/ε = 8 radians per coordinate unit. All these quantities are dimensionless after choosing the scales of the test bench.

    i is the imaginary unit, defined by i × i = −1. exp denotes the exponential function. For a complex number u + iv, with u and v real, exp(u + iv) = exp(u)(cos v + i sin v): its size, or modulus, is exp(u), and its angle is v up to a whole turn. Re and Im denote the real and imaginary parts. The functions cos and sin are cosine and sine.

    Iε(a)=∫−∞+∞exp(−x22ε)exp(iaxε)dx

    The first factor is the Gaussian weight; the second rotates the contributions without changing their modulus on ℝ. The complex integral adds their real and imaginary parts separately. It converges absolutely because its modulus is bounded by the integrable Gaussian. The infinite bounds mean a limit as the cutoffs move away.

    To move the path, set z = x + iy, where y is the imaginary coordinate. The function φ below is the exponent before division by ε. A function is holomorphic on a region if it has a complex derivative there; it is entire if this property holds throughout the plane. Our polynomial φ and its exponential are entire, with no singular point.

    φ(z)=−z22+iaz

  2. Find the saddle point in the complex plane

    Here a saddle point z* is a point where φ′ vanishes. The prime denotes the derivative with respect to z; the double prime denotes its second derivative. We must search in the complex plane: on the real axis alone, the imaginary term in φ′ cannot vanish when a is positive.

    φ′(z)=−z+ia=0⇒z*=ia=2i

    The curvature φ″(z*) is -1 and does not vanish: the saddle point is nondegenerate. Substituting ia for z in the initial expression, the quadratic term gives (a × a)/2 and the linear term gives −a × a; their sum is −(a × a)/2 = -2. The negative sign follows from i × i = −1.

    φ(z)=−(z−ia)22−a22

    This identity, obtained by completing the square, is exact for every z. It will let us identify the directions of decay without ignoring the oscillations.

  3. Choose the constant phase direction that descends

    Write the horizontal path as z = t + ia, with t real. The completed square identity gives:

    φ(t+ia)=−a22−t22

    Its imaginary part is constantly 0; its real part decreases as |t| increases. The modulus of the integrand on this path is the product of the exponential at the saddle point and a decaying Gaussian in t.

    In contrast, the vertical direction z = ia + is, with s real, gives φ(ia + is) = −(a × a)/2 + (s × s)/2. Its phase is also constant, but its size increases as we move away from the saddle point. Constant phase is therefore insufficient: we must also choose the sign that gives decay.

    Why “steepest”? For a small displacement of length r and angle θ, write z − z* = r exp(iθ). Here the change in Re φ is −(r × r) cos(2θ)/2; at fixed r it is smallest when cos(2θ) = 1, hence along the horizontal direction. r is a dimensionless length and θ is an angle in radians. At the saddle point itself, the derivative is 0: the comparison concerns the quadratic term.

    For another holomorphic function with a nondegenerate saddle point, we similarly examine the product of φ″(z*) and the square of (z − z*), divided by 2: the directions we seek make this term real and negative. This selects local tangents. Finding a suitable full contour remains a further question.

  4. Prove that the contour shift is allowed

    Cauchy’s theorem states that the integral of a holomorphic function around the boundary of a rectangle is 0 when the rectangle and its interior lie in the region of holomorphy. Apply it to exp(φ(z)/ε) on the rectangle with vertices −R, R, R + ia and −R + ia, where R is a positive real cutoff. Traverse its boundary by going up on the right, then back towards the left along the top.

    We still need to show that the vertical sides no longer contribute as R tends to +∞. On z = ±R + iy, with 0 ≤ y ≤ a, expansion gives Re φ = −(R × R)/2 + (y × y)/2 − ay. Since (y × y)/2 − ay ≤ 0 in this strip, the modulus is bounded above by exp(−(R × R)/(2ε)). Each side has length a. If V denotes the sum of the moduli of the integrals along the sides, we therefore have:

    V≤2aexp(−R22ε)

    This bound tends to 0 for every fixed, strictly positive ε. The horizontal integrals also converge by Gaussian decay. Taking the reversed direction of the upper boundary into account, Cauchy’s theorem finally gives the same integral from left to right on ℝ and on ℝ + ia. Being entire allows the finite deformation; the bound on the sides allows passage to infinite contours. Both arguments are needed.

  5. Parametrize and integrate the remaining Gaussian

    On the new path z(t) = t + ia, the derivative dz/dt is 1, so dz = dt. A contour integral also depends on this parametrization factor; here it changes neither size nor phase. Taking the value of the exponential at the saddle point outside the integral gives:

    Iε(a)=exp(−a22ε)∫−∞+∞exp(−t22ε)dt

    Recall the Gaussian calculation: setting t = √ε u, where u is a new dimensionless real variable, makes the remaining integral √ε times J = ∫ℝ exp(−(u × u)/2) d u. The square of J can be written as an integral over the plane; in polar coordinates with radius ρ and angle θ, it equals 2π ∫ from 0 to +∞ of ρ exp(−(ρ × ρ)/2) dρ = 2π. The factor ρ comes from the change of area. π denotes the ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter; since J is positive, J = √(2π).

    Iε(a)=2πεexp(−a22ε)

    The equality is exact here, for every positive ε. We have not truncated any expansion: φ is already quadratic and the amplitude multiplying the exponential is 1. The response can be very small even though the modulus of the initial integrand is largest at the centre: cancellation matters.

  6. Substitute the data and read the dominant scale

    Exact data: ε = 1/4 and a = 2. First calculate the square of a: 2 × 2 = 4, then φ(z*) = −4/2 = -2. Dividing this value by ε gives -2 ÷ (1/4) = -8: this is the exponent, rather than the response itself.

    For the width factor, 2ε = 2 × (1/4) = 1/2. We keep π and the exponential symbolic so that a decimal approximation is not turned into an exact equality.

    I1/4(2)=π2exp(-8)

    The characteristic width ℓ of the peak on the contour is √ε = 1/2 = 0.50, since ℓ × ℓ = 1/4 = ε. This is not a cutoff: at t = ±ℓ, the modulus is multiplied by exp(−1/2) relative to the saddle point; contributions remain further away. The response, coordinate and width remain dimensionless on this test bench.

  7. Check without deforming the contour

    On the initial real axis, the imaginary part is odd in x and integrates to 0; the response is therefore real. The triangle inequality gives |Iε(a)| ≤ Iε(0) = √(2πε). Our formula satisfies this bound because its factor exp(−(a × a)/(2ε)) lies between 0 and 1.

    Let us also check the sign of the exponent by another method. At fixed ε, denote the initial integrand by G(x,a) = exp(−(x × x)/(2ε) + iax/ε). Its derivative with respect to x is (−x + ia)G/ε. The integral of this derivative is 0 because G tends to 0 at both ends. Thus ∫ℝ xG dx = ia Iε(a).

    Now differentiating under the integral with respect to a gives dIε/da = (i/ε)∫ℝ xG dx = −(a/ε)Iε(a). This differentiation is justified by the integrable bound |x| exp(−(x × x)/(2ε))/ε. With the condition Iε(0) = √(2πε), the differential equation gives exactly our decaying exponential again. A positive sign would fail this check.

  8. Place the method in context and state its validity limits

    As ε tends to 0 with a fixed, the contour concentrates the integral within a width of order √ε around the saddle point. This is the link with Laplace’s method: after deformation, the phase is constant and a decaying real peak remains. An algorithm that adjusts real parameters using a gradient solves a different task; here we have preserved an integral by changing its path.

    For a more general function φ, the quadratic term gives only local behaviour. We must check the remainder of the expansion on the scale √ε, the other saddle points, the endpoints and the global deformation. Crossing a singularity or having 0 curvature makes our argument insufficient. No result on large deviations or short maturities is needed for this calculation; these topics belong to the following units.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The phase is constant along the vertical direction, so I can integrate in that direction.”

    On z = ia + is, the term (s × s)/2 makes Re φ increase. Compare the modulus at equal distances from the saddle point: we need constant phase and decay, then a globally admissible contour.

    Reread the diagram of directions, then explain the arrows

  • “Simply replacing x by t + ia is enough to shift an infinite integral.”

    This shift leaves the initial axis. Justify it using Cauchy’s theorem on a finite rectangle, then check the vertical sides before taking the limit. In the final parametrization, also write dz = dt.

    Revisit the rectangle proof and the parametrization

  • “The term at the saddle point is the whole response, and the result is always exact.”

    We must also integrate across the width of the peak: here this gives the factor √(2πε). Our result is exact because of the global quadratic form and the proven deformation. A nondegenerate saddle point in another function provides only a local starting point.

    Change ε and compare the exponent with the width

Exercise 1 · Local application

Same distance, two signs

Keep the settings of the test bench. Starting from z* = 2i, make a horizontal displacement of length h = 1/2, then a vertical displacement of the same length from the saddle point. Calculate Re(φ/ε) at the endpoint in each case. Which direction descends? Is constant phase enough?

Hint 1

Use the completed square form in the example. A vertical displacement is ih.

Hint 2

The square of ih is −h × h. Then divide the change in φ by ε.

Compare with the solution

At the centre, φ(z*)/ε = -8. The squared displacement is h × h = 1/4; (h × h)/(2ε) = 1/2.

Horizontally, Re(φ/ε) = -8 − (1/2) = -17/2 = -8.50. The modulus decreases by a factor exp(−1/2). Vertically, Re(φ/ε) = -8 + (1/2) = -15/2 = -7.50: the modulus increases by a factor exp(1/2).

Both values of φ are real, so the phase is constant in both directions. The sign of the quadratic term selects the horizontal direction. This local comparison does not yet prove that shifting the initial contour is allowed.

  • I kept the same displacement length.
  • I used the negative square of ih and divided by ε.
  • I distinguish constant phase, decay and an allowed deformation.

Exercise 2 · Global justification

Do the sides of the rectangle still matter?

With a = 2 and ε = 1/4, first take R = 3. Give a bound on the sum of the moduli of the integrals along the vertical sides. Then let R tend to +∞. Explain separately the role of holomorphy and the role of this bound.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At every point on a side, the modulus is at most exp(−(R × R)/(2ε)). Multiply by the total length of the sides.

Hint 2

Do not replace a limit with a finite cutoff: for finite R, the bound is positive.

Compare with the solution

The total length is 2a = 4. For R = 3, R × R = 9, then −(R × R)/2 = -9/2 and −(R × R)/(2ε) = -18. The required bound is therefore 4 exp(-18), dimensionless; it is not 0.

For variable R, the bound 2a exp(−(R × R)/(2ε)) tends to 0. Cauchy’s theorem relates the integrals along the four edges exactly for each finite rectangle because the integrand is entire. The bound then allows the sides to be removed in the limit. The upper boundary is traversed in the reverse direction: the final equality does compare the two horizontal paths in the same direction.

Having only a saddle point and a direction of decay would give none of these guarantees. If another integrand had a singularity in the swept region, the justification would have to be reconsidered before any shift.

Check your reasoning

  • I multiply the bound on the modulus by the total length.
  • I distinguish a bound at finite R from its 0 limit.
  • I give the separate reasons for applying Cauchy’s theorem and taking the limit, including the orientations.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same scene

Narrow the window without moving the saddle point

The test bench keeps a = 2 and replaces ε with ε′ = 1/16. Here the prime names the new setting, rather than a derivative. Reconstruct the saddle point, contour, width ℓ′, exact response and its ratio to the initial response. State what still needs checking if φ is no longer quadratic.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The saddle point solves φ′(z) = 0: this equation does not depend on ε.

Hint 2

Separate √(2πε′) and exp(φ(z*)/ε′). The ratio of widths is the square root of the ratio of the ε values.

Hint 3

In the ratio of exponentials, subtract the old exponent from the new one.

Compare with the solution

The saddle point remains z* = 2i and the contour remains ℝ + 2i. The angular frequency a/ε changes from 8 to 32: this change of setting modifies both the window and the oscillation according to the rule of the test bench.

Since (1/4) × (1/4) = 1/16 = ε′, the width becomes ℓ′ = 1/4 = 0.25. The ratio ε′/ε is 1/4; the ratio ℓ′/ℓ is 1/2. The exponent is -2 ÷ (1/16) = -32 and 2ε′ = 1/8.

I1/16(2)=1/8πexp(-32)

The ratio to the initial response is 1/2 × exp(-24), since -32 − (-8) = -24. It is strictly positive and less than 1. Keeping only the change in exponent would omit the change in width.

These equalities remain exact for our quadratic φ. For another φ, we would need to check the neglected terms across the width of the peak and verify that the deformation remains admissible and the other parts of the contour remain admissible and negligible in the stated regime. A smaller ε alone does not provide this proof.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What defines our saddle point, and why search in the complex plane?

It makes the complex derivative φ′ vanish. For φ(z) = −(z × z)/2 + iaz, φ′(z) = −z + ia, so z* = ia. With a positive, no real point solves this equation.

Recall 2Which criteria distinguish descent from constant phase alone?

The phase must stay constant and Re φ must decrease as we move away from the saddle point. Here the horizontal direction adds −(t × t)/2 to φ(z*), and the vertical direction adds +(s × s)/2: both have constant phase, but only the horizontal direction descends.

Recall 3What proofs allow our translation of the infinite contour?

Cauchy’s theorem on the finite rectangle, because the integrand is entire, followed by the bound 2a exp(−(R × R)/(2ε)) on the vertical sides and its 0 limit. We also need convergence of the horizontal integrals and the correct orientations.

Recall 4What is missing if we keep only the exponential at the saddle point?

Integration across the peak, hence the factor √(2πε). Its characteristic width is √ε; it is 1/2 at the initial setting. The parametrization factor dz must also be taken into account.

Recall 5Why is our response exact, and what does not generalize automatically?

The quadratic form is exact throughout the plane, the amplitude is 1 and the deformation is justified. For another function, a local saddle point is insufficient: the remainder of the expansion, singularities, endpoints and other saddle points must be checked.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct this chain of reasoning: an oscillatory sum can cancel; the saddle point makes φ′ vanish; a contour with constant phase and a decaying modulus reveals a peak; Cauchy’s theorem and bounds on the sides allow the deformation; parametrization followed by integration across the width gives the response. Finish by explaining why this Gaussian gives an identity and what proofs are missing for a general function. An assisted answer or fluent reading does not amount to unaided recall after a delay.
