Level 4 · C253·M3

Densities

Your goalGo from a joint exchange rate–variance density to a marginal density and a conditional moment, checking masses and units before calculating the leverage.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

On a map, imagine grains of probability spread according to the euro–dollar exchange rate and its instantaneous variance at the same date. Looking only at the exchange rate axis brings together all the grains in a column. Looking at variance for a fixed exchange rate requires keeping the vertical distribution within that column.

Our map is entirely fictitious. At a horizon of 1 year, the exchange rate lies in A, from 1.00 to 1.10 euros per dollar, or in B, from 1.10 to 1.30 euros per dollar. Variance lies in the lower band, from 0.00 to 0.04 per year, or in the upper band, from 0.04 to 0.08 per year. The numbers in the cells of the first diagram are cell probabilities.

A density measures concentration per unit of area; a mass measures the probability contained in a cell. Column B is wider than A. Two cells with the same mass therefore have different densities. This difference becomes decisive on an irregular grid.

To calibrate a local stochastic volatility model, we need to recover the mean variance among states that share the same exchange rate. The marginal distribution of the exchange rate does not tell us the heights of those states. We will reconstruct this information from the joint density, without claiming to have already solved its evolution over time.

The rule to remember

Integrating a density gives a mass; summing over variance gives the marginal distribution of the exchange rate; dividing a slice by its positive marginal density gives the conditional distribution.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Recover the moment needed for calibration

The fictitious map gives the following masses: lower A 0.30, upper A 0.10, lower B 0.10, upper B 0.50. We define a density that is constant in each rectangle and 0 elsewhere. Let us calculate its marginal density, its conditional mean in A and the square of the leverage associated with a target local variance of 0.04 per year.

  1. Specify the distribution, symbols and domain

    t is the fixed horizon, here 1 year. St is the random exchange rate and Vt its instantaneous variance under the model convention: the coefficient of relative noise in the exchange rate is L(t,St)√Vt. A unit of exchange rate, denoted by u in the units below, equals 1 euro per dollar. s and v denote possible values of St and Vt, in u and per year. All distribution calculations use the same domestic pricing measure Q, meaning the model probability used for pricing in euros.

    p(t,s,v) is the joint density with respect to ds dv: its height multiplied by a small area gives approximately the corresponding probability. The units of p are year/u. The following integral adds up all the small masses over positive exchange rates and variances; the ∫ signs denote this continuous summation, and ∞ denotes an unbounded upper limit.

    p≥0,∫0∞∫0∞p(t,s,v)dvds=1

    We assume a distribution with a density, with no point mass at the boundary where variance is 0, and a finite mean of Vt. Our distribution defined by rectangles is a construction at this single date, rather than a verified output of a calibrated dynamic model. The intervals are closed on the left and open on the right; their boundaries have 0 probability.

  2. Go from masses to joint density heights

    The width of A is Δs_A = 1.10 − 1.00 = 0.10 u. The width of B is Δs_B = 1.30 − 1.10 = 0.20 u. Each band has width Δv = 0.04 per year. Here Δ means “interval width”. The areas are therefore 0.004 u/year in A and 0.008 u/year in B.

    For a constant density, mass = height × area. We therefore recover the height by dividing the mass by the area. In lower A: 0.30 ÷ 0.004 = 75 year/u. In upper A: 0.10 ÷ 0.004 = 25 year/u. In lower B: 0.10 ÷ 0.008 = 12.5 year/u. In upper B: 0.50 ÷ 0.008 = 62.5 year/u.

    The upper A and lower B cells have the same mass 0.10, but their densities differ: B is wider. A density can exceed 1; its integrated mass must stay between 0 and 1.

  3. Integrate out variance

    q(t,s) denotes the marginal density of the exchange rate, in units of per u. We keep s fixed and add up masses along v. On our grid, the integral becomes a sum of heights p multiplied by Δv, never a sum of heights alone.

    q(t,s)=∫0∞p(t,s,v)dv

    In A: (75 + 25) × 0.04 = 4 per u. In B: (12.5 + 62.5) × 0.04 = 3 per u. Check by another route: A contains 0.40, so q_A = 0.40 ÷ 0.10 = 4 per u; B contains 0.60, so q_B = 0.60 ÷ 0.20 = 3 per u.

    Normalization is preserved: 4 × 0.10 + 3 × 0.20 = 0.40 + 0.60 = 1. Here the densities are 0 outside the intervals shown.

  4. Normalize a slice to obtain a conditional distribution

    h(t,v|s) is the conditional density of variance when the exchange rate equals s; its units are years. The vertical bar means “given”. For strictly positive q(t,s), we divide the joint density slice by its mass per unit of exchange rate.

    h(t,v|s)=p(t,s,v)q(t,s),∫0∞h(t,v|s)dv=1

    In A, the conditional probability of the upper band is 0.10 ÷ 0.40 = 0.25. Integrating p/q over this band would give the same result. An exact exchange rate value has 0 probability in this continuous distribution: the ratio of densities defines a version of the conditional distribution, rather than dividing by the probability of St = s.

    Our density is constant in each rectangle, so the conditional distribution is identical for all s in the interior of the same column. Thus, conditioning on a value s in A and conditioning on the event that St is in A give the same result here. This particular equality generally disappears when p varies within cells.

  5. Integrate variance, then divide

    m(t,s) = E_Q[Vt|St = s] is the required conditional mean, in units of per year; E_Q denotes an expectation under Q. Replacing h with p/q, its calculation uses an unnormalized moment in the numerator and the marginal density in the denominator.

    m(t,s)=∫0∞vp(t,s,v)dvq(t,s)

    In a uniform band, the mean of v is the midpoint of its bounds: 0.02 per year in the lower band, 0.06 per year in the upper band. This is exact here, because the integral of v between the bounds equals the width multiplied by their midpoint. In an actual grid approximation, using cell centers can instead introduce a quadrature error.

    In A, the moment weighted by the masses is 0.02 × 0.30 + 0.06 × 0.10 = 0.006 + 0.006 = 0.012 per year. We divide by the mass of A: 0.012 ÷ 0.40 = 0.030 per year. The width factor Δs_A cancels between numerator and denominator; this is why we can use the column masses directly.

    Check: the mean 0.030 lies between the band means 0.02 and 0.06, closer to the lower band, which carries more conditional mass. Without division by 0.40, we would retain an unnormalized moment.

  6. Interpret the consequence for leverage

    σ_loc(t,s) is the target local volatility, in units of per square root of a year. Its square is a variance per year. L(t,s) is the dimensionless, nonnegative leverage. We reuse the projection relation from the previous unit, for a continuous exchange rate diffusion with deterministic interest rates and finite conditional moments. It relates instantaneous variances under the same measure Q:

    L2(t,s)m(t,s)=σloc2(t,s)

    When m is strictly positive, we can divide by it. With the target 0.04 per year in A, the square of L is 0.04 ÷ 0.030 = 4/3. L is the positive square root of this fraction. Check: (4/3) × 0.030 = 0.04 per year. Distinguish L from its square and variance from volatility.

    This quotient alone does not perform the calibration: the density depends on the dynamics and on the leverage we are trying to find. The map provides only a slice for checking the conditional calculation. Constructing a density that is consistent across all dates remains a separate problem.

  7. Check before trusting the quotient

    In B, the same calculation gives m_B = 4/75 per year, or approximately 0.0533 per year. The reconstructed overall mean is 0.40 × 0.030 + 0.60 × (4/75) = 0.044 per year. Independent check: the total masses of the lower and upper bands are 0.40 and 0.60; their weighted average 0.02 × 0.40 + 0.06 × 0.60 gives the same value. The equality of band masses and column masses is specific to the chosen data.

    For a numerically computed density, check positivity, masses using the actual widths, moments and sensitivity to grid refinement. A truncated domain must account for masses that cross its boundaries. If the distribution has a mass at the boundary Vt = 0, it requires separate treatment from the interior density.

    If q(t,s) = 0, this quotient does not define m at that point. If q is very small, errors in the numerator and denominator can be amplified. Global renormalization does not repair negative cells, an incorrect vertical distribution or a truncated tail; the source of the error must be diagnosed.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Cell probabilities are the values of the density.”

    A cell carries a dimensionless mass. Divide it by Δs Δv to obtain a density in year/u. For the same mass, a wider cell has a smaller height. To recover the probability, multiply by the area again.

    Review the conversion from masses to heights

  • “The marginal distribution of the exchange rate is enough to recover leverage.”

    It gives the mass of each column, but removes the vertical position. Leverage also requires the conditional mean of variance. The last exercise preserves the marginal distribution while changing this mean.

    Construct the second distribution in the last exercise

  • “If the quotient is unstable, I replace the denominator with a small constant.”

    A small marginal density signals a sparsely represented region where relative error can be large. An added constant changes the conditional distribution; it must be justified and its bias studied. Start by checking masses, positivity, domain and resolution; where the marginal density is 0, state that the quotient does not define the value.

    Reread the checks before the quotient

Same mass, different area

Hint 2

Compare with the solution

Area: 0.20 × 0.04 = 0.008 u/year. Density: 0.50 ÷ 0.008 = 62.5 year/u. In reverse, 62.5 × 0.008 = 0.50, dimensionless.

The bound 1 applies to a probability. A density is a concentration with units; its value can exceed this bound while still giving admissible masses through integration.

Exercise 2 · Conditional calculation

Column B leads to a different leverage

In B, the lower and upper masses are 0.10 and 0.50. Calculate the conditional probability of the upper band, the mean m_B and the square of L for the same target 0.04 per year. Keep exact fractions until the final check.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The conditional denominator is the entire mass of B.

Hint 2

The mean of each uniform band is its midpoint; weight these midpoints before dividing.

Compare with the solution

Mass of B: 0.10 + 0.50 = 0.60. The conditional probability of the upper band is 0.50 ÷ 0.60 = 5/6.

Moment before normalization: 0.02 × 0.10 + 0.06 × 0.50 = 0.002 + 0.030 = 0.032 per year. Thus m_B = 0.032 ÷ 0.60 = 4/75 per year, approximately 0.0533 per year. The mean is closer to the upper band, which carries most of the weight.

The square of L is 0.04 ÷ (4/75) = 3/4. Exact check: (3/4) × (4/75) = 0.04 per year. The leverage itself is the positive square root of 3/4, dimensionless.

Check your reasoning

  • Condition using the mass 0.60, without substituting the total mass.
  • Obtain 4/75 per year and justify its position between the band midpoints.
  • Obtain 3/4 for the square of the leverage, then check the exact product and distinguish L from its square.

Exercise 3 · Counterexample and diagnosis

The same marginal hides another distribution

On the same map, swap only the lower and upper masses of A: they become 0.10 below and 0.30 above. B stays the same. Has the marginal distribution of the exchange rate changed? Calculate the new conditional mean in A. Are a correct total mass and an identical marginal distribution enough to certify the joint density?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The total for a column does not tell us where its mass is placed.

Hint 2

Recalculate the vertical moment in A, then divide by its preserved mass.

Compare with the solution

The mass of A remains 0.40 and its width remains 0.10 u: q_A remains 4 per u. B stays the same, so the entire marginal distribution of the exchange rate is preserved, along with the total mass 1.

The new moment in A is 0.02 × 0.10 + 0.06 × 0.30 = 0.002 + 0.018 = 0.020 per year. The new mean is 0.020 ÷ 0.40 = 0.050 per year, different from 0.030 per year.

The identical marginal distribution preserves expectations of functions of the terminal exchange rate alone, when those expectations exist. It does not determine the joint distribution with variance. This counterexample at a single date does not prove that both maps come from consistent local stochastic volatility dynamics; it shows precisely what information a marginal check leaves undetermined.

Check your reasoning

  • Demonstrate preservation of the marginal distribution using masses and widths.
  • Calculate 0.050 per year and compare it with 0.030 per year.
  • Explain the limit of the check without claiming to have constructed a second dynamic calibration.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you go from a constant joint density height to a cell probability?

Multiply p by the exchange rate width and the variance width. The mass is dimensionless; here p has units of years per unit of exchange rate. On an irregular grid, the widths specific to each cell are essential.

Recall 2What does the marginal density q(t,s) preserve, and what does it lose?

It integrates p over variance: it preserves the distribution of the exchange rate and loses the distribution of variances for a given exchange rate. Different joint densities can have the same marginal.

Recall 3Reconstruct the conditional moment from p, rather than just reciting its name.

For a positive marginal density, integrate v p over v, then divide by the integral of p over v. The result is a mean variance per year. In a column of our map with a uniform density in each rectangle, we can also weight the band midpoints by the masses, then divide by the column mass.

Recall 4What checks come before calculating leverage from a numerical density?

Check positivity, masses using the correct areas, moments, boundary treatment and sensitivity to the grid. A 0 marginal density does not define the quotient; a very small marginal density can amplify error. A point mass at the boundary requires separate treatment.

Recall 5Why is a density needed to relate local variance and leverage?

It provides m, the conditional mean of variance for a fixed exchange rate. The target local variance divided by m gives the square of L when m is positive. The density itself depends on the dynamics and the leverage: calculating this quotient on an isolated map does not prove a complete calibration.
