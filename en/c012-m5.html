Level 1 · C012·M5

Counterexamples

Your goalConstruct a counterexample that respects the domain and conditions of a claim, then state exactly what it refutes.

Before you start
c004-m5 : rational equations · c006-m5 : composition · c012-m1 : logical connectives · c012-m2 : sets · c012-m3 : quantifiers · c012-m4 : necessary and sufficient

✿01 See the idea

A batch contains boxes A, B and C. Someone announces: “In this batch, every labelled box is closed.” You look at A and B: they have labels and their lids are closed. This gives 2 confirmations. The label on C is visible, but a screen still hides its lid.

Before reading on, state the observation that would disprove the claim. Another labelled, closed box would support it. To contradict it, you need to find a labelled box in the stated batch whose lid is not closed.

A counterexample is a case that meets the initial conditions of a claim and makes its conclusion fail. You can point to it and check its properties. It is more than an objection: “I do not believe it” identifies no case to examine.

The boxes and their states are fictional. The batch is fixed and contains only A, B and C. During each inspection, the labels and lids do not change. In this scene, each lid is either closed or open; none is partly open or impossible to observe once the screen is removed. These assumptions make our verdicts precise.

The rule to remember

To refute “every labelled box in the batch is closed”, keep the same batch and the label present, then show an open lid. Confirmations do not cancel out this exception.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The lid on C disproves “every”

In our fictional batch, A and B are labelled and closed. C is labelled; its lid, initially hidden, turns out to be open. Let us write the exact claim, look for its counterexample, then count and check what the inspections allow us to conclude.

  1. Fix what we are talking about

    U denotes the batch: U = {A, B, C}. The braces list its members; x denotes any box. The symbol ∈ is read “belongs to”. The letter x is a name for a box, rather than a length measurement. The domain U is part of the claim: a box outside this batch cannot refute it.

    E(x) means “x has a label”; F(x) means “the lid of x is closed”. Each statement is true or false after inspection. These properties have no physical unit. The numbers that follow count boxes; they express neither a probability nor a degree of truth.

  2. Write the claim and the search target

    The symbol ∀ is read “for every”. The arrow ⇒ denotes implication: if the condition E(x) is true, the conclusion F(x) must also be true. The claim is written:

    ∀x∈U,(E(x)⇒F(x))

    To refute it, we look for a box in the domain that has a label and is not closed. The symbol ∃ means “there exists at least one”; ∧ means “and”, and ¬ means “not”. The search is written:

    ∃x∈U,(E(x)∧¬F(x))

    This target keeps the condition and negates only the conclusion for the chosen case. Here, under the assumption about lids, “not closed” means “open”. A box without a label, even if open, is not a counterexample to this implication.

  3. Count what remains unknown

    The batch contains 1 box A + 1 box B + 1 box C, making 3 boxes. The separate inspections of A and B cover 2 of them. We subtract the inspected boxes from the total to find how many are still hidden:

    3−2=1 boîte à inspecter

    The 2 supporting observations tell us nothing about the remaining lid. Before its inspection, the available information still leaves the universal claim undecided: C could be closed or open. “Not yet refuted” therefore does not mean “proved”.

  4. Produce the decisive witness

    We remove the screen: C is open. Let us check each requirement separately. C belongs to U; its label is present, so E(C) is true; its lid is open, so F(C) is false and ¬F(C) is true. Together, these findings give:

    C∈U∧E(C)∧¬F(C)

    The letter C now replaces the box x we were looking for. We have a verified witness: precisely the case ruled out by the claim. It is enough to declare the claim false; we do not need to wait for a majority of counterexamples.

  5. Complete the count

    To check the record, we classify each inspected box. A contributes 1 confirmation and 0 counterexamples; B contributes 1 confirmation and 0 counterexamples; C contributes 0 confirmations and 1 counterexample. Here, a confirmation means a labelled, closed box; a counterexample means a labelled, open box.

    Confirmations: 1 + 1 + 0 = 2 boxes. Counterexamples from A and B: 0 + 0 = 0 boxes. Including C: 0 + 1 = 1 box. We add the contributions of distinct boxes, without counting the same inspection more than once.

    The claim requires 0 counterexamples in the fully inspected batch. We find 1, so it is false. The 2 confirmations remain true as individual observations; they do not cancel out the counterexample.

  6. Check the total and the scope of the verdict

    2+1=3 boîtes

    The total 3 does match the 3 members of U. This check works here because all the boxes are labelled and each is either open or closed. Reviewing the names A, B, C also checks that no box has been counted twice. The count alone would not replace checking the properties of C.

    We have shown that “there exists a labelled box in this batch that is not closed”. We have not shown that “all labelled boxes are open”: A and B are closed. If the last lid had also been closed, a complete inspection of this finite batch would have been enough to verify the claim about U. Partial inspections, or a different batch, would not justify the same verdict.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the example. Justify the roles of the batch, the label and the lid in the refutation using C. Then explain why looking only at A and B could not settle the claim, and why C does not prove that all the boxes are open.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “There are more confirmations than exceptions, so the claim holds.”

    The word “every” does not call for a vote. The 2 confirmations describe A and B; C is still a member of the batch that satisfies the condition and makes the conclusion fail. The number of counterexamples is 1, whereas it would have to be 0 for the claim to be true for the inspected batch.

    Review the witness and the complete count

  • “Any open box is a counterexample.”

    Also check membership of the batch and the presence of the label. A box outside the batch changes the domain. A box in the batch without a label does not satisfy the initial condition; its open lid does not refute “labelled ⇒ closed”.

    Construct a valid case in the warm-up

  • “Since “all closed” is false, they are all open.”

    The refutation provides an exception, rather than a new rule for every box. Keep A and B in your reasoning: they are labelled and closed. They immediately contradict “all labelled boxes are open”.

    Restate what the negation actually asserts

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · choose and justify

What state should you give C?

In a new fictional trial with the same batch U, you choose the state of C to challenge the same claim. Which state works: unlabelled and open; labelled and open; labelled and closed? C remains in U in every choice. Also justify rejecting the other choices.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start by keeping the condition E(C) true.

Hint 2

Then F(C), the conclusion “C is closed”, must be false.

Compare with the solution

Choose C labelled and open. It belongs to U, satisfies E(C) and makes F(C) fail: it is a valid counterexample.

Unlabelled and open, it does not satisfy the condition E(C). Labelled and closed, it satisfies the conclusion and confirms the rule for this case. A failure of the condition is not the failure of the implication we are looking for.

Check your reasoning

  • You choose the labelled, open case.
  • You name the domain, the true condition and the false conclusion.
  • You explain why each other state is rejected, rather than simply giving the correct choice.

You next inspect C directly and find that it is labelled and open: do you need to look at A and B again before declaring the claim false? Give the number of decisive counterexamples and justify the verdicts.

Hint 1

Subtract the 2 distinct inspected boxes from the 3 boxes in the batch.

Hint 2

To refute the claim, you look for a witness that meets the conditions. To establish the claim by inspecting this batch, you would need to cover all the relevant members.

Compare with the solution

There is 3 − 2 = 1 unknown box left. The observations of A and B leave open the possibility that C is a counterexample: the claim has not been proved.

After inspecting C, we have 1 valid counterexample. This witness is enough to refute the claim about U, even without looking at A and B again. The calculated number of boxes still hidden, 1, describes the information missing before this inspection; it did not allow us to guess their state.

Check your reasoning

  • You write the subtraction with its result in boxes.
  • You distinguish the unknown verdict before C from the false verdict after C.
  • You explain why refuting the claim with a witness does not require recounting the confirmations.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · written refutation

Repair an excessive conclusion

After seeing C open, someone writes: “So no labelled box in U is closed. And if I remove C from the batch, my original claim was true.” Write the exact conclusion warranted by C, refute the claim “no labelled box”, then explain what removing C changes. Keep the properties of A and B in your answer.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The negation of “all” requires an exception; it does not replace “all” with “none”.

Hint 2

After removing C, the domain is {A, B}. It is no longer U = {A, B, C}.

Compare with the solution

Exact conclusion: C belongs to U, has a label and is not closed. So there exists a labelled box in U that is not closed; the original claim about U is false.

“No labelled box in U is closed” is also false: A is labelled and closed, and so is B. A alone would already be enough to refute this new claim.

Removing C leaves 2 labelled, closed boxes. The claim restricted to {A, B} is true, since we have inspected this entire new batch. This does not make the original claim about U true: the domain has changed. Revising a statement and proving the old statement are different acts.

Check your reasoning

  • You give a complete witness for the exact negation of the original claim.
  • You use A or B to refute “none”, without erasing the exception C.
  • You explicitly distinguish the original batch from the reduced batch and the claims about each.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What properties must a counterexample to “in this batch, labelled ⇒ closed” have?

It belongs to the stated batch, has a label and is not closed. It respects the domain and the initial condition, then makes the conclusion fail.

Recall 2Why do closed boxes A and B not settle the claim while the lid of C is hidden?

Their states do not determine the state of C. C could still be labelled and open; 2 confirmations in a batch of 3 boxes do not cover the whole domain.

Recall 3Why is open box C enough, despite the confirmations from A and B?

C is a box in the batch, labelled and not closed: it provides the case ruled out by the claim. A claim about every relevant box fails as soon as such a witness exists; it does not depend on a vote.

Recall 4What exact conclusion follows from the refutation, and what excessive conclusion should you avoid?

There exists a labelled box in the batch that is not closed. We cannot conclude that all labelled boxes are open: A and B are labelled and closed.

Recall 5Why does an open box outside U, or a box in U without a label, fail to refute the claim?

The first does not belong to the stated domain. The second does not satisfy the condition “labelled”. Neither meets the requirements for the counterexample we are looking for.
