Level 3 · C222·M3

Interaction

Your goalCalculate a group–time interaction, identify it in a cross term, and distinguish the observed contrast from statistical or causal evidence for it.

Before you start
c074-m5 : identification assumptions · c145-m5 : policy evaluation · c073-m5 : interaction and repeated measures · c081-m5 : experimental ethics · c222-m1 : protocol · c222-m2 : repeated measures

✿01 See the idea

A team wants to evaluate attention training. In our entirely fictional study, one group completes this training and a control group completes a comparison activity of the same duration. Each person takes the same type of test before and after. The score counts correct answers out of 30 questions; one point corresponds to one correct answer. These values serve only to explain the calculation: they are not evidence of effectiveness.

The training group improves. But the controls also improve: having encountered the test before may, for example, contribute to a change shared by both groups. The useful question becomes: how much do their changes differ? A group–time interaction here means that the mean change depends on the group, on the chosen point scale.

Look at the means in the first diagram. The training group starts lower and remains slightly lower afterwards. Yet its change is larger. Comparing only the final scores and comparing the changes therefore answer different questions.

We follow the same people at both times, with no missing measurements in this example. The calculation is descriptive: it concerns this small fictional sample. By itself, it settles neither uncertainty in a population nor whether the change can be attributed to the training.

The rule to remember

Fix the score and the order of subtraction; calculate after minus before in each group; subtract the control change from the training change. An interaction compares changes.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Training, a comparison, and a contrast

The fictional design specifies random allocation to training or comparison, an identical schedule, and complete measurement of the same people. The before → after pairs, in points, are: training, person A 10 → 13, person B 14 → 22; control, person C 14 → 15, person D 18 → 21. Each person answers all the questions, so an error is an incorrect answer.

  1. Define the means, the scale, and the comparison

    E denotes the training group and T the control group. The labels av and ap denote before and after. For example, m(E,av) is the mean of the scores observed before in E, in points. Each mean uses 2 people with equal weights; the individual pairs remain identified.

    Δ, the letter delta, here means mean change: after minus before. I denotes our interaction contrast, training minus control. Means, changes, and I are in points; the group and time labels have no unit.

    To make these subtractions, we keep the same scoring system, the same criterion, and the same people. Interpreting I as a causal effect would additionally require adherence to random allocation, an actually comparable comparison, and no influence between groups; a sentence describing the design does not verify its implementation.

  2. Reconstruct the observed means

    Add the scores in a group at a given time, then divide by the number of people in that group: each person contributes with equal weight.

    Training before: (10 + 14)/2 = 24/2 = 12 points. After: (13 + 22)/2 = 35/2 = 35/2 points, or 17.5 points.

    Control before: (14 + 18)/2 = 32/2 = 16 points. After: (15 + 21)/2 = 36/2 = 18 points. A mean need not be a whole number even when every individual score is.

  3. Calculate the changes while preserving the pairs

    For E, A changes by 13 − 10 = 3 points; B changes by 22 − 14 = 8 points. Their mean is (3 + 8)/2 = 11/2 = 5.5 points. This also gives 17.5 − 12 = 5.5 points.

    For T, C changes by 15 − 14 = 1 point; D changes by 21 − 18 = 3 points. Their mean is (1 + 3)/2 = 4/2 = 2 points, the same as 18 − 16.

    The mean of the changes equals the difference between the means because the same people have the same weights before and after. Quantifying uncertainty would also require preserving this dependence between measurements: a person’s before and after observations are not additional people.

  4. Compare the changes and check by another method

    I=ΔE−ΔT=(m(E,ap)−m(E,av))−(m(T,ap)−m(T,av))

    The control change provides the comparison: 5.5 − 2 = 3.5 points. In this fictional sample, the score increase is therefore 3.5 points larger in E. This number does not mean that each person receives this extra amount.

    Check independently of how the terms are grouped: the E minus T gap is 12 − 16 = -4 points before and 17.5 − 18 = -0.5 point after. The change in this gap is -0.5 − (-4) = 3.5 points. This gives I again. The final gap remains negative despite a positive I.

  5. Recognize the interaction in a model of means

    Code the group as g: 0 for T, 1 for E; code time as t: 0 before, 1 after. The codes g and t are dimensionless. Write a representation of the means in this sample, m(g,t), in points. The letters a, b, c, and d are coefficients in points.

    m(g,t)=a+bg+ct+dgt

    a = 16 points is the control mean before. b = -4 points is the initial E minus T gap. c = 2 points is the control change. The product g × t is 0 except in the training after cell, where it is 1. Its coefficient d therefore adds the extra change specific to this cell: d = I = 3.5 points.

    In T, the change is c. In E, it is c + d. Subtracting them gives d. Check the last cell: a + b + c + d = 16 + (-4) + 2 + 3.5 = 17.5 points, the same as the E mean after. The model represents our means exactly; this does not yet provide a certain estimate of the coefficients in a population.

  6. State the exact scope of the result

    With no additive interaction, d would be 0 points: the change would be the same in both groups, even if their starting levels differed. In our example, this would put E after at 12 + 2 = 14 points. The gap from the observed 17.5 points gives 3.5 points again.

    Evaluating a study still requires examining the uncertainty of the contrast itself, using an analysis that accounts for the pairs. A statistically detected change in E and an undetected change in T do not constitute a test of their difference. No test, confidence interval, or decision threshold is calculated on this page.

    Finally, an interaction on the score does not identify a brain mechanism. The validity of the measurement, the implementation of the design, and the causal assumptions require their own evidence. The very small fictional sample makes the calculation transparent without supporting a conclusion about the effectiveness of actual training.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results. Reconstruct I from the pairs, then from the gaps between groups. Explain why the coefficient of the product g × t represents this contrast and why a positive I can coexist with a lower final score in E.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The training group finishes lower: there is therefore no interaction.”

    You are comparing the final levels. Reconstruct the initial gap too: it goes from -4 points to -0.5 point, a change of 3.5 points. This change in the gap is what the group–time interaction describes.

    Repeat the check using the gaps between groups

  • “The change is detected in one group and not in the other: their difference is proven.”

    The separate analyses examine each change relative to no change. The interaction examines their difference. Ask for an estimate and uncertainty for this contrast, preserving the dependence between a person’s measurements.

    Reread the statistical scope of the contrast

  • “A positive interaction is always favorable.”

    First read what the score measures and in what order you subtract. Here, more correct answers is favorable. If you count errors, the same favorable change will give a negative interaction: the coding changes, while the people’s answers stay the same.

    Repeat the same test by counting errors

Exercise 3 · Transfer and critical reading

The same test, scored as errors

Return to the original observations. Every question was answered: the error score is 30 minus the correct answer score. Calculate the mean numbers of errors, their changes, and the E minus T interaction, still using after minus before. Someone claims: “The sign has become negative, so the training is unfavorable.” Respond, then state what is missing for a conclusion about a population.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The mean number of errors is 30 minus the mean number of correct answers. The constant cancels when you calculate a change.

Hint 2

Fewer errors is favorable on this criterion. Separate the meaning of the score from the uncertainty of the contrast.

Compare with the solution

E before: 30 − 12 = 18 errors; E after: 30 − 17.5 = 12.5 errors. T before: 30 − 16 = 14 errors; T after: 30 − 18 = 12 errors.

The E change is 12.5 − 18 = -5.5 errors; the T change is 12 − 14 = -2 errors. The interaction is -5.5 − (-2) = -3.5 errors. It is the negative of the interaction in correct answers.

The error score decreases more in E. The negative sign here expresses this additional decrease; it does not prove an unfavorable effect. The observed answers have not changed. Generalizing requires quantifying the uncertainty of the contrast using an analysis that respects the pairs and examining the target population; a causal conclusion also requires justifying the comparison and its implementation.

Check your reasoning

  • Give the means with the new unit: number of errors.
  • Recover -3.5 errors without reversing the order of subtraction.
  • Explain the sign through the score and distinguish descriptive interpretation, uncertainty, and causality.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can a group–time interaction be reconstructed from the means?

On a fixed scale: calculate after minus before in each group, then training change minus control change. You can check by subtracting the initial gap between groups from their final gap. The contrast keeps the unit of the score.

Recall 2In m(g,t) = a + bg + ct + dgt, why does d represent the difference between the changes?

With g = 0 for T, g = 1 for E, t = 0 before, and t = 1 after, T changes by c and E changes by c + d. Their difference is d. The product g × t activates this extra amount only in E after; the coefficients are in points.

Recall 3Does no additive interaction require identical means across groups?

It requires equal changes, or d = 0 points, and allows a constant initial gap. In the variant, E after is 14 points and T after is 18 points: the levels still differ.

Recall 4Why is a change detected in one group and not in the other insufficient?

These conclusions concern two separate changes. Their difference and its uncertainty must be analyzed directly, accounting for repeated measures. An observed nonzero contrast alone does not prove an effect in the population or its cause.

Recall 5Why does the contrast change sign when switching from correct answers to errors?

The new score is 30 minus the old one. The constant disappears in each change, which becomes the negative of the previous change; so does the contrast. Here 3.5 points becomes -3.5 errors. The change being described remains the same.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without support: specify the score and the pairs; recover the changes in each group; form and check their difference; explain the coefficient of the group × time product; interpret the sign according to the scoring system; separate the descriptive contrast from its uncertainty and from a causal conclusion.
