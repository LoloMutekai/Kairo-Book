Level 3 · C172·M3

Ornstein-Uhlenbeck

Your goalSolve the Ornstein-Uhlenbeck equation, derive the mean and variance at time t, then propagate them hour by hour using the factor r and the limiting variance.

Before you start
c171-m5 : multidimensional case · c052-m5 : convergence protocol · c172-m1 : existence and uniqueness · c172-m2 : GBM

✿01 See the idea

A container of water is placed in a room kept at 20 °C. It has just come out of a bath at 28 °C. Its temperature approaches that of the room, faster when the gap is larger, but irregular draughts give it random shocks. The entire scene is fictional: the numbers are used for calculation and do not describe any real readings.

Two effects act against each other. Mean reversion is deterministic: it reduces the gap from room temperature in proportion to that gap. The shock is random: it adds uncertainty, with the same magnitude whatever the temperature. Without shocks, the temperature would follow an exponential decay; without mean reversion, it would be Brownian motion, whose spread grows without bound. The Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process combines these effects.

We want to find the mean temperature of the water and its spread after one hour, then two. Mean reversion eventually balances the shocks, keeping the spread bounded: this is the difference from the GBM in the previous unit.

The rule to remember

Each hour, the deviation from the mean is multiplied by r; the variance is multiplied by r², then the shock variance, v∞(1 − r²), is added.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A container of water returning towards room temperature

The water starts at 28 °C, known exactly, in a room at 20 °C. Over one hour, mean reversion multiplies the gap from room temperature by r = 0.6, and the limiting variance is 6.25 °C². The values are fictional. Let us calculate the temperature distribution after one hour, then two.

  1. Set up the model and name each symbol

    The fictional container follows a linear stochastic differential equation: at each instant, a deterministic restoring effect pushes the temperature towards room temperature, and a Brownian shock is added.

    dXt=θ(μ−Xt)dt+σdWt

    t is time, in hours. Xt is the water temperature, in °C. μ is the equilibrium temperature, that of the room, in °C. θ is the mean reversion rate, strictly positive, in inverse hours: the larger θ is, the faster the gap from μ shrinks. σ is the shock amplitude, strictly positive, in °C per square root of an hour. Wt is standard Brownian motion: its increments are Gaussian, with 0 mean and variance equal to the duration, in hours; it is independent of the initial temperature X0.

    Assumptions: μ, θ and σ are constant; the shock is additive, with magnitude σ whatever the temperature, whereas in the GBM of the previous unit it was proportional to the state; the coefficients are affine, hence Lipschitz, which places the model within the framework of the unit on existence and uniqueness.

  2. Solve by multiplying by e to the power θt

    Without shocks, the deviation X − μ would decay exponentially. To handle mean reversion and shocks together, apply the Ito formula to the product of e to the power θt and Xt, where e to the power θt is deterministic and differentiable: the formula then adds no quadratic variation term, and only the product rule is needed. Substituting the equation for dXt makes the terms in Xt cancel.

    d(eθtXt)=θμeθtdt+σeθtdWt

    Integrating from time 0 to t, then dividing by e to the power θt, gives the explicit solution:

    Xt=μ+(X0−μ)e−θt+σ∫0te−θ(t−s)dWs

    s is an integration variable, in hours. The last term is an Ito integral with a deterministic integrand: the shock at time s is damped by the factor e to the power −θ(t − s) up to time t.

  3. Read off the mean, variance and distribution

    An Ito integral with an adapted, square integrable integrand has 0 mean. The mean of Xt therefore reduces to the deterministic term:

    E[Xt]=μ+(X0−μ)e−θt

    The Ito isometry gives the variance of the random term: it is the integral of the squared integrand. Evaluating the integral in s gives:

    V(t)=σ2∫0te−2θ(t−s)ds=σ22θ(1−e−2θt)

    We accept here that the integral of a deterministic function against W is Gaussian: Xt then follows a normal distribution with this mean and variance. As t increases, the mean tends to μ and the variance tends to a limit, in °C²:

    v∞=σ22θ

  4. Move to an hourly step

    The calculations proceed hour by hour. Apply the same solution between times nΔt and (n + 1)Δt, with Δt = 1 h. Set r = e to the power −θΔt, a dimensionless factor strictly between 0 and 1. Xn is the temperature at time nΔt.

    Xn+1=μ+r(Xn−μ)+εn
    εn∼N(0,v∞(1−r2)), avec r=e−θΔt

    εn is the accumulated shock over the hour: its mean is 0, its variance is v∞(1 − r²) in °C² from the previous variance formula evaluated at duration Δt, and it is independent of Xn because it depends only on future increments of W. Let Vn denote the variance of Xn. Independence removes the cross term:

    Vn+1=r2Vn+v∞(1−r2)

    The factor r acts on the deviation, so r² acts on the variance: a variance is a mean of squared deviations. We specify r and v∞ rather than θ and σ; they give θ = −ln r / Δt and σ² = 2 θ v∞, without needing their decimal values here. Fictional data: μ = 20 °C; X0 = 28 °C known exactly, so V0 = 0; r = 3/5 = 0.6; limiting standard deviation 2.5 °C, giving v∞ = 6.25 °C².

  5. Calculate the first hour

    Initial deviation: 28 − 20 = 8 °C. Mean reversion multiplies it by r. The mean is μ plus the remaining deviation:

    m1=20+0.6×8=20+4.8=24.8 °C

    The shock over one hour has variance v∞(1 − r²) = 6.25 × 0.64 = 4 °C². Since the initial temperature is known exactly, the variance after one hour comes from this shock alone:

    V1=0.62×0+6.25×0.64=0+4=4 °C²

    After one hour, the model therefore gives a normal distribution with mean 24.8 °C and variance 4 °C², or a standard deviation of 2 °C.

  6. Calculate the second hour

    Start again from the mean value and variance just obtained. The new deviation from μ is 0.6 × 4.8 = 2.88 °C:

    m2=20+0.6×4.8=20+2.88=22.88 °C

    The existing variance is contracted by r² = 0.36, then the new shock is added:

    V2=0.36×4+4=1.44+4=5.44 °C²

  7. Check using the direct formula and the limit

    Variance check: the closed form over two hours replaces two steps with a single step, using r to the power four.

    V2=6.25×(1−0.1296)=6.25×0.8704=5.44 °C²

    Mean check: the direct deviation is r² × 8 = 0.36 × 8 = 2.88 °C, as with the recurrence. Limit check: the variance stays below v∞, and its distance from that limit is given by the closed form:

    6.25−5.44=0.81=6.25×0.1296 °C²

    Unit check: means and deviations are in °C, variances in °C²; keep them separate. An independent check appears in calculs.py: enumeration of a finite distribution, then numerical integration of the mean and variance equations using θ derived from r.

  8. Interpret the result within its limits

    Under these assumptions, after two hours, the water temperature is described by a normal distribution with mean 22.88 °C, still 2.88 °C away from room temperature, and variance 5.44 °C². The mean never becomes exactly μ: the deviation is multiplied by r each hour without becoming 0. An individual path can cross μ, however, because the shocks persist. These numbers do not describe any real container: θ and σ have not been estimated from any readings.

Explain it in your own words

Why is the variance after two hours greater than after one hour while still below the limit? Explain using r² and the shock variance.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The shocks add up, so the variance keeps growing like that of Brownian motion.”

    Each hour, mean reversion contracts the existing variance by r². It rises towards σ²/(2θ), without exceeding it: in the example, 5.44 °C² stays below the limit 6.25 °C².

    Review the limit check

  • “The mean reaches room temperature after a finite time, once mean reversion has brought it all the way back.”

    The deviation is multiplied by r, never by 0: after two hours, 2.88 °C remains on average. It decreases without becoming 0, although an individual path can cross μ.

    Review the calculation for two hours

  • “For the variance, multiply by r as for the mean.”

    A variance is a mean of squared deviations: multiplying a deviation by r multiplies its square by r². Using r alone, the third hour would give 7.264 °C², above the limit 6.25 °C², which is impossible here.

    Recalculate the third hour

Exercise 3 · Transfer

An uncertain initial temperature

The initial temperature is no longer known exactly: it is modelled by a normal distribution with mean 28 °C and variance v∞ = 6.25 °C², independent of future shocks. Calculate the mean and variance one hour later, compare with the example, then state what remains of the initial uncertainty.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The initial variance is now 6.25 °C², rather than 0.

Hint 2

Use the same recurrence: r² times the initial variance, plus the shock variance.

Compare with the solution

The mean does not depend on the variance: it is still 24.8 °C. Variance: r² × v∞ + v∞(1 − r²) = 2.25 + 4 = 6.25 °C².

Compared with the example (4 °C²), the variance has increased by 2.25 °C², which is r² times the initial uncertainty: this has been contracted by r², rather than removed. The result is exactly v∞: starting at the limiting variance leaves it unchanged, because v∞ = r² v∞ + v∞(1 − r²). Independence between the initial temperature and the shocks is essential for adding the two terms.

Check your reasoning

  • r² applied to the initial variance, rather than r.
  • Independence of the initial temperature and the shocks stated.
  • Numerical comparison: 4 °C² versus 6.25 °C².

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do θ, μ and σ represent in dX = θ(μ − X) dt + σ dW, and what are their units?

θ is the mean reversion rate, in inverse time; μ is the equilibrium level, in the units of X; σ is the shock amplitude, in the units of X per square root of time.

Recall 2What is the factor r for a step Δt, and what does it multiply?

r = e to the power −θΔt. It multiplies the deviation from μ; its square multiplies the existing variance.

Recall 3What variance does the process converge to, and why?

It converges to σ²/(2θ): each step adds v∞(1 − r²) and contracts the previous variance by r², making v∞ a fixed point.

Recall 4What assumptions does the exact solution use?

Constant parameters, additive shocks, standard Brownian motion independent of the initial temperature, and the accepted property that the integral of a deterministic function against W is Gaussian.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Write dX = θ(μ − X) dt + σ dW with the units; multiply by e to the power θt and integrate; read off the mean, then the variance using the isometry; move to a step Δt: deviation multiplied by r, variance r² V + v∞(1 − r²); check using the direct formula and the limit; draw no conclusions about a real system.
