Level 3 · C102·M1

Matrix chain rule

Your goalCalculate the sensitivity of a score to pigment masses by composing Jacobians in the right order, at the right point and with the right units.

Before you start
c057-m5 : Hessian · c100-m5 : learning curves · c059-m6 : discrete deterministic Bellman equation · c015-m5 : diagnosis

✿01 See the idea

In an ink workshop, you adjust the masses of red and blue pigment. A model first displays intermediate indices, then combines them into a score. You want to know how the score would respond to a small addition of red pigment while keeping the blue mass fixed.

Red pigment affects each index. To predict the final effect, follow each path: what change in an index does the addition of red produce, and then what change in the score does that index produce? Multiply the sensitivities along each path and add the contributions from the paths.

A Jacobian arranges these sensitivities in a table: each row corresponds to an output, each column to an input. Matrix multiplication performs exactly the sums of products we need. The order must follow the path taken by the changes.

The data and the scoring rule are fictional. We do not claim to measure the actual colour or quality of an ink. Masses are in grams; the indices and score are dimensionless. The question concerns the local sensitivity of this model, without automated training.

Before reading the solution, make a prediction: would ignoring one of the paths underestimate the sensitivity to red in this scene, where all contributions are positive?

The rule to remember

Define the rows and columns; evaluate the outer Jacobian at the inner output; multiply with the outer Jacobian on the left. A local sensitivity alone does not give an exact change for a finite addition.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What change in the score should an addition of pigment produce?

Initially, r = 1 g of red and b = 2 g of blue. The model gives u = 2r + 1b and v = 3r + 2b, with each coefficient in inverse grams, then q = uv. Let us calculate the sensitivities of the score to the masses and check what they tell us.

  1. Define the objects, units and starting point

    x = (r, b)ᵀ is the column of masses, in g. z = (u, v)ᵀ is the column of dimensionless indices. The symbol T denotes transposition: it exchanges rows and columns. The function f transforms x into z; g transforms z into the score q. H = g ∘ f names the composite function, so H(x) = g(f(x)). The symbol ∘ means “apply f first, then g”.

    The coefficients of f are constant and equal to the stated numbers per gram. We assume the functions are differentiable in a neighbourhood of the point under consideration: they have a linear approximation there whose remainder becomes negligible relative to the size of the displacement. Here, f is linear and g is a product, so this assumption holds. No cutoff or saturation is added.

    z=f(x)=[2132][rb];g(z)=uv

    Calculate f: u = 2 × 1 + 1 × 2 = 2 + 2 = 4. Then v = 3 × 1 + 2 × 2 = 3 + 4 = 7. The units g cancel with the coefficients per gram. The initial score is q = 4 × 7 = 28. We will denote these starting points by x⋆ and z⋆; the star identifies them without indicating an optimum.

  2. Read the inner Jacobian

    The partial derivative ∂u/∂r indicates how u varies when r changes and b stays fixed. In J_f, the rows follow (u, v) and the columns follow (r, b). An entry of this matrix is therefore expressed in inverse grams. Keeping the other mass fixed does not mean that it equals 0.

    Jf=[∂u∂r∂u∂b∂v∂r∂v∂b]=[2132]

    For example, ∂u/∂r = 2 per gram and ∂v/∂r = 3 per gram: red does change both indices. J_f has 2 rows and 2 columns; it is constant here because f is linear.

  3. Evaluate the outer Jacobian at the intermediate point

    For g(u, v) = uv, differentiating with respect to u while keeping v fixed gives v; differentiating with respect to v while keeping u fixed gives u. J_g is a row because the score has 1 output component, and it has 2 columns, one per index. Its entries are dimensionless.

    Jg(z)=[vu];Jg(z⋆)=[74]

    The function g receives the indices z⋆ = (4, 7)ᵀ, rather than the masses x⋆ = (1, 2)ᵀ. That is why the local weights are 7 and 4. These weights would change at another point, while J_f would stay the same.

  4. Compose the linear maps in their order

    δx denotes a small perturbation of the masses; δz is the linear change in the indices it produces; δq is the linear change predicted for the score. The symbol δ marks this local calculation. We have δz = J_f(x⋆)δx, then δq = J_g(z⋆)δz. Substituting for δz gives the product J_g(z⋆)J_f(x⋆): the matrix on the right acts first.

    JH(x)=Jg(f(x))Jf(x)

    This is the matrix chain rule, using the convention “outputs in rows, inputs in columns”. More generally, if f maps ℝⁿ to ℝᵐ and g maps ℝᵐ to ℝᵖ, n counts the input components, m the intermediate components and p the final outputs. The dimensions are m × n for J_f, p × m for J_g and p × n for J_H. The symbol ℝ denotes the real numbers.

    In our scene, (1 × 2) multiplied by (2 × 2) does give (1 × 2). The reversed product cannot even be formed: its inner dimensions do not match.

  5. Calculate the sums of products and interpret the result

    JH(x⋆)=[74][2132]=[2615]

    For red, follow the first column of J_f: ∂q/∂r = 7 × 2 + 4 × 3 = 14 + 12 = 26 per gram. The first term passes through u; the second passes through v. For blue: ∂q/∂b = 7 × 1 + 4 × 2 = 7 + 8 = 15 per gram.

    Thus δq = 26δr + 15δb, when δr and δb are expressed in g. Each coefficient in inverse grams multiplies a change in g; the result is dimensionless, like q. These numbers give slopes at the starting point, rather than extra score automatically gained by adding a whole unit of mass.

  6. Move from a row Jacobian to a column gradient

    For a scalar output, the gradient ∇H is defined here as the column of partial derivatives, in the order (r, b). It is the transpose of the row J_H. Similarly, the gradient ∇g is the column in the order (u, v). Transposing the product reverses the order of its factors.

    ∇H(x⋆)=Jf(x⋆)T∇g(z⋆)=[2132]T[74]=[2615]

    The two forms describe the same sensitivities: J_H = J_g J_f for Jacobians, and ∇H = J_fᵀ∇g for column gradients. Do not place a transpose from memory: first state whether the object is a row or a column. Here the components of ∇H are in inverse grams.

  7. Check with a finite addition and distinguish it from the slope

    Keep red fixed and add ε = 1/10 g, that is, 0.10 g of blue. ε names the size of this addition. The predicted changes in the indices are δu = 1 × 0.10 = 0.10 and δv = 2 × 0.10 = 0.20, both dimensionless. Since f is linear, these changes in the indices are exact.

    The local rule gives δq = 7 × 0.10 + 4 × 0.20 = 0.70 + 0.80 = 1.50. It predicts the score 28 + 1.50 = 29.50. Check using the original rule: u becomes 4.10, v becomes 7.20, so q becomes 4.10 × 7.20 = 29.52. The exact change Δq is 29.52 − 28 = 1.52, which is dimensionless; Δ denotes a difference actually calculated between the two states of the model.

    Δq=(u+δu)(v+δv)−uv=vδu+uδv+δuδv

    The term omitted by the approximation is δuδv = 0.10 × 0.20 = 0.02. This is exactly the difference 29.52 − 29.50 = 0.02. Here this term is proportional to the square of ε: as ε tends to 0, it becomes negligible relative to ε. The chain rule gives an exact derivative; using it for a finite addition gives an approximation.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I multiply the matrices in the order in which I say f then g.”

    Apply the product to the column δx: J_f first transforms it into δz, then J_g acts on δz. So the product must be J_g J_f. Also check the dimensions before calculating anything; if you use column gradients, transpose the entire formula.

    Review the composition and the column convention

  • “The outer derivative must be calculated at the initial masses.”

    Identify the arguments received by g: they are the indices u and v. Evaluate J_g at f(x⋆), hence at (4, 7), and keep J_f evaluated at x⋆. Masses and indices do not have the same units.

    Reread where to evaluate the outer Jacobian

  • “Multiplying the slope by the addition must give the exact score.”

    The slope gives the linear term of the change. For the product uv, expanding the score after a displacement also reveals δuδv. Compare the predicted change, the exact change and the remaining term instead of declaring the rule wrong.

Compare with the solution

Through u: ∂q/∂u equals v = 7, and ∂u/∂b equals 1 per gram; their product is 7 per gram. Through v: ∂q/∂v equals u = 4, and ∂v/∂b equals 2 per gram; the product is 8 per gram.

The sum is 7 + 8 = 15 per gram. Keeping only the path through u would omit 8 per gram. Changing b changes u and v simultaneously; keeping r fixed does not hold either of these indices fixed.

  • Add the contributions to obtain 15 per gram.
  • Distinguish the mass held fixed from the indices that vary.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and algebraic check

This time, add red

Start again from the initial masses and add ε = 0.10 g of red while keeping blue fixed. Calculate the changes in the indices, the linear change in the score, then the exact change and the new score. Identify the term that explains their difference.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The column for r gives δu = 2ε and δv = 3ε. The slope of the composite function gives δq = 26ε.

Hint 2

Expand (u + δu)(v + δv). The term δuδv must be added to the linear change to recover the exact change.

Compare with the solution

The indices change by δu = 2 × 0.10 = 0.20 and δv = 3 × 0.10 = 0.30, both dimensionless. The linear change in the score is δq = 26 × 0.10 = 2.60.

The remaining term is 0.20 × 0.30 = 0.06. The exact change is therefore Δq = 2.60 + 0.06 = 2.66, and the new score is 28 + 2.66 = 30.66, which is dimensionless.

You can check directly that (4 + 0.20) × (7 + 0.30) = 30.66. The difference comes from the product of the changes in the indices, rather than an error in the derivative. A finite addition is distinct from a derivative at the starting point.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the column for red and express ε in g.
  • Distinguish the linear change 2.60, the exact change 2.66 and the final score 30.66.
  • Recover the term 0.06 by expanding the product.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start from J_H = J_g J_f, then transpose the entire product.

Hint 2

The matrix J_f is square here. Its transpose has the same dimensions, but does not have the same coefficients in the same positions.

Compare with the solution

The correct formula is ∇H = J_fᵀ∇g. The transpose of J_f is needed because the gradient column must collect the same derivatives as the row J_H. The proposed multiplication does have the right dimensions in this square case, but it uses the wrong coefficients.

[2132]T[74]=[2615]

The correct first component is 7 × 2 + 4 × 3 = 26 per gram; the second is 7 × 1 + 4 × 2 = 15 per gram. The proposed calculation instead produces 14 + 4 = 18 and 21 + 8 = 29 per gram. Checking dimensions is necessary, but interpreting the rows and columns remains essential.

  • Justify the transpose from the convention of writing the gradient as a column.
  • Obtain (26, 15)ᵀ per gram and assign the components to r then b.
  • Explain why compatible dimensions do not prove that the calculation is correct.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do a row and a column of a Jacobian represent on this page?

A row specifies an output component; a column specifies an input component. Each cell is the partial derivative of that output with respect to that input, with the other inputs held fixed.

Recall 2Reconstruct the rule for H = g ∘ f, including its evaluation points and order.

J_H(x) = J_g(f(x))J_f(x). The input perturbation first passes through J_f(x), then through J_g evaluated at the intermediate point f(x). Each final coefficient adds the contributions from the intermediate indices.

Recall 3Why does the written form of the rule change with column gradients?

For a scalar output, ∇H = J_Hᵀ and ∇g = J_gᵀ. Transposing J_g J_f gives J_fᵀ J_gᵀ, so ∇H(x) = J_f(x)ᵀ∇g(f(x)). The sensitivities stay the same; their arrangement changes.

Recall 4Does an exact derivative guarantee the exact change in the score for a finite addition?

No. It gives the linear part of the change. For q = uv, the exact change also includes δuδv. In our model, this remainder is proportional to the square of the size of the addition; it becomes negligible relative to the addition as the addition tends to 0.

Recall 5What unit should the sensitivity of the score to a mass have? How can you check it?

The score is dimensionless and the mass is in g: the sensitivity is in inverse grams. Multiplying it by a change in g gives a dimensionless change in the score. The product of the Jacobians must preserve this consistency.
