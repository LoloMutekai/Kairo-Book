Level 1 · C035·M5

Finite covariance and conditioning

Your goalCalculate a covariance for a finite joint distribution, then reconstruct the probabilities and mean after receiving information about distance.

Before you start
c030-m5 : Poisson · c034-m5 : mean value · c035-m1 : univariate density · c035-m2 : cumulative distribution · c035-m3 : normal and exponential distributions · c035-m4 : finite joint distribution

✿01 See the idea

At a bicycle delivery workshop, a bag contains 8 cards describing fictional trips. You draw a card at random, with each card equally likely to be drawn. It gives a distance X, in kilometres, and a duration Y, in minutes. The whole bag defines our model; we are not trying to estimate a hidden distribution from real records.

Short trips are often quick, and long trips are often slow. Covariance assigns a number to this tendency: it examines whether deviations from the means tend to go in the same direction. It also includes cases that go against the tendency; it does not turn “often” into “always”.

You are then told that the card gives a distance of 4 km. You do not change any duration: you set aside the cards that are incompatible with this information. There are 4 equally likely possibilities left. The probability of a long duration is now calculated within this group, and its mean may differ from that of the whole bag.

This unit stays within a finite sample space. No normal density, multiple integral, or rule of causality is needed. Conditioning describes information received about the draw; it does not describe an intervention that would actually make a trip longer.

The rule to remember

For covariance, centre both quantities, then average the products of their deviations. To condition, keep the compatible cases and rescale their total probability mass to one.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the whole bag to the revealed distance

Calculate the covariance between distance and duration, then the mean duration given X = 4 km. In the order short–quick, short–slow, long–quick, long–slow, the counts are 3, 1, 1, 3. The cards are fictional and equally likely; the distance–duration pairs are not equally likely.

  1. Turn the counts into a joint distribution

    X and Y are the values written on the drawn card. Lowercase x and y denote their possible values. D is the set of possible distances, and T is the set of possible durations. p(x,y) denotes P(X = x and Y = y), the joint probability. P means probability, which has no unit; “and” requires both properties on the same card.

    The short row contains n_c = 3 + 1 = 4 cards; the long row contains n_l = 1 + 3 = 4. The total is n = 4 + 4 = 8. Divide each count by n: p(2,10) = 3/8, p(2,20) = 1/8, p(4,10) = 1/8, p(4,20) = 3/8.

    Check: (3 + 1 + 1 + 3)/8 = 1. All these probabilities are nonnegative. The joint distribution is correctly normalized.

  2. Calculate the means used for centring

    A marginal probability ignores the other quantity: p_X(x) = P(X = x) sums the row for x; p_Y(y) = P(Y = y) sums the column for y. Here each row and each column contains 4 cards: each value of X, and each value of Y, therefore receives weight 1/2.

    E denotes expectation, the weighted average using probabilities as weights. μ_X = E(X), in km, and μ_Y = E(Y), in min, are our means. The symbol Σ tells us to add all the terms specified beneath it.

    μX=Σx∈DxpX(x);μY=Σy∈TypY(y)

    Since the marginal weights are equal, μ_X = (2 + 4)/2 = 6/2 = 3 km. Similarly, μ_Y = (10 + 20)/2 = 30/2 = 15 min. This simplification using 2 depends on the weights being equal; it is not a rule for every distribution.

    The deviations of X are 2 − 3 = -1 km and 4 − 3 = 1 km. Those of Y are 10 − 15 = -5 min and 20 − 15 = 5 min. Each mean lies between the extreme values of its quantity.

  3. Average the products of deviations

    Covariance, written Cov(X,Y), is the expectation of the product of the deviations from the means. In the following sum, x ranges over D and y ranges over T; each cell contributes its product of deviations multiplied by p(x,y). Its unit is the product of the units of X and Y: here km·min.

    Cov(X,Y)=Σx∈DΣy∈T(x−μX)(y−μY)p(x,y)

    Short–quick: (-1) × (-5) = 5 km·min. Long–slow: 1 × 5 = 5 km·min. These positive products correspond to deviations with the same sign. Together, the 6 cards in these cells contribute 6 × 5 = 30 km·min to the sum over the cards.

    Short–slow: (-1) × 5 = -5 km·min. Long–quick: 1 × (-5) = -5 km·min. These 2 cards contribute 2 × (-5) = -10 km·min.

    The centred sum is 30 + (-10) = 20 km·min. Each card has weight 1/8: Cov(X,Y) = 20/8 = 5/2 km·min, or 2.50 km·min. Dividing by n − 1 would address a different estimation problem; here we are calculating the exact covariance of the distribution defined by the bag.

    The positive sign describes a shared tendency around the means. It does not guarantee the duration on a particular card: the long–quick cell also exists. The value is neither a probability nor a number of minutes per kilometre.

  4. Check using another expression

    Expanding the centred product and using E(X) = μ_X and E(Y) = μ_Y gives another way to calculate the covariance. XY is the product of the two values on the same card; E(XY) is its mean, in km·min.

    Cov(X,Y)=E(XY)−μXμY

    Without centring, add the products over the cards: E(XY) = (3 × 2 × 10 + 1 × 2 × 20 + 1 × 4 × 10 + 3 × 4 × 20)/8 = 47.50 km·min.

    The product of the means is 3 × 15 = 45 km·min. The difference 47.50 − 45 = 2.50 km·min matches the centred calculation. E(XY) and E(X)E(Y) are different quantities in this distribution; confusing them would erase the association we have just calculated.

  5. Change the reference group after receiving information

    P(Y = y | X = x) is read as “the probability that Y equals y given that X equals x”. The vertical bar means that the information X = x is given. We keep only the cards in this row; p_X(x) must be strictly positive for us to divide by its probability mass.

    P(Y=y|X=x)=p(x,y)pX(x),pX(x)>0

    With X = 4 km, we keep 4 cards: 1 quick and 3 slow. The new probabilities are 1/4 = 1/4 for 10 min and 3/4 = 3/4 for 20 min. We can check the second using the joint distribution: 3/8 ÷ 1/2 = 3/4.

    Their sum is 1/4 + 3/4 = 1. Keeping the weights 1/8 and 3/8 without renormalizing them would give only the probability mass 1/2 of the row, rather than a complete distribution over its durations.

    No card gives X = 6 km: P(X = 6) = 0. The preceding formula therefore does not define a conditional distribution for this information in our model. We do not replace this impossibility with a duration of 0.

  6. Calculate the mean in the retained group

    E(Y | X = x) is the mean of Y using the conditional weights of row x. It is a fixed number once x is given, expressed in minutes. It is not necessarily a duration written on a card.

    E(Y|X=x)=Σy∈TyP(Y=y|X=x)

    For X = 4 km: 1 × 10 = 10 min and 3 × 20 = 60 min. The sum of the durations on the retained cards is 10 + 60 = 70 min. Dividing it by the number of these cards gives 70/4 = 35/2 min, or 17.50 min.

    Another way: 1/4 × 10 + 3/4 × 20 = 17.50 min. The result remains between 10 and 20 min, and it exceeds the overall mean of 15 min. Information about distance changes the weights of the possible durations; it does not determine the actual duration or prove a causal relationship.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Covariance is simply the mean of the products.”

    E(XY) is 47.50 km·min, but it also includes the product of the means, 45 km·min. Subtract that product, or start with the centred deviations, to obtain 2.50 km·min.

    Review the two covariance calculations

  • “Given the distance, I keep the total number of cards in the bag as the denominator.”

    The original bag contains 8 cards, and the compatible group contains 4. Divide the counts in this group by 4, then check that the conditional weights total 1. A condition with 0 probability prevents this division.

    Review the retained group in the diagram

  • “Positive covariance gives the exact duration and proves the cause.”

    In the row X = 4 km, the durations 10 and 20 min remain possible. Their mean of 17.50 min does not predict the exact value on each card. An association in this bag is not enough to describe the effect of an intervention on a real distance.

    Distinguish the whole bag from the conditioned group

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · choose the reference group

The same cell, a different question

For the cell X = 4 km and Y = 20 min, write the joint probability and the probability of this duration given this distance. Explain why they differ, then check the sum of the conditional probabilities in the row.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The numerator counts the same 3 cards; the denominator depends on what you already know.

Hint 2

The joint question concerns the whole bag. The conditional question concerns only the long row.

Compare with the solution

Joint probability: 3/8 = 3/8. Conditional probability: 3/4 = 3/4. The second is greater because the long distance is already known: the probability mass is no longer spread over the whole bag.

The other duration receives 1/4 = 1/4. The check gives 1/4 + 3/4 = 1. Probabilities have no unit, even when the values they refer to are in km and min.

Check your reasoning

  • You give 3/8 and 3/4 with the two distinct questions they answer.
  • You justify the denominators 8 and 4 and check that the sum equals 1.

Exercise 2 · Application · reconstruct a mean

Recover the whole bag from its rows

Calculate E(Y | X = 2 km), then recover the overall mean of Y by combining the means of the short and long rows. Explain why the rows have equal weight here and what would need to change if their card counts differed.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In the short row, 3 cards give 10 min and 1 gives 20 min.

Hint 2

To return to the whole bag, the weight of a row mean is the probability of drawing a card from that row. Do not give equal weight to rows of different sizes.

Compare with the solution

The sum of the durations in the short row is 3 × 10 + 1 × 20 = 30 + 20 = 50 min. Its mean is 50/4 = 12.50 min. It is below the mean of the long row, 17.50 min.

Each row contains 4 cards out of 8, so each has weight 1/2. We recover 1/2 × 12.50 + 1/2 × 17.50 = (12.50 + 17.50)/2 = 30/2 = 15 min, the same as μ_Y.

This reconstruction works because each card is counted in its row, whose mean is then weighted by the size of that row. With different counts, we would use the respective row probabilities; a simple average of the means would no longer suffice.

Check your reasoning

  • You find 12.50 min and state the numerator and denominator explicitly.
  • You recover 15 min by weighting with the row probabilities.
  • You explain why the equal weights are specific to this bag.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · change the centring too

A covariance that becomes 0 after receiving information

We know X = 4 km. Someone claims that the covariance between X and Y must remain 2.50 km·min, since the cards have not changed. Recalculate this covariance under the conditional distribution, using the group means. Can we conclude that distance and duration are independent in the whole bag?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In the retained group, X is constant. Its conditional mean is therefore 4 km.

Hint 2

Look at the factor X − E(X | X = 4). To check independence in the whole bag, compare a joint probability with the product of the marginal probabilities.

Compare with the solution

In the long row, the mean of X is 4 km and that of Y is 17.50 min. On each retained card, X − 4 = 0 km. All products of deviations are therefore 0, whatever the duration: the conditional covariance is 0 km·min.

The cards are the same, but the reference distribution and its means have changed. Duration still varies in this group; it is the constant value of X that makes each product 0. Using the overall means would calculate a different quantity.

Independence in the whole bag would require p(x,y) = p_X(x)p_Y(y) for every cell. However, p(4,20) = 3/8, whereas the product of the marginal probabilities is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. These numbers differ: the quantities are not independent under the original distribution. The conditional result does not justify a conclusion about another distribution.

Check your reasoning

  • You replace both means with those of the retained group.
  • You justify the covariance of 0 km·min by showing that a factor is 0 on every card.
  • You disprove independence in the whole bag using a cell and the marginal probabilities of the original distribution.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you construct covariance from a finite joint distribution, and what is its unit in the bag of trip cards?

Calculate the means, then the product of the deviations for each pair. Multiply each product by its joint probability and add. The unit is km·min. The sign indicates the balance between deviations in the same direction and in opposite directions, without guaranteeing any individual duration.

Recall 2What other calculation checks the covariance without starting from centred deviations?

Cov(X,Y) = E(XY) − E(X)E(Y). XY multiplies the values on the same card. In our bag: 47.50 − 45 = 2.50 km·min. The mean of the products and the product of the means are distinct.

Recall 3What does the “given that” bar do to the probabilities in a row? What condition makes the operation possible?

It restricts the distribution to compatible cases and divides each joint probability by the strictly positive probability of the row. The new weights must sum to 1. If the condition has probability 0, this formula does not define a conditional distribution.

Recall 4Why can the mean duration change after the distance is revealed even though no duration is changed?

The mean is recalculated using the weights of the compatible group. Given X = 4 km, the durations 10 and 20 min have weights 1/4 and 3/4, giving 17.50 min. The duration of the drawn trip remains uncertain; receiving this information is not an intervention on a trip.

Recall 5Why does 0 covariance in the long row not make the quantities independent in the whole bag?

In this row, X is constant: every deviation from its conditional mean is 0. In the whole bag, we must examine the original distribution; the long–slow cell has weight 3/8, different from the product of the marginal probabilities, 1/4. The change of reference distribution must be stated.
