Level 2 · C060·M2

Conditioning random variables

Your goalConstruct a conditional distribution and its mean by naming the information retained, then recognize the limit of the formula when that information is continuous.

Before you start
c035-m5 : covariance and conditioning in finite settings · c058-m5 : change of variables · c046-m5 : approximation error · c060-m1 : transformations

✿01 See the idea

Before setting off by bike, you ask how long the journey will take. A box contains fictional cards for the same route: each records a duration and whether it was raining. You draw a card at random. You are told only “rain”. You still do not know its duration, but you now know which part of the box to look in.

Conditioning means revising the distribution of possibilities using this information. Keep the cards compatible with “rain”, then distribute all the probability among them. This thought process does not make any journey longer: the reference group changes.

The whole box contains 20 equally likely cards. Among those marked “rain”, 2 show 10 min and 6 show 20 min. The relevant denominator therefore becomes 8 cards. All values are fictional; this box defines our model for drawing cards, without predicting real traffic or measuring the effect of rain.

The rule to remember

Name what is known; keep the compatible cases; divide their weights by the total weight retained; check that the new distribution sums to 1 before calculating its mean.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What is the mean duration when the card says “rain”?

Draw a card uniformly from the box described above. Let us calculate the distribution of its duration, then its mean, given that the card says “rain”. The distribution we seek concerns this specific draw from a complete fictional box.

  1. Define the variables and the drawing rule

    X is the recorded duration, in minutes; it is 10 min or 20 min. Y is the rain indicator: Y = 1 for “rain”, Y = 0 for “dry”. Y has no units. Here, a random variable assigns a value to the card drawn. The lowercase letters x and y denote possible values of X and Y.

    P denotes the probability given by our uniform draw: each card has weight 1/20. The = sign in X = x describes an event. The comma in P(X = x, Y = y) means that the events occur together; the vertical bar in P(X = x | Y = y) is read “given that”.

    The categories cover the whole box and do not overlap. No independence between duration and weather is assumed. The uniform draw allows us to use counts; with cards of different weights, we would need to add their probabilities.

  2. Move from the joint distribution to the conditional distribution

    The joint distribution describes pairs. For rain, P(X = 10, Y = 1) = 2/20 = 1/10, and P(X = 20, Y = 1) = 6/20 = 3/10. Adding this row gives the marginal probability of rain: P(Y = 1) = 8/20 = 2/5.

    Write p(x,y) for P(X = x, Y = y) and pY(y) for P(Y = y). The marginal pY(y) sums p(x,y) over all possible values x. When pY(y) is strictly positive, the conditional distribution is:

    P(X=x|Y=y)=P(X=x,Y=y)P(Y=y)

    We divide by the probability of the information received to give the entire retained row a total weight of 1. The denominator counts all cases compatible with the information, including those whose duration is not the one we are interested in.

  3. Calculate the complete distribution after receiving the information

    P(X=10|Y=1)=1/102/5=28=1/4

    The factor 1/20 common to the counts cancels. We could therefore count the short journeys directly among the rainy cards. Their weight is 0.25, with no units.

    For the long duration, the same denominator gives P(X = 20 | Y = 1) = 6/8 = 3/4, or 0.75 with no units. Other durations have probability 0 in this model.

    Distribution check: 1/4 + 3/4 = 1. Each term is between 0 and 1. Dividing by 20 would have left a sum of 2/5, the weight of rain in the whole box, instead of normalizing the retained group.

  4. Calculate the conditional mean

    E denotes expectation, meaning the weighted average under the chosen distribution. Set g(y) = E[X | Y = y]. The symbol Σ asks us to sum over all possible values of x. In a finite sum, each duration is multiplied by its conditional probability:

    g(y)=E[X|Y=y]=∑xxP(X=x|Y=y)

    The contribution from short journeys is 10 × 1/4 = 2.5 min. The contribution from long journeys is 20 × 3/4 = 15 min. Probabilities have no units; each product and their sum are in minutes.

    g(1)=2.5+15=17.5 min

    17.5 min is a mean under partial information. No card shows this duration: the card drawn still shows 10 min or 20 min. The mean summarizes the distribution; it does not replace the unknown outcome.

  5. Check using the cards, then interpret g(Y)

    Another approach: add the durations directly in the rain row. The short journeys contribute 2 × 10 = 20 min; the long ones contribute 6 × 20 = 120 min. The sum is 140 min for 8 cards. Its mean is 140 ÷ 8 = 17.5 min, identical to the weighted calculation.

    This result is indeed between 10 min and 20 min. Checking the bounds and summing the weights detect different errors: a plausible mean does not guarantee a normalized distribution.

    g is a function of the information. Before learning the weather on the card, g(Y) is itself a random variable; it is written E[X | Y]. After learning Y = 1, it takes the value g(1) = 17.5 min. The exercise on dry weather will ask you to calculate the other value of this function.

  6. If the information is a continuous rainfall depth

    Keep the same route, but now replace the box with a continuous model: X describes the duration in minutes, and Y the rainfall depth in millimetres. Y is no longer the previous indicator. Suppose the pair has a joint density fXY that is nonnegative and has total integral 1. A density describes probabilities of intervals through integration; its value at a point is not a probability.

    In this model, P(Y = y) = 0 for every exact value y. We therefore cannot evaluate the event formula by dividing by this probability. We use densities. t is a duration serving as the variable of integration; ℝ is the set of real numbers. The marginal density fY is obtained by integrating the joint density over all durations:

    fY(y)=∫ℝfXY(t,y)dt

    For a value y such that fY(y) is finite and strictly positive, the density formula gives a version of the conditional distribution:

    fX|Y(x|y)=fXY(x,y)fY(y)

    fXY has units of the reciprocal of the product minute × millimetre; fY has units of the reciprocal of a millimetre. Their quotient therefore has units of the reciprocal of a minute. Integrating it over all durations gives 1: this is the continuous normalization check.

  7. Keep the assumptions and scope of the calculation in view

    In the continuous extension, we obtain the conditional mean by replacing the weighted sum with an integral, provided that the integral of the absolute value of t multiplied by the conditional density is finite:

    g(y)=∫ℝtfX|Y(t|y)dt

    The result is still in minutes. If fY(y) = 0, this quotient defines nothing. More generally, the joint distribution determines a conditional distribution only up to changes on a set of values of Y with probability 0: we say “almost everywhere” with respect to the distribution of Y. The density formula is a convenient version where its denominator is suitable; it does not automatically give a unique meaning to an exceptional condition.

    If the information actually received is an interval of rainfall depths with positive probability, we can condition on that event using the original formula. We do not replace it with an exact equality without justification. Finally, comparing durations under different weather conditions in our model does not prove a causal effect of rain.

Hide the formulas. Explain why the rain group must receive all the probability, why its mean might not appear on any card, and then what changes when rain is described by a continuous depth.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I keep the total for the whole box in the denominator.”

    6/20 = 3/10 describes “long AND rain”. Once “rain” is known, the reference set contains 8 cards: the required probability is 6/8 = 3/4. Also check the sum of the entire conditional distribution.

    Review the construction and normalization of the distribution

  • “The “given” bar can be read in either direction.”

    “Long given rain” retains the rain row; “rain given long” retains the column of long journeys. The numerators happen to match here, but the retained groups differ. The exercise on reversing the information asks you to count the correct denominators.

    Return to the exercise on reversing the information

  • “When it rains, the card must show the mean duration.”

    The mean 17.5 min summarizes a distribution between 10 min and 20 min. It is not the duration on any card in the box. Keep both the distribution, which describes the remaining uncertainty, and the mean, which summarizes it.

    Review the mean and its check using the cards

Exercise 1 · Application · change the information

The card says “dry”

Return to the original discrete box. We learn Y = 0. Construct the complete conditional distribution of X, check its sum and calculate g(0) in minutes.

Hint 1

The dry row contains 9 short journeys and 3 long ones. Start with its total.

Hint 2

Multiply each duration by its new weight. The mean duration must remain between the possible durations.

Compare with the solution

We retain 9 + 3 = 12 cards. The probability of 10 min given dry weather is 9/12 = 3/4; that of 20 min is 3/12 = 1/4. Other durations have weight 0. The sum is 1.

The mean is g(0) = 10 × 3/4 + 20 × 1/4 = 7.5 + 5 = 12.5 min. The weights change, while the possible durations remain the same. It is 5 min lower than g(1), within this box model only.

  • Give all the weights and check that their sum is 1.
  • Obtain 12.5 min using a weighted average, without presenting it as the certain duration.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis · reverse the information

You are told only that the duration is long

We now know X = 20 min, without knowing Y. What is P(Y = 1 | X = 20)? Compare it with P(X = 20 | Y = 1). Does this calculation demonstrate the causal effect of rain on duration?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Keep the entire 20 min column and count the rainy cards within it.

Hint 2

The conditional question describes a subgroup. It does not compare the same journey with and without an intervention on the weather.

Compare with the solution

The long-duration column contains 3 + 6 = 9 cards, of which 6 record rain. So P(Y = 1 | X = 20) = 6/9 = 2/3. Keep this fraction exact, without rounding it for the comparison.

In the other direction, P(X = 20 | Y = 1) = 6/8 = 3/4. These fractions differ because the condition retains a different group. Here we describe an association in a fictional box. No intervention design or control of other causes of journey time is provided; a causal effect is therefore not established.

Check your reasoning

  • Retain 9 cards for long journeys and obtain exactly 2/3.
  • Explain the change of denominator using the words “given that”.
  • Distinguish the calculated association from a causal effect, which would require further evidence.

Exercise 3 · Reconstruction · then the continuous limit

The weather is hidden again

Before anything is revealed, reconstruct E[X] by weighting g(0) and g(1) by the respective probabilities of dry weather and rain. Check by counting the durations directly throughout the box. Finish with a sentence explaining why an exactly known continuous rainfall depth cannot be handled by dividing by P(Y = y).

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The function g(Y) takes the value 12.5 min on the 12 dry cards and 17.5 min on the 8 rainy cards.

Hint 2

For the second calculation, group all cards with the same duration, regardless of their weather. For the continuous limit, distinguish the probability of an event from a density.

Compare with the solution

The weather weights are P(Y = 0) = 12/20 = 3/5 and P(Y = 1) = 8/20 = 2/5. The mean of the conditional means is 12.5 × 3/5 + 17.5 × 2/5 = 7.5 + 7 = 14.5 min. Each card belongs to a row; weighting by the row sizes reconstructs the whole box.

Directly, 11 cards show 10 min and 9 show 20 min. E[X] = 10 × 11/20 + 20 × 9/20 = 5.5 + 9 = 14.5 min. The methods agree. Taking the unweighted mean of g(0) and g(1) would give the weather categories equal weight even though they do not occupy the same share of the box.

In the joint-density model, an exact depth satisfies P(Y = y) = 0. We do not divide by this probability. We use the conditional density fXY(x,y)/fY(y) when fY(y) is finite and positive, then integrate it to obtain probabilities or the mean. The version defined in this way is determined almost everywhere with respect to the distribution of Y; an exceptional condition is not arbitrarily assigned a quotient.

Check your reasoning

  • Weight by 3/5 and 2/5, without assuming the weather categories are equally likely.
  • Recover 14.5 min from the durations and from the conditional means.
  • State where division by a 0 probability fails and name the joint-density assumption of the other formula.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What happens to the probabilities when we learn the weather on the card?

We retain the compatible cards and divide their weights by the strictly positive total weight retained. The new distribution must sum to 1. With a uniform draw, we can count the cards.

Recall 2Why do “long duration given rain” and “rain given long duration” differ?

The condition chooses the reference group. The rain row gives 6/8 = 3/4; the column of long journeys gives 6/9 = 2/3.

Recall 3What is the difference between g(y), g(Y) and the duration X?

g(y) is the mean of X for a given value y of the information. g(Y), written E[X | Y], is a variable depending on the information that will be revealed. X remains the duration drawn: it is not necessarily equal to this mean.

Recall 4How can we recover the mean before receiving information from the conditional means in the box?

Weight the mean of each row by the probability of that row, then add. Each card thereby regains its original weight; an unweighted mean of the rows would be unjustified if their counts differ.

Recall 5What precaution do we take if the information is an exact continuous value?

Its point probability is 0 in the density model. If the pair has a joint density, divide it by the marginal density where the latter is finite and positive. This quotient is a conditional density, not a point probability; it gives a version determined almost everywhere.

Then explain why g(Y) depends on the information and why an exact continuous condition requires a density formula, under its own assumptions.
