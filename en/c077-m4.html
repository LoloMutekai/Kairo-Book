Level 2 · C077·M4

Synthesis as a service and asynchronous playback

Your goalFollow an instruction from text to sound, calculate its duration, and explain what can be concluded when an asynchronous playback call returns.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

At the reception desk of a hall, an interface must say, “The hall is closing soon.” It already has the text. It still needs to produce audio, then ask the audio player to play it. While the sentence is being played, the person at the desk wants to keep using the interface.

Speech synthesis transforms text into an audio signal. A synthesis model calculates the samples; an adapter prepares the request, calls this model, and returns a result in a form the rest of the program understands. The service is this input and output contract. It can run locally: the word service does not require a network connection.

Playback is a separate step: it sends the available audio to the audio output. Asynchronous playback returns control to the calling program without waiting for the audio to finish. The caller can then handle another task. This return alone does not say whether the player has started, finished, or encountered a problem.

The whole scene is fictional: the sentence, samples, times, and events are exercise data. No real audio is produced here. We follow a complete synthesis operation, then uninterrupted playback; we study neither model training nor audio generation in chunks.

The rule to remember

Request the audio, check the synthesis result, start playback, then look for separate evidence that it has finished. Also state what happens to a new request if the player is busy.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The same instruction, from the result to the last sample

At time 0 ms, the interface requests “The hall is closing soon.” Synthesis finishes at 600 ms and returns mono audio with 72000 samples at 24000 Hz. The playback call returns at 620 ms; the player actually starts at 650 ms. Let us calculate the duration and expected end, then check an observation at 1000 ms.

  1. Set out the contract and assumptions

    The service input contains the text to speak and the selected voice. The output distinguishes available audio from a failure with an explanation. The result must include the audio and its actual sampling frequency; any stated duration still needs to be checked. A simple success indicator does not establish pronunciation quality.

    We assume that the model is available, the entire text is accepted, and all the audio is produced before playback. Mono means that the signal uses 1 channel. N is the number of samples in this channel, with no physical unit; fs is the frequency in hertz, or samples per second; D will be the duration in seconds. Here N = 72000 and fs = 24000 Hz.

    The player plays at the declared frequency, without pauses, speed changes, or interruptions. The times use the same clock, with an origin of 0 ms. tp denotes the time when the audio is ready, tᵣ the return of the playback call, tᵈ the actual start of playback, and tᶠ its expected end, all in milliseconds. The letters p, r, d and f label these times.

  2. Calculate how long the audio should last

    The frequency tells us how many samples occupy each second. Dividing the total number of samples by this number per second therefore gives a duration. The number of characters in the text does not give this duration: the voice, pace, and pauses of the model affect the signal produced.

    D=Nfs=7200024000=3 s

    Reverse check: 3 s × 24000 samples/s = 72000 samples. We recover the sample count of the audio. The time needed to produce it, 0.60 s here, describes a different duration.

  3. Separate the synthesis wait from the playback return

    In our mechanism, the synthesis call waits for the result from the model: the calling program stays in this call until 600 ms. The word asynchronous applies here to the playback launch that follows. Keeping the whole interface available during synthesis would also require moving this wait out of its main execution path.

    Launching playback passes the audio to a thread: a sequence of instructions whose progress is scheduled separately from that of the caller. The caller resumes at 620 ms, or 0.62 s after the origin. The player starts at 650 ms, or 0.65 s. This order is an assumption in our fictional trace; another execution could start the player before the call returns.

    From the audio being ready to the actual start, 650 − 600 = 50 ms elapse. Preparing the player and scheduling tasks can take up this interval. Starting a thread does not guarantee an immediate start.

  4. Place the end on the correct clock

    A second is 1000 ms. We must therefore convert D before adding it to a time in milliseconds: 3 s × 1000 ms/s = 3000 ms. We then add this duration to the actual start, because that is when playback begins consuming the samples.

    tf=td+1000D=650+3000=3650 ms

    In this formula, the numerical values of the times are in ms and that of D is in s; the factor 1000 has the unit ms/s. The expected end is 3.65 s after the origin. When the call returns, 3650 − 620 = 3030 ms remain until this end. This interval still includes the wait for playback to start: it exceeds the duration of the audio.

    This end is calculated under our assumptions. On a real player, a stop, pause, or failure would mean that this time could no longer confirm completion. A clock reaching the expected time does not, by itself, provide evidence that playback has finished.

  5. Check a time during playback

    At 1000 ms, the start has passed and the end has not been reached. The duration already played is 1000 − 650 = 350 ms. The duration still to play is 3650 − 1000 = 2650 ms.

    Check by splitting the duration: 350 + 2650 = 3000 ms, recovering the 3000 ms of the signal. Check the boundaries: start, observation, and end occur in that order. These checks compare the audio content with the timeline; they do not check the loudspeaker volume.

    The interface can already accept input during this interval. In a design that waited for playback to finish on the same execution path, it would stay in this call longer. Here we establish distinct times in a chosen trace, without measuring a real improvement in responsiveness.

  6. Choose what happens to a new request

    The reception desk requests playback of the same ready audio again at 1200 ms. At that point, 2450 ms of playback remain in our trace. A lock grants temporary exclusive use of a resource. Suppose the player holds it throughout playback and immediately rejects a new request when busy.

    Under this policy, the new request is dropped: it does not automatically enter a queue. Of 2 playback requests, 1 is accepted and 1 is rejected. An additional thread therefore does not mean an additional playback. Playing the audio later would require a new request, or a queue explicitly designed for that purpose.

    After the expected end, at 3700 ms, the margin is 50 ms. A new request could be accepted if the lock has actually been released and the player is available. The earlier rejected request remains rejected: the passage of time does not recreate it.

  7. State the evidence needed at each boundary

    The synthesis result establishes that the adapter has returned audio according to its contract. The return from the launch establishes only that this call has returned control. A task status tied to an identifier and updated by the worker could then distinguish accepted, playing, finished, and failed; this tracking must be built and checked if the product needs it.

    Even an end confirmed by the playback program does not prove that someone heard the instruction: the output could be muted, the wrong device could be selected, or nobody could be present. For our calculation, we assumed successful playback through to the last sample. Establishing that an instruction was actually heard would require an observation suited to that requirement.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Synthesis succeeded, so the instruction was heard.”

    The result provides available audio. You still need to establish that playback was accepted and went all the way to the end, then choose suitable evidence if the requirement is that someone heard it.

    Review the boundaries and their evidence

  • “The call is asynchronous, so everything is instantaneous.”

    Identify which call returns control. Here synthesis still waits for the model; only the playback launch returns before the end. The 3 s of audio are still needed to play it at the declared rate.

    Review the path and the distinct times

  • “The lock keeps my request for later.”

    A lock controls who can use a resource; the policy decides what happens to the request. Our policy rejects it immediately if the player is busy. Without an explicit queue, nothing guarantees another attempt after the lock is released.

At 1200 ms, the initial playback is in progress: 3650 − 1200 = 2450 ms remain. The lock is therefore held. We count 1 accepted playback and 2 − 1 = 1 rejected request.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do the synthesis model and adapter each do?

The model transforms text into an audio signal. The adapter prepares the call, checks the inputs, and returns a usable result or a failure with an explanation. The presence of the adapter does not prove that the model is available.

Recall 2How can you find and check the duration of the mono audio for the instruction?

D = N/fₛ, with N samples and fₛ in samples per second. Here 72000/24000 = 3 s. Multiplying the duration by fₛ should recover 72000 samples. Playback must use the declared frequency.

Recall 3What does the return of an asynchronous playback call establish?

The call has returned control to its caller without waiting for the audio to finish. It does not, by itself, confirm that playback has started, finished, or been heard. In our mechanism, the preceding synthesis still waits for the model.

Recall 4Why is a lock not enough to guarantee that the second request will be played?

It protects exclusive use of a resource. Under our policy, a request that finds the player busy is dropped. A queue or an explicit retry would be needed to play it later.

Recall 5Why does reaching the calculated end not prove that the instruction was heard?

The time assumes playback without incidents. Tracking the player is needed to confirm completion; even this does not guarantee that a person heard the audio through the correct audio output.
