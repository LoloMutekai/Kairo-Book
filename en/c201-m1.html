Level 3 · C201·M1

Simulation clock

Your goalReplay a decision in the correct order: use only information already received, then wait for an eligible execution event.

Before you start
c147-m5 : costs and out-of-sample testing · c041-m5 : transactions and windows · c199-m5 : residuals · c017-m5 : reproduction protocol

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a reader stepping through a price log, like a film whose frames cannot be skipped ahead. The program knows the complete file, but the decision rule must operate in the past: it sees only messages that would already have reached it. This simulated clock advances with the events in the log; it does not have to wait for seconds to pass on your computer.

We follow the fictional stock Aster. A closing price of 102 € per share is observed at 60 s after the start of our session, but the message arrives only at 62 s. The strategy can decide after this arrival. It cannot use this price as early as 60 s simply because the row in the file has that timestamp.

Deciding creates an intention to buy. Executing it requires a separate rule: our small engine waits for the next price received late enough to meet a latency of 3 s. Even though the intention is ready at 65 s, no new price arrives at that instant. The clock still has to advance.

All relative times, delays and prices on this page are fictional. The decision rule serves only to test a sequence of events. We calculate no position, financing, cost or performance; those mechanisms require the following units and their own assumptions.

The rule to remember

An observation time describes the market; an availability time makes information usable; a decision creates a pending order; an execution rule then selects an eligible event.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Advance through the Aster log without seeing what comes next

The log contains A: observation and receipt at 0 s, price 100 €; B: observation at 60 s, receipt at 62 s, price 102 €; C: observation at 120 s, receipt at 122 s, price 101 €; D: observation at 180 s, receipt at 182 s, price 103 €. Each price is per share. A single decision is planned, just after B is received: request a purchase if B exceeds A. Let us calculate its first eligible execution with latency 3 s.

  1. Define the times and the replay contract

    For a row i in the log, τᵢ is the instant when price pᵢ was observed; aᵢ is the instant when its message becomes available. The index i identifies a row and has no unit. pᵢ is in euros per share. τᵢ, aᵢ, the current clock time t, the decision time d and the execution time x are all in seconds after the same starting point. ℓ, the letter ell, denotes the minimum latency between decision and execution, in seconds.

    Here the log is complete, with no later corrections or lost messages. Its receipts are ordered, and its observation times also advance. We have chosen a constant receipt delay of 2 s for B, C and D; A is already available at the start. The decision calculation is modeled as instantaneous: its phases are ordered but take no time. Receipt delay and order latency are different assumptions.

    τi≤ai;ai≤d

    The first inequality describes our log. The second tests whether a row is available for a decision: its receipt must already have occurred. Equality is allowed only because messages timestamped d are incorporated before the decision. A price not yet received remains unknown.

  2. Also fix the order at equal timestamps

    At a given timestamp, the engine first incorporates new messages, then processes older orders that have become executable, then allows new decisions. If several messages have the same timestamp, a stable number in the log breaks the tie; this number describes an order, rather than a duration. This convention is part of the reproducible inputs.

    The clock jumps directly to the next relevant event: receipt of a price or the time an order becomes due. It never moves backward. We can write t′ ≥ t, where the prime means “after the jump”. Reaching the due time makes an order ready; it does not create a quote. If no eligible price remains before the end of the log, the order stays unexecuted.

    This separation makes the replay independent of its speed on the machine. Actually waiting between rows or reading the current computer time must not change the historical decision.

  3. Make the information available, then take the decision

    B was observed at 60 s. Its receipt delay gives the time 60 + 2 = 62 s. At d = 62 s, the engine incorporates B, then gives only A and B to the rule. Rows C and D do not pass the availability test.

    The rule requires a comparison, so we calculate the difference B − A: 102 − 100 = 2 € per share. This difference is greater than 0 € per share: an intention to buy is created and timestamped d = 62 s. No information about C was needed. This signal does not predict that the purchase will be profitable.

  4. Turn latency into an eligibility time

    To find the first instant when the order can be executed, we add a duration to its creation time: d + ℓ = 62 + 3 = 65 s. This is a time bound, not yet an execution.

    x>d;x≥d+ℓ

    Our execution convention requires the first received price that meets both conditions: it arrives strictly after the decision and at least ℓ seconds after it. The strict inequality prevents a new order from using the price of the message that just triggered it, even if ℓ is 0 s. An older order can, however, use this message.

    The selected price is the price in the newly received message; we do not reuse the last price at the due time. This is a deliberately simple model of replay using closing-price messages, with no intermediate prices. The ability to actually obtain this price when it is received has not been demonstrated.

  5. Step through the events to the first eligible price

    At 65 s, the order is ready, but no new message is received. B arrived at 62 s: it is too early and has the decision timestamp itself. The next message is C, received at 122 = 120 + 2 s.

    C arrives after d and after the bound 65 s. It therefore qualifies. The actual waiting time is 122 − 62 = 60 s; adding this duration to the decision time gives x = 62 + 60 = 122 s again. The margin beyond the bound is 122 − 65 = 57 s: we waited longer than the minimum latency.

    The simulated execution event is therefore timestamped 122 s, at the price of 101.00 € per share, the price of C. We do not rewrite its time as the observation time of either C or B. The quantity and the accounting entries for this purchase are not covered in this unit.

  6. Check the sequence of events by another route

    From the observation of B, the total delay is 122 − 60 = 62 s. We obtain the same total by adding the receipt delay and the wait after the decision: 2 + 60 = 62 s. The wait alone represents 60 ÷ 60 = 1 min; it does exceed the latency 3 s. Durations are added; times are subtracted.

    Causality check: prepare a copy of the log in which only the prices of C and D change, after time d. Replaying up to d must produce exactly the same decision based on A and B. The future execution may then have a different price. This check on a prefix distinguishes leakage from the future from a legitimate change in a future result.

    Order check: the test log must preserve the receipt of B, the decision, the due time, then the receipt of C and the execution. Changing the reading speed leaves these events identical. These checks test the chosen contract; they do not validate how faithfully it represents the real market.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the steps and explain why the order is executed neither at the close for B, nor at its receipt, nor exactly at the due time. Redraw the boundary of the available information, then explain which event finally allows execution to proceed.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The row has the closing timestamp, so I can already use its price.”

    Look at the availability time, rather than just the observation time. Between 60 s and 62 s, B has already been observed but is still unknown to the rule. Filter every input by its receipt before calculating the signal.

    Review the boundary of the visible log

  • “Adding latency is enough to obtain the execution time and price.”

    The addition gives 65 s, a bound. Then look for the first eligible newly received price. Here, no message arrives at that bound: the engine waits for C at 122 s and does not create an intermediate price.

    Revisit the due time, then step through the events

  • “Identical timestamps make the processing order irrelevant.”

    Receipt, execution of an older order and a new decision are distinct phases. An order decided after reading C cannot be executed on C under our strict convention, whereas the order arising from B can. Record the phases and a stable tie-breaking rule in the trace.

    Audit the older and new orders

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · information availability

An instant before B

Stop the same replay at t = 61 s. Which prices are available to the strategy? Can it compare B with A? How much time remains before B is received?

Hint 1

An observation that has already occurred may still be in transit. Compare the receipt time of each row with the clock.

Hint 2

For the remaining duration, subtract the current time from the receipt time of B.

Compare with the solution

Only A, received at 0 s, is accessible. B was observed at 60 s but its receipt at 62 s is later than 61 s. C and D will arrive even later. The comparison B − A therefore cannot be calculated from the available inputs; it cannot arbitrarily be set to 0.

There are 62 − 61 = 1 s remaining. After this jump, the engine incorporates B before letting the rule decide.

Check your reasoning

  • You list only A and justify excluding B by its receipt time.
  • You give a duration of 1 s and keep the comparison unavailable.

Exercise 2 · Application · select an event

The order takes longer to become ready

Start again from the decision at 62 s and keep the same log. Only the latency changes to 70 s. Calculate the bound, the first execution event, its price and the waiting time. Explain why simply advancing by a row would be wrong.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate d + ℓ before selecting a row.

Hint 2

C is the next message, but its arrival may precede the bound. Then test D.

Compare with the solution

The bound is 62 + 70 = 132 s. C arrives at 122 s, before this bound: it is too early despite its position in the file. D arrives at 182 s and meets the conditions.

The wait is 182 − 62 = 120 s. Execution is therefore at 182 s, at a price of 103.00 € per share. Its margin beyond the bound is 182 − 132 = 50 s. Advancing by a row would count events without checking durations.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain the bound 132 s before selecting D.
  • You reject C using an explicit time inequality.
  • You distinguish the time 182 s, the duration 120 s and the price 103.00 € per share.

Exercise 3 · Challenge · repair a sequence of events

Same message, different orders

Return to the initial latency of 3 s. An engine reads C at 122 s, creates a new order at that instant and immediately executes it at the price of C. It justifies this by saying “we already have the price”. However, it refuses to let the older order arising from B use C. Diagnose these choices, correct the times, then propose a check that detects use of future information in the decision arising from B. The new order is an audit variant, rather than a second decision in the original replay.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Separate the creation time of each order from the time of the message that might execute it.

Hint 2

For the new order, calculate the bound from the receipt of C. To check for use of future information, change only the rows still hidden at the decision arising from B.

Compare with the solution

The older order was decided at 62 s and has been ready since 65 s: C, received at 122 s, is its first eligible event. Rejecting it therefore contradicts the contract. Its execution must be kept at 122 s at a price of 101.00 € per share.

The new order is created at 122 s, after C has been incorporated. Executing it at the same instant violates x > d, and its bound is 122 + 3 = 125 s. D, received at 182 s, is the next eligible event. It waits 182 − 122 = 60 s and uses the price of 103.00 € per share, under our convention. These orders must therefore be distinguished in the log by their creation and due times.

To look for leakage from the future, replace the prices of C and D in a copy, then compare the traces stopped just after the decision arising from B. The information received and the decision at 62 s must remain identical. A later change in execution price is allowed; a change in this past decision reveals an improper dependency in this test.

Check your reasoning

  • You allow the older order to execute on C and reject the new order on that same message.
  • You calculate 125 s, then select D for the new order.
  • Your check compares the shared past without requiring future executions to remain identical.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which time gives permission to use a row to decide?

The availability time aᵢ must be at most d, and the message must have been incorporated before the decision. The observation time τᵢ alone is not enough.

Recall 2How does replay time advance without actually waiting?

It jumps to the next relevant event in the fixed order, without moving backward. The computer time and the reading speed must not alter the historical sequence of events.

Recall 3Why does d + ℓ not directly give an execution?

It is a bound. Our model then selects the first new price received at x with x > d and x ≥ d + ℓ. Without an eligible price in the log, the order remains unexecuted.

Recall 4Which phases determine the processing order at the same timestamp?

Under our convention: incorporate messages, process eligible older orders, then decide. A stable number breaks ties between messages with the same timestamp. A new order waits for a later price.

Recall 5What simple test looks for use of future information?

Change only the messages still hidden, then replay up to the earlier decision. Its information and decision must remain identical; future executions can change. This test does not demonstrate how faithfully the model represents the market.
