Level 1 · C029·M5

Bayes

Your goalFind the probability of a source after an alert, weighting each source before changing the reference group.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A parcel passes under a label reader. The “alert” light comes on. This light comes on more often for parcels from workshop A than for parcels from workshop B. Before concluding “so it comes from A”, ask how many parcels each workshop sends: a workshop that supplies a large share of the parcels can produce many alerts, even if the light rarely comes on for its parcels.

Let us construct a fictional batch of 100 parcels: 10 come from A and 90 from B. Within each source group, the fraction with an alert is 4/5 and 1/5, respectively. These are exact proportions chosen for the exercise, rather than measurements from a real device. Each parcel has a single source, and the reader gives either an alert or no alert.

We draw a parcel at random, with the same chance for each parcel, then learn only that it has an alert. Imagine removing all the parcels without an alert. In this restricted group, how many come from A? Bayes answers by changing the group in which we count.

The first diagram divides the batch by source and by the state of the light. The second keeps only the parcels with alerts. Try to name the denominator of the new fraction before reading the calculation: it counts parcels with an alert, whatever their source.

The rule to remember

To work back from the alert to the source, calculate the proportion with both “source and alert”, then divide it by the proportion with any alert. The direction of “given” changes the reference group.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Where does the parcel with an alert come from?

In the fictional batch of 100 parcels, the share from A is 1/10. The reader flags 4/5 of the parcels from A and 1/5 of those from B. The parcel drawn uniformly at random has an alert. Let us find the probability that it comes from A.

  1. Name the source, the observation, and the assumptions

    A means “the parcel comes from workshop A”; B means “it comes from B”. These events are mutually exclusive and cover the whole batch: B is therefore the complement of A, also written Ā. S denotes the event “the parcel has an alert”; S̄ means “it has no alert”. P denotes probability under uniform random selection from this batch.

    The symbol ∩ means “and”: A ∩ S consists of the parcels from A that have an alert. The vertical bar | is read as “given”: P(S | A) uses only the parcels from A as its reference group; P(A | S) uses only the parcels with an alert. All these probabilities have no unit and lie between 0 and 1; the counts, however, are in parcels.

    Before seeing the light, P(A) = 10/100 = 1/10: this is the prior. P(B) = 90/100 = 9/10. The likelihood P(S | A) = 4/5 tells us how probable the alert is when the source is fixed as A. The posterior probability we seek is P(A | S), after taking the alert into account. The terms “before” and “after” describe our information; the source of the parcel does not change.

    The reader’s proportions are known exactly for this constructed batch. An alert is not certain proof of the source. We do not assume independence between S and the source: the alert proportions differ precisely because of the workshop.

  2. Derive Bayes from the same joint event

    Conditioning gives the probability of A and S by starting from A, or by starting from S. It is the same set of parcels, counted in a different order.

    P(A∩S)=P(A)P(S|A)=P(S)P(A|S)

    Since P(S) is strictly positive, we can divide by it. This gives Bayes’ formula. The product in the numerator weights the likelihood by the frequency of the source; the division changes the reference group.

    P(A|S)=P(A)P(S|A)P(S)

    This equality requires P(A) > 0 to use P(S | A) and P(S) > 0 to use P(A | S). Our scene meets these conditions. It does not require A and S to be independent.

  3. Count the alerts from each source

    Within A, the fraction flagged applies to the 10 parcels from A: 10 × 4/5 = 8 parcels with an alert. Within B, we apply its own fraction to its 90 parcels: 90 × 1/5 = 18 parcels with an alert.

    Relative to the whole batch, these branches have probabilities P(A ∩ S) = 1/10 × 4/5 = 2/25 and P(B ∩ S) = 9/10 × 1/5 = 9/50. These are joint probabilities, with no unit. Multiplying by a conditional probability here does not assume independence: we are counting a fraction within the starting group.

  4. Include every way the alert can occur

    An alert can come from A or from B. Since a parcel cannot come from both workshops at once, the branches do not overlap. Since no other source exists in the scene, no branch is missing. We therefore add 8 and 18: 26 parcels have an alert.

    P(S)=P(A)P(S|A)+P(B)P(S|B)

    Numerically, P(S) = 2/25 + 9/50 = 13/50, or 0.26 with no unit. Counting also gives 26/100 = 13/50. Directly adding 4/5 and 1/5 would ignore the different sizes of the source groups.

  5. Restrict the group to alerts and read the answer

    Among all the parcels with alerts, we keep those from A in the numerator. Bayes gives exactly the same change of reference group as the second drawing.

    P(A|S)=810026100=826

    The fraction simplifies to 4/13, or approximately 0.308 with no unit. The exact result remains 4/13; the decimal only helps us read it. Before the alert, the probability of A was 1/10; after the alert, it increases while remaining below that of B.

    P(S | A) = 4/5 answers a different question: what fraction of the parcels from A triggers the light? It does not have the same denominator as P(A | S) = 4/13. The reader does not know the source when it turns on its light.

  6. Check normalization and recount the batch

    For B, the same calculation gives P(B | S) = 18/26 = 9/13. Since the sources cover the entire group with alerts, 4/13 + 9/13 = 1. Normalization is this division by the total that makes the probabilities sum to 1. Each result must remain between 0 and 1.

    Reverse check: P(A | S) × P(S) = 4/13 × 13/50 = 2/25, recovering the joint probability calculated from A. We can also check the counts without an alert: 10 − 8 = 2 parcels from A and 90 − 18 = 72 from B. The 74 parcels without an alert and the 26 with an alert give 100 parcels in total again.

    These checks can detect a missing branch or an incorrect denominator, for example. They do not validate the proportions of a real reader: our batch is fictional and fully specified.

  7. Know where the calculation stops

    If P(S) = 0, no alert can be observed under the model for this batch. The conditional fraction is then undefined; we do not replace this impossible division with a probability of 0. An alert actually observed would require us to reconsider the model or the data.

    Bayes transforms given probabilities consistently. By itself, it does not supply the source frequencies, the reliability of the reader, or proof of the source of a particular parcel. Here, the reasoning stops at an update based on an observation in a finite sample space.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the fractions and explain the move from the whole batch to the group with alerts. Why must you first take the sizes of the source groups into account, then add their alerts, before dividing? Derive Bayes’ formula by writing the same event “A and S” in opposite directions.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The reader flags 4/5 of the parcels from A, so an alert means a 4/5 chance of coming from A.”

    Write in words the group that comes after “given”. P(S | A) divides by the 10 parcels from A; P(A | S) divides by the 26 parcels with an alert. In these cases, the numerator counts the 8 parcels that have properties A and S, but the reference group changes.

    Compare the whole batch and the group with alerts

  • “To compare A and B, I can ignore their starting frequencies.”

    The light comes on more often within A, but A sends far fewer parcels. P(S) adds the products P(A) × P(S | A) and P(B) × P(S | B). Using only the reader’s proportions here amounts to treating the sources as equally frequent; the last exercise lets you check the difference.

    Review the weighting and the total of the branches

  • “The product 1/10 × 4/5 already answers the question after the alert.”

    This product is 2/25 and still uses all 100 parcels as its reference group. It describes “A and alert”. To answer “A given alert”, divide this product by P(S) = 13/50. You thereby move from the whole batch to the 26 parcels with alerts and obtain 4/13.

    Repeat the change of denominator and its reverse check

Exercise 1 · Interpreting events

Same numerator, different questions

After the vertical bar, the group is fixed: which parcels remain if you already know that the source is A?

Hint 2

An intersection keeps the whole batch as the reference group. For “given an alert”, you must instead restrict the group to the flagged parcels.

Compare with the solution

P(S | A) = 8/10 = 4/5, or 0.80 with no unit: we count the 8 parcels from A with an alert among the 10 parcels from A.

P(A ∩ S) = 8/100 = 2/25, or 0.08 with no unit: the numerator stays the same, but the denominator is the whole batch. Neither expression answers the question asked. We need P(A | S) = 8/26 = 4/13, because the known group is the group with alerts.

Exercise 2 · Independent application

The light stays off

Start again from the initial batch and the same uniform random selection. This time, you learn that the parcel has no alert. Calculate P(A | S̄) using the counts, then recover this result with Bayes. Why is it not the complement of P(A | S)?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In each workshop, subtract the flagged parcels from the starting count. Then add those that remain.

Hint 2

The complement of “A given S” describes “B given S”. Here, it is the information S that is replaced by S̄.

Compare with the solution

There remain 10 − 8 = 2 parcels from A without an alert, and 90 − 18 = 72 from B. The new denominator counts 2 + 72 = 74 parcels. Thus P(A | S̄) = 2/74 = 1/37, approximately 0.027 with no unit.

Using probabilities, P(S̄ | A) = 1 − 4/5 = 1/5 and P(S̄ | B) = 1 − 1/5 = 4/5. The joint probability P(A ∩ S̄) is 1/10 × 1/5 = 1/50. P(S̄) = 1 − 13/50 = 37/50. Bayes gives 1/50 ÷ 37/50 = 1/37.

The complement of P(A | S) is P(B | S) = 9/13, because we remain among the parcels with alerts. P(A | S̄) changes the reference group. Here, the absence of an alert lowers the probability of A relative to its prior 1/10, which is consistent with a light that comes on more often within A.

Check your reasoning

  • You count all parcels without an alert in the denominator, including those from B.
  • You obtain the same exact result using counts and using probabilities.
  • You explain why changing the observed event does not amount to taking the complement of the source event.

Exercise 3 · Variation and justification

The reader stays the same, the batch changes

Let us construct a fictional variation of the same batch: out of 100 parcels, 50 come from A and the others from B. The alert fractions remain 4/5 within A and 1/5 within B. Before calculating, predict whether P(A | S) increases or decreases compared with the initial batch. Calculate it, then respond to the claim “the same reader always gives the same probability of a source after an alert”.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The reader does not change the fractions within the source groups. But how many parcels does it now receive from each workshop?

Hint 2

Count the alerts from A and from B separately, then divide the alerts from A by their new total.

Compare with the solution

A becomes more frequent at the start: P(A) = 50/100 = 1/2, instead of 1/10. B supplies 50 parcels, so P(B) = 1/2. We expect a higher probability of A after an alert, with unchanged alert fractions.

A supplies 50 × 4/5 = 40 alerts; B supplies 50 × 1/5 = 10. Their total is 50 parcels, giving P(S) = 1/2. The result becomes P(A | S) = 40/50 = 4/5, or 0.80 with no unit, greater than 4/13.

The claim is false: the probability of a source after an alert also depends on the distribution of sources before the observation. Here, P(A | S) coincides with P(S | A) because the priors are equal and the chosen alert fractions sum to 1. This numerical coincidence does not allow us to reverse conditioning in general.

Check your reasoning

  • You state your prediction before calculating, then compare it with the result.
  • You change the source frequencies and keep the alert fractions specific to the reader.
  • You refute the claim using the results from the batches and distinguish equality of numerical values from equality of meaning.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do the reference groups for P(S | A) and P(A | S) differ?

P(S | A) counts alerts among the parcels from A; P(A | S) counts parcels from A among those with alerts. The group after the vertical bar is the one to which we restrict our attention.

Recall 2How can you derive Bayes by starting from the same event “A and S”?

Write P(A ∩ S) = P(A) × P(S | A) = P(S) × P(A | S), then divide by P(S). If P(A) and P(S) are strictly positive, P(A | S) = P(A) × P(S | A) / P(S).

Recall 3How do you calculate P(S) when A and B are the only possible sources and are mutually exclusive?

Add P(A) × P(S | A) and P(B) × P(S | B). The products weight each group by its frequency; the branches are disjoint and cover all the parcels with alerts.

Recall 4Why is knowing the reader’s alert proportions insufficient to know the probability of a source after an alert?

The source frequencies before the observation, the priors, are missing. They weight the alert proportions. The same alert can lead to different posterior probabilities depending on the batch.

Recall 5What should you do if the model gives P(S) = 0 when you want to calculate P(A | S)?

Conditioning through this fraction is undefined because its denominator is 0. If S has actually been observed, reconsider the model and the data; do not invent a division or a probability of 0 as an answer.
