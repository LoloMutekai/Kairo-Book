Level 1 · C030·M5

Poisson

Your goalChoose a counting window, calculate its Poisson parameter and distinguish “exactly” from “at least” in an arrival probability.

Before you start
c029-m5 : Bayes · c030-m1 : discrete distributions · c030-m2 : expected value · c030-m3 : variance · c030-m4 : binomial distribution

✿01 See the idea

A service desk receives requests at unpredictable times. We count arrivals over 30 min, without counting replies or requests still waiting. One window may remain empty; another may contain several arrivals. The average number does not determine the count in each window.

The entire scene is fictional. We assume a steady average rate of 2 requests per hour. Over half an hour, this gives an average of 1 request. The drawing shows possible windows; it does not report any measured frequency.

The Poisson distribution assigns a probability to each integer count: no arrivals, one arrival, several arrivals. It does not require a fixed number of customers each making an attempt. In a binomial distribution, the number of trials is fixed in advance; here, the observation period is fixed.

To justify this arrival model, assume a constant rate, independent counts over intervals that do not overlap, and individual arrivals. Over a very short interval, the probability of one arrival is approximately the rate multiplied by the duration; the probability of several arrivals is negligible relative to that duration. Requests sent in batches or a changing rate would require us to reexamine these assumptions.

Before using the formulas, predict: does “an average of 1” make a window with no arrivals impossible? Then identify what is fixed in the drawings: the window and the assumed rate, rather than the observed count.

The rule to remember

Choose what to count and for how long; multiply the rate by that duration using matching time units; calculate the probability of the event actually requested.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Will the service desk receive several requests?

For the same fictional service desk, keep the rate of 2 requests per hour and the window of 30 min. Calculate the probability of exactly 2 arrivals, then that of at least 2 arrivals, under the stated assumptions.

  1. Name the rate, duration and count

    r is the assumed average rate, in requests per hour; t is the duration, in hours. X is the random variable that counts arrivals within this window; k is a possible dimensionless integer value of X. P(X = k) denotes the probability that the count is exactly k. This probability has no unit.

    λ, read “lambda”, is the distribution parameter for the window: λ = rt. The rate r and the parameter λ play different roles. We use a given r for the exercise; we have not estimated it from actual records.

  2. Express the duration in the correct unit

    The rate is per hour. Dividing 30 min by 60 min per hour gives t = 0.5 h. Multiplying by r then gives the average number of arrivals during this period.

    λ=rt=2×3060=1

  3. Read the Poisson distribution

    For strictly positive λ, writing X ∼ Poisson(λ) means that the possible counts k = 0, 1, 2, … are assigned the following probabilities. The symbol ∼ means “has the distribution”. The exclamation mark denotes the factorial: k! is the product of the integers from 1 to k; by convention, 0! = 1. Thus 2! = 2 × 1 = 2.

    P(X=k)=e−λλkk!

    e is the unitless constant used as the base of the exponential function, with e ≈ 2.71828. e to the power a is calculated using the exp(a) key; it is positive and satisfies e to the power (a + b) = e to the power a multiplied by e to the power b, where a and b are unitless numbers. For a negative exponent, take the reciprocal of the positive power. Here, we only need to evaluate exp(−λ).

    The formula defines a distribution over all nonnegative integers, with no fixed maximum. If λ = 0, the count is always 0: P(X = 0) = 1. Handle this case directly, without interpreting an indeterminate power.

  4. Distinguish the exact formula from decimal approximations

    With λ = 1, the constant we need is e to the power −1. A numerical evaluation rounded to 5 decimal places gives q = 36788/100000 = 0.36788. q is an exact fraction that we use to approximate this constant, rather than the exact value of the exponential.

    e−1≈q=0.36788

    The absolute error from this rounding is less than 0.000005. We keep q throughout the calculations, then round the percentages at the end. The fractions calculated below are exact for q; the Poisson probabilities remain approximate.

  5. Calculate the probability of an exact count

    For k = 2, square λ, then divide by the factorial to obtain the factor 1/2 = 0.5. Then multiply by e to the power −λ. The square of λ is 1, because λ = 1.

    P(X=2)=e−1122!≈0.36788×0.5=0.18394

    The fraction used in the calculation is 9197/50000. To express the result as a percentage, multiply by 100: the probability of exactly 2 requests is about 18.39 %. This result does not include any windows with more arrivals.

  6. Move from “exactly” to “at least”

    The event X ≥ 2 includes 2, 3 and all higher counts. It is simpler to subtract the excluded cases from the total: no arrivals or exactly one arrival. These cases are disjoint, so their probabilities add.

    Since λ = 1, P(X = 0) = e to the power −1, and P(X = 1) is also e to the power −1. Each is approximated by 0.36788, or about 36.79 % and 36.79 % respectively. Their equality is specific to this parameter.

    P(X≥2)=1−P(X=0)−P(X=1)

    The calculation using q gives 1 − (0.36788 + 0.36788) = 1 − 0.73576 = 0.26424. The required probability is therefore about 26.42 %. The error from q can be multiplied by 2 here, because q appears in each of the subtracted cases.

  7. Check the result and its meaning

    The result lies between 0 and 1. It exceeds 0.18394, the approximation for the single case X = 2, because “at least” also includes higher counts. Checking the categories gives 0.73576 + 0.26424 = 1. This checks the partition of events; it does not demonstrate that the model describes the actual service desk.

    For a Poisson distribution, the expected value E(X), the predicted average count, and the variance Var(X), the average squared deviation from that expected value, have the same numerical value λ. Here E(X) = 1 request per window and Var(X) = 1 request squared. Counts are mathematically dimensionless; the words “request” and “request squared” remind us of the quantities being described. The variance is not the expected count.

    These identities are properties of the distribution. A mean and variance that are close in a set of records would not be enough to prove a Poisson distribution. Conversely, observing 0 arrivals in a window does not contradict a mean of 1: that window is among the possibilities allowed by the model.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The hourly rate is the parameter for any window.”

    The rate r remains expressed per hour. The parameter λ = rt belongs to the chosen window: convert the duration into hours before multiplying. Repeating this step avoids treating half an hour as a whole hour.

    Review the duration conversion and the calculation of λ

  • ““At least 2” is calculated by setting k = 2 in the formula.”

    The formula with a fixed k describes “exactly”. For “at least 2”, add all the allowed counts or subtract the cases 0 and 1 from the total. The complement is the shortest route here.

    Review the complement and its check

  • “A steady average rate guarantees a Poisson distribution.”

    A steady rate is not enough: requests may arrive in groups or depend on previous arrivals. Return to the assumptions, then explain which aspect of sending requests in batches invalidates the proposed justification.

    Reread the assumptions of the arrival model

At least one request

Which count is the only one that makes “at least one arrival” false?

Hint 2

Compare with the solution

The complement of X ≥ 1 is X = 0. Thus P(X ≥ 1) = 1 − e to the power −1 ≈ 1 − 0.36788 = 0.63212, or about 63.21 %.

Exactly 1 counts only windows with one arrival: about 36.79 %. “At least 1” also includes all windows with several arrivals. The result must therefore be greater, as these values confirm.

A longer window, then requests sent in batches

For the same service desk and the same assumed rate, extend the window to 60 min. You are given exp(−2) ≈ 0.13534. Calculate the probability of at least 2 arrivals. Then suppose that requests are sent in batches of 2 at the same instant: can you keep the Poisson justification without further examination?

Compare with the solution

The duration is 1 h, so λ = 2 × 1 = 2. Using the approximate constant provided, P(X = 0) ≈ 0.13534 and P(X = 1) ≈ 2 × 0.13534 = 0.27068.

The excluded sum is 0.40602; the complement is 0.59398, or about 59.40 %. This result is greater than the one for half an hour: extending a window at the same service desk cannot remove requests already included.

Simultaneous batches of 2 change the justification: a single transmission can now add several requests. The probability of several requests over a very short duration is no longer negligible relative to that duration in the required way. The previous calculation remains conditional on the initial model; it does not validate this new mechanism. ✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you convert an hourly rate into the Poisson parameter for a window?

Define the count and duration; convert that duration into hours; calculate λ = rt. r is the hourly rate, t is the duration in hours and λ is the dimensionless average count within this window.

Recall 2Reconstruct the probability of a count k and the role of each factor.

For positive λ and a nonnegative integer k: P(X = k) = exp(−λ) × λ to the power k / k!. k! is the product of the integers from 1 to k, with 0! = 1. The formula concerns an exact count; the exponential constant is positive. If λ is 0, X is always 0.

Recall 3How do you calculate “at least 2 arrivals” without adding infinitely many counts?

Subtract from 1 the probabilities of counts 0 and 1. These excluded cases are disjoint. The single count equal to 2 does not represent all the allowed counts.

Recall 4What does λ tell us about the mean and variance, and what does it fail to guarantee?

For a Poisson distribution, E(X) = Var(X) = λ numerically. The mean describes the average count and the variance describes its squared dispersion. This guarantees neither the next count nor the suitability of this distribution for actual arrivals.

Recall 5Which assumptions support the model here, and why do batches cause a problem?

A constant rate, independent counts over disjoint intervals and individual arrivals: over a very short duration, the probability of one arrival is approximately proportional to the duration and several arrivals are negligible relative to it. Sending requests in a batch can add several requests at once; the average rate alone is then no longer enough to justify this model.
