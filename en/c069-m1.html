Level 1 · C069·M1

Equivalence relations

Your goalProve that a grouping rule is an equivalence relation, construct its classes, and identify a form of closeness that does not allow this grouping.

Before you start
c014-m5 : invariants · c012-m5 : counterexamples · c006-m5 : composition

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional sewing workshop, you sort 6 fabric pieces, named A, B, C, D, E and F. A, C and F are striped; B and E are plain; D is spotted. You want a box for each pattern, even though the pieces have different sizes and colours.

Saying “A goes with C” means only “same pattern”. A and C remain distinct pieces. The rule must also accept A with itself, accept C with A whenever it accepts A with C, and accept A with F whenever it accepts A with C and then C with F. Otherwise, sorting into boxes can lead to contradictions.

These requirements have names: reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity. Together, they define an equivalence relation. An equivalence class is the whole box of objects related to a chosen object. The diagram shows the grouping; what follows explains why the rule allows it.

Each piece has exactly 1 pattern in this scene, known unambiguously and fixed throughout the reasoning. No piece combines patterns. The counts are fictional; they are not observations from a real workshop.

The rule to remember

To group objects unambiguously, check reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity over the whole set. A local resemblance is not enough.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Justify and check the grouping by pattern

The 6 pieces form the set Ω = {A, B, C, D, E, F}. The rule R relates pieces exactly when they have the same pattern. Let us prove its properties before constructing the classes, then count the pairs to check that nothing has been left out.

  1. Define the relation and its symbols

    A relation on Ω is a set of ordered pairs (x, y), where x and y belong to Ω. Ordered means that position matters: (A, C) and (C, A) are different pairs. Ω × Ω denotes all possible pairs. Writing x R y means that (x, y) belongs to R; it does not assert x = y.

    The function f assigns each piece its pattern: f(A) = f(C) = f(F) = striped, f(B) = f(E) = plain, f(D) = spotted. It is defined for every piece and does not change during sorting. We define:

    xRy⇔f(x)=f(y)

    The symbol ⇔ means “if and only if”: the rule and the equality of patterns each follow from the other. x, y and, later, z denote arbitrary pieces; f(x) is a pattern label, with no physical unit. Counts are expressed in pieces or pairs, depending on what is being counted.

  2. Establish reflexivity

    ∀x∈Ω,xRx

    ∀ is read as “for all” and ∈ as “belongs to”. Every pattern is equal to itself: f(x) = f(x). So every piece is related to itself. Piece D, the only spotted piece, must therefore contribute the pair (D, D). Reflexivity requires the whole diagonal, not just the existence of a pair (x, x).

  3. Establish symmetry

    ∀x,y∈Ω,xRy⇒yRx

    ⇒ means “implies”. If x R y, then f(x) = f(y). Reversing this equality gives f(y) = f(x), so y R x. For example, accepting (B, E) requires accepting (E, B). Symmetry does not require relating different patterns: the cells (A, B) and (B, A) both remain unmarked.

  4. Establish transitivity

    ∀x,y,z∈Ω,(xRy∧yRz)⇒xRz

    ∧ is read as “and”. If x R y and y R z, the equalities f(x) = f(y) and f(y) = f(z) give f(x) = f(z). So x R z. In the striped box, A R C and C R F require A R F. The middle piece is the same in both premises.

    We have reasoned with arbitrary pieces, rather than just checking this particular chain. The 3 properties have been established: R is an equivalence relation on Ω.

  5. Construct the classes and understand the partition

    [x]={y∈Ω:xRy}

    [x] denotes the class of x; the braces and the sign : here mean “the set of y such that”. We obtain [A] = [C] = [F] = {A, C, F}, [B] = [E] = {B, E}, and [D] = {D}. Choosing C rather than A to name the striped class changes none of its elements. A is a representative of this class: a piece chosen to identify it.

    These 3 classes are nonempty, cover all of Ω and do not overlap. Such a division is called a partition. Reflexivity places each x in [x], so no piece is missing.

    Why can classes not overlap partially? If a piece t belongs to [x] and to [y], then x R t and y R t. Symmetry gives t R y, then transitivity gives x R y. For every u in [x], y R x and x R u give y R u, so u belongs to [y]. Exchanging x and y gives the reverse inclusion: [x] = [y]. The letters t and u here denote auxiliary pieces.

  6. Count the pairs, including pairs of a piece with itself

    In the striped class, each first piece can be paired with each of the 3 striped pieces. There are therefore 3 × 3 = 9 pairs. The same reasoning applies to the plain pieces: 2 × 2 = 4; for D: 1 × 1 = 1. There are no pairs between different classes.

    N=3×3+2×2+1×1=14

    N denotes the number of pairs in R, with no physical unit. Addition is valid because each pair is counted in just one class. The subtotal for striped and plain pieces is 13 pairs; adding (D, D) gives 14 pairs. This count checks the list; on its own, it proves none of the properties.

  7. Check by going through the rows

    Let us count in a different order: in row A, the second pieces are A, C, F; in row B, B, E; in row C, A, C, F; in row D, D; in row E, B, E; in row F, A, C, F. The row sizes give 3 + 2 + 3 + 1 + 2 + 3 = 14 pairs, matching the calculation by classes.

    The diagonal contains 6 pairs, one for each piece. This leaves 14 − 6 = 8 pairs off the diagonal; each has its reversed pair. The total number of pieces is also consistent: 3 + 2 = 5, then 5 + 1 = 6 pieces.

    A missing entry on the diagonal would refute reflexivity; an entry without its mirror entry would refute symmetry. For transitivity, you must also check that every permitted chain has its direct pair: symmetry of the table alone is not enough.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the boxes and reconstruct them from the rule “same pattern”. Explain why A ≠ C can hold alongside [A] = [C], then recover the number of pairs without forgetting D. Which property prevents classes from overlapping partially, and where does symmetry enter the proof?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If A is equivalent to C, A and C are the same piece.”

    The rule compares only the pattern. A and C remain distinct, but [A] and [C] are the same set {A, C, F}. Distinguish the object, its pattern and its class.

    Reread the definition of R and the construction of the classes

  • “Pieces that are close in size must form classes.”

    A small difference can accumulate along a chain. Look for x related to y and y related to z, but x not related to z. The last exercise provides the test and a revised rule based on exactly equal lengths.

    Test transitivity using the lengths

  • “D has no partner, so its class is empty.”

    Reflexivity requires D R D. Its class is {D} and contributes 1 pair to the calculation. An equivalence class always contains its representative.

    Review reflexivity, then the count

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Change the representative

Keep the relation R based on pattern. Write [E] and [F]. Do the pairs (E, B), (E, E) and (E, A) belong to R? Justify each answer using the rule, then say whether choosing E rather than B changes the class.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Do not keep only the representative: look for all the pieces with the same pattern as it.

Hint 2

E is plain; F is striped. For a pair, compare the patterns of its two members.

Compare with the solution

[E] = {B, E} and [F] = {A, C, F}. The pairs (E, B) and (E, E) belong to R: their patterns are equal. The pair (E, A) does not belong to it, because plain and striped are different.

Choosing E instead of B does not change the class: [E] = [B]. This does not make E and B identical. The pair (E, E) illustrates reflexivity even if no other piece had been plain.

Check your reasoning

  • Give the complete classes, including the representative.
  • Decide membership for each pair using the pattern.
  • Distinguish equality of classes from identity of pieces.

Exercise 2 · Construction and justification

Start from a different grouping

The workshop now uses the boxes {A, C}, {B, D, E} and {F}, regardless of patterns. Define S as “being in the same box”. Prove its properties, give [D] for S and count its pairs. Is S identical to R because the total number of pairs is the same?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Each piece belongs to exactly one box, and every box is nonempty.

Hint 2

For transitivity, the box containing the middle piece must be the same in both premises.

Hint 3

Compare how (C, F) is treated under R and under S.

Compare with the solution

Every piece shares its box with itself: S is reflexive. Sharing a box does not depend on the order of the pair: S is symmetric. If x and y share a box, and then y and z do too, the unique box containing y contains x and z: S is transitive. These arguments apply to all the pieces.

The class of D for S is {B, D, E}. The number of pairs is 2 × 2 + 3 × 3 + 1 × 1 = 4 + 9 + 1 = 14.

The total does not determine the pairs. C R F is true, but C S F is false. Conversely, B S D is true and B R D is false. R and S are therefore different relations, both equivalence relations. The class representative and the relation being used must be specified when the rules change.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify the properties for all the pieces, rather than citing only pairs that support them.
  • Use the fact that the middle piece belongs to a unique box.
  • Count ordered pairs, including the diagonal.
  • Exhibit a pair treated differently by R and S.

Exercise 3 · Counterexample and revised rule

The tolerance does not carry through a chain

Still in the fictional workshop, the fixed lengths are A: 10 cm, B: 11 cm, C: 12 cm, D: 20 cm, E: 11 cm and F: 10 cm. Define x T y when the difference in length is at most 1 cm, including the endpoints. The difference is the greater length minus the smaller. Is T an equivalence relation? Test each property, then revise the rule to “exactly the same length” and give the new classes.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The length of a piece minus itself is 0 cm. Does exchanging the pieces change the difference?

Hint 2

Calculate the differences between A and B, between B and C, then between A and C, in that order.

Hint 3

For the revised rule, use the function that assigns each piece its fixed length.

Compare with the solution

T is reflexive: the difference between a piece and itself is 0 cm, less than or equal to 1 cm. T is symmetric because the greater length minus the smaller does not depend on the order of the pieces.

Between A and B, the difference is 11 − 10 = 1 cm: A T B. Between B and C, it is 12 − 11 = 1 cm: B T C. Yet between A and C, 12 − 10 = 2 cm exceeds 1 cm: A T C is false. This chain refutes transitivity, so T is not an equivalence relation.

Revise the rule by defining x U y when the fixed lengths are exactly equal. A length is equal to itself; equality can be reversed and carried through a chain. U is therefore an equivalence relation, by the same reasoning as for patterns. Its classes are {A, F}, {B, E}, {C} and {D}, giving 4 classes. They contain 2 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 6 pieces and produce 2 × 2 + 2 × 2 + 1 + 1 = 8 + 2 = 10 pairs.

This revision changes the criterion: it groups exact fixed lengths. If the workshop measures uncertain lengths, another rule will have to be chosen explicitly; proving a mathematical equivalence relation does not guarantee that the sorting suits its intended use.

Check your reasoning

  • Establish reflexivity and symmetry despite the failure of transitivity.
  • Give a chain with both premises true and the conclusion false, using centimetres.
  • Name the property that fails; a single counterexample is enough to refute it.
  • Describe the revised rule and all its classes, without confusing it with the original tolerance.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which requirements must be established for an equivalence relation on a set?

For every x, x R x; for all x and y, x R y implies y R x; for all x, y and z, x R y and y R z imply x R z. These are reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity.

Recall 2What does [A] contain, and why does [A] = [C] not mean A = C?

[A] contains all the pieces related to A, here {A, C, F}. C gives the same class because it is striped too. Pieces A and C remain distinct objects.

Recall 3Why do the classes cover the whole set without partially overlapping?

Reflexivity places each piece in its class. If classes share a piece, symmetry and then transitivity allow us to show that each of their elements also belongs to the other class: the classes are equal.

Recall 4How can you reconstruct the number of pairs from the boxes of patterns?

Within each class, each first piece can be paired with each second piece in that class. Add 3 × 3, 2 × 2 and 1 × 1, giving 14 ordered pairs, including the diagonal.

Recall 5Which chain refutes equivalence based on a length tolerance?

A and B differ by 1 cm; B and C by 1 cm; A and C by 2 cm. With a tolerance of 1 cm, the first two links exist and the last is missing: transitivity fails.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the boxes, reconstruct in this order: the set and the rule; the proofs of reflexivity, symmetry and transitivity; the complete classes and their partition; the count of pairs with a check by rows. Finish with a chain of lengths that shows why closeness is not enough. Justify each step in your own words.
