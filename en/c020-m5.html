Level 1 · C020·M5

Counterexamples and sensitivity

Your goalRefute a claim about a collective decision, then find which changes in weight preserve, suspend or reverse that decision.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

Ines, Leo and Nora are choosing a room for their workshop. Ines and Leo prefer Elms; Nora prefers Linden. Counting people, Elms wins. But the group is also considering giving more weight to some opinions. Will the same choice necessarily win?

Their cards list weights of 3 points for Ines, 2 points for Leo and 6 points for Nora. The first diagram shows the opinions and these weights side by side. A weight point is an agreed unit of influence in the calculation; it does not represent an additional person.

The whole scene, the opinions and the weights are fictional. Everyone is present, nobody abstains and no weight is missing. We are comparing stated rules; we are measuring neither real expertise nor the quality of the rooms.

A counterexample is an allowed case that makes the conclusion of a general claim false while satisfying its assumptions. Here we will examine: “Whenever a room has a majority of the people, it also has a majority of the weight.” We must check the conditions and calculations before rejecting this statement.

Studying sensitivity then means changing one input, stating what stays fixed, and examining the effect on the result. The second diagram changes only Nora’s weight. A tie can occur between the initial result and its reversal.

The rule to remember

Write down the claim and its assumptions; construct a case that satisfies them; recalculate the decision; vary one input and state the boundary and the domain of your conclusion.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A majority that changes sides depending on the rule

The 3 people keep their opinions: Ines and Leo support Elms, Nora supports Linden. Let us compare a majority of the people with a majority of the weight, then vary only Nora’s weight. Each rule requires a share strictly greater than 1/2; in a tie, it names no winner.

  1. Set the conventions and quantities

    When counting people, each person contributes 1 vote to their room. The reference population consists of the 3 people present. In the weighted count, each person contributes their entire weight to their room; we add the weights of the supporters on each side.

    W denotes the sum of all weights, in weight points. M denotes a share of this total, with no unit. The subscripts O and T label Elms and Linden respectively. The weights are known and nonnegative, with W strictly positive. The rooms are the only possible choices; each person supports just one. These assumptions allow us to add the shares and compare them against the majority requirement.

  2. Check the majority of people

    Elms has 1 vote from Ines + 1 vote from Leo = 2 votes. Nora contributes 1 vote to Linden. The total is 2 + 1 = 3 votes.

    The share of people supporting Elms is 2/3 = 2/3, greater than 1/2. To check this exactly, twice 2 is greater than 3. The majority of people therefore chooses Elms.

  3. Add the weights, then normalize

    Support for Elms totals 3 + 2 = 5 weight points. Support for Linden totals 6 points. To obtain the shares of weight, divide each side’s support by the same total W = 5 + 6 = 11 points.

    MO=511=5/11;MT=611=6/11

    Linden’s 6 points exceed Elms’s 5 points: M_T is greater than 1/2 and M_O is below it. Linden wins under this rule. These shares describe the influence assigned, without giving a probability that the room is the best.

  4. Check the counterexample

    Check the total: 5/11 + 6/11 = 1. Check the ordering without division: 6 − 5 = 1 weight point in Linden’s favour. The difference between the shares, called the margin here, is 1/11 = 1/11.

    Our case satisfies the assumptions: all votes are recorded, the weights are admissible and the total is positive. The premise “a majority of people support Elms” is true; the conclusion “a majority of weight supports Elms” is false. This single case therefore refutes the claim “whenever”. It does not follow that the rules always disagree or that one is fairer.

  5. Find the boundary without testing many cases

    Let t be Nora’s new weight, in weight points, with t greater than or equal to 0. The opinions and the weights of Ines and Leo stay fixed. Support for Elms remains 5; the total becomes 5 + t. The denominator must be recalculated after every change.

    MO(t)=55+t;MT(t)=t5+t

    The Greek letter Δ, read “delta”, denotes the difference M_T − M_O, with no unit. Its denominator is strictly positive, so its sign depends only on the numerator.

    Δ(t)=MT(t)−MO(t)=t−55+t

    If t is greater than 5, Linden wins. If t equals 5, the shares are equal and neither exceeds 1/2. If t is less than 5, Elms wins. This proves the boundary for all admissible weights t, without assuming that a few trials cover every case.

  6. Pass through a tie, then reverse the result

    Starting from t = 6, subtract 1 point from Nora’s weight alone: t = 5. The total becomes 5 + 5 = 10 points. Each side holds 1/2 of the weight, and the raw difference is 0. A strict majority chooses neither side; a fallback rule, if the group has one, would require a separate step.

    Subtract another 1 point: t = 4. The total becomes 9 points; the shares are 5/9 for Elms and 4/9 for Linden. The raw difference, calculated as Linden minus Elms, is -1 point: Elms wins. The votes are still 2 against 1.

    Check: at the tie, the total is twice the support of either side. After the reversal, the support totals 5 and 4 do add up to 9. We have changed the decision without changing opinions or the population.

  7. State the scope of the sensitivity result

    From the initial weight 6, the decrease needed to reach a tie is 1 point. A strictly smaller decrease preserves Linden’s win; a decrease of exactly that size suspends the decision; a larger decrease reverses it, as long as t remains admissible. The share margin 1/11 and this distance of 1 point are different quantities.

    Here a decision is called robust over a domain of changes if it stays the same for every change in that domain. You must state this domain: a result that is stable near the initial setting may reverse further away. This calculation does not say which weights the group should adopt.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the numbers. Explain why the same opinions give different winners, then recover the boundary for Nora’s weight. What statement must you add before claiming that the decision withstands changes?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The room supported by the most people necessarily has the most weight.”

    Write the counts separately: 2 people against 1, but 5 points against 6. Check whether the rule requires a majority of people or a majority of weight. This case refutes the claim that they always agree.

    Review the count and the check of the counterexample

  • “I lower Nora’s weight while keeping the initial total in the denominator.”

    Only support for Elms stays fixed. When t changes, the total becomes 5 + t. Recalculate the shares with this same new total and check that they sum to 1.

    Review the shares that depend on Nora’s weight

  • “Nearby trials give the same winner, so it will win with every set of weights.”

    Trials establish only their own results. Find a boundary using the sign of t − 5, then state the interval over which the winner is preserved. A case beyond this boundary can contradict the generalization.

    Test the scope of robustness in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Check a claim

The rules can also agree

Keep the workshop opinions and assign 1 weight point to each person. Give the winner under each rule. Is this enough to repair the claim “the rules always give the same winner”, refuted in the example?

Hint 1

With all weights equal, adding weights here amounts to counting people.

Hint 2

Does a case where the conclusion is true erase an allowed case where it is false?

Compare with the solution

Elms has 1 + 1 = 2 points out of 3; its weighted share is 2/3, exactly its share of the people. Elms wins under both rules being compared.

This result shows that the rules can agree. It does not repair a universal claim: the initial case is still allowed and contradicts it. A true statement here is: with equal weights and all votes recorded in our scene, the weighted shares coincide with the shares of people.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate Elms’s share using the new total.
  • Distinguish “can agree” from “always agree”.
  • Keep the initial counterexample as a refutation.

Exercise 2 · Exact calculation and checking

A change that does not reverse the decision

Use the initial weights of Ines and Leo. Nora’s weight changes from 6 to 5.5 points, a decrease of 0.5 point. Predict the winner, then calculate the new total, the exact shares and their difference as Linden minus Elms. Compare the difference between the shares with the decrease in weight.

Hint 1

Is Nora’s new weight still greater than 5?

Hint 2

Write 5.5 in the form 11/2 and keep fractions throughout.

Compare with the solution

Since 5.5 is greater than 5, Linden should still win. The total is 5 + 11/2 = 21/2 weight points, or 10.5 points.

MO=521/2=10/21;MT=11/221/2=11/21

The raw difference is 11/2 − 5 = 1/2 weight point. The difference between the shares is 1/2 ÷ (21/2) = 1/21, positive and without a unit: Linden wins. Check: 10/21 + 11/21 = 1 and M_T is strictly greater than 1/2.

The weight decreased by 0.5 point; the new share margin is 1/21. They cannot be treated as the same quantity: their units and roles differ. The chosen decrease did not reach the boundary.

Check your reasoning

  • Predict the winner before dividing.
  • Recalculate the total and keep exact fractions.
  • Check the sum of the shares and the sign of their difference.
  • Distinguish a change in weight points from a margin with no unit.

Exercise 3 · Justify a conclusion and limit its scope

How far does the claim of robustness hold?

The opinions and the weights of Ines and Leo remain as in the example. Nora proposes: “Linden wins for every weight t between 5.5 and 6.5 points, including both endpoints; therefore it wins for every nonnegative weight t.” Justify the first statement without listing values, refute the second and give the boundary case with no winner.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For the first domain, compare its lower endpoint with the boundary.

Hint 2

For the generalization, look for an allowed weight on the other side of the boundary.

Compare with the solution

In the proposed interval, t is at least 5.5, which exceeds 5. The numerator t − 5 is therefore positive throughout this interval, and so is the denominator 5 + t. Thus Δ(t) is positive everywhere in this domain: Linden wins throughout it. This reasoning covers the entire interval, beyond its tested endpoints.

The conclusion about all admissible weights is false. With t = 4, Elms has 5/9 of the weight and wins; this weight is positive and therefore allowed. With t = 5, each side has 1/2: neither exceeds the strict majority threshold.

The precise repair is: Linden wins for every t strictly greater than 5 points, with the other opinions and weights fixed. The stated interval lies within this domain; it does not represent all possible changes. Success with a hint remains an assisted answer, to be reconstructed without help on another day.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify the entire interval using the sign of the margin.
  • Give an admissible counterexample to the generalization.
  • Treat the tie separately from a win and a reversal.
  • State explicitly which opinions and which weights stay fixed.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must you check for a case to refute a general claim?

The case must satisfy all the assumptions of the claim and make its conclusion false. A single counterexample is enough to refute “always”; it does not prove the opposite for every case.

Recall 2How can Elms win by people and lose by weight?

It has 2 people out of 3, but only 3 + 2 = 5 points against Nora’s 6. The reference populations and the contributions being counted must be stated.

Recall 3What must you fix before studying sensitivity to Nora’s weight?

The rule, the opinions, the weights of Ines and Leo, and the admissible domain of t. When t changes, the total becomes 5 + t; it must be recalculated in each share.

Recall 4Recover the boundary and the role of a tie without rereading the trials.

The difference Linden minus Elms is (t − 5)/(5 + t), with a positive denominator. Above 5, Linden wins; below it, Elms wins; at the tie, neither share is strictly greater than 1/2.

Recall 5What detail is missing from “the decision is robust”?

The domain of changes considered and what stays fixed. In this scene, all t strictly greater than 5 preserve Linden as the winner; this does not cover all admissible weights. Matching results from trials alone do not establish a universal property.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct with the lesson closed: the scene and its rules; the count of people; the count of weights; the case that refutes “always”; the input being varied; the boundary and the tie; the domain over which the winner stays the same. Finish by stating what this calculation does not decide about the quality of the choice.
