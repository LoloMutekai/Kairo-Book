Level 1 · C030·M4

Binomial distribution

Your goalRecognize a binomial count, calculate a probability by distinguishing a sequence from the number of ways to arrange its outcomes, then check the assumptions of the model.

Before you start
c029-m5 : Bayes · c030-m1 : discrete distributions · c030-m2 : expected value · c030-m3 : variance

✿01 See the idea

A workshop inspects 3 lamps before packing them. We want to know the probability of finding exactly 1 defective lamp. Before calculating, imagine the possible positions of the defect: at the start of the inspections, in the middle, or at the end. The number of defects is the same, but the sequences of outcomes differ.

The scene and all its probabilities are fictional. For this exercise, each lamp is defective with the same probability p = 1/4 = 0.25, with no unit. Defects in different lamps are assumed to be independent: knowing the results of the other inspections does not change this probability. We inspect all 3 lamps, even if a defect appears early.

Let D stand for the outcome “defective”, C for “compliant”, and X for the number of defective lamps in the batch. Each inspection has only these outcomes, and the classification (ranking) is assumed to be error-free. The word success will mean the outcome being counted, so D here: it makes no judgment about the quality of the lamp.

The binomial distribution describes the number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials that have the same probability of success. A probability for a particular sequence is not enough: we must include all sequences that give the required count.

The rule to remember

Fix the number of inspections and the outcome being counted; check independence and a common probability; multiply for a sequence, then count its possible arrangements.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Exactly 1 defective lamp in the batch

The fictional batch contains n = 3 lamps. The common probability of a defect is p = 1/4, the inspections are independent, and their number is fixed before we begin. Let us calculate P(X = 1), then check the resulting distribution.

  1. Define what is being counted

    X is the random variable that assigns to each sequence its number of defective lamps; its value is expressed in lamps. n is the whole number of lamps inspected, fixed in advance. k is a possible number of defects: an integer between 0 and n. P(X = k) denotes the probability that the count X equals k.

    p is the probability of a defect at each inspection, and q = 1 − p is the probability of compliance. p, q, and P(X = k) have no unit. Here q = 1 − 1/4 = 3/4 = 0.75, with no unit. For the formula below, take n to be strictly positive and p to be strictly between 0 and 1.

    The assumptions concern the outcomes, not how we write them: fixed n, classification as D or C, identical p, and mutual independence of all inspections. A shared production fault can make defects dependent; using the formula would not remove this problem.

  2. Read the binomial rule

    The notation X ∼ B(n, p) means that X follows the binomial distribution with parameters n and p. Here the symbol ∼ is read as “follows the distribution”. The symbol to the left of p in the formula, with n above k inside parentheses, is the binomial coefficient, also written C(n, k). It counts the ways to choose the k positions of the defects among the n available positions, without counting the same choice more than once.

    P(X=k)=(nk)pkqn−k

    The product of p to the power k and q to the power n − k gives the probability of a particular sequence: k defects and n − k compliant lamps. Independence allows this multiplication. The common probability p means that all sequences with the same count have the same weight. The coefficient counts these sequences; we add their weights because they are mutually exclusive for the same batch.

  3. Calculate the probability of a particular sequence

    Take D C C. We need 1 defect, then a compliant lamp, then another compliant lamp. Multiply the probabilities of these independent outcomes. q to the power 2 means q multiplied by q; the square accounts for the 2 compliant lamps.

    P(D C C)=pq2=1/4×3/4×3/4=9/64

    Intermediate step: q × q = 9/16, then p × 9/16 = 9/64. So far, this result covers only D C C. It excludes a defect appearing in another position.

  4. Include every possible position of the defect

    The required sequences are D C C, C D C, and C C D. There are 3 of them, each with probability 9/64. We have therefore counted the coefficient C(3, 1) = 3 directly in the diagram, without assuming an additional counting rule.

    P(X=1)=3×9/64=27/64=0.421875

    The probability has no unit. Its decimal form is exact here. It concerns exactly 1 defect in the entire batch, rather than the probability p of a defect in a particular lamp.

  5. Build the other counts

    No defects corresponds only to C C C: P(X = 0) = q × q × q = 27/64. All inspections finding defects corresponds only to D D D: P(X = 3) = p × p × p = 1/64.

    For exactly 2 defects, the sequences are D D C, D C D, and C D D. A sequence has weight p × p × q: p × p = 1/16, then 1/16 × 3/4 = 3/64. The total weight for this count is therefore 3 × 3/64 = 9/64. The position of the compliant lamp plays the same role as the position of the defect in the previous calculation.

    All sequences are thus grouped by their number of D outcomes. The possible counts are not equally likely: listing the values of X does not justify assigning them equal shares.

  6. Check the sum and the meaning of the question

    The probabilities must be positive and total 1, because each batch has exactly one of these counts. Add without rounding: 27/64 + 27/64 = 27/32; 9/64 + 1/64 = 5/32; 27/32 + 5/32 = 1. No possible outcome has been left out.

    “At least 1 defect” includes every count except 0. Its probability is 1 − 27/64 = 37/64; it exceeds 27/64, since batches with several defects are now included. The corresponding exercise will ask you to recover this result through another addition.

  7. Connect the count to its expected value and variance

    For this binomial count, E(X) = np and Var(X) = npq. E denotes the expected value, meaning the mean predicted by the distribution; Var denotes the variance, the mean of the squared deviations from this expected value. The expected value is expressed in lamps and the variance in lamps squared.

    We can recover these rules by assigning each lamp 1 if it is defective and 0 otherwise. This small count has expected value p and variance p − p × p = p(1 − p). X adds the counts for all lamps. Their expected values add; their independence also allows their variances to add.

    E(X)=3×1/4=3/4 lampe
    Var(X)=3×1/4×3/4=9/16 lampe2

    An independent check of the mean using the distribution: 0 × 27/64 + 1 × 27/64 + 2 × 9/64 + 3 × 1/64 = 3/4 lamp. This mean can be fractional even though each batch has a whole number of defects. It does not predict the exact count for the next batch; neither does the variance.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculation. Why must you multiply by the number of arrangements after calculating the probability of D C C? Then recall the assumption that allows the product, followed by the assumption that makes sequences with the same count equally likely.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “p × q × q already answers “exactly 1 defect”.”

    This product describes only D C C. List the other positions of the defect, then add the probabilities of the distinct sequences. An event defined by the count includes several sequences.

    Review the calculation for a sequence and the grouping by count

  • “There are 2 outcomes per inspection, so it must be binomial.”

    Also check the fixed number of inspections, the common probability, and independence. A shared cause affecting all lamps still leaves 2 outcomes per lamp but can change the entire distribution of the count.

    Work through the final exercise on the shared cause

  • “Exactly 1 defect and at least 1 defect are the same thing.”

    Write down the counts accepted by each phrase. “At least” also includes higher counts. The complement of “no defects” is “at least 1”, not “exactly 1”.

    Review the check on the meaning of the question

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Change the required count

Keep the same batch and assumptions. Recover P(X = 2) by writing out all possible sequences before using the formula. Distinguish the probability of a sequence from that of the count.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First place the only C; all other positions must contain D.

Hint 2

Calculate p × p × q for a sequence, then count the positions for C.

Compare with the solution

D D C, D C D, and C D D are the required sequences. They are mutually exclusive, and there are 3 of them. For each one, independence gives p × p × q = 1/16 × 3/4 = 3/64.

Adding their weights: P(X = 2) = 3 × 3/64 = 9/64 = 0.140625, with no unit. The coefficient C(3, 2) equals 3 here, because choosing the positions of the defects amounts to choosing the only compliant position.

Check: this result is less than 27/64, the probability of a single defect. In our model, where p is less than q, replacing a compliant lamp with a defective one reduces the weight of each sequence; the numbers of sequences are equal here.

Check your reasoning

  • List the sequences without omissions or duplicates.
  • Justify the product through independence, then the sum through the mutual exclusivity of the sequences.
  • Give a probability with no unit and distinguish a particular sequence from a count.

Exercise 2 · Independent reconstruction

A batch that needs rework

The workshop reworks a batch whenever it contains at least 1 defect. Using our fictional model, calculate the probability of rework through the complement, then check it by adding the probabilities of the relevant counts. Explain why np is not the answer.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The only case that avoids rework is C C C.

Hint 2

The sum used to check the result includes X = 1, X = 2, and X = 3.

Compare with the solution

No rework means X = 0, with probability 27/64. The probability of rework is therefore 1 − 27/64 = 37/64 = 0.578125, with no unit.

Another route: P(X = 1) + P(X = 2) + P(X = 3) = 27/64 + 9/64 + 1/64 = 27/64 + 5/32 = 37/64. This gives exactly the same result as the complement.

np = 3/4 lamp is a mean number of defects per batch. Rework is an event whose probability we want to find. The quantities have different meanings and units. No observed frequency of rework in a real workshop has been measured here.

Check your reasoning

  • Name the complementary event before subtracting.
  • Obtain the same fraction by adding the probabilities of the accepted counts.
  • Distinguish the probability of rework from the expected number of defects.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and critique of the model

The same probability is not enough

In a fictional variant of the same workshop, a shared cause makes all 3 lamps defective with probability 1/4; otherwise they are all compliant. Each lamp therefore keeps the same individual probability p of being defective. Can we keep the binomial distribution? Give P(X = 1), the probability of rework, and the expected value of X in this variant.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write down the only sequences allowed by the shared cause.

Hint 2

Knowing that a lamp is defective then tells us everything about the others.

Hint 3

For the expected value, multiply each possible count by its probability and add.

Compare with the solution

The only sequences are D D D, with probability 1/4, and C C C, with probability 3/4. The number of inspections remains fixed and each lamp retains p, but the outcomes are no longer independent. As soon as a lamp is defective, the others are certain to be defective too.

P(X = 1) is now 0, because this count is impossible. The probability of rework is 1/4 = 0.25, with no unit, instead of 37/64 in the independent model. We cannot retain a formula when an essential assumption has been removed.

The expected value remains 0 × 3/4 + 3 × 1/4 = 3/4 lamp. Recovering np therefore does not validate the binomial distribution: the mean does not identify an entire distribution. The new distribution is consistent because its probabilities 3/4 and 1/4 total 1. These variants are exercise models; choosing between them for a real workshop would require authorized observations and an examination of the causes of defects.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify independence precisely as the assumption that has been lost.
  • Reconstruct the distribution from the only allowed sequences.
  • Calculate the probability of rework and the mean using the new distribution.
  • Explain why the same mean does not prove that the distributions are the same.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What conditions must be checked before calling a count binomial?

A fixed number of trials, an outcome being counted versus its opposite, the same probability of success, and mutual independence of all trials. Success is simply the outcome being counted.

Recall 2What role does the binomial coefficient play in P(X = k)?

It counts the arrangements of k successes among n trials. A particular sequence has weight p to the power k multiplied by q to the power n − k; adding the probabilities of the sequences with this count amounts to multiplying by their number.

Recall 3How can we calculate “at least 1 defect” without adding the probabilities of all positive counts?

Take the complement of “no defects”. For independent lamps, no defects has probability q to the power n; subtract it from 1. This does not give the probability of exactly 1 defect.

Recall 4What are the expected value and variance of the binomial count, and what are their units?

The expected value is np, in lamps in our scene. The variance is npq, in lamps squared, with q = 1 − p. A fractional mean is compatible with a whole number count for each batch.

Recall 5Why is checking the probability of a defect for each lamp not enough?

Individual probabilities do not describe their dependence. A shared cause can concentrate all defects in the same batches. The mean can remain the same while the probabilities of the counts change.
