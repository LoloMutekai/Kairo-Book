Level 1 · C055·M2

Law of large numbers

Your goalExplain why a frequency stabilizes around a probability under specific assumptions, without promising that it gets closer after every draw.

Before you start
c035-m5 : finite covariance and conditioning · c024-m5 : measurement quality · c055-m1 : sampling distribution

✿01 See the idea

A bag contains 4 tokens, of which 1 is blue and 3 are not blue. You draw a token without looking, record its color, put it back, then mix again. All tokens are equally likely to be drawn. The contents therefore stay the same from one draw to the next.

Your record counts the blue outcomes. After 10 draws, it contains 4: the observed frequency is 0.40, whereas the model probability is 0.25. The frequency describes this record; the probability describes the drawing rule. They can differ.

As you continue making independent draws under this same rule, observing a frequency that differs from the probability by more than a fixed tolerance becomes less and less likely. This is the idea behind the law of large numbers. Yet a new blue outcome can temporarily move the frequency further away: chance does not correct every step.

The bag and all numerical records on this page are fictional. They support the reasoning; no real series or measured performance is presented. We study only the frequency of blue outcomes, and have not yet reached the shape of its distribution or a confidence interval.

The rule to remember

Accumulating independent draws with the same distribution stabilizes the frequency in probability; this guarantees neither getting closer at every step nor compensation on the next draw.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From a record to a probability guarantee

The first block contains 4 blue outcomes in 10 draws. The next block contains 2 in 10 draws. We calculate the cumulative frequency, then what the law allows us to state before seeing a longer record. All draws in the model are made with replacement and independently.

  1. Name the objects before counting

    i is the position of a draw; n is the number of draws, a strictly positive integer. Xᵢ is 1 for a blue outcome and 0 otherwise. Sn counts the blue outcomes among the first n draws. Fn is their frequency: a sum of these indicators divided by the number of draws.

    Fn=Snn=X1+⋯+Xnn

    p is the fixed probability of a blue outcome: 1/4 = 1/4 = 0.25. The draws all have this same distribution; they are independent, so knowing the past colors does not change this probability. n and Sn are counts of draws; the indicators, frequencies and probabilities have no physical unit.

  2. Read the first block without changing the target

    Divide the number of blue outcomes by all draws, including non-blue outcomes: 4/10 = 2/5 = 0.40. Dividing by blue outcomes alone would not give a frequency for the whole record.

    The distance from p is the absolute value of Fn − p, written |Fn − p|: we keep the size of the difference, regardless of its sign. Here the frequency is above p, so the distance is 0.40 − 0.25 = 0.15, with no unit. This observation does not change the known contents of the bag.

  3. Combine the blocks and check the denominator

    The total number of draws becomes 10 + 10 = 20. The total number of blue outcomes becomes 4 + 2 = 6. Add the counts before dividing, because the frequency covers the whole record.

    F20=4+210+10=620=0.30

    The distance becomes 0.30 − 0.25 = 0.05. It has decreased in this particular continuation. Check by recounting: 6 blue outcomes and 14 non-blue outcomes do give 20 draws. Another approach: since the blocks have the same size, the mean of their frequencies is (0.40 + 0.20)/2 = 0.30. Different sizes would require a weighted average.

  4. State exactly what the law promises

    Fix a tolerance ε, the Greek letter epsilon, that is strictly positive and has no unit. P(A) denotes the probability of an event A in our model. For each size n, compare all possible records of that size. The weak law of large numbers states that the probability of a frequency at least ε away from p tends to 0 as n grows without limit.

    P(|Fn−p|≥ε)→0 lorsque n→∞

    The arrow means “tends to” and ∞ means that n increases without bound. This is convergence in probability: for a tolerance fixed in advance, records that are too far away become arbitrarily unlikely at a sufficiently large size. It imposes neither equality at a finite position nor a decrease in the distance after every draw. Let us verify this statement in our binary model.

  5. Understand why the spread of the mean decreases

    E denotes expectation: the mean of the possible values weighted by their probabilities. Var denotes variance, the expectation of the squared difference from that mean. For Xᵢ, E(Xᵢ) = p and Var(Xᵢ) = p(1 − p) = (1/4) × (3/4) = 3/16. These are numbers with no physical unit; variance has the square of the indicator’s unit.

    The expectation of Fn remains p. Independence makes the covariances between draws 0: the variance of their sum is n times the variance of a draw. Dividing the sum by n divides its variance by n squared. This leaves a division by n:

    Var(Fn)=np(1−p)n2=p(1−p)n

    The spread of an individual outcome stays the same. It is the spread of the mean of many draws that decreases. We have not assumed any normal distribution.

  6. Turn this spread into a bound

    For each record whose distance reaches ε, the squared distance is at least the square of ε. The other squares are nonnegative. Thus the variance of Fn, equal to the expectation of the square of Fn − p, is at least the square of ε multiplied by the probability of these records. Dividing by this strictly positive square gives the Chebyshev bound, justified here for our frequency:

    P(|Fn−p|≥ε)≤p(1−p)nε2

    The symbol ≤ means “less than or equal to”. For fixed ε, the numerator and the square of ε stay constant while n increases. The bound therefore tends to 0; the probability, which is always nonnegative, also tends to 0. This proves the weak law in this model. It does not claim to cover all versions of the law of large numbers.

  7. Calculate the bound and keep its scope clear

    Choose ε = 1/10 = 0.10 and n = 1000 draws, before looking at their result. The squared tolerance is 0.10 × 0.10 = 0.01. The variance of Fn is (3/16)/1000 = 3/16000.

    P(|F1000−0.25|≥0.10)≤3/160000.01=3/160=0.01875

    0.01875 is a probability bound with no unit, rather than the exact probability of the event or a measured result. Check the scale: with only 100 draws, the variance would be 3/1600 and the bound 3/16 = 0.1875, which is larger. The product of each bound and its sample size is identical: the factor that changes is indeed the denominator n.

    Even with 1000 draws, a record outside the tolerance remains possible. The calculation assumes replacement, the same p and independence; adding copies to a file or changing the drawing rule does not give this guarantee.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “With more draws, every new frequency will be closer to p.”

    The law concerns the probability of a deviation at a given size. A blue outcome after the record of 20 draws can increase the distance; calculate this step and compare the distances, as well as the counts.

    Calculate the next step in the fluctuation exercise

  • “Blue outcomes are overdue: they will have to occur to compensate.”

    Under independence, colors already drawn change neither the bag nor the probability of the next blue outcome, which remains 1/4. Convergence of frequencies is not a debt that subsequent draws repay.

    Review replacement and the drawing rule

  • “A very long record is enough, even if I keep copying the same draw.”

    The number of rows does not measure the number of independent observations. If all rows copy a single color, the covariances no longer vanish. Revisit the record of copies in the last exercise.

    Test dependence in the copied record

Exercise 1 · Numerical application

A longer record from the same bag

Another fictional record from the same bag contains 27 blue outcomes among 100 independent draws with replacement. Calculate its frequency and its distance from p. Does this prove the law of large numbers? What probability do you assign to the next blue outcome?

Hint 2

Compare with the solution

The frequency is 27/100 = 0.27, with no unit. The distance is |0.27 − 0.25| = 0.02. In this record, the frequency is close to the known probability for the bag.

A finite record illustrates a possible behavior; it does not prove a limit concerning the distribution of all records. The proof in the lesson comes from the bound and its assumptions. The next blue outcome keeps the probability 1/4 = 0.25 through independence, even after reading this record.

Exercise 2 · Continuation and objection

A step in the wrong direction

Return to the 6 blue outcomes in 20 draws in the example. The next draw is blue. Calculate the new frequency and its exact distance from p; compare it with the previous distance 0.05. Does this contradict the law?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The blue count and the total count both increase by 1.

Hint 2

Keep the exact fraction before subtracting 1/4; then compare the distances.

Compare with the solution

We get 6 + 1 = 7 blue outcomes in 20 + 1 = 21 draws. The frequency becomes 7/21 = 1/3 ≈ 0.3333.

The exact distance is (1/3) − (1/4) = 1/12 ≈ 0.0833, greater than 0.05. The sample has grown and its frequency has moved further away. This possible step does not contradict convergence in probability: convergence does not require improvement after every draw.

Check your reasoning

  • Increase both counts and obtain 1/3.
  • Compare the distances from p, using an exact calculation before rounding.
  • Explain why a fluctuation does not refute the law.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and assumptions

Many rows, a single draw

You draw just once from the same bag, replace the token, then copy the recorded color onto 1000 rows instead of drawing again. What frequencies can the record have? For ε = 0.10, what is the probability that its distance from p is at least ε? Why can you not apply the bound 0.01875?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

If the first token is blue, every row says so; otherwise, none does.

Hint 2

Compare each possible frequency with 0.25. Then check independence, as well as the distribution of each row.

Compare with the solution

If the initial draw is blue, the frequency is 1000/1000 = 1. Otherwise it is 0. The distances from p are respectively 0.75 and 0.25, both greater than 0.10. The event “distance of at least ε” therefore occurs with probability 1, regardless of the number of copies.

Taken separately, each row does have the same probability p of showing blue, but all are copies of the same variable. They are dependent: knowing a row reveals all the others. The mean is always equal to the indicator of the initial draw; its variance remains 3/16 and does not become 3/16000. The bound in the lesson used independence to remove the covariances. That assumption is missing here, so the result does not apply.

Check your reasoning

  • Find the only possible frequencies 0 and 1.
  • Justify the probability 1 of remaining outside the tolerance.
  • Identify the dependence and the step in the reasoning that is no longer valid.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do Fₙ and p distinguish in the bag model?

Fₙ is the observed number of blue outcomes divided by the number of draws in the record. p is the fixed probability in the drawing model. The former fluctuates; the latter remains 1/4 for this bag with replacement.

Recall 2What does the weak law promise for a positive tolerance fixed in advance?

Under the assumptions of the lesson, the probability that |Fₙ − p| reaches or exceeds this tolerance tends to 0 as n increases without limit. This does not determine the result of a particular record.

Recall 3What assumptions connect our bag to the law we have proved?

Independent draws, all with the same probability p, and indicators bounded between 0 and 1, so with finite variance. Replacement and mixing maintain this ideal model; copying a result destroys independence.

Recall 4Why does the variance of the frequency include a division by n?

Independence gives n variances to add for the sum, then the mean’s division by n divides the variance by n squared. This leaves p(1 − p)/n. The bound on the probability of a large deviation follows from this.

Recall 5Why does a greater distance after the next draw not contradict the law?

The law describes convergence in probability, rather than improvement at every step. The next blue outcome keeps the probability p; past outcomes impose no compensation on it.
