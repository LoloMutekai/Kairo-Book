Level 1 · C056·M2

Decision errors

Your goalDistinguish a false signal from a missed change, calculate their probabilities in a simple model, and explain why not rejecting a hypothesis does not prove it.

Before you start
c055-m5 : confidence interval · c056-m1 : hypotheses

✿01 See the idea

A machine fills bottles. An inspection takes a sample of bottles and checks whether their liquid reaches a mark. An unusual sample may suggest that the machine is out of adjustment; it may also occur while the machine is still operating under its reference condition. The inspection result and the actual state of the machine are therefore two different things.

We are looking for an increase in the proportion of bottles below the mark. Rejecting the reference hypothesis means triggering a statistical signal. If the operating condition has actually stayed the same, the signal is a Type I error, or false signal. If the proportion has increased and the inspection does not reject the reference hypothesis, this is a Type II error: a missed change.

To name an error, we need to know the actual situation as well as the decision. During a real inspection, the actual situation usually remains unknown. Saying “no signal” therefore describes the decision made by the rule; it does not certify the state of the machine.

The scenario and all its probabilities are fictional. We assume a model in which each bottle is below the mark with probability p. The draws are independent: knowing the result of the first does not change the probability for the next. The proportion p stays constant during the inspection. No readings from a real machine are presented.

The rule to remember

Always consider the actual situation together with the decision. Calculate false signals among inspections under the reference condition, and missed changes among inspections under a precisely specified increase.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

One inspection, two ways to be wrong

The reference condition gives p = 1/4. We sample 2 independent bottles and reject the reference hypothesis only if all are below the mark, giving outcome SS. Let us calculate the probability of a false signal, then the probability of missing an increase that would bring p to 1/2.

  1. Separate the parameter, hypotheses, and observations

    p is the probability that a bottle is below the mark. It lies between 0 and 1 and has no unit. In this stable model, it represents the theoretical proportion of such bottles. S denotes this result; C denotes its opposite, with probability 1 − p. The letters do not say whether the operating condition of the machine has changed.

    The null hypothesis is the reference condition. The alternative is an increase in p. The hypothesis subscripts are labels; they do not count bottles or trials.

    H0:p=1/4;H1:p>1/4

    We are studying an increase and draw no conclusion about a possible decrease. To calculate an error probability under the alternative, we need to fix a precise value of p. Here we will choose 1/2. The number of bottles, independence, the stability of p, and the rejection rule are fixed before we see the results.

  2. List the paths and assign each a probability

    Let D denote the decision “reject the reference hypothesis”. It occurs exactly on SS. P denotes a probability; the vertical bar in P(D | p) means that we calculate under a fixed value of p. The symbol P is therefore distinct from the parameter p.

    Under the reference condition, S has probability 1/4 and C has probability 3/4 = 1 − 1/4. Independence allows us to multiply the probabilities of successive results. Thus SS has probability 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16; SC has probability 1/4 × 3/4 = 3/16; CS has probability 3/4 × 1/4 = 3/16; CC has probability 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16.

    These outcomes are mutually exclusive: a single inspection produces only one of them. We therefore add their probabilities to combine several outcomes. The diagram does not represent observed inspections; it represents all possible results in the model.

  3. Calculate false signals among inspections under the reference condition

    α, read as “alpha”, is the probability of rejecting when the reference hypothesis is true. Since only outcome SS triggers the signal, we have already calculated this error probability.

    α=P(D|p=1/4)=14×14=1/16

    To express this fraction as a percentage, multiply it by 100: 1/16 × 100 = 6.25 %. This value is the exact error probability of our rule in the fictional model, rather than a conventional threshold to memorize or a proportion of false signals measured on a machine.

    The condition is essential: all inspections considered here have p = 1/4. α does not give the probability that the reference hypothesis is true after seeing a signal. That reverse question would require other information.

  4. Calculate missed changes under a chosen increase

    β, read as “beta”, denotes the probability of not rejecting under a given alternative. We write β(p) to remind ourselves that it depends on the true value of p. Set p = 1/2: each result S or C then has probability 1/2. The outcomes SS, SC, CS, and CC each have probability 1/4.

    Not rejecting means obtaining SC, CS, or CC. We add these mutually exclusive outcomes: 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2, then 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4. The errors are counted among inspections where this increase actually exists.

    β(1/2)=P(ne pas rejeter|p=1/2)=3/4=75.00 %

    Check using the complementary event: under this increase, rejecting has probability 1/4. So not rejecting has probability 1 − 1/4 = 3/4, as with the sum. A low probability of false signals can therefore coexist with many missed changes in the model.

  5. Check the totals and limit the conclusion

    Under the reference condition, outcomes without rejection total 3/16 + 3/16 + 9/16 = 15/16. Including rejection: 1/16 + 15/16 = 1. Under the chosen increase: 1/4 + 3/4 = 1. Each actual situation does give a total probability equal to 1.

    It would be incorrect to add α and β as though they referred to the same set of inspections: the first assumes the reference hypothesis is true, and the second assumes a fixed increase. To obtain an overall error probability, we would also need to know how often each operating condition occurs.

    If the observed result is SC, the rule does not reject. SC remains possible under both the reference condition and the increase; this result alone therefore does not reveal whether the decision is correct or is a Type II error. Concluding “this inspection has not established an increase” respects what we know.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results. Explain why we multiply for SS, why we add for a missed change, and why the two error probabilities are calculated under different actual situations. What can you state after observing SC?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The inspection does not reject, so the reference hypothesis is true.”

    Under p = 1/2, SC, CS, and CC trigger no rejection, yet their probabilities total 3/4. Not rejecting can therefore miss a real increase. The decision alone does not tell us which row of the diagram applies.

    Consider the actual situation together with the decision again

  • “α is the probability that my signal is false.”

    α assumes the reference hypothesis is true before examining the possible results. It counts signals among those inspections. Looking for the proportion of false signals among all signals reverses the conditioning; our example does not provide the frequencies of the operating conditions needed for this new calculation.

    Revisit the condition in the alpha calculation

  • “Making rejection harder is enough to eliminate errors.”

    With the sampling design fixed, removing outcomes from the rejection set reduces false signals but may increase missed changes. Compare our SS rule with the rule that rejects as soon as a bottle is below the mark. Check both error probabilities before deciding which rule is suitable.

    Compare the rules in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Recognize and justify

The same rule, different actual situations

In a fictional audit, the actual operating condition is exceptionally known. An inspection under p = 1/4 gives SS; another under p = 1/2 gives SC. For each, state the decision and name any error. If the audit did not provide p, what could we still state from SC?

Hint 1

First apply the rule to each sequence of letters, without using the actual situation.

Hint 2

Then compare each decision with the operating condition reported by the audit.

Compare with the solution

SS triggers rejection when p = 1/4: this is a false signal, hence a Type I error. SC does not trigger rejection when p = 1/2: the increase exists and the inspection misses it, so this is a Type II error.

Without knowing p, SC only allows us to report “no rejection with this rule”. It does not allow us to declare the reference hypothesis true or to state that an error has actually occurred. Naming the error requires the actual situation that the audit provided.

Check your reasoning

  • You separate applying the rule from knowing the operating condition.
  • You associate a false signal with a true reference hypothesis and a missed change with a real increase.
  • You maintain uncertainty about the actual situation when p is not given.

Exercise 2 · Exact calculation and interpretation

A larger increase

The rule remains SS and the sample still contains 2 independent bottles. Calculate the Type II error probability if the true proportion becomes p = 3/4. Does it predict more or fewer missed changes than under p = 1/2? Has the error probability α changed?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate the probability of SS under the new value of p.

Hint 2

The outcomes without rejection form the complementary event. For α, ask yourself under which value of p the calculation is defined.

Compare with the solution

The probability of SS becomes 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16. The probability of missing the increase is therefore 1 − 9/16 = 7/16, or 43.75 %.

We can check by summing the other outcomes. C now has probability 1/4. SC and CS each have probability 3/16; CC has probability 1/16. Their sum is 3/16 + 3/16 + 1/16 = 7/16. These probabilities recur because of the symmetry of the products, even though the actual situation has changed.

7/16 is less than 3/4: this larger increase is missed less often by the same rule. α remains 1/16, or 6.25 %, because its definition keeps p = 1/4 and the rule has not changed. You need to state the chosen alternative when reporting β; a single number does not describe all increases.

Check your reasoning

  • You use 3/4 for each factor in the probability of SS.
  • You find 7/16 using the complement and check it with the other outcomes.
  • You distinguish a change in the alternative from a change in the rule.

Exercise 3 · Compare and argue

A signal that is easier to trigger

In the same scenario, replace the rule with “reject as soon as at least 1 bottle is below the mark”. Keep 2 independent draws. Calculate the new Type I error probability under p = 1/4, then the Type II error probability under p = 1/2. Someone claims this rule is better in every respect: respond using both error probabilities.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Identify the only outcome that triggers no signal under the new rule.

Hint 2

Under the reference condition, calculate the probability of this outcome, then take its complement. Under the increase, this same outcome is already the error we are looking for.

Compare with the solution

The new rule rejects on SS, SC, and CS. Only CC gives no rejection. Under the reference condition, CC has probability 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16. The probability of a false signal becomes 1 − 9/16 = 7/16, or 43.75 %.

Check by addition: 1/16 + 3/16 + 3/16 = 7/16. Under p = 1/2, missing the increase now requires CC, with probability 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, or 25.00 %.

Compared with the SS rule, the Type II error probability decreases from 75.00 % to 25.00 %, while the Type I error probability increases from 6.25 % to 43.75 %. These conditional probabilities describe two different actual situations. Choosing a rule requires specifying the consequences of each error and the constraints of the inspection; these calculations do not identify a universally better rule.

Check your reasoning

  • You identify CC as the only outcome without rejection under the new rule.
  • You calculate each error probability under the corresponding operating condition.
  • You justify the tradeoff using both results, without adding their percentages.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which combinations of actual situation and decision define Type I and Type II errors?

Type I: rejecting a true reference hypothesis, producing a false signal. Type II: not rejecting when the alternative is true, missing the change. A decision alone does not tell us whether an error has occurred.

Recall 2What actual situation do we fix to calculate α? Why is it not the probability that the observed signal is false?

We fix the reference hypothesis as true, here p = 1/4, then find the probability of rejecting. The proportion of false signals among all signals reverses the question and also depends on the frequencies of the operating conditions. α does not provide this information.

Recall 3Why do we need to specify a value of p when reporting β?

The alternative includes several possible increases. For the same rule, the probability of not rejecting may change with their size: here β equals 3/4 under p = 1/2 and 7/16 under p = 3/4. These numbers are calculated in the fictional model.

Recall 4Our rule observes SC and does not reject. What statement respects the scope of the inspection?

This inspection has not established an increase. SC remains possible under both operating conditions: the result does not prove the reference hypothesis and cannot by itself identify a Type II error.

Recall 5With the sampling design fixed, what does switching from the SS rule to the “at least 1 S” rule show?

Adding rejection outcomes makes the signal easier to trigger. In our model, this increases the probability of a false signal and reduces the probability of missing the chosen increase. We need to compare both error probabilities under their respective conditions before choosing.

Finish with a sentence stating what not rejecting allows us to conclude and what it leaves unknown.

What you understand
A test rule can produce a false signal or miss a change. A valid non-rejection leaves uncertainty about the actual situation; an invalid calculation also requires suspending interpretation. The bottle example helps explain this distinction.
