Level 2 · C062·M5

Auditing priors and sensitivity

Your goalSeparate assumptions from observations, then determine whether a decision holds under several stated priors.

Before you start
c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c056-m5 : effect size · c009-m5 : reading notes · c062-m1 : measurement, score and probability · c062-m2 : missing value and error · c062-m3 : Brier score and reliability by group · c062-m4 : threshold and cost of errors

✿01 See the idea

A part leaves a workshop. Its sensor triggers an alarm. Should the part be released or held for inspection? Everyone has the same alarm, but one person assumes defects are rare and another assumes they are frequent. They may therefore propose different decisions without having received different alarms.

The prior is what the model assigns to the possible outcomes before the observation under consideration. In this scene, it distributes probability between a defective part and a conforming part. The word “priors” in the title refers to these initial distributions. The posterior is the distribution after incorporating the alarm. Neither is the actual state of the part.

Auditing means making choices open to examination: where does the initial probability come from, which parts does it apply to, does it come from the same production process, and does it already include the alarm? Testing sensitivity then means repeating the calculation with other defensible choices, stating what remains fixed.

The entire scene is fictional: the sensor probabilities, priors and costs. No workshop readings justify them here. We compare the assumptions p = 0.10, 0.25 and 0.50, without treating the central choice as the truth. The test does not automatically select the best prior.

The rule to remember

Keep the observations fixed, state which assumptions you vary, recalculate the results and the decision, then frame a conclusion limited to that variation.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The same part under several initial assumptions

Alarm A has been observed once. We keep the same model: s = 0.75 and f = 0.25, dimensionless probabilities. Correct decisions cost 0 €; releasing a defective part or holding a conforming part costs 8 € in each case. When expected losses are equal, the rule chosen before the calculation is to hold the part.

  1. State what is known and what is chosen

    D means “defective part”, C means “conforming part”, and A means “the sensor triggers an alarm”. D and C are mutually exclusive and cover all states in this simplified model. P denotes a probability; the vertical bar means “given”. Thus, P(A | D) concerns the sensor on defective parts, whereas P(D | A) concerns parts that have triggered an alarm.

    We write p = P(D), s = P(A | D), f = P(A | C) and q = P(D | A). All these quantities are dimensionless and lie between 0 and 1. Here, s and f are strictly positive. The likelihood ratio L = s/f is 0.75 / 0.25 = 3: an alarm is 3 times as likely under D as under C, according to this model.

    The audit record distinguishes: the available observation, A; the assumed initial probability, p; the assumed sensor behaviour, s and f; the decision rule and costs. Using these parameters in a real workshop would require a dated source and a justification for transferring them. Their source is missing in our fiction: no claim of calibration follows from the calculation.

    We assume that these sensor probabilities apply to this part and that the alarm was not used to choose p. Counting it again in the update would reuse the same information. We have only one alarm: no independence between several sensors is assumed.

  2. Reconstruct the fraction to recalculate

    The probability of “defect and alarm” is sp. The probability of “conforming and alarm” is f(1 − p). We add them because these paths are mutually exclusive. Their sum is P(A). Among these alarms, the fraction attributed to a defect is therefore:

    q=spsp+f(1−p)=LpLp+1−p

    The second expression divides the numerator and denominator by f. P(A) must be strictly positive to condition on A. The quotient is dimensionless. This rule is a Bayesian update for states D and C; it does not turn assumed parameters into measured parameters.

  3. Calculate the case where defects are rare in full

    With p = 1/10, the probability of conformity is 1 − 1/10 = 9/10. We weight each sensor response by the probability of its initial state: defect and alarm, 3/4 × 1/10 = 3/40; conformity and alarm, 1/4 × 9/10 = 9/40.

    The total alarm probability is 3/40 + 9/40 = 3/10. Dividing by this sum amounts to looking only at cases that triggered an alarm.

    q=3/403/10=1/4=0.25

    Check: 1/4 × 3/10 = 3/40, the initial joint probability. The result remains between 0 and 1. It exceeds p, because the alarm favours D, but remains well below s: reversing the condition changes the question.

  4. Repeat without changing the alarm

    With p = 1/4, the complement is 3/4. The alarm paths have probabilities 3/16 and 3/16; the sum is 3/8. Hence q = (3/16) ÷ (3/8) = 0.50. The paths have the same weight: their shares after the alarm are equal.

    With p = 1/2, the complement is 1/2. The paths have probabilities 3/8 and 1/8; the sum is 1/2. Hence q = (3/8) ÷ (1/2) = 0.75. The sensor has not changed; only the assumed initial defect frequency has changed.

    The range of the tested results is 3/4 − 1/4 = 0.50 in probability. This is neither a confidence interval nor a probability assigned to the priors. The extremes describe chosen scenarios, rather than statistical uncertainty estimated from a sample.

  5. Move from probabilities to decisions

    R denotes “hold” and V denotes “release”. ℓ denotes expected loss, the average cost under the model after the alarm, in euros per decision. Holding incurs a cost here only if the part is conforming; releasing incurs a cost only if it is defective. These are simplified costs fixed for the exercise.

    ℓ(R)=8(1−q);ℓ(V)=8q

    We hold the part if ℓ(R) ≤ ℓ(V). Collecting terms gives the threshold t as 8 / (8 + 8) = 8 / 16 = 0.50. Equality is included by our initial rule.

    Low case: ℓ(V) = 2 € and ℓ(R) = 6 €; releasing minimises loss. Central case: 4 € and 4 €; equality, so hold. High case: 6 € and 2 €; holding minimises loss.

    Independent check on the units: in each case, the losses sum to 8 € because q and its complement sum to 1. An expected loss does not predict the cost actually observed for this part.

  6. Find the boundary and limit the conclusion

    The threshold t is a probability after the alarm. To find which prior causes the decision to switch, we solve q ≥ t in the preceding formula. Its denominator is positive: multiplying by it preserves the direction of the inequality. Collecting the terms in p gives p[L(1 − t) + t] ≥ t.

    p≥tL(1−t)+t=1/23×1/2+1/2=1/4

    The contribution L(1 − t) is 3/2, the denominator is 2, and the prior at the boundary is 0.25. We therefore hold the part from p = 0.25 inclusive. Check: this starting point is exactly our central case, which gave q = t.

    If the set of priors to examine is [0.10 ; 0.50], the decision is not robust over this set: it contains values on both sides of 0.25. For the narrower set [0.25 ; 0.50], the decision remains to hold. This does not prove that the narrower set is justified.

    The result applies to the fixed sensor and costs. A separate examination would be needed if s, f, the costs or the categories changed. A stable decision can accompany a varying probability; it can also remain systematically wrong if the assumptions are wrong.

  7. Write an audit verdict, even with a missing reading

    Our reproducible verdict fits in a single sentence: with an observed alarm, s = 0.75, f = 0.25 and the stated costs, the tested priors from 0.10 to 0.50 give probabilities from 0.25 to 0.75, and the decision switches at p = 0.25; the decision therefore remains dependent on the initial assumption.

    If the sensor reading were missing, we would no longer have observed A. We could neither apply this calculation nor treat the missing reading as a conforming part. Keeping p without an update would require stating that the missing reading provides no information about D; if sensor failure depends on the defect, we need a model of this missingness.

    The useful next step is to document the origin and defensible range of the priors, then check the sensor probabilities conditional on each state using authorised, separate observations. Choosing the starting point that gives the desired decision after seeing the results would invalidate the audit.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why the alarm stays the same but the decision changes. Then reconstruct the boundary between decisions and distinguish the initial assumption, the assumed sensor behaviour and the cost of an error.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The sensor has a value of 0.75, so the probability of a defect is 0.75 after the alarm.”

    0.75 is P(A | D), a sensor response probability when D is already assumed. To obtain P(D | A), we also need p and the possibility of an alarm under C. The low case gives 0.25, rather than 0.75.

    Revisit the alarm paths and their sum

  • “I choose the prior that gives the desired result: the audit is finished.”

    State the scenarios and their justification before looking at their results. A conclusion that changes must remain visible. Narrowing the tested set to obtain a stable decision requires a reason independent of the desired verdict.

    Test the conclusion in the final exercise

  • “The decision does not change, so the prior is validated.”

    The decision can stay on the same side of the threshold while q varies greatly. Robustness concerns a decision, a set of assumptions and specific costs. It demonstrates neither model calibration nor the actual quality of the choices.

    Reread the boundary and the limits of robustness

Compare with the solution

s concerns alarms among defective parts; q concerns defects among parts that triggered an alarm. These are different populations defined by conditioning.

With p = 1/10, the D and A path has probability 3/40 and the C and A path has probability 9/40. Thus P(A) = 3/10 and q = (3/40) ÷ (3/10) = 0.25. The reasoning left out the initial defect probability and alarms on conforming parts.

  • Reconstruct the two paths and their normalization, without substituting s for q.
  • Obtain the dimensionless result 0.25 and identify the omitted assumptions.

Exercise 3 · Defend an audit

Stability obtained by narrowing the assumptions

The stated audit covered p in [0.10 ; 0.50]. Someone eventually publishes only the cases p = 0.25 and p = 0.50, then writes “the decision holds under different priors”. Reconstruct the boundary in p and write a correct conclusion for each set. Finally, can they use the same calculation if the alarm reading was not obtained?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start from q ≥ t, multiply by its positive denominator, then isolate p.

Hint 2

A conclusion about a subset does not replace the conclusion about the stated set; a missing reading is not an observed alarm.

Compare with the solution

q ≥ t is equivalent to Lp ≥ t[Lp + 1 − p], then to p[L(1 − t) + t] ≥ t. The coefficient is positive. The boundary is therefore 1/2 / (3 × 1/2 + 1/2) = 0.25.

On [0.10 ; 0.50], the choices include values below and at or above the boundary: the decision varies. On [0.25 ; 0.50], it remains to hold, including equality. This establishes conditional stability on this subset, without justifying the removal of the low prior.

Without an alarm reading, q = P(D | A) no longer answers the observed situation. The missing reading must be reported. Keeping p explicitly assumes that the missing reading gives no information about the defect; otherwise, this link must be modelled. No choice of prior replaces this missing information.

Check your reasoning

  • Isolate 0.25 and handle equality according to the stated rule.
  • Name the set of assumptions in each conclusion.
  • Point out the unjustified removal of the low case and the lack of evidence of calibration.
  • Distinguish an observed alarm from a missing reading.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What should you ask about the origin of a prior?

Its source, its date, which parts it applies to, its justification for the current case and which information it already contains. An assumption without a source must still be stated as such.

Recall 2How can you reconstruct P(D | A) without reversing the conditions?

Weight s by p and f by 1 − p. Add the alarm paths, then divide the defect and alarm path by this sum: q = sp/[sp + f(1 − p)].

Recall 3What must stay fixed to isolate the effect of p in our audit?

The same alarm, s, f, the costs and the rule for equality. Vary p, recalculate q and then the losses, and keep the stated scenarios even if they contradict the desired conclusion.

Recall 4Why does a stable decision not prove that the model is valid?

It can stay on the same side of the threshold despite a large variation in q, and the model can be wrong in every tested case. Stability holds only for the stated assumptions and costs.

Recall 5What does a missing reading change in the reasoning?

A is no longer an observed alarm: the update conditioned on A does not apply. Keeping p requires treating the missing reading as uninformative; this must be justified, rather than inferred from the lack of a reading.

Reconstruct the reasoning
