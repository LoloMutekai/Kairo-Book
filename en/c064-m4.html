Level 2 · C064·M4

Update and Gaussian assumptions

Your goalUpdate an estimate and its variance, then state the assumptions under which this pair exactly describes the distribution after observation.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A temperature sensor has just displayed 24 °C. Just before this reading, the model predicted 20 °C in the room. Updating means moving the estimate towards this new evidence. But the sensor also has uncertainty: copying its display would discard what we already knew too quickly.

Each piece of information comes with uncertainty. The prediction variance is 5 °C²; the sensor noise variance is 15 °C². Variance is the mean of a squared deviation under the model. It describes dispersion, rather than the error actually made during this minute. Here, the model assigns more uncertainty to the sensor than to its prediction: the updated estimate will therefore stay closer to the prediction than to the reading.

The gain derived in the previous unit sets the fraction of the gap to cover. With these data, it is 1/4, or 0.25, with no unit. The estimate becomes 21 °C, and its variance becomes 3.75 °C². We must keep this new pair: updating only the temperature would leave outdated uncertainty for the next step.

All the values in this scene are fictional and chosen so that they can be checked with pencil and paper. No actual temperature is provided for assessing the final error. We will establish a result of the model, then identify what allows us to interpret it as an exact Gaussian distribution.

The rule to remember

Apply the gain to the gap, add this shift to the prediction, then recalculate the variance. The Gaussian interpretation requires a prediction distribution and measurement noise that are compatible, independent and correctly specified.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A new reading, a new pair

Return to the same room: the previous estimate is 20 °C, the variance 4 °C², the transition factor F = 1 with no unit, and the process noise variance Q = 1 °C² for this step. The new reading is z = 24 °C with noise variance R = 15 °C². We observe the temperature directly, with no change of scale or sensor offset.

  1. Receive the predicted pair

    x is the unknown temperature at the current instant; a hat denotes an estimate. The superscripts − and + mean “before” and “after the current reading”. They do not indicate the sign of the temperature. P⁻ and P⁺ are the corresponding error variances, in square degrees. We leave the time indices implicit to focus on this step.

    The prediction from the previous units gives x̂⁻ = 1 × 20 = 20 °C. For the variance, the squared factor is 1 and the propagated contribution is 4 °C²: P⁻ = 1 × 4 + 1 = 5 °C². The process noise represents new variability in the temperature change. It is assumed to have mean 0 °C and variance Q, and to be independent of the previous error; these assumptions justify adding Q.

    No reading has yet been incorporated into this pair. The other operations below concern the same minute: they add no new process noise.

  2. State the measurement model precisely

    The reading z and the noise v are in degrees Celsius. Their relationship is z = x + v: the sensor measures x directly. Before seeing z, and given the previous information, we assume that x has a normal distribution with mean x̂⁻ and variance P⁻. Here, the notation N(m, V) means a normal distribution with mean m, in degrees, and variance V, in square degrees; its second parameter is a variance.

    x∼N(x^−,P−);v∼N(0,R)

    We assume that v is independent of x and of the previous information, with mean 0 °C. P⁻ and R are strictly positive and treated as known. The normal distribution is a bell-shaped distribution on the real line; its mean and variance suffice to describe it. An unbiased reading and an adequate noise model remain assumptions, rather than properties proved by the calculation alone.

    For a complete filter, a linear evolution model, a Gaussian initial state and Gaussian noises independent of one another and across steps allow this form to be preserved before each reading. For this update alone, we need the prediction distribution stated here and the independent measurement noise.

  3. Recover the gap and its weight

    The innovation y is the reading minus the prediction, in degrees. S is its variance, in square degrees: the variances of the two independent errors add. K is the gain, with no unit, already justified in the previous unit.

    y=z−x^−;S=P−+R;K=P−S

    Thus y = 24 − 20 = 4 °C; S = 5 + 15 = 20 °C²; K = 5/20 = 1/4 = 0.25. The gap indicates which direction to move in. The gain sets the fraction of this gap to use; it is not the probability that the reading is true.

  4. Update the value, keeping the units

    x^+=x^−+Ky

    The shift is K × y = 0.25 × 4 = 1 °C. Add it to the starting point, giving x̂⁺ = 20 + 1 = 21 °C. The quantity K × z would not be a shift: it would omit subtracting the prediction.

    Expanding gives x̂⁺ = (1 − K)x̂⁻ + Kz. The prediction weight is therefore 3/4 and the reading weight 1/4. Their sum is 1. Check using this alternative expression: (3/4) × 20 + (1/4) × 24 = 21 °C. The result lies between the starting values and closer to the prediction.

  5. Update the variance too

    Let e⁻ = x − x̂⁻ and e⁺ = x − x̂⁺ be the errors before and after the update, in degrees. Substituting x + v for z in the update formula gives e⁺ = (1 − K)e⁻ − Kv. Before observation, e⁻ and v have 0 mean and are independent. The variance of this updated error is therefore the sum of the variances of the two terms:

    P+=(1−K)2P−+K2R

    Calculate without simplifying: the square of the first weight is 9/16, and that of the second is 1/16. Their contributions are (9/16) × 5 = 45/16 °C² and (1/16) × 15 = 15/16 °C². The sum is 15/4 °C², or 3.75 °C².

    With the gain K = P⁻/(P⁻ + R), this sum simplifies to P⁺ = (1 − K)P⁻. Check: 0.75 × 5 = 3.75 °C², the same result. The short form uses the specific Kalman gain; it is not valid for an arbitrary weight.

    This error calculation gives an average variance before the reading is known. Under the stated Gaussian assumptions, it is also the variance of the distribution after this particular reading. The next step explains this connection, which does not follow from the variances alone.

  6. Understand what the Gaussian assumption provides

    A density describes how probability is distributed over small temperature intervals. The density after observation is obtained by multiplying the prediction density by the density expressing how compatible the reading is with each possible temperature, then applying normalization so that the total probability is 1.

    For our normal distributions, the shape of the product is determined by a sum: the square of (t − x̂⁻) divided by P⁻, plus the square of (z − t) divided by R. The letter t is a candidate temperature, in degrees. Expanding and then completing the square rewrites this sum as a single squared deviation, plus a term independent of t that is absorbed into the normalization. The normalized product is still Gaussian. The coefficient of the square is the sum of the precisions. Here, “precision” means the inverse of a variance, in °C⁻², rather than observed accuracy.

    1P+=1P−+1R

    The prediction precision is 1/5 °C⁻², and the sensor precision is 1/15 °C⁻². Their sum is 4/15 °C⁻². Taking its inverse gives P⁺ = 15/4 °C² = 3.75 °C².

    x^+=P+(x^−P−+zR)

    For the centre, 20 × (1/5) = 4 °C⁻¹ and 24 × (1/15) = 8/5 °C⁻¹. The sum is 28/5 °C⁻¹. Dividing this sum by the total precision 4/15 °C⁻² gives 21 °C, recovering the update. The distribution after observation, called the posterior, is therefore N(21 °C, 3.75 °C²).

    Under this linear Gaussian model, x̂⁺ is the exact conditional mean and P⁺ the exact conditional variance. Without the Gaussian assumption, the same operations still give an affine update with minimum mean squared error if the means, variances and independence assumptions are correct. They no longer suffice to describe the entire distribution after observation; its mean may depend on the reading in a different way, and its variance may vary with the observed value.

  7. Check and retain the result

    Positive variances require 0 < K < 1. Our value 0.25 lies in this interval and places 21 °C between 20 and 24 °C. The variance 3.75 °C² is positive and smaller than both 5 and 15 °C²: the combination has added independent information under the model.

    The gain and the updated variance do not depend on the value z when P⁻ and R are fixed. A very different reading will shift the mean further without automatically changing this variance. If the sensor is biased or its noise depends on the prediction error, the formulas and their interpretation must be reconsidered.

    Keep (21 °C; 3.75 °C²) for the next prediction. The actual temperature remains unknown: a smaller calculated dispersion does not guarantee a smaller actual error during this minute. The reading is new only once; reusing it does not create independent information.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Updating means adding the gain multiplied by the displayed value.”

    The gain multiplies the innovation z − x̂⁻. This product is the shift to add to the prediction. First write the gap with its sign, then apply the weight; check that the updated estimate stays between the prediction and the reading.

    Revisit “Update the value”

  • “The variance gets the same weight as the temperature.”

    For an arbitrary weight, calculate the square of (1 − K) multiplied by P⁻ and the square of K multiplied by R, then add them. The reduction to (1 − K)P⁻ is an identity specific to the Kalman gain. Recalculating both contributions checks the short form and the units.

    Revisit the contributions to the variance

  • “A small variance proves that the temperature is correct.”

    The variance belongs to the model; counting the same information again can make it decrease artificially. Test what happens when the same reading is sent again: the numerical update changes even though no new information arrives. Also revisit the assumptions of 0 mean and independence.

Exercise 1 · Direct application

The room seems colder

Start again from x̂⁻ = 20 °C and P⁻ = 5 °C². The same sensor still has R = 15 °C², but its new reading is now 16 °C. Before calculating, state the expected direction of the update. Calculate the innovation, the updated estimate and the updated variance.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Only the displayed value changes. The gain depends on the variances, which have stayed the same.

Hint 2

Write the reading minus the prediction. A negative gap must produce a negative shift.

Compare with the solution

The estimate must decrease towards the reading. The gain remains 0.25. The innovation is 16 − 20 = -4 °C. The shift is 0.25 × (-4) = -1 °C, giving x̂⁺ = 20 + (-1) = 19 °C.

The variance remains (1 − 0.25) × 5 = 3.75 °C². Changing the reading changes the mean without changing the conditional variance in this Gaussian model with fixed parameters. 19 °C is indeed between 16 and 20 °C.

Check your reasoning

  • Predict a decrease and retain the sign of the innovation.
  • Obtain 19 °C and 3.75 °C² with their units.
  • Explain why changing z alone changes neither K nor P⁺.

Exercise 2 · Reconstruction by another method

A sensor with less uncertainty

In the same room, keep x̂⁻ = 20 °C, P⁻ = 5 °C² and z = 24 °C. This time, assume a known R = 5 °C², with the same Gaussian and independence assumptions. Reconstruct the updated pair from the precisions, then check using the gain. Is the midpoint justified here?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Each precision is the inverse of the corresponding variance. Invert their sum to recover the updated variance.

Hint 2

Equal precisions give equal weights to the two centres. Then check K = P⁻/(P⁻ + R).

Compare with the solution

Each precision is 1/5 °C⁻². The total precision is 2 × (1/5) °C⁻²; its inverse is P⁺ = 5/2 °C² = 2.50 °C². The formula for the centre divides the sum of the weighted centres by this total precision: the two normalized weights are equal, so x̂⁺ = 22 °C.

Check: S = 5 + 5 = 10 °C², K = 5/10 = 1/2. The shift is 0.50 × 4 = 2 °C; the estimate is 20 + 2 = 22 °C. Finally, P⁺ = 0.50 × 5 = 2.50 °C².

The midpoint is justified because the variances are equal, rather than because there is a prediction and a reading. This variance is smaller than the 3.75 °C² in the example because the noise assigned to this sensor is smaller. This remains a comparison of fictional models.

Check your reasoning

  • Add the precisions and invert the sum to recover a variance.
  • Recover the same pair using the precisions and using the gain.
  • Connect the midpoint to equal variances; retain positive uncertainty.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and repair

The same reading arrives again

Take exactly the pair from the example (21 °C; 3.75 °C²). With no time elapsed, the system sends the same record z = 24 °C again. This is not another reading. Someone nevertheless uses it as an independent reading with R = 15 °C². Calculate the pair they would report, then explain which pair should actually be kept.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

To reproduce the faulty calculation, take the previous updated variance as the new P⁻, without adding Q because time has not advanced.

Hint 2

Does sending the same record again add any information to what is already known? Distinguish the result of the formula from whether it is valid to apply it.

Compare with the solution

By wrongly treating the repeated record as independent, S becomes 15/4 + 15 = 75/4 °C² and K becomes 15/4/(75/4) = 1/5. The gap is 24 − 21 = 3 °C; the shift is 1/5 × 3 = 3/5 °C. The false new mean is 108/5 °C, or 21.60 °C.

The reported variance would be (1 − 1/5) × (15/4) = (4/5) × (15/4) = 3 °C². It is smaller than 3.75 °C² despite the absence of new information: the same evidence has been counted again.

Keep (21 °C; 3.75 °C²). Once the reading is known, knowing its identical duplicate does not change the conditional distribution. The noise is no longer independent of the information already used. The repair is to recognize the same record and prevent this second update; a decrease in variance alone does not validate the operation.

Check your reasoning

  • Reproduce the faulty result 21.60 °C and 3 °C² without accepting it as valid.
  • Name the missing assumption of new independent information.
  • Keep the original pair and justify the repair by the absence of additional information.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which quantity must the gain multiply, and what happens to the result?

It multiplies the innovation y = z − x̂⁻. Ky is a shift, to be added to the prediction to obtain x̂⁺ = x̂⁻ + Ky. In our model, y and Ky are in degrees, and K has no unit.

Recall 2How can you check the variance update without using its short form?

Write e⁺ = (1 − K)e⁻ − Kv. With independent errors of 0 mean, add the square of (1 − K) multiplied by P⁻ and the square of K multiplied by R. For the Kalman gain, this sum simplifies to (1 − K)P⁻.

Recall 3What makes the updated pair sufficient to describe the entire distribution after observation?

A Gaussian prediction and Gaussian measurement noise with 0 mean, independent of the prediction, with known variances and a direct linear measurement. Their normalized product is still Gaussian: its mean and variance describe the entire conditional distribution.

Recall 4How can you recover the updated variance from the precisions?

A precision is the inverse of a positive variance. Under our model, 1/P⁺ = 1/P⁻ + 1/R. Add the precisions in inverse square degrees, then invert the sum to recover a variance in square degrees.

Recall 5Why does a variance decrease after the same record is sent again fail to prove that the estimate is better?

The formula has treated the same evidence as additional independent information. Without new information, the conditional distribution stays the same. Keep the previous pair and prevent this second incorporation.
