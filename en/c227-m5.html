Level 4 · C227·M5

Ito-Tanaka

Your goalDecompose the excess above a threshold for a real-valued semimartingale: predictable integral, local time and jump correction, with an explicit convention at the threshold.

Before you start
c226-m5 : integration with jumps · c165-m5 : duality · c171-m5 : multidimensional case · c227-m1 : finite variation · c227-m2 : Doob-Meyer · c227-m3 : general quadratic variation · c227-m4 : general integral

✿01 See the idea

In an imaginary room, we track a temperature X and the excess above the threshold a = 20 °C. At 19 °C, this excess is 0 °C; at 21 °C, it is 1 °C. This is the positive part of X − a: keep the difference when it is positive and replace a negative difference with 0.

The graph of this excess has a corner. Below the threshold, the slope is 0; above it, the slope is 1. Tracking only these slopes misses a contribution when small continuous fluctuations concentrate at the threshold. The Ito-Tanaka formula accounts for this contribution through local time.

A jump directly from below to above the threshold raises another question: the slope taken before the jump does not predict the excess afterwards. We must add a jump remainder. This remainder and local time have distinct origins; local time weights visits near the threshold by continuous quadratic variation. Jumps make no direct contribution to it.

The temperature provides a calculation scenario, without a validated physical model. All numerical data are fictional. We study the positive part, with a single corner; we develop neither the full theory of convex functions nor a method for estimation from readings taken at intervals.

The rule to remember

At the corner, keep the slope known just before the change, add half the right local time and correct each jump separately.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Accounting for the temperature excess

Over the time interval from 0 to t, the fictional temperature starts at 19 °C and ends at 21 °C. The threshold remains a = 20 °C. The integral I = -1/4 °C and the right local time L = 3/2 °C are given. The only jump goes from 19.75 to 20.50 °C. These aggregate quantities are the inputs on which the accounting is conditional: the starting and ending temperatures alone do not allow us to calculate them.

  1. Set the framework and the slope at the threshold

    X is a real-valued semimartingale adapted to the information available over time, with right-continuous paths and left limits. The filtration, an increasing family of this information, is assumed complete and right-continuous. We write X = initial X + M + A, where M is a local martingale and A is a process of finite variation, both starting at 0. The local martingale becomes a martingale after suitable stopping; no finite global expectation of X is required here. The units of X, M, A and the fixed threshold a are degrees Celsius; s and t are times, in minutes.

    h(x) denotes the positive excess. The sign ⁺ on a parenthesis means positive part, never a right limit. The left derivative h′- is 0 when x ≤ a, including at the threshold, and 1 when x > a. It is dimensionless. X just before s, written Xs-, gives the slope to use; this slope is bounded and predictable, so it is integrable with respect to X. Predictable here means that the integrand is determined from the state just before the time under consideration.

    h(x)=(x−a)+=max(x−a,0)

  2. Define the chosen local time

    Mᶜ is the continuous local martingale part of M; C = [Mᶜ] is its quadratic variation, continuous and increasing, in °C². C is also the continuous part [X]ᶜ of the quadratic variation of X. The symbol 𝟙 is the indicator: 1 if the condition is true, 0 otherwise. We define the right local time at the threshold a by the following limit in probability, as the strictly positive width ε, in °C, tends to 0:

    Lta,+=limε↓01ε∫0t𝟙{a≤Xs−<a+ε}dCs

    The superscript + on L denotes this convention to the right of the threshold. L starts at 0, is continuous and increasing in time, and increases only on contact with the threshold. Its unit is °C²/°C = °C: it is not a duration in minutes. The convention matters: using symmetric local time without changing the slope at the threshold can change the formula for some semimartingales.

  3. Write Ito-Tanaka and understand the corrections

    Set It = ∫ from 0 to t of 𝟙{Xs- > a} dXs. This integral already includes the slope before each jump multiplied by that jump. At a time s, ΔXs = Xs − Xs- is the jump. Its remainder rs replaces this linear prediction with the actual change in h. Jt is the sum of these remainders over times 0 < s ≤ t. Each remainder is nonnegative because h is convex: its graph stays above every supporting line.

    rs=h(Xs)−h(Xs−)−𝟙{Xs−>a}ΔXs
    h(Xt)=h(X0)+It+12Lta,++Jt,Jt=∑0<s≤trs

    The equality holds almost surely, simultaneously over time for the usual versions. The sum of the remainders is finite on every bounded time interval. All its terms, like I, L and h, are in °C. The theorem applies to a semimartingale, beyond Brownian motion alone; independence of increments is not required.

    Why does local time appear? We can approximate the corner by smooth convex functions whose slope rises from 0 to 1 in a band of width ε to the right of a. In the smooth Ito formula, the second derivative term is weighted by 1/2. The curvature concentrates at the threshold; integrated against dC, it gives L in the limit, while the jump remainder keeps its form. The existence of local time and the passages to the limit are accepted here as the theoretical content of the theorem, without claiming to prove them through our numerical accounting.

  4. Calculate the jump remainder

    Before the jump, the difference is 19.75 − 20 = -0.25 °C. It is negative: h before = 0 °C and the slope before = 0. After the jump, the difference is 20.50 − 20 = 0.50 °C: this is the excess afterwards.

    The jump itself is 20.50 − 19.75 = 0.75 °C. The part already included in I is 0 × 0.75 = 0 °C. The remainder is therefore J = 0.50 − 0 − 0 = 0.50 °C. The excess does not increase by the full size of the jump: part of the jump goes towards reaching the threshold.

  5. Assemble the exact accounting

    At the start, h is 0 °C. The given local time is L = 3/2 °C; its coefficient gives L/2 = 3/4 °C. The accounting starts with 0 + (-1/4) = -1/4 °C, then adds 3/4 °C: 1/2 °C. Finally the jump adds 1/2 °C, giving 1 °C.

    h(Xt)=0−14+12×32+12=1 °C

    A negative integral I is allowed: the weight is positive, but changes in X above the threshold can be negative. Only the final excess must remain nonnegative. We do recover the final temperature a + h = 21 °C, since the stated ending temperature is above the threshold.

  6. Check using the endpoints and the absolute value

    Direct check: h at the end is max(21 − 20, 0) = 1 °C. The residual, the accounting result minus the direct value, is 0 °C. Omitting J would give only 0.50 °C; the check would detect the difference.

    Another check uses y⁺ = (y + |y|)/2, where y = X − a, in °C. With the left slope, the slope of |y| is −1 for y ≤ 0 and 1 for y > 0. Its integral is therefore 2I − (final X − initial X) = -1/2 − 2 = -5/2 °C. Its jump remainder is |1/2| − |-1/4| + 3/4 = 1 °C. This calculation checks the geometry of the jump separately.

    The accounting for the absolute value gives -5/2 + 3/2 + 1 = 0 °C, matching |1| − |-1|. Combining this accounting with the change final X minus initial X, then dividing by 2, recovers the excess of 1 °C. These checks verify the conditional algebra; they construct neither a path nor an actual measurement of local time.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The second derivative is 0 away from the threshold, so there is no correction.”

    The function is not smooth at the threshold. Smoothing concentrates the curvature in a band; integrating it against continuous quadratic variation leaves half the right local time. Return to the convention and the assumptions before removing this term.

    Revisit the definition of L and the smoothing of the corner

  • “Local time measures the minutes spent exactly at the threshold.”

    The definition weights a band by dC, in degrees squared, then divides by its width, in degrees. The result is in degrees. A quotient obtained for a fixed width does not establish the limit as that width tends to 0.

    Test the units and the limit in the last exercise

  • “Local time is also enough to correct the jump.”

    Local time comes from continuous quadratic variation. For the jump in our scenario, the slope before the jump is 0 and predicts 0 °C, whereas the excess increases by 0.50 °C. Calculate this remainder separately; do not confuse it with L.

Compare with the solution

Exercise 2 · Reconstructing the accounting

Recover the missing local time

The fictional notebook retains the start, the end, I = -1/4 °C and J = 1/2 °C, but has lost L. Recover L using the convention on this page. A calculation that omits J states L = 2 × 5/4 °C: explain its error before calculating the correct result.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First isolate L/2: difference in excesses minus I minus J.

Hint 2

The initial excess is 0 °C. Subtract the contributions already accounted for before multiplying by 2.

Compare with the solution

The difference in excesses is 1 − 0 = 1 °C. Subtracting I gives 1 − (-1/4) = 5/4 °C. Then subtracting J gives 5/4 − 1/2 = 3/4 °C: this is L/2.

Thus L = 2 × 3/4 = 3/2 °C. The incorrect calculation attributed the excess produced by the jump to continuous contact. Check: L/2 = 3/4 °C and the full accounting again gives 1 °C.

  • Subtract I with its negative sign.
  • Subtract the jump remainder before isolating L.
  • Recover the positive local time given in the example and check the accounting.

Exercise 3 · Critiquing a numerical proof

Is a single band enough?

For the same fictional signal, at width ε = 0.125 °C, we are given a weighted occupation Qε = ∫𝟙{a ≤ X just before < a + ε} dC = 3/16 °C². Calculate Qε/ε. Can we claim to have measured L exactly, or replace dC with ds? Finish by explaining what the decomposition would become for a continuous path of finite variation.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Dividing by the width gives a temperature. L is defined by a limit, not by a single quotient.

Hint 2

For a continuous path of finite variation, continuous quadratic variation is 0 and there are no jumps.

Compare with the solution

The exact quotient is (3/16)/(1/8) = 3/2 °C, or 1.50 °C. Here it coincides with the L given in the accounting. However, a single width proves no convergence; we have neither an error bound nor a reconstruction of the continuous signal.

Replacing dC with ds changes the quantity: we would integrate minutes, then divide by degrees. We would no longer obtain our local time unless we introduced a justified relationship between C and time and retained its units.

In the continuous variant with finite variation, C is 0, so L = 0, and J = 0 because there are no jumps. The formula reduces to the chain rule for the positive part, with the integral against finite variation. This variant does not arbitrarily retain the integral and local time given for our original accounting.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the exact quotient and its unit.
  • Distinguish a numerical coincidence at a fixed width from an established limit.
  • Explain why the continuous case with finite variation removes the corrections without changing the theorem.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why does the smooth Ito formula not apply directly to the excess?

h(x) = (x − a)⁺ has a corner at the threshold. The concentrated curvature there produces half the right local time, with the left derivative 0 at the threshold.

Recall 2Which convention links the slope at the threshold and L on this page?

The slope is 0 for x ≤ a and 1 for x > a. L is the right local time: the limit of occupation weighted by continuous quadratic variation in [a, a + ε), divided by ε. Its coefficient is 1/2.

Recall 3What does the jump remainder correct, and why is it nonnegative?

It equals h after minus h before minus the slope before multiplied by ΔX. Convexity places h above its supporting line. The remainder corrects the instantaneous change that this line does not account for.

Recall 4What unit does the local time of our temperature have?

Degrees Celsius: occupation weighted by dC is in °C² and the width ε is in °C. The quotient is not a duration; a single width does not establish its limit.

Recall 5Must X be Brownian motion, and what remains in the continuous case with finite variation?

A real-valued semimartingale is enough; the indicator constructed from its left limit is bounded and predictable. In the continuous case with finite variation, continuous quadratic variation and jumps are 0: only the chain rule remains.
