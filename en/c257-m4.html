Level 4 · C257·M4

Fourier estimator

Your goalCalculate a Fourier estimator of integrated covariance from asynchronous observations, then check its normalization and scope.

Before you start
c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c227-m5 : Ito-Tanaka · c179-m5 : ARCH-GARCH · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c234-m5 : existence conditions · c073-m5 : interaction and repeated measures · c257-m1 : microstructure noise · c257-m2 : asynchrony · c257-m3 : integrated variation

✿01 See the idea

Two fictional securities, A and B, are observed over 4 minutes. Between readings, A changes by 2 and then 1 reading units; B changes by 1 and then 2. Their intermediate readings occur at 2 minutes for A and 1 minute for B. Multiplying their first changes together would treat different intervals as simultaneous observations.

Another approach asks each series how its changes are distributed over time: multiply them by a cosine, then by a sine, evaluated at their own times. These sums are its Fourier coefficients. Two series can then be compared at the same frequency without inventing a missing reading.

Here a frequency describes a number of cycles over the entire horizon. The 0 mode ignores the times; the other modes vary the weights over time. The estimator combines products of corresponding coefficients. The second diagram introduces another interpretation of the same calculation: each pair of changes receives a weight depending on the difference between their times.

All values are fictional. A reading unit u equals 1/100 of a logarithm of price: this is a teaching scale, not a currency. The target is the covariance accumulated over the horizon. Integrated variance is the case where a series is compared with itself; a covariance between A and B can have either sign.

The rule to remember

Keep each series at its own observation times; calculate the same modes; combine their products with the stated normalization; distinguish integrated covariance from its average rate and from instantaneous covariance.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A complete calculation without aligning the clocks

A has observation times (0, 2, 4) min and increments (2, 1) u. B has observation times (0, 1, 4) min and increments (1, 2) u. Set the cutoff to N = 1 to learn the mechanism; this choice is not claimed to be optimal.

  1. Name the target, data and assumptions

    X denotes A or B. P_X(t) is its strictly positive price; P_ref is a fixed positive reference price, expressed in the same currency. We write p_X(t) = 100 × ln(P_X(t)/P_ref). The logarithm therefore acts on a dimensionless ratio. p_X is expressed in our unit u. A starting level no longer matters after taking differences.

    t_j^X is the time of the reading with index j, in minutes; n_X is the number of intervals for X. The times run from 0 to T and are strictly increasing. Here T = 4 min and each series has 2 intervals. The increment r_j^X, in u, is the difference between the next reading and the current reading. It is assigned to t_j^X, the start of the interval:

    rjX=pX(tj+1X)−pX(tjX)
    IAB=∫0TΣAB(t)dt

    I_AB is the target integrated covariance, in u². Σ_AB(t) is the instantaneous covariance rate, in u²/min; dt is the duration element in minutes. This is not the covariance of two lists of levels. In the latent model, the log prices are continuous Ito diffusions, with drifts and volatilities bounded over the horizon and continuous volatilities; their quadratic covariation gives this integral.

    The teaching calculation adds neither quote errors nor jumps. With microstructure noise, the formula can still be calculated, but its statistical quality must be studied separately. The observation times are given and no arrival distribution is inferred. Such a short sample cannot demonstrate convergence of the estimator or reveal I_AB.

  2. Convert times into phases and define the sums

    N is the cutoff, a nonnegative integer. k is an integer frequency index. θ_j^X = 2π × t_j^X/T is the dimensionless phase in radians; π is the circle constant. Do not confuse mode k with a frequency in hertz: k counts cycles over the horizon. C_X(k) and S_X(k) denote the cosine and sine sums, in u.

    CX(k)=∑j=0nX−1rjXcos(kθjX)
    SX(k)=∑j=0nX−1rjXsin(kθjX)

    We use raw sums of increments: no inverse factor of T or 2π is included in C and S. This fixes the normalization for the following calculation. The scaled start times of A are 0 and 1/2; those of B are 0 and 1/4. At mode 1, the phases are therefore (0, π) for A and (0, π/2) for B.

    At phases 0, π/2, π, the pairs (cosine; sine) are respectively (1; 0), (0; 1), (-1; 0). These exact values avoid any trigonometric rounding in this example.

  3. Calculate the coefficients by hand

    At the 0 mode, all cosines equal 1 and all sines equal 0. Thus C_A(0) = 2 + 1 = 3 u; C_B(0) = 1 + 2 = 3 u. The sine sums are 0. The 0 mode retains only the total change of each series.

    For A at mode 1: C_A(1) = 2 × 1 + 1 × (-1) = 1 u; S_A(1) = 2 × 0 + 1 × 0 = 0 u. The later change is subtracted in the cosine component.

    For B at the same mode: C_B(1) = 1 × 1 + 2 × 0 = 1 u; S_B(1) = 1 × 0 + 2 × 1 = 2 u. Its later change appears here in the sine component. No value of B at the intermediate observation time of A has been fabricated.

  4. Combine the modes with the correct normalization

    I^AB,N=CA(0)CB(0)+2∑k=1N[CA(k)CB(k)+SA(k)SB(k)]2N+1

    The hat indicates an estimate. The denominator counts the modes from −N to N. For real data, C_X(−k) = C_X(k) and S_X(−k) = −S_X(k). In the product between a frequency of one series and the opposite frequency of the other, the imaginary terms of opposite modes cancel: their common real contribution is C_A(k)C_B(k) + S_A(k)S_B(k). The factor 2 combines this pair; the 0 mode is counted only once.

    Here the 0-mode product is 3 × 3 = 9 u². The real product of the positive mode is 1 × 1 + 0 × 2 = 1 + 0 = 1 u². The pair of opposite modes contributes 2 × 1 = 2 u².

    I^AB=9+23=11/3 u²

    The result is approximately 3.6667 u². Sines and cosines are dimensionless: each product correctly retains the unit u². No additional factor T is required for this convention of raw sums and this integrated target.

  5. Check using pairs of increments

    Expanding the products of sums yields cos(a)cos(b) + sin(a)sin(b) = cos(a − b), where a and b are two angles. Each pair of increments therefore receives a weight D_N(δ), the normalized Dirichlet kernel; δ = (t_j^A − t_l^B)/T is a dimensionless scaled time difference. l is the interval index for B.

    DN(δ)=1+2∑k=1Ncos(2πkδ)2N+1

    With N = 1, the weights are: D_N(0) = 1; D_N(±1/4) = 1/3; D_N(1/2) = -1/3. This reproduces the second diagram exactly. The negative weight must be retained.

    For start times (0; 0) min: 2 × 1 × 1 = 2 u². For (0; 1) min: 2 × 2 × 1/3 = 4/3 u².

    For (2; 0) min: 1 × 1 × (-1/3) = -1/3 u². For (2; 1) min: 1 × 2 × 1/3 = 2/3 u².

    The sum is 2 + 4/3 + (-1/3) + 2/3 = 11/3 u². Products of coefficients and products of increments give the same exact rational number: this is an algebraic check, not a comparison with the unknown true covariance.

  6. Interpret the estimate without treating it as the truth

    Since 1 u corresponds to 1/100 of a logarithm, the squared scale factor is 10000. The covariance on the scale of the unmultiplied logarithms is 11/3/10000 = 11/30000 logarithm², or approximately 0.000367 logarithm². Keep the exact fraction; the decimal representation is rounded.

    Dividing by T would produce a different quantity: the estimated average rate over the horizon, 11/3/4 = 11/12 u²/min, approximately 0.9167 u²/min. This average rate does not reconstruct Σ_AB(t) at each instant. No square root is taken: a cross-covariance is not a standard deviation.

    The absence of prior synchronization is a property of the calculation. It removes neither finite-sample biases nor the effects of noise. Establishing convergence would require a sequence of increasingly dense observations, controlled growth of N and appropriate probabilistic assumptions; this tiny example does not provide that proof.

    Mathematical verification reference: the monograph by Mancino, Recchioni and Sanfelici cited in the architecture, the chapter on integrated covariance, and the formulas for the estimator and kernel. The present expression uses raw cosine and sine coefficients; the source also uses complex coefficients divided by 2π, which leads to different normalization factors before simplification.

Explain it in your own words

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A price for B must be invented at every observation time of A before starting.”

    Calculate the sums for B at its own observation times and for A at its own times. Their common reference is frequency k over the same horizon. Interpolation would change the increments and therefore the quantity calculated.

    Review the two clocks and the intervals

  • “I divide the sum of products by T: it is always an average.”

    The normalization of the estimator comes from the number of modes, 2N + 1, with the raw sums defined here. Dividing the result by T afterwards gives an average rate, with a different unit. Check the convention before adding a factor.

    Review the combination of modes and the unit check

  • “Fourier uses all the data, so noise and asynchrony are resolved.”

    The observation times are preserved, but the choice of modes and the assumptions remain decisive. The check using pairs proves a computational identity; it measures neither bias, statistical error nor improvement in a real case.

Recover the missing component

Hint 1

Divide the start times by T; the final time closes the last interval but carries no new increment.

Hint 2

At a quarter cycle, the cosine is 0 and the sine equals 1.

Compare with the solution

The start times are 0 and 1 min, giving phases 0 and π/2. C_B(1) = 1 × 1 + 2 × 0 = 1 u. S_B(1) = 1 × 0 + 2 × 1 = 2 u.

The second change of B is carried by the sine. Removing it would distort its coefficients. Only in this case, S_A(1) = 0, so the sine cross product is 0 u²; this particular cancellation does not justify a general rule that removes sines.

Check your reasoning

  • I use interval starts rather than end times.
  • I give the coefficients 1 u and 2 u with their phases.
  • I distinguish a 0 term in this particular product from information that does not exist.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

The same result after shifting observation times?

In the same setting, keep only N = 0. Calculate the estimator. Then imagine that the intermediate observation time of B changes while its increments and endpoints stay fixed. Does the result at this single mode change? Someone concludes that asynchrony therefore never causes a problem: respond to this claim.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A sum with no positive mode retains only the product of total changes.

Hint 2

Identify the information lost by the 0-mode coefficients before drawing a conclusion about the real phenomenon.

Compare with the solution

For N = 0, the denominator is 1 and the sum over positive modes is empty. The estimator equals 3 × 3 = 9 u². It no longer depends on intermediate observation times: moving the reading of B without changing the increments leaves this number unchanged.

This invariance comes from the loss of timing information at the 0 mode. It proves neither that asynchrony has no effect on a useful estimate nor that the error is smaller. Comparing 9 u² with 11/3 u² shows dependence on the cutoff; without the true covariance or a protocol for measuring error, we cannot tell which is better. The next unit will study the choice of frequencies and mean squared error.

Check your reasoning

  • I recover 9 u² and its lack of dependence on observation times.
  • I explain the lost information instead of announcing that a problem has been solved.
  • I refuse to rank cutoffs solely by the size of the result.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can A and B be compared without a common observation grid?

For each series, calculate the cosine and sine sums at the starts of its own intervals, then compare the coefficients at the same frequencies over the same horizon. This calculation requires no interpolation.

Recall 2Where do the denominator and the doubling of positive modes come from?

There are 2N + 1 modes from −N to N. Opposite modes give the same real contribution to the cross product: double the positive ones while counting the 0 mode only once. This formula assumes the raw sums defined on the page.

Recall 3What would an additional division by T change?

It would give the average rate over the horizon, in u²/min, instead of the integrated covariance in u². This average rate is not the instantaneous covariance function.

Recall 4Why expand the products into pairs of increments?

The normalized Dirichlet kernel weights each pair according to its time difference. Recovering the same rational number checks the signs, phases and normalization; it does not prove the absence of bias or noise.

Recall 5Why does 0-mode stability under shifts in observation times fail to prove good estimation?

The 0 mode sees only total changes. Its invariance reflects lost timing information. Assessing a cutoff requires error analysis, not just a stable or small result.
