Level 1 · C055·M4

Standard error

Your goalCalculate the estimated standard error of a mean and explain what it measures, the assumptions it requires and how sample size affects it.

Before you start
c035-m5 : finite covariance and conditioning · c024-m5 : measurement quality · c055-m1 : sampling distribution · c055-m2 : law of large numbers · c055-m3 : introduction to the central limit theorem

✿01 See the idea

At a workshop reception desk, you time the delay between a ticket being handed in and a member of staff starting to handle it. The times in a fictional batch are 16 s, 16 s, 16 s and 28 s. Their mean is 19 s. If you repeated this with new tickets, the mean would change: it depends on the tickets sampled.

We need to distinguish the spread of individual times from the spread of the means of batches of the same size. The standard deviation of the times describes the first. The standard error of the mean describes the second: it is the standard deviation of its sampling distribution, the distribution of means that repeated sampling under the same procedure would produce.

The word “error” here means neither a timing mistake nor an observed distance from a known true value. The mean of all possible times remains unknown. We will estimate the standard error from the batch, then ask what would change with more tickets that are actually new.

The times and all the variations are fictional. We assume that tickets are sampled independently from the same stable population: knowing one time gives no information about another, and the sampling rule does not change between batches. A congested queue where waiting times affect one another can violate this assumption. The diagrams represent a batch and a procedure for repeated sampling, rather than a measurement campaign that has taken place.

The rule to remember

For a mean of independent times with the same distribution, divide the individual standard deviation by the square root of the sample size. State whether the standard deviation is known or estimated.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What uncertainty accompanies the mean ticket waiting time?

We have 4 times: 16 s, 16 s, 16 s and 28 s. Let us calculate their mean, the corrected sample standard deviation of the times and then the estimated standard error of the mean. We keep exact values and units at every step.

  1. Name the quantity whose spread we are studying

    n is the number of tickets and has no unit; i is their index. Before sampling, Xᵢ represents a random time, in seconds. After observation, xᵢ is the time actually recorded. X̄, with a bar, is the random mean of the batch; x̄ is its observed value. μ, pronounced “mu”, is the population mean; σ, “sigma”, is its standard deviation. Both are in seconds and are unknown here.

    We assume the Xᵢ are independent and have the same distribution, with finite variance equal to the square of σ, in square seconds. Var denotes variance: the expected squared deviation from the mean. ES(X̄) is the standard deviation of the random mean, and therefore the square root of Var(X̄). It concerns the means of batches with a fixed sample size n, rather than the individual times.

  2. Understand the square root in the divisor

    Under independence, the variances of the times add up: the variance of their sum is n times the individual variance. Moving from the sum to the mean divides the times by n, and therefore their variance by the square of n. This leaves the individual variance divided by n. Taking the square root returns us to seconds and gives σ/√n.

    Var(X¯)=nσ2n2=σ2n
    ES(X¯)=σn

    This identity does not require a normal distribution. If the times depend on one another, adding only the variances is no longer enough. The formula must then be reconsidered.

  3. Calculate the observed mean

    The mean gives each ticket the same weight. The 3 short times total 3 × 16 = 48 s. Adding the long time gives 76 s. Dividing this total by 4 gives the mean time per ticket.

    x¯=48+284=764=19 s

    Check: 19 s does lie between 16 s and 28 s. We have calculated x̄; we have not revealed μ.

  4. Estimate the individual spread

    Since σ is unknown, we replace it with s, the corrected sample standard deviation of the times in the batch, in seconds. Take care: in formulas, the letter s denotes this standard deviation; after a number, the unit s means second. Its square is the corrected sample variance. To calculate it, we add the squared deviations from x̄ and divide by n − 1. The deviations from a mean calculated from the batch sum to 0; this correction accounts for the constraint imposed by fitting the mean to the same observations.

    Each short time differs from the mean by 16 − 19 = -3 s; its square is 9 square seconds. The long time differs from it by 28 − 19 = 9 s; its square is 81 square seconds. The sum of squares is therefore 3 × 9 + 81 = 27 + 81 = 108 square seconds.

    s2=1084−1=1083=36 secondes carrées

    Thus s = √36 = 6 s, because 6 × 6 = 36 square seconds and the standard deviation is positive. The divisor n − 1 is used here to estimate the individual spread; it is not the sample size to put under the square root in the standard error.

  5. Move from the standard deviation to the estimated standard error

    We now use s in place of σ. The hat over ES indicates that the standard error is estimated from the batch; its value would also change with another batch. There are still n = 4 independent tickets. Since 2 × 2 = 4, the square root of n is 2.

    ES^(X¯)=sn=64=62=3 s

    The result to report is therefore: observed mean 19 s; estimated standard error 3 s, assuming independent sampling from the same distribution. The 6 s describes the times; the 3 s estimates the spread of their mean across batches.

  6. Check the result and limit its interpretation

    Another calculation route: first divide the corrected sample variance of the times by the sample size to estimate the variance of the mean: 36/4 = 9 square seconds. Its square root is indeed 3 s, since 3 × 3 = 9 square seconds. The units agree: a variance in square seconds becomes a standard deviation in seconds again.

    Dividing s directly by n would give 1.5 s, which fails this check. With the individual spread held fixed, multiplying the sample size by 4 divides the standard error by 2: the square root explains why the number of tickets must grow faster than the desired precision.

    Finally, 3 s is neither a guaranteed bound on the error x̄ − μ, nor a confidence interval, nor evidence of an absence of bias. A stopwatch with the same offset at every reading can leave little spread around an incorrect mean. The next unit will address the confidence interval; here we stop at the scale of fluctuation of the mean.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, draw what varies within a batch and then what would vary across new batches. Explain the step from the individual variance to the variance of the mean, and why the standard error divisor contains n rather than n − 1.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The standard deviation of the tickets and the standard error describe the same spread.”

    Name the quantity: s describes the individual times; ES describes the means of batches of a fixed size. In our batch, we obtain 6 s for the corrected sample standard deviation of the times and 3 s for the estimated standard error of the mean. Revisit the diagrams and name what is being repeated.

    Distinguish the times from the means

  • “A mean divides by the sample size, so its standard error does too.”

    Dividing by n divides the variance by the square of n. After adding the variances of independent times, we are left with the individual variance divided by n; its square root gives σ/√n. Calculating via 9 square seconds lets us identify the incorrect answer 1.5 s.

    Recover the square root and check using the variance

  • “More rows in my table are enough to make the mean more precise.”

    The sample size in the formula counts independent observations. Copying the same tickets does not change their random mean. Even new tickets do not correct a systematic stopwatch offset. The copying exercise asks you to separate a formal calculation from the conditions for using it.

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Waiting times with more spread

For another fictional batch at the same reception desk, the given corrected sample standard deviation is s = 8 s and the sample size remains n = 4 independent tickets with the same distribution. Predict the direction of the change, then calculate the estimated standard error. What spread does the result describe?

Hint 1

With sample size fixed, increasing s increases the estimated standard error.

Hint 2

The square root of 4 is 2. It divides the individual standard deviation.

Compare with the solution

It increases, because only the given individual standard deviation is larger. The calculation is 8/√4 = 8/2 = 4 s, greater than the 3 s in the example.

This 4 s estimates the spread of means across new batches of the same size. It does not directly describe the spread of the tickets or give the actual error relative to μ.

Exercise 2 · Inverse reasoning

Divide the standard error by 2

To plan a new set of independent ticket observations, we keep a standard deviation of s = 6 s as a calculation assumption. What sample size would make s/√n equal to 1.5 s? Compare this sample size with the original 4 tickets. This planning calculation does not promise that the standard deviation observed in the future will remain the same.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Find the divisor that turns 6 s into 1.5 s.

Hint 2

This divisor is √n. You still need to square it to obtain n.

Compare with the solution

We need √n = 6/(3/2) = 4. Therefore n = 4 × 4 = 16 tickets. Check: 6/√16 = 6/4 = 1.5 s.

The sample size is multiplied by 16/4 = 4, while the standard error is divided by 3/(3/2) = 2. These are new samples under the stated assumptions. The future value of s and the stability of the population will need to be examined when the observations are collected.

Check your reasoning

  • Find √n before n.
  • Obtain 16 tickets, then check by substitution.
  • Distinguish a calculation with fixed spread from a guarantee about future observations.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

The table has grown; the observations have not

Someone copies the entire original batch 4 times without timing any new tickets. They count 16 rows and report a more precise mean. Work out the mean of the copied table, then explain whether this copying can reduce the standard error. What change in procedure would be needed to use a larger sample size?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The total time and the number of rows are multiplied by the same factor.

Hint 2

A copy of a time is determined by the original. It is not a new independent sample.

Compare with the solution

The copied total is 4 × 76 = 304 s. The mean is 304/16 = 19 s: it remains the mean of the original 4 observations.

For every possible batch, the copied mean is exactly equal to the original mean. Its sampling distribution is therefore unchanged, as is its standard error. The original estimate remains 3 s; the number of copied rows justifies no reduction. Recalculating the spread using these copies does not restore independence.

We would need to collect new tickets under the same sampling plan, check their independence and the stability of the population, then recalculate s from the observations obtained. Even then, a reduced standard error would not establish an absence of stopwatch bias.

Check your reasoning

  • Check that the new total and the new sample size still give 19 s.
  • Explain the failure of independence rather than mechanically replacing n.
  • Propose new observations and a check of the procedure.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1The standard error is the standard deviation of what?

The sampling distribution of the estimator. For the mean, it describes the spread of means obtained by repeating the same sampling procedure at a fixed sample size, rather than the spread of individual observations.

Recall 2How do you calculate and estimate the standard error of a mean?

For independent observations with the same distribution and finite variance: ES(X̄) = σ/√n, where σ is the individual standard deviation and n is the sample size. If σ is unknown, estimate it with s and calculate s/√n. The standard error has the same unit as the observations.

Recall 3With the individual spread fixed, why must you multiply the sample size by 4 to divide the standard error by 2?

Because the sample size appears under a square root. In the example, moving from 4 to 16 independent observations changes the divisor from 2 to 4, and the estimated standard error from 3 s to 1.5 s, if s remains 6 s.

Recall 4What can an estimated standard error of 3 s not tell us?

It does not give the actual distance from the observed mean to μ, guarantee an error smaller than 3 s, constitute a confidence interval or detect systematic bias. It also depends on the sampling plan and its assumptions.

Recall 5Why does copying the same batch not reduce the standard error of its mean?

The mean of the copied table is identical to the original mean for every possible batch. Its distribution and standard error therefore remain identical. The copied rows are dependent; their number is not a new independent sample size.
