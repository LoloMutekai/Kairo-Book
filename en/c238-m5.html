Level 4 · C238·M5

Limits of standard Ito calculus

Your goalUse squared increments to test why replacing Brownian motion with fractional Brownian motion does not automatically preserve the Ito formula.

Before you start
c235-m5 : divergence · c232-m5 : short-time limits · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c238-m1 : fractional covariance · c238-m2 : Hurst index · c238-m3 : simulation · c238-m4 : memory and roughness

✿01 See the idea

A fictional test bench produces fluctuations from a vibration sensor over a fixed time window. We want to track the square of its centered signal. With more frequent readings, each change becomes smaller; yet the sum of the squares of these changes can grow. This is precisely the total we need to examine before copying a Brownian formula.

Time is divided by the duration of the window, and amplitude by a reference amplitude. Time and signal are therefore dimensionless in all the calculations that follow. We model this signal as standard fractional Brownian motion, with Hurst index H = 1/4. This model and these settings are fictional; no vibration readings are used.

Standard Ito calculus retains a term that the ordinary differentiation rule would miss: the accumulated small squares. For standard Brownian motion, their sum tends to the elapsed time. For our fractional signal, we need to check this again; continuous paths and the Gaussian distribution of each increment are not enough.

The diagrams compare averages predicted by the model over repeated windows. They do not represent a particular path. We will distinguish calculating these averages from proving the behavior of the random sum.

The rule to remember

Before using the Ito correction, check the noise model and the limit of the sum of squares. A growing expectation does not, by itself, prove divergence in probability.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The squared signal over a single window

We observe the same sensor model with n = 4, 16 and then 64 equal intervals, over the normalized duration 1. We calculate the expectations of the totals, then justify why the Brownian rule fails as the step size tends to 0.

  1. Specify the model and symbols

    B denotes our fractional signal; we omit its superscript H to keep the formulas short. B_t is its value at normalized time t. It is Gaussian, centered, continuous and starts at 0. Its index H lies strictly between 0 and 1. E denotes expectation under this model, and Var denotes variance. Its increments are stationary: their distribution depends only on the time between readings.

    E[Bt]=0;E[(Bt−Bs)2]=|t−s|2H

    Here s and t are times within the window; n is a positive integer counting the intervals. Time t_i equals i/n; i runs through the intervals from 1 to n. The step size Δt is 1/n, and the increment ΔB_i is B at the end of the step minus B at its beginning. All these quantities are dimensionless after normalization. The increments are not assumed to be independent.

  2. Identify exactly what the small squares correct

    Q_n denotes the sum of squared increments. S_n denotes the sum of the products of the value at the beginning of each step and the increment over that same step: it is the candidate left-endpoint sum for integrating B with respect to B. The symbol Σ adds the terms from the indicated starting index up to n.

    Qn=∑i=1n(ΔBi)2;Sn=∑i=1nBti−1ΔBi

    The algebraic identity (x + d) squared − x squared = 2xd + d squared applies to each step; x is the value at its beginning and d is its increment. When we add the terms, the intermediate squared values cancel. This leaves the following identity, exact for every grid and every path:

    B12−B02=2Sn+Qn

    For H = 1/2, B is standard Brownian motion. As the grid is refined, Q_n tends in probability to 1 and S_n to the Ito integral of B with respect to B. The Ito formula for the square therefore retains an elapsed-time term 1. The symbol ∫ denotes this integration limit, not a finite sum.

    B12=2∫01BtdBt+1

    This last equality concerns only the Brownian comparison H = 1/2. The integral bounds cover the window; dB_t indicates that the signal B itself serves as the integrator. We will test what prevents us from carrying this equality over unchanged to H = 1/4.

  3. Calculate the expectation without assuming independence

    Each increment is centered. The expectation of its square is therefore its variance, equal to (1/n) to the power 2H. The expectation of a sum is the sum of the expectations, even for dependent increments. There are n terms:

    E[Qn]=n(1n)2H=n1−2H

    For H = 1/4, the exponent 1 − 2 × 1/4 equals 1/2. The expectation of Q_n is therefore the positive square root of n. This calculation alone does not yet establish that Q_n diverges: rare, very large values can make an expectation grow.

  4. Work through the calculation on the coarse grid

    With n = 4, each step has duration 1/4. The expected square of an increment is the square root of 1/4, namely 1/2, since 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. We multiply by the number of steps to add up the expected squares.

    E[Q4]=4×1/2=2

    Check by another method: the square root of n is 2, since 2 × 2 = 4. This gives the same result. For a realization of the sensor signal, Q_n remains random: we are not claiming that every window will give this total.

  5. Refine the grid and check the direction of change

    With n = 16, Δt = 1/16. The expected square is 1/4, since its square is 1/16. The expected total is 16 × 1/4 = 4. With n = 64, the same reasoning gives 64 × 1/8 = 8; the step size is 1/64.

    The square roots of 16 and 64 are 4 and 8: squaring them does recover the numbers of steps. Another check: the grid has 4 times as many steps, but each expected square is divided by 2; their total is therefore multiplied by 2.

    The terminal fluctuation does not grow as the grid is refined: the expectation of the square of B at the end is always 1. Taking expectations in the exact identity gives E[S_n] = (1 − E[Q_n])/2. The successive grids give -1/2, -3/2 and -7/2. The left-endpoint sum offsets the increase in Q_n; it does not behave like a centered Brownian integral.

  6. Move from expectation to a probabilistic proof

    For our H = 1/4, let G_i = n to the power H × ΔB_i. These normalized increments are centered Gaussian variables with variance 1. For an integer lag k, their covariance ρ(k) measures the expectation of the product of two normalized increments k steps apart. The normalized total R_n = Q_n/E[Q_n] is exactly the average of the squared G_i: the amplitude normalization compensates for the normalization of Q_n. The fractional covariance gives:

    ρ(k)=12(|k+1|2H−2|k|2H+|k−1|2H)

    The square root is concave: ρ(k) is negative for k ≥ 1. Summing its second differences cancels the intermediate first differences; the sum of |ρ(k)|, for k from 1 to infinity, equals 1/2. The sum of their squares is then at most (1/2) squared, namely 1/4. This bound accounts for dependence.

    To make the telescoping explicit, write d_k for the square root of k + 1 minus the square root of k. The sum of ρ(k), from k = 1 to an integer m, is (d_m − the initial d)/2. The initial d equals 1, and d_m tends to 0. Since the ρ(k) are negative, the sum of their absolute values therefore equals 1/2. The sum of squares is at most the square of this sum of absolute values, because the cross products are positive.

    For two centered Gaussian variables with unit variance, the covariance of their squares equals 2 times the square of their covariance. Expanding the variance of the average of the squared G_i, the n diagonal terms and the pairs of lagged terms give the following bound. R_n denotes Q_n divided by its expectation; the left-hand side is its mean squared error about 1:

    Var(Rn)=2n2(n+2∑k=1n−1(n−k)ρ(k)2)
    E[(Rn−1)2]=Var(Rn)≤2n(1+2×1/4)=3n

    This bound tends to 0: R_n converges to 1 in mean square, and hence in probability. Since E[Q_n] = the square root of n tends to infinity, Q_n tends to infinity in probability. In other words, the probability that Q_n exceeds any fixed threshold tends to 1. The bounds for our grids are 3/4, 3/16 and 3/64; they are upper bounds, never success rates.

  7. Identify the precise limitation of Ito calculus

    The standard framework uses semimartingales. In the continuous case, these are processes that decompose into a local martingale and a finite-variation term. The first generalizes noise whose changes have conditional mean 0, after stopping at thresholds if necessary; the second has a bounded sum of absolute changes over the window, even as the partitions are refined. A continuous semimartingale has finite quadratic variation over this window.

    Our divergent Q_n therefore rules out this framework for H = 1/4. Moreover, the square identity requires S_n = (the square of B at the end − Q_n)/2. The final value remains finite almost surely; S_n therefore tends to minus infinity in probability. The raw left-endpoint sum has no finite limit for the integrand B in this example. Replacing its correction with the duration 1 cannot repair this divergence.

    The general result for standard fractional Brownian motion is broader: it is a semimartingale in its natural filtration only for H = 1/2. Its natural filtration means all the information supplied by its past. For H > 1/2, Q_n tends to 0, but the total variation of the paths is infinite almost surely. A continuous semimartingale with 0 quadratic variation would instead have to have finite total variation. A vanishing Q_n is therefore not enough to restore the standard framework. This last fact about total variation is used here without developing its general proof.

  8. Know what to choose before proceeding

    Leaving the standard framework does not mean that all integration is impossible. We need to specify another definition, its assumptions on the integrand and its change-of-variable formula. The divergence integral encountered in the prerequisites is one such construction; its definition does not reduce to the raw limit of the sums S_n. This unit constructs neither that fractional integral nor a rough path calculus.

    For the same sensor with another H, the check to repeat remains concrete: calculate the size of the squared increments, justify their limit, then examine the formula compatible with that limit. A program that produces finite sums has not yet justified the continuous integral it claims to approximate.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The increments are Gaussian, so I can keep the Brownian formula.”

    A Gaussian distribution does not determine the behavior of the accumulated squares. Start again from their variance (Δt) to the power 2H and the exact square identity. For H = 1/4, the total diverges instead of tending to the elapsed time.

    Revisit the sum of squares and its limit

  • “The expectation of Q_n diverges: divergence in probability is already proved.”

    An average can be dominated by rare events. Check the normalized total R_n: the bound 3/n on its mean squared error shows its concentration about 1 here. This is the step that completes the proof.

    Revisit the control of dependence

  • “With 0 quadratic variation, I must have a semimartingale.”

    You also need to examine total variation. For fractional Brownian motion with H > 1/2, it remains infinite. The transfer exercise separates this fact from a simple vanishing limit of squared increments.

    Distinguish the two kinds of variation in the transfer exercise

Exercise 2 · Reasoning

Test the Brownian correction

Still with H = 1/4, find E[S_n] on the grid with 16 steps. What average discrepancy would result from replacing Q_n with the duration 1 in the square identity? Then explain why the problem persists in the limit, going beyond the expectation calculation.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The expectation of the terminal square is 1, regardless of the grid.

Hint 2

Then use the concentration of R_n and the square identity, which holds for every path.

Compare with the solution

E[S_n] = (1 − 4)/2 = -3/2. Replacing Q_n with 1 removes an average of 4 − 1 = 3 from the right-hand side of the identity.

A discrepancy on a finite grid is not enough to refute a limiting formula: even Brownian sums are discrete before taking the limit. Here, R_n tends to 1 and E[Q_n] to infinity; Q_n therefore diverges in probability. Since the terminal square does not depend on n and remains finite, S_n diverges to minus infinity. No finite elapsed-time term transforms this raw sum into the expected Brownian integral.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover -3/2 and the discrepancy 3 from the identity.
  • Do not confuse a discretization discrepancy with an obstacle in the limit.
  • Link the divergence of Q_n to that of S_n, rather than concluding from its expectation alone.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

Does a 0 limit change the conclusion?

Change only the index of the same sensor model: H = 3/4. Calculate E[Q_n] for n = 4, 16 and 64. Justify the convergence of Q_n to 0. Can we conclude that B is a semimartingale? If S_n had a finite limit, what correction term would the square identity leave?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The exponent 1 − 2H becomes negative. Q_n is always nonnegative.

Hint 2

A nonnegative variable whose expectation tends to 0 converges in mean absolute value to 0. For the Ito framework, also consider total variation.

Compare with the solution

The exponent is 1 − 3/2 = -1/2. Thus E[Q_n] = 1/the square root of n, giving 1/2, 1/4 and 1/8. Since Q_n ≥ 0, E[|Q_n|] = E[Q_n] tends to 0: this is convergence in mean absolute value, which implies convergence in probability.

This does not give a semimartingale: the result for fractional Brownian motion guarantees infinite total variation here, incompatible with a continuous semimartingale with 0 quadratic variation.

If S_n converged, the identity would require the square of B at the end = 2 × the limit of S_n, with no added elapsed-time term. This implication alone constructs neither the integral nor its convergence: an appropriate integration framework still needs to be chosen and justified.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain the three expectations and justify the limit using nonnegativity.
  • Distinguish total variation from quadratic variation.
  • State the conclusion about S_n conditionally, without claiming an integral that has not been constructed.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What total appears in the exact square identity on a grid?

Q_n is the sum of squared increments; S_n adds the values at the beginnings of steps multiplied by their increments. The terminal square minus the initial square equals 2S_n + Q_n.

Recall 2Why does E[Q_n] equal n to the power (1 − 2H) over our window?

There are n centered increments with variance (1/n) to the power 2H. We add their expected squares; linearity of expectation does not require their independence.

Recall 3What does the bound on R_n add to a divergent expectation?

R_n = Q_n/E[Q_n] has a mean squared error about 1 bounded by 3/n for H = 1/4. This concentration proves that Q_n follows its divergent scale in probability; the expectation alone would not prove it.

Recall 4Exactly what obstacle does the left-endpoint sum encounter for H = 1/4?

Q_n diverges, while the terminal square remains finite. The square identity therefore forces S_n to minus infinity in probability. This raw sum has no finite limit for integrating B with respect to itself.

Recall 5Why does Q_n tending to 0 fail to restore Ito for H > 1/2?

Fractional Brownian motion retains infinite total variation. A continuous semimartingale with 0 quadratic variation would have finite total variation. Another form of integration requires its own definition and assumptions.

Finally, explain why choosing another integral requires a new definition.
