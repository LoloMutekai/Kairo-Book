Determinant

Your goalCalculate the determinant of a matrix acting on the plane, interpret its sign and absolute value, then recognize a loss of invertibility.

Before you start
c004-m5 : rational equations · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c032-m1 : elimination · c032-m2 : matrix multiplication · c032-m3 : inverse

✿01 See the idea

You are preparing a grid for cutting cardboard. In the original drawing, each cell is a square with side length 1 cm. A setting transforms the displacements in the drawing: moving horizontally by 1 cm becomes moving 3 cm to the right and 1 cm up; moving vertically by 1 cm becomes moving 1 cm to the right and 2 cm up. These data are fictional: we are studying the geometry of a setting, without describing a real machine.

The cell becomes a parallelogram, a figure whose opposite sides remain parallel. The sides can be slanted while still enclosing an area. But if the two transformed displacements lie on the same straight line, the cell collapses: no area remains between them.

The determinant is the number that summarizes this change in area with a sign. Its absolute value gives the area scale factor. Its sign indicates whether the order of the directions is preserved or reversed. A physical area remains positive or 0: it is the determinant that can be negative.

In the drawings, the horizontal axis points right and the vertical axis points up, with the same scale in centimetres. The directions u and v are the images, in that order, of the original horizontal and vertical displacements. Here, turning from u to v through the smaller angle means turning counterclockwise: orientation is preserved.

The rule to remember

The determinant measures signed area: its absolute value multiplies areas; a 0 determinant signals a collapse that prevents recovery of all original positions.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Measure what the setting does to the grid

The initial setting is A = [[3, 1], [1, 2]]. Calculate its determinant, the area of the image of a cell with side length 1 cm, and whether its orientation is preserved or reversed. Check the result by elimination, then decide whether this setting is invertible.

  1. Set up the model and read the columns

    We assume a linear transformation of the plane: the origin remains fixed and displacements combine through addition and multiplication. x and y are the original coordinates, X and Y those of the image, all in centimetres. The rule is X = ax + by and Y = cx + dy. The real coefficients a, b, c, d are dimensionless: a = 3, b = 1, c = 1, d = 2.

    A=[abcd]=[3112]

    Here, a matrix is the array of these coefficients. Its first column gives the image of the displacement (1 ; 0) cm: this is u = (3 ; 1) cm. Its second column gives the image of (0 ; 1) cm: this is v = (1 ; 2) cm. Reading them as rows would change the interpretation of the sides.

  2. Define the number to calculate

    For this square matrix of size 2 × 2, the determinant, written det(A) or D, is defined by the following difference. ad means a multiplied by d; bc means b multiplied by c. The result is a number, not a new matrix.

    D=det(A)=ad−bc

    Geometrically, D compares the signed area enclosed by the columns with that of the unit square. The first product uses the diagonal from top left to bottom right; the second uses the other diagonal and is subtracted. This subtraction makes the result 0 when the directions lie on the same line.

  3. Calculate each product before subtracting

    The first product is ad = 3 × 2 = 6. The other is bc = 1 × 1 = 1. These products are dimensionless, like the coefficients. Subtract the second from the first; do not add them.

    D=3×2−1×1=6−1=5

    The determinant of this setting is therefore 5, with no unit. This number is neither a side length nor a sum of all the entries.

  4. Interpret the area and orientation

    The original area is side length × side length = 1 cm × 1 cm = 1 cm². The area of the image is |D| times this area. The bars |D| denote the absolute value: keep the magnitude of the number by removing its negative sign if it has one.

    Simage=|D|Sdépart=5×1=5 cm²

    S denotes an area; the first subscript identifies the image and the second the original cell. Since D is greater than 0, orientation is preserved. Here the setting multiplies the area by 5, without necessarily multiplying all lengths by the same factor. This interpretation assumes orthonormal coordinate systems, meaning perpendicular axes with identical units of length.

  5. Check by another method: the pivots

    The coefficient a is nonzero. Using the elimination method seen earlier, subtract the first row multiplied by c/a from the second row. The factor is 1/3 = 1/3. The first entry of the second row becomes 0; its last entry becomes d − (c/a)b = 2 − 1/3 = 5/3. This calculation remains exact using fractions.

    A→[31053]

    Adding a multiple of another row to a row preserves the determinant. In the resulting triangular matrix, it is the product of the diagonal entries: 3 × (5/3) = 5. Elimination gives 5 again, checking the sign and the subtraction in the direct calculation.

    This method shows why the formula works when a is nonzero: a[d − (c/a)b] = ad − bc. The formula ad − bc remains defined even if a is 0; only this division used for checking becomes forbidden. We would then look for a row to swap before dividing, accounting for the change of sign.

  6. Draw the conclusion about the inverse and its scope

    The pivots 3 and 5/3 are nonzero. For every image position, elimination therefore recovers exactly one original position: A is invertible. For a real square matrix, the exact criterion is det(A) different from 0. A negative determinant also works; it simply reverses orientation.

    If the determinant is 0, a transformation of the plane collapses at least one direction. Distinct original positions can then have the same image, as in the last exercise. This does not mean that all entries are 0, or that every associated system has no solution.

    The determinant is defined for square matrices. For larger sizes, the shortcut ad − bc no longer applies. We can follow an elimination: adding multiples of rows preserves the determinant, each row swap changes its sign, and then we multiply the diagonal entries of the triangular form. We do not carry out this general calculation in this unit. In approximate calculations, the magnitude of the determinant alone is not enough to judge sensitivity to errors.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations. Explain why a dimensionless number changes an area in square centimetres here. Recover the result using the diagonals, then the pivots, and distinguish the sign of the determinant from the physical area.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I multiply along the diagonals, then add the products.”

    Addition would give 7 for our setting. First write ad − bc, then substitute for the letters: 6 − 1 = 5. The elimination check gives 5 again and helps identify this error.

    Review the definition and the check using pivots

  • “A negative determinant gives a negative area, so the setting is impossible.”

    Area uses the absolute value of the determinant. The negative sign indicates a reversal in the order of the directions. Swapping the columns of the same setting separates this change in orientation from the change in area.

    Try swapping the columns

  • “A 0 determinant means everything is 0 and no solution exists.”

    Nonzero but proportional columns are enough to collapse the grid. Some image positions remain reachable and have several possible original positions; others are unreachable. What is lost is uniqueness for all image positions.

    Construct the original positions with the same image in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Guided application

A larger original cell

Keep the setting A from the example, but take a square cell with side length 2 cm. Without changing A, recover its determinant and then calculate the area of the image. Explain why doubling the original side length does more than double its area.

Hint 1

The change concerns the cell, not the coefficients of A.

Hint 2

Calculate side length × side length before applying the area scale factor of the setting.

Compare with the solution

The determinant remains 3 × 2 − 1 × 1 = 5, with no unit. The original area is 2 × 2 = 4 cm². The image therefore has an area of 5 × 4 = 20 cm².

The factor 2 acts on both sides of the square, so it acts on the area by 2 × 2 = 4. Check: the image of the large cell has 4 times the area 5 cm² of the image of the original cell. The area scale factor of the transformation itself remains 5.

  • Calculate the original area before the area of the image and give the result in cm².
  • Distinguish the scale factor for a side from the scale factor for the area.

Exercise 2 · Prediction followed by calculation

Swap the order of the directions

The setting B swaps the columns of A: its first row is (1 ; 3), its second (2 ; 1). Predict the area of the image of the original cell, then calculate det(B). Is the setting still invertible?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The parallelogram uses the same sides, but in the reverse order.

Hint 2

The main diagonal of B multiplies b by c. Then keep the correct order of subtraction.

Compare with the solution

The sides have swapped roles: the enclosed area remains the same, while orientation reverses. The calculation gives det(B) = 1 × 1 − 3 × 2 = 1 − 6 = -5.

The absolute value of -5 is 5. The image of the original cell therefore still has an area of 5 cm². The determinant is nonzero: B remains invertible. The negative sign describes orientation, not an inability to recover the original position.

Check your reasoning

  • Make a prediction about the area before calculating.
  • Obtain -5 and explain the difference between its sign and its absolute value.
  • Justify invertibility using a nonzero determinant, without requiring a positive one.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and proof by construction

Two original positions give the same image

The setting C keeps u but replaces v with 2 × u: C = [[3, 6], [1, 2]]. Calculate its determinant. Then compare the images of the original points P = (2 ; 0) cm and Q = (0 ; 1) cm. What does this prove about the inverse?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The two diagonal products can be equal without any entry being 0.

Hint 2

Calculate the images using the columns: P uses 2 times the first and Q uses the second.

Compare with the solution

det(C) = 3 × 2 − 6 × 1 = 6 − 6 = 0. The second column is 2 times the first: the cell collapses, as in the second drawing.

The image of P is 2 × (3 ; 1) cm = (6 ; 2) cm. The image of Q is directly the second column, namely (6 ; 2) cm. P and Q are distinct but have the same image position: no inverse can unambiguously recover which was the original position.

This single counterexample is enough to rule out an inverse. It also proves that a 0 determinant does not mean “no image position is possible”: the image position (6 ; 2) cm has just been constructed. The area is 0, not all the displacements.

Check your reasoning

  • Show that the two products are equal and the determinant is 0.
  • Calculate the two images separately, with their units.
  • Use the distinct original positions to disprove the existence of an inverse, without concluding that every equation is impossible.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you calculate the determinant of A = [[a, b], [c, d]]?

det(A) = ad − bc. Multiply along each diagonal, then subtract the second product from the first. The result is a number; this shortcut applies only to size 2 × 2.

Recall 2What is the difference between D and |D| for the grid?

|D| is the factor that multiplies the original area. The sign of D indicates whether the order of the directions is preserved or reversed, in the chosen oriented coordinate systems. The coefficients here are dimensionless; areas are in cm².

Recall 3Why does a 0 determinant rule out an inverse for a transformation of the plane?

At least one direction collapses: different original positions can have the same image. Recovering a unique original position for each image position becomes impossible. A square matrix is invertible exactly when its determinant is nonzero.

Recall 4How can you check the calculation without just repeating the diagonal products?

Add a multiple of one row to the other until reaching triangular form, then multiply the diagonal entries. These additions preserve the determinant; each row swap changes its sign. Never divide by a 0 pivot.
