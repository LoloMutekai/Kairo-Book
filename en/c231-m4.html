Level 4 · C231·M4

Length

Your goalCalculate the length of a route by integrating the norm of its tangent vector in a given metric, then check that a change of parameter preserves this length.

Before you start
c127-m5 : Green and Stokes · c059-m6 : Deterministic discrete Bellman · c143-m5 : uniform convergence · c049-m5 : series solutions · c165-m5 : duality · c231-m1 : multilinearity and tensors · c231-m2 : local manifolds · c231-m3 : metric

✿01 See the idea

You walk along a straight aisle in a greenhouse. On its digital map, markers A, C and B are evenly spaced. On the ground, the stretch from C to B is longer than the stretch from A to C: the map progressively compresses distances. Counting intervals on the map is therefore not enough to find how many metres you travel.

The metric provides the local measuring rule. At each position, it converts a small coordinate displacement into a length. To measure the whole route, imagine a string laid along the aisle: you need to add up the lengths of its small pieces, each measured using the rule that applies at that location.

The parameter of the route indicates how you progress across the map. Making it advance faster changes the amount travelled per unit of parameter; this does not change the length of the string if you follow the same path once.

This greenhouse, its map and all the values are fictional. The ground is assumed to be flat, the aisle clear and the map calibration exact. We will calculate the length of a given path. Finding a geodesic, meaning studying paths that are locally shortest, is the subject of the next unit.

The rule to remember

Differentiate the route, measure its tangent vector using the metric at the current position, take the square root, then integrate over the whole route.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Measure the aisle using a rule that changes across the map

In the fictional greenhouse, A has coordinates (0, 0), C has coordinates (1, 0) and B has coordinates (2, 0). We follow the aisle directly from A → B, without turning back. The coordinates x and y are unitless labels; X and Y denote physical positions in metres.

  1. Specify a calibration and its units

    The calibration is the function that maps positions on the digital map to positions on the ground. We are given s = 1 m, a positive reference length, and the following relations. Uppercase X and Y denote physical coordinates along perpendicular axes; lowercase x and y denote map coordinates.

    X=s(x+x22);Y=sy

    The local change is dX = s(1 + x) dx and dY = s dy. The symbol d indicates an infinitesimal change. The metric g measures the square of a physical length: its matrix G in map coordinates is therefore the following, obtained by expanding the squares of dX and dY.

    G(x,y)=[s2(1+x)200s2]

    Each coefficient of G is expressed here in square metres, since x and y are unitless. In the region studied, 0 ≤ x ≤ 2, the matrix is symmetric and positive definite: its diagonal coefficients are positive. The calibration is regular on a neighbourhood of this region. We stay in this same coordinate chart throughout the route.

  2. From local measurement to length

    Write γ(t) for the point along the route and (x(t), y(t)) for its coordinates. The parameter t is unitless; it varies from a to b, with a < b. A dot above a symbol means its derivative with respect to t. The tangent vector γ̇ therefore has components ẋ and ẏ. We assume the path is continuous and piecewise continuously differentiable with finitely many pieces, and the metric is continuous and positive definite along the path.

    Denote the diagonal coefficients of G by gxx and gyy, and its cross coefficient by gxy, equal to gyx by symmetry. The letters xx, xy and yy are the coefficient indices in the following formula. The squared norm q(t) of the tangent is calculated at the current position γ(t). The symmetry of the metric explains the factor 2 in front of the cross term.

    q(t)=gγ(t)(γ˙,γ˙)
    q=gxxx˙2+2gxyx˙y˙+gyyy˙2

    In this expanded expression, all the coefficients are evaluated at γ(t). The square q is nonnegative and is expressed in square metres. Its positive square root is the metric speed: metres travelled per unit of parameter. This is not a speed in metres per second, because t does not denote physical time.

    Over a small positive interval Δt, we approximate the length by √q(t) × Δt. Adding these quantities over increasingly fine intervals leads to the integral. The notation L_g(γ) denotes the length of the path γ measured using g; dt indicates integration with respect to t.

    Lg(γ)=∫abq(t)dt

    The square root is inside the integral: we add local lengths, not their squares. The final product is in metres. A pause in the parametrization gives a 0 tangent and a 0 contribution, without making the definition impossible.

  3. Write the route and its tangent

    For the aisle A → B, choose γ(t) with coordinates (t, 0), where 0 ≤ t ≤ 2. Its differentiated components are ẋ = 1 and ẏ = 0: the route advances along the x axis and stays on the line y = 0.

    The cross term and the y term vanish. This leaves the metric coefficient in the x direction, evaluated at x = t, multiplied by the square of ẋ.

    q(t)=s2(1+t)2;q(t)=s(1+t)

    The last equality uses s > 0 and 1 + t > 0 throughout our interval. In general, the square root of the square of a real number is its absolute value. Here, the sign is known; no approximate numerical square root calculation is needed.

  4. Integrate and evaluate the bounds

    The metric speed increases with t. We therefore integrate s(1 + t) from 0 to 2. Define the following function F. It is an antiderivative of 1 + t: its derivative recovers the integrand without the constant factor s.

    F(t)=t+t22
    Lg(γ)=s∫02(1+t)dt=s[F(2)−F(0)]

    At the end: F(2) = 2 + 4/2 = 2 + 2 = 4. At the start: F(0) = 0 + 0/2 = 0. We subtract the value at the start to retain only the interval actually travelled.

    The length is therefore 1 m × (4 − 0) = 4 m, or 4.00 m in decimal notation. The coordinates have changed by 2; this unitless difference is not a length in metres.

  5. Check why the starting rule is not enough

    At the start, the metric speed is 1 m per unit of parameter. At the end, it is 3 m per unit of parameter. The coefficient gxx at the end is 1 × 9 = 9 square metres: taking its square root gives 3 m for our tangent ẋ = 1.

    Applying the starting rule everywhere would give 2 × 1 = 2 m. This calculation underestimates the length: each interval farther along the map represents more metres. In a variable metric, the norm of the total displacement evaluated at the start does not replace the integral of the local norms.

  6. Check using a physical ruler and a trapezium

    First check: the calibration gives X(A) = 0 m and X(B) = 4 m. The aisle is straight and followed without turning back: the difference in physical positions is 4 − 0 = 4 m. This check uses the ground ruler rather than the metric coefficients.

    Another check: the speed s(1 + t) is affine in t. The area under its graph is therefore exactly that of a trapezium: average the heights, then multiply by the width of the interval.

    1+32×2=2×2=4 m

    The results agree. The trapezium formula is exact here because the speed is affine; it does not become an exact rule for all metrics or all paths.

  7. Distinguish the path from its parameter

    Length describes the path travelled, counting any retraced sections. It must not depend on the pace chosen to describe it. Let u be another unitless parameter and t = φ(u) a continuously differentiable change of parameter with positive derivative that traverses exactly the same interval of t. The letter φ denotes this function, and φ′ its derivative with respect to u.

    By the chain rule, the new tangent is the old tangent multiplied by φ′. Its squared norm is multiplied by the square of φ′; the norm is therefore multiplied by |φ′|. The symbol | | means absolute value. Since φ′ is positive here, this factor becomes the factor for the change of variable in the integral.

    q(φ(u))φ′(u)du=q(t)dt

    The integral therefore retains its value with the corresponding bounds. Traversing the path in reverse also preserves its length because of the absolute value, with the bounds reordered. An outward journey followed by a return journey adds more path to travel: it is not simply a new description of the same route followed once. The exercises will check these distinctions in our aisle.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculation. Explain why the metric is applied at the current position, why we take the square root before integrating, and how the result can stay the same when the speed per unit of parameter changes. State the units again at each step.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I use the metric at the start to measure the total displacement directly.”

    This substitution is valid for a line segment in a constant metric, but our rule changes with x. Here, it would give 2 m instead of 4 m. Evaluate the metric along the path and add up the local contributions.

    Review the variable measuring rule and its checks

  • “The squared quantity measured by the metric is already a length to add up.”

    q(t) is in square metres in our parametrization. Its square root gives the norm of the tangent; this is the norm we integrate. Integrating q does not calculate L_g. Taking a single square root after integrating q also fails to reproduce the sum of local lengths.

    Review the definition of q and the length integral

  • “If the parameter advances faster, the path becomes longer.”

    For a monotone change that traverses the same path once, the derivative of the change multiplies the norm of the tangent and alters the integration interval in a compensating way. Recalculate the tangent and the bounds together.

    Check the change of parameter in the aisle

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · split the route

Measure on either side of C

In the same aisle, C is at x = 1. Calculate the lengths of A → C and C → B using the antiderivative from the example. Add them, then explain why the second part is longer despite equal coordinate differences.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate F(1) = 1 + 1/2, then subtract the values of F at the bounds of each part.

Hint 2

The local coefficient increases with x; the part farther to the right must represent more metres.

Compare with the solution

F(1) = 1 + 1/2 = 3/2. A → C measures s[F(1) − F(0)] = 3/2 m, or 1.50 m.

C → B measures s[F(2) − F(1)] = 4 − 3/2 = 5/2 m, or 2.50 m. We use the same calibration and the same unit.

Sum: 3/2 + 5/2 = 4 m, as for A → B. The coordinate differences are identical, but the metric assigns a larger norm to the same tangent in the part C → B.

Check your reasoning

  • I calculate a difference of antiderivative values for each part.
  • I obtain 1.50 m then 2.50 m, with a sum of 4.00 m.
  • I explain the unequal lengths through the variation of the metric.

Exercise 2 · Application · recover invariance

Traverse the same aisle with another parameter

Now describe A → B using the coordinates (2u, 0), where 0 ≤ u ≤ 1. The ground and calibration remain those of the example. Calculate the new tangent, its metric speed, then the length. Why would keeping the tangent from the example give an incorrect result?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The derivative of x with respect to u is 2. Then evaluate G at x = 2u.

Hint 2

The norm of the new tangent is s × 2 × (1 + 2u). Integrate over the new interval, not the old one.

Compare with the solution

The new tangent has components (2, 0). The metric is evaluated at x = 2u. The metric speed is therefore s × 2 × (1 + 2u), positive throughout the interval.

Lg=s∫012(1+2u)du=4 m

To show the integration in detail, an antiderivative of the integrand without s is 2u + 2 times u squared. Its value is 4 at the bound 1 and 0 at the bound 0. Multiplying their difference by s = 1 m gives 4 m.

Check: the speeds at the endpoints are 2 and 6 m per unit of u. Their average is 4; multiplied by the width 1, it gives 4 m. The speeds have been multiplied by 2, while the interval has shrunk by a factor of 2.

Keeping ẋ = 1 would omit the derivative of 2u. A parametrization changes both the speed and the bounds; it does not create extra metres.

Check your reasoning

  • I differentiate the new coordinates with respect to u.
  • I evaluate the metric at the current point (2u, 0).
  • I integrate over [0, 1] and recover 4.00 m, explaining the compensation.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · explain a return journey

Go to B, then return to C

You go from A to B, then return from B to C along the same aisle. Calculate the total length travelled. Compare it with the length of the direct route A → C. Why is a signed difference in physical positions no longer enough?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The return B → C has the same length as C → B, but its change in physical position has the opposite sign.

Hint 2

On the return journey, take x = 2 − v with 0 ≤ v ≤ 1. The derivative is negative, but its norm is positive.

Compare with the solution

The outward journey A → B measures 4 m. For the return journey, v is a new unitless parameter; its coordinates are (2 − v, 0). The tangent has components (−1, 0). Its norm is s(1 + 2 − v), because the absolute value of −1 is 1.

Integrating from 0 to 1 gives s × (3 − 1/2) = 5/2 m for the return journey. This is indeed the same length as C → B, with the direction reversed.

Total length: 4 + 5/2 = 13/2 m, or 6.50 m. The direct route A → C measures only 1.50 m. The difference is 5 m: the part C → B was travelled on both the outward and return journeys.

The signed difference X(C) − X(A) is 3/2 m. It describes the change in position, not all the string laid out. The norm removes the local sign, then the integral counts all the pieces. Retracing your steps adds length.

Check your reasoning

  • I keep a positive contribution on the return journey.
  • I find 6.50 m and distinguish this result from the 1.50 m of the direct route.
  • I explain the difference between length travelled and change in position, without claiming to have solved a geodesic problem.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What sequence of operations converts a parametrized path and a metric into a length?

Differentiate the path, calculate the squared norm of the tangent using the metric at the current position, take its positive square root, then integrate between the parameter bounds. Take the square root before integrating.

Recall 2On our unitless map, what units do the metric, the squared norm of the tangent and the length have?

The metric coefficients and the squared norm of the tangent are in square metres. The norm is in metres per unit of parameter. With a unitless parameter, its integral is in metres. This is not a physical speed in metres per second.

Recall 3Why does measuring the displacement A → B using only the metric at the start fail in our aisle?

The local measuring rule grows with x. The starting rule would give 2 m, whereas the integral gives 4 m. Each tangent must be measured at its own location.

Recall 4Why does a regular, monotone change of parameter preserve the length of the same route?

The derivative of the change multiplies the tangent; its absolute value multiplies the norm. This factor is compensated by the change of variable and the bounds in the integral. The path must be traversed once, with the same endpoints.

Recall 5Why does the outward journey A → B followed by the return B → C have a different length from the direct route A → C?

Length adds the positive contributions of everything travelled. The outward and return journeys give 6.50 m; the direct route gives 1.50 m. The endpoints alone do not describe the route actually followed.
