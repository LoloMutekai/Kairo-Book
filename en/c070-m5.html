Level 2 · C070·M5

Minimum spanning tree

Your goalConnect all the workstations in a workshop at the lowest total cost, work through Kruskal’s algorithm and justify why its choices preserve an optimum.

Before you start
c025-m5 : O, Omega and Theta · c028-m5 : decision trees · c069-m5 : congruences and Euclid · c070-m1 : graphs · c070-m2 : traversals · c070-m3 : trees · c070-m4 : Dijkstra

✿01 See the idea

The workshop has workstations A, B, C, D and E. Approved routes allow cables to be laid between certain workstations. You want every workstation to be able to communicate with all the others, possibly through a neighbour. Which set of cables should you install to use the least total length?

A cable already laid can serve several paths: you pay for it once. Adding a cable between workstations that are already connected closes a loop. In our model, this loop is an avoidable use of extra material: you can remove one of its cables and keep the workstations connected. Redundancy against failures is not our objective here.

A spanning tree keeps all the vertices and enough edges to connect them without a cycle. Minimum here means that the sum of the weights is the smallest among all such trees. This requires comparing entire networks; finding a tree that spans everything is not enough yet.

The routes and their lengths are fictional. Each weight is a cable length in metres, measured along the approved route. The cables work in both directions, their lengths add together and no extra cost is added at the workstations. The drawing shows the possible routes; its distances on the page are not to scale.

The rule to remember

Kruskal examines edges in increasing order of weight: keep an edge that connects groups that are still separate, reject an edge that closes a cycle, then check coverage and total cost.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Choose the cables, then prove the minimum

The only approved routes are AB = 2 m, BC = 3 m, AC = 4 m, BD = 5 m, CD = 6 m, CE = 7 m and DE = 8 m. Let us find a spanning tree with minimum total cost. The weights stay fixed throughout the calculation.

  1. State exactly what is being minimized

    Write the graph as G = (S, E): S is the set of workstations and E is the set of approved routes. An edge e is an unordered pair of workstations; w(e) is its weight in metres. We have n = |S| = 5 vertices and |E| = 7 edges. The bars | | count the elements; n and these counts have no physical unit.

    We choose a set F contained in E. The network T = (S, F) must be connected, meaning that it links every pair of workstations by a path, and acyclic, meaning that it has no loop returning along distinct edges. It spans all the workstations in S, even if some routes are not chosen. Its cost W(T), in metres, is the sum of the weights of the chosen edges.

    W(T)=∑e∈Fw(e)

    The sum is written W(T) = ∑ of w(e), for e belonging to F. We compare W(T) across spanning trees. A tree with n vertices has n − 1 edges, so we will need 5 − 1 = 4. This count alone does not prove connectivity.

    The graph is finite, undirected and connected; the weights are positive and additive. These assumptions match the cabling considered here. If the routes left groups permanently separate, no tree could span all the workstations: we would obtain separate trees, a forest.

  2. Sort and apply the right decision rule

    The increasing order is AB (2 m), BC (3 m), AC (4 m), BD (5 m), CD (6 m), CE (7 m), DE (8 m). This order is global: it does not start from a source workstation.

    Initially F is empty: 0 cables and 5 isolated groups. A group, or connected component, contains the workstations already connected by the cables kept. For each edge, ask whether its endpoints belong to different groups. If they do, keep it and merge the groups. Otherwise, a path already exists between its endpoints: adding it would form a cycle, so reject it.

    The edges kept therefore remain acyclic at every step. Stop when all the workstations are in the same group. Since acyclicity is preserved, the count n − 1 also gives this stopping criterion.

  3. Keep AB, then BC

    AB costs 2 m. A and B are isolated: keeping AB creates the group {A, B} and leaves C, D, E separate. There are 4 groups left; the length installed is 2 m.

    BC costs 3 m. B is in {A, B}, C is isolated: keeping BC creates {A, B, C}. D and E remain separate, giving 3 groups. The running total becomes 2 + 3 = 5 m. We do not require every new cable to touch A.

  4. Reject AC, then connect D with BD

    AC costs 4 m, but A already reaches C along A–B–C. Adding AC would close A–B–C–A. Reject it: the running total stays at 5 m. Without this check, the cheapest edges could stay confined to a single group.

    BD costs 5 m and connects {A, B, C} to D, which is still isolated. Keep it: {A, B, C, D} forms a group, E remains alone. There are 2 groups left; the running total is 5 + 5 = 10 m.

  5. Reject CD, then connect E with CE

    CD costs 6 m. The path C–B–D already exists: CD would close the cycle C–B–D–C. Reject it without adding its weight to the running total.

    CE costs 7 m. C belongs to the large group, E is isolated: keep CE. All the workstations are now connected, with 4 edges and no cycle. DE, with weight 8 m, no longer needs to be examined; if we continued, it would be rejected, because D–B–C–E already connects its endpoints.

  6. Add up what is installed and check the structure

    Pay for each edge kept once: AB and BC give 5 m; BD brings the running total to 10 m; CE gives 10 + 7 = 17 m. The weights of rejected edges do not enter this total.

    W(T)=2+3+5+7=17 m

    Coverage check: from B, you reach A, C and D directly, then E through C. Acyclicity check: each addition merged distinct groups. Count check: 4 = 5 − 1. These checks establish that T spans the graph and is a tree; its minimum cost still needs to be justified.

  7. Why the cheapest choice can preserve an optimum

    Here is the exchange proof. Before an addition, suppose an optimal tree R contains all the edges already kept. This holds initially, since F is empty and an optimum exists among a finite number of choices. Let e be the next accepted edge, and U the group containing one of its endpoints. A cut here separates U from all the other workstations. No edge of F crosses this cut, by the definition of a group.

    The edge e has minimum weight among those crossing this cut. Otherwise a lighter edge would have been examined earlier: its endpoints, still separate at that time, would have been joined. They could no longer belong to distinct groups now, because groups merge without ever splitting.

    If e is already in R, nothing needs to change. Otherwise, adding it to R closes a unique cycle. The path in R between its endpoints also crosses the cut through another edge f. This f does not belong to F, and w(f) ≥ w(e). Removing f from the cycle gives a spanning tree that keeps F and adds e, with cost W(R) + w(e) − w(f) ≤ W(R). Since R was optimal, the new tree is optimal too. The reasoning repeats at every addition; at the end, F itself is an optimal tree.

    In the workshop, the successive cuts are {A}, then {A, B}, then {A, B, C}, then {A, B, C, D}. Their cheapest crossing edges are respectively AB (2 m), BC (3 m), BD (5 m), CE (7 m). This justification concerns the order of the choices and their cuts; a total smaller than that of a single competing tree would not have been enough.

  8. Compare with Dijkstra’s objective

    With the same positive weights, Dijkstra starting from A keeps the shortest paths: A–B of 2 m, A–C of 4 m, A–B–D of 7 m and A–C–E of 11 m. The corresponding tree uses AB, AC, BD and CE. Its installed length is 2 + 4 + 5 + 7 = 18 m.

    Our minimum tree uses 17 m, but its path A–B–C measures 5 m instead of 4 m for A–C. Its path A–B–C–E measures 12 m instead of 11 m. It accepts these detours to reduce the total length of installed cables by 18 − 17 = 1 m.

    Path distances are not added together to charge for the installation: that would count some cables several times. Dijkstra optimizes paths from a source; Kruskal optimizes the total weight of a spanning tree, without a preferred source.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the method and work through the edge order again, naming the groups before each decision. Then explain the exchange that guarantees the minimum and why the path from A to C can become longer despite this minimum.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I simply take the 4 cheapest cables.”

    AB, BC, AC and BD cost little, but the triangle A–B–C wastes an edge and E remains isolated. Sorting proposes an order; the group test decides whether to keep or reject an edge. Work through the rejections of AC and CD again before calculating the total.

    Work through the groups and rejections again

  • “If I cannot remove any cable without disconnecting the network, my tree is the cheapest.”

    That characterizes a connected tree with no unnecessary edge. AB, AC, BD and CE have this property, for 18 m. You must compare weights across trees: replacing AC with BC still gives a tree and reduces the cost to 17 m. The exchange proof explains why Kruskal reaches the global minimum.

    Review the exchange justification

  • “The minimum spanning tree also gives the shortest paths from A.”

    In the minimum tree, A–B–C costs 5 m; the direct route A–C costs 4 m in the original graph. The total installed weight and the length of a path address different objectives. Write down the quantity to minimize before choosing the algorithm.

Exercise 3 · Reasoning · optimality and uniqueness

Two routes have the same weight

BC is approved again. Only the weight of AC changes: AC is now 3 m, like BC. All the other weights remain as originally given. Can different minimum spanning trees be obtained depending on the chosen order of AC and BC? Construct them and explain why the exchange proof remains valid.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

After AB, try keeping BC before AC, then AC before BC. In each case, what happens to the other edge of the triangle?

Hint 2

The proof uses w(e) ≤ w(f). Does equality prevent the exchange or allow another tree with the same cost?

Compare with the solution

If BC comes before AC, keep AB, BC, BD, CE and reject AC. If AC comes before BC, keep AB, AC, BD, CE and reject BC. In both cases, the last side of the triangle would close a cycle.

Each tree costs 2 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 17 m, because AC and BC now have the same weight. They are distinct, connected and acyclic. Sorting can break the tie in either order; the choices still have minimum weight across the cuts encountered.

The exchange can preserve exactly the cost when w(e) = w(f): the proof guarantees an optimum without requiring it to be unique. Equal weights do not always suffice to create several optima; here we established this by explicitly constructing two minimum trees. To reproduce a particular choice, you must set a stable rule for breaking ties.

Check your reasoning

  • Give the edge sets of the 2 trees and check their structures.
  • Use the new weight of AC and obtain 17 m in each case.
  • Distinguish minimality, uniqueness and reproducibility when breaking ties.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What constraints and cost define a minimum spanning tree?

Keep all the vertices, connect them without a cycle using approved edges, and minimize the sum of the weights of these edges. Each chosen edge counts once. The count n − 1 accompanies these constraints without replacing them.

Recall 2What decision does Kruskal make for each edge, and when does it stop?

Examine edges in increasing order of weight; accept if their endpoints are in different components, reject otherwise. Stop when all the vertices are connected; the edges kept remain acyclic. If the graph is disconnected, no spanning tree for the whole graph exists.

Recall 3How does the exchange justify choosing an edge that crosses a cut?

Start from an optimum containing the previous choices. If the chosen edge is missing, adding it forms a cycle; removing another edge that crosses the same cut and is no cheaper preserves a tree without increasing the cost. An optimum containing the new choice therefore remains.

Recall 4Why can a minimum spanning tree lengthen the path from A to C?

It minimizes the total installed weight without choosing a source. Here A–B–C measures 5 m, compared with 4 m for A–C, but the whole tree costs 17 m compared with 18 m for the shortest-path tree from A.

Recall 5What does Kruskal still guarantee when edges have the same weight?

A spanning tree with minimum cost on a connected graph. The optimum may be non-unique, as when AC and BC are both 3 m in the exercise. A fixed rule for breaking ties helps reproduce a choice; it does not change the optimal cost.
