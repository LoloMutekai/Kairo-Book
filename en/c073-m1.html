Level 2 · C073·M1

ANOVA

Your goalCompare several means by separating variability between groups from variability among the observations within each group.

Before you start
c056-m5 : effect size · c065-m5 : prediction interval

✿01 See the idea

In a greenhouse, you compare growing media A, B and C for growing the same plant variety. Each growing medium has 3 separate pots. After the same growing period, you measure the dry mass of one plant per pot. The masses are fictional, chosen so that you can redo every calculation with a pencil.

The means may differ simply because plants vary. To assess their separation, you also need to look at how much the masses vary within each group. Widely separated centres in tightly clustered groups indicate something different from the same centres surrounded by a large spread.

Analysis of variance, abbreviated ANOVA, compares the group means here. Its name comes from the method it uses: it decomposes squared deviations and then compares measures of variability. It does not directly test whether the variances are equal.

We study a single factor, the growing medium, with 3 levels, meaning 3 possible choices. Pairwise comparisons and designs with several factors belong to the following units.

The rule to remember

Measure the separation of the centres, measure the spread around each centre, divide these two sums by their degrees of freedom, then form their ratio.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Do the growing media produce different mean masses?

Fictional dry masses in g: A = (7, 8, 9), B = (9, 10, 11), C = (11, 12, 13). Each value comes from a separate pot. The fictional protocol assigns growing media at random and sets the threshold α = 0.05 before observing the data. Let us calculate a classical one-way ANOVA.

  1. Define the model before calculating

    g denotes a group A, B or C; i denotes a pot in that group. y with subscripts g and i is its dry mass in g. n with subscript g is the number of pots in the group, here 3 in each. k = 3 counts the groups; N = 9 counts all the pots. These counts have no physical unit.

    ygi=μg+εgi

    μ with subscript g is the unknown population mean for plants grown in medium g, in g; ε is the random deviation from this mean, in g. For the exact test, we assume that the deviations are independent and follow a normal distribution, a bell-shaped distribution, with mean 0 g and the same strictly positive variance σ squared, in g squared. The masses alone do not establish these assumptions, especially with so few pots.

    H0:μA=μB=μC

    The null hypothesis states that the population means are equal. The alternative is that at least one differs. A difference between observed means is therefore not enough to decide. The degrees of freedom and the reference distribution will calibrate our comparison.

  2. Find the centres for comparison

    A bar above y indicates an observed mean. The group mean, written ȳ with subscript g, is the sum of the masses in the group divided by its sample size. For A: 7 + 8 = 15 g, then 15 + 9 = 24 g; 24/3 = 8 g.

    For B: 9 + 10 = 19 g; 19 + 11 = 30 g; 30/3 = 10 g. For C: 11 + 12 = 23 g; 23 + 13 = 36 g; 36/3 = 12 g.

    The grand mean ȳ uses all the pots: 24 + 30 = 54 g, then 54 + 36 = 90 g. With N = 3 × 3 = 9, it is 90/9 = 10 g. Taking the simple average of the means would give the same result here because the sample sizes are equal; with different sizes, each centre would need to be weighted by its sample size.

  3. Measure the spread within groups

    The residual for a pot is its mass minus its group mean. In A: 7 − 8 = -1 g; 8 − 8 = 0 g; 9 − 8 = 1 g. Their sum is 0: adding the signed residuals would hide the spread.

    W=∑g∑i(ygi−y¯g)2

    ∑ means “add”: sum the squares over the pots i in each group g, then over all the groups. W denotes this within-group sum, in g squared. For A: (-1) squared + 0 squared + 1 squared = 1 + 0 + 1 = 2 g squared.

    B and C have exactly the same residuals around their own centres. Each therefore contributes 2 g squared. In total, W = 3 × 2 = 6 g squared. This variability remains after assigning each pot the mean for its growing medium.

  4. Measure the separation between centres

    The centres deviate from the grand mean by 8 − 10 = -2 g for A, 0 g for B and 12 − 10 = 2 g for C. We square these deviations and weight them by the number of pots represented by each centre.

    B=∑gng(y¯g−y¯)2

    B denotes the between-group sum, in g squared; in this formula, the letter B is a quantity rather than the label for growing medium B. The calculation gives 3 × [(-2) squared + 0 squared + 2 squared] = 3 × (4 + 0 + 4) = 3 × 8 = 24 g squared. Without the weight 3, each group would count as a single pot.

  5. Check the partition with a direct calculation

    T denotes the sum of squared deviations of all pots from the grand mean, in g squared. For a pot, its overall deviation splits as follows:

    ygi−y¯=(ygi−y¯g)+(y¯g−y¯)

    When we expand the square and then sum, the cross term cancels because the residuals in each group sum to 0 g. The identity T = B + W remains: 24 + 6 = 30 g squared. This is an algebraic identity; it does not depend on normality.

    T=∑g∑i(ygi−y¯)2

    Direct calculation: square the deviations 7 − 10, 8 − 10, 9 − 10 for A; 9 − 10, 10 − 10, 11 − 10 for B; 11 − 10, 12 − 10, 13 − 10 for C. Adding all these squares does give 30 g squared. This second approach checks that we have not left out any pot.

  6. Convert the sums into mean squares

    Here, a degree of freedom counts a variation that remains free after the constraints imposed by the means. The group centres have k − 1 = 2 degrees of freedom around the grand centre. Within each group, the residuals summing to 0 impose a constraint: n with subscript g − 1 free variations remain. Across all groups, this gives N − k = 9 − 3 = 6 within-group degrees of freedom.

    The total has N − 1 = 8; check: 2 + 6 = 8. A mean square is a sum of squares divided by its number of degrees of freedom. CM with subscript B and CM with subscript W denote the between-group and within-group mean squares, both in g squared.

    CMB=Bk−1=242=12 g au carré
    CMW=WN−k=66=1 g au carré
    f=CMBCMW=121=12

    f is the observed statistic, with no unit: the squared units cancel. Under equality of means and the model assumptions, the two mean squares estimate the same common variance. A large ratio therefore gives grounds to doubt equality. However, we do not decide by mechanically comparing f with 1: its fluctuation depends on the degrees of freedom. W must be strictly positive for this ratio; here W = 6 g squared.

  7. Calibrate the ratio and state the overall conclusion

    Under the null hypothesis and our independent normal model with a common variance, the random statistic U follows the F distribution with 2 numerator degrees of freedom and 6 denominator degrees of freedom. U is the ratio that a new sample generated under this model would give. The p-value is the probability of obtaining a ratio at least as large as f under this model.

    For this particular pair of degrees of freedom, the F distribution provides a simple expression for this probability. For t, a possible value of the ratio, with t ≥ 0, we have the following identity; the derivation of the F distribution is beyond this unit.

    Pr(U≥t)=(66+2t)3

    The exponent is 6/2 = 3. At t = f = 12, the product 2 × 12 is 24 and the denominator becomes 6 + 24 = 30. The fraction is therefore 6/30 = 1/5; its square is 1/25, then its cube is 1/25 × 1/5 = 1/125.

    Thus p = 0.008, with no unit, is below the chosen threshold α = 1/20 = 0.05. Under the stated assumptions, the test rejects overall equality of means at threshold α. This p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true.

    Rejection indicates that at least one population mean differs. It proves neither that all pairs differ nor that C would be greater than each of the others according to separate tests. A causal conclusion about the growing medium also requires an appropriate assignment and measurement design; a small p alone does not provide it. Multiple comparisons will be studied in the next unit.

  8. Report a result that can be checked, with its limits

    We can report together: observed means A = 8 g, B = 10 g, C = 12 g; between-group sum 24 g squared, within-group sum 6 g squared, total 30 g squared; f = 12, degrees of freedom (2, 6), p = 0.008. The means show the size and direction of the observed differences, which p alone does not describe.

    Unit check: if all the masses were converted to another unit using the same factor, both sums of squares would be multiplied by the square of that factor. Their ratio of mean squares, and therefore p, would remain identical. An f with a mass unit would be an error.

    The result is exact for these fictional data and the stated model. With 3 pots per group, we have not empirically established either normality or equality of variances. Repeated weighings of the same pot would not replace independent pots. The calculation alone does not validate the protocol.

Explain it in your own words

Why do we compare mean squares when the question concerns means? Then explain what overall rejection allows us to say and what it leaves open.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The name analysis of variance means that we test whether the variances are equal.”

    Write the null hypothesis: it concerns the population means. A common variance is an assumption of this classical test; the mean squares are used to compare these means.

    Review the model and the null hypothesis

  • “I divide the between-group sum by the within-group sum; that is the F ratio.”

    First divide each sum by its own degrees of freedom. The sums do not involve the same number of free variations. The raw quotient is 4 here; the correct statistic is 12.

    Repair the calculation in the degrees of freedom exercise

  • “A small p proves that each growing medium differs from all the others.”

    The test is overall: it concerns equality of all the means, and rejection indicates at least one difference. Identifying the pairs requires another analysis; a small p does not validate the independence of the pots either.

    Reread the overall conclusion and its limits

Exercise 3 · Interpretation and transfer

A correct result, two claims to examine

We read p = 0.008 and α = 0.05. Someone claims: “C differs from every other growing medium; to repeat the study, I can replace the 3 pots in each group with 3 weighings of the same pot.” Separate the conclusion allowed by the test, the claim about pairs and the problem with the new protocol.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The alternative of the overall test says “at least one mean”.

Hint 2

Does another weighing of the same plant create a new independent plant?

Compare with the solution

Since 0.008 < 0.05, we reject overall equality under the model assumptions. This is not enough to establish separately the difference between C and A, then between C and B. The ranking of the observed means does not provide these tests.

The 3 weighings of a single pot share the same plant and its environment. They provide information about, among other things, the repeatability of the measurement, but do not provide 3 independent experimental units. Reusing N = 9 as if all these numbers came from separate pots would make the calibration of the test unjustified. We need independent pots again or a model suited to related measurements, beyond this unit.

Finally, p does not give the probability that equality is true: it describes a probability of obtaining a ratio at least as extreme if this equality and the model are assumed true.

Check your reasoning

  • State an overall rejection conditional on the assumptions.
  • Leave pairwise comparisons open.
  • Identify the pot as the experimental unit and explain why repeated weighings do not multiply it.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does this ANOVA compare, and what is its null hypothesis?

It compares the population means under the growing media. The null hypothesis states that they are all equal; the alternative says that at least one differs. It does not directly test equality of variances.

Recall 2Which centres do W and B use, and why is T = B + W?

W measures squared deviations of the pots from their group centre. B measures squared deviations of the centres from the grand centre, weighted by sample sizes. The signed residuals sum to 0 within each group, so the cross term disappears when decomposing the squared overall deviation.

Recall 3How do we construct f from the sums of squares?

Divide B by k − 1 and W by N − k, then divide the first mean square by the second. Here f = (24/2)/(6/6) = 12, with no unit and with W strictly positive.

Recall 4Which assumptions give the exact F distribution in our model?

Independent, normal errors with mean 0 and the same strictly positive variance in all groups. The design must provide independent units; repeating a weighing of the same pot does not provide new pots.

Recall 5What does an overall rejection mean, and what does p not tell us?

Under the model assumptions, it leads us to reject equality of all the means at the chosen threshold. It does not tell us which pairs differ. p is a probability of a ratio at least as large under the null hypothesis, rather than the probability that this hypothesis is true.
