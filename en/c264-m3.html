Explanation and causality

Your goalDistinguish a model’s partial effect, which describes a change in prediction, from the causal effect of an action, which requires identification assumptions, using a fictional example where a raw gap of 12 points becomes 4 points after comparing students with the same motivation.

Before you start
c263-m5 : distribution shift · c074-m5 : identification assumptions · c220-m5 : numerical reproducibility · c017-m5 : reproduction protocol · c264-m1 : permutation · c264-m2 : partial effects

✿01 See the idea

A fictional research team observes 200 students. Some used a study app before the final test; the others did not. The team trains a model to predict test scores, then asks it: “If this student used the app, what would you predict?”

A model explanation answers this question about the model, rather than the world. The partial effect of an input is the change in prediction when that input changes while the others stay fixed. It summarizes patterns learned from the data, including those arising from a common cause of the input and the outcome.

Here, motivated students use the app more often and already perform better without it. A model that ignores motivation attributes to the app some of what is due to motivation. A model that includes motivation corrects this mixing of effects, but only if motivation is the only important common cause.

The units on permutation and partial effects provide two tools for explanation. This unit asks what conclusions they support. The data on this page are fictional: they are for calculations, rather than for judging a real app or a teaching method.

The rule to remember

An explanation describes the model. A causal claim must state its assumptions: without them, a partial effect remains an interpretation of a prediction, rather than the effect of an action.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The study app: 12 points or 4 points?

200 fictional students took the final test. T equals 1 if the student used the app and 0 otherwise. Z equals 1 if a fictional questionnaire, administered before use, classifies the student as motivated, and 0 otherwise. Y is the test score, in points. The student count and mean score in each cell are: motivated with the app, 30 students at 70 points; motivated without the app, 30 at 66; unmotivated with the app, 20 at 50; unmotivated without the app, 120 at 46. Let us examine what two minimal models say, then what we can conclude.

  1. Define the symbols and the two models

    Each student has an input T, possibly an input Z, and an observed output Y. Model A receives only T. Model B receives T and Z. Both predict the observed mean of their cell: each is a toy model built from the table, rather than a model trained by optimization.

    fA(t)=moyenne de Y si T=t
    fB(t,z)=moyenne de Y si T=t et Z=z

    Assumption for this analysis: the means in the table are treated as exact. This unit does not calculate estimation uncertainty; that would be essential with real data.

  2. Read the partial effect in model A

    For T = 1, A predicts the mean for the 50 users. Each product of student count × mean gives the total points in a cell; adding these gives the group’s total points; division gives its mean.

    fA(1)=30×70+20×5050=310050=62 points

    For T = 0, follow the same procedure for the 150 nonusers.

    fA(0)=30×66+120×46150=7500150=50 points

    The partial effect of T in A is the difference between the two predictions, expressed in points.

    ΔA=fA(1)−fA(0)=62−50=12 points

    This statement describes A: when input T changes, its prediction changes by 12 points. It does not yet say what would happen to a real student.

  3. Read the partial effect in model B

    B holds Z fixed, so it compares students with the same motivation. For a motivated student, it predicts 70 points with the app and 66 without it: a gap of 4 points. For an unmotivated student, 50 minus 46: a gap of 4 points.

    To summarize this in a single number, have B predict for each of the 200 students in the sample, first setting T to 1, then to 0, while keeping each student’s own Z. Average the predictions: this is the partial dependence of T. It uses the counts of 60 motivated and 140 unmotivated students as weights.

    gB(t)=1n∑ifB(t,Zi) avec n = 200 élèves
    gB(1)=60×70+140×50200=11200200=56 points
    gB(0)=60×66+140×46200=10400200=52 points

    The average partial effect of T in B is therefore 56 minus 52, giving 4 points.

  4. Explain the gap between A and B

    Of the 60 motivated students, 30 use the app: a proportion of 0.5. Of the 140 unmotivated students, 20 use it: 0.143. Use depends on Z, and Z also affects Y: this is confounding. A compares users who are more often motivated with nonusers who are more often unmotivated.

    To separate the two sources, ask B what mean it would predict for the 50 users without the app, keeping each student’s motivation fixed.

    30×66+20×4650=290050=58 points

    Without the app, these users would therefore already score 58 points, compared with 50 for nonusers: a baseline gap of 58 minus 50, giving 8 points. With the app, they score 62 points: their predicted effect is 62 minus 58, giving 4 points. This calculation uses predictions from B: it has a causal meaning only under the assumptions in the next step.

    4+8=12 points : effet et écart de départ recomposent l’écart brut

  5. State the assumptions before saying “cause”

    Interpreting 4 points as the average causal effect of the app requires three assumptions that must be justified beyond the table. One: at a fixed level of motivation, there is no other common cause of use and score, so two students with the same Z are comparable. Two: overlap, meaning that there are students in every cell. Three: stability, meaning that “using the app” means the same thing for everyone and that one student’s use does not change another student’s score.

    The table checks only the second assumption: the four cells contain 30, 30, 20 and 120 students. The first may be false, for example if free time encourages both app use and better performance. If the assumptions hold, the result describes this population; with a different distribution of Z, the average could change when the effect is not the same everywhere.

  6. Check the result

    Check one: the cell effects are 4 and 4 points. They are equal, so any weighted average of these two numbers is 4 points, which is exactly what the partial dependence calculation gave.

    Check two: 4 plus 8 equals 12, the raw gap read from A. The three numbers fit together with no remainder.

    Check three, a limiting case: if the proportion using the app were the same among motivated and unmotivated students, users would have the same composition as nonusers, the baseline gap would be 0, and A would give the same result as B. Assigning T at random aims precisely for this case. Here the proportions are 0.5 and 0.143, so the baseline gap is not 0.

Explain it in your own words

Why does the partial effect in A accurately describe A without proving that the app adds 12 points? What assumption still needs justification to interpret 4 points as a causal effect?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The model predicts 12 more points with the app, so the app adds 12 points.”

    This statement describes A, which does not see Z. Users are more often motivated: 8 of these points are a baseline gap, and only 4 points remain when motivation is held fixed, under the stated assumptions.

    Review the gap between A and B

  • “Adding a variable to the model makes the explanation causal.”

    Moving from A to B reduced the gap from 12 to 4 points, but B says nothing about causes missing from the table. Write down the remaining assumption of no confounding and what would undermine it before discussing an effect.

    Reread the stated assumptions

  • “High permutation importance, or a good prediction, proves that the variable causes the score.”

    Permutation importance measures what the model loses when the link between an input and the output is broken: it reflects patterns useful for prediction, whether causal or not. In A, T is the only input and remains useful even if the gap comes partly from Z.

    Return to the rule to remember

Exercise 1 · Explained application

Two partial effects, two models

Using the example’s data, give the partial effect of T in model B for a motivated student, then for an unmotivated student, then the partial effect of T in model A. Explain in one sentence why A gives a different number. The values are fictional.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

B holds Z fixed: compare the two predictions within the same motivation category.

Hint 2

A does not receive Z: compare the composition of the two groups defined by app use.

Compare with the solution

B, motivated students: 70 − 66 = 4 points. B, unmotivated students: 50 − 46 = 4 points. A: 62 − 50 = 12 points.

A gives a different number because use is more frequent among motivated students (0.5 versus 0.143): A compares groups with different compositions, whereas B compares students with the same motivation. Each effect is still expressed in score points.

Check your reasoning

  • Both effects in B calculated, each within a motivation category.
  • Effect in A calculated from the means of the groups defined by app use.
  • Gap explained by the dependence between use and motivation, without claiming a causal effect.

Exercise 2 · Guided variation

When the effect changes from one cell to another

Suppose the app helps motivated students more: their mean with the app rises to 74 points, with everything else unchanged (a fictional value). Calculate the average partial effect of T in B using the observed student counts, then using equal weights for the two motivation categories. Compare the two results and explain what they say about the chosen population.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate the new effect in the motivated category; the other category keeps its effect of 4 points.

Hint 2

With the observed counts, weight by 60 and 140, then divide by 200. With equal weights, divide the sum of the two effects by 2.

Compare with the solution

Effect for motivated students: 74 − 66 = 8 points. Observed counts: (60 × 8 + 140 × 4) / 200 = (480 + 560) / 200 = 1040 / 200 = 5.2 points.

Equal weights: (8 + 4) / 2 = 12 / 2 = 6 points.

The two results differ because the effect is no longer the same in the two categories: “the average effect” depends on the population over which we average. A different distribution of motivation changes the answer, even if the model stays the same.

Check your reasoning

  • New cell effect calculated before taking any average.
  • Both weighting schemes made explicit, including their final division.
  • Conclusion linked to the population, rather than to a universal property of the app.

Exercise 3 · Reasoned diagnosis

Review a hasty conclusion

A colleague writes: “Model A predicts 12 more points with the app, so its causal effect is 12 points.” Write a verdict with four elements: what this number actually describes; the missing assumption, with numerical evidence from the table; the number you would propose and under which assumptions; a study that would make the assumption more credible. The values are fictional.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare the proportion using the app among motivated and unmotivated students.

Hint 2

Split the raw gap into the effect on users and the baseline gap.

Hint 3

Assigning T at random breaks the link between Z and T: say what this changes.

Compare with the solution

One. The number describes A: the difference in prediction between the two groups defined by app use, namely the raw gap of 12 points. Two. The missing assumption is the absence of confounding: the proportion using the app is 0.5 among motivated students and 0.143 among unmotivated students; without the app, users would already score 58 points compared with 50, giving a baseline gap of 8 points.

Three. If motivation is the only common cause, if there is overlap across cells and if use is stable without interference, B proposes 4 points, with 4 + 8 = 12 reconstructing the raw gap. Four. A study in which use is assigned at random would make T independent of Z and of unmeasured causes; it would also document a protocol precise enough to be repeated.

Even then, statistical uncertainty would still need to be estimated: this unit has not addressed it.

Check your reasoning

  • Number from A presented as a description of the model.
  • Numerical evidence of the dependence between use and motivation.
  • Identification assumptions named before the proposed number.
  • Proposed study linked to the assumption it strengthens, without promising certainty.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the partial effect of an input in a model describe?

The change in prediction when this input varies and the others stay fixed. It describes the model, rather than necessarily what happens after an action in the world.

Recall 2Why does A give 12 points and B 4 points?

Motivation affects both use and score. A does not see it and mixes the effect of 4 points with a baseline gap of 8 points; B compares students with the same motivation.

Recall 3What assumptions allow 4 points to be interpreted as an average causal effect?

No further confounding at a fixed level of motivation, overlap across cells, and stable use without interference between students. These assumptions need justification beyond the table.

Recall 4Why does the population over which we average matter when the effect varies?

The average depends on the weight of each cell. In the variant, it is 5.2 points with the observed counts and 6 points with equal weights.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Name T, Z, Y and the two models; read each partial effect as a change in prediction; explain the gap between 12 and 4 points through confounding; reconstruct 12 as 4 plus 8; state the absence of confounding, overlap and stability before using any causal language; recall that the population over which we average affects the result when the effect varies.
