Level 1 · C024·M3

Distributions

Your goalBuild the distribution of a set of recorded observations, convert counts to relative frequencies, and explain what information grouping loses.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

In a small workshop, you record the time from taking an order until it is ready for collection. At the end of the record, you have a sequence of durations. To see what occurs often, imagine moving each card above the duration written on it: cards with the same value form a stack.

The observed distribution, also called the empirical distribution, describes this pattern: which values appear and how many times each appears. It retains the repetitions. A simple list of distinct values does not tell you how many orders took each duration.

A count tells you how many orders there are. A relative frequency compares this count with all the orders in the record. The diagrams therefore show the same distribution in two ways: dots to count, then shares of the total. The shape stays the same because all the shares use the same denominator.

The record is entirely fictional. Each order has exactly 1 recorded waiting time, rounded to the nearest minute using the same rule. We describe these recorded durations without claiming to recover the actual times in seconds. No order is missing or counted twice in the original record.

The rule to remember

Associate each value with its number of observations, then divide by the total number of observations. Check the counts before interpreting the shape.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From workshop cards to their distribution

Here are the fictional durations in order of arrival of the orders, in minutes: 2; 4; 3; 2; 5; 3; 4; 2; 3; 3. Let us build the complete distribution without replacing these data with a single summary duration.

  1. Define what is being counted

    Here, an observation is the recorded waiting time for an order. N denotes the total number of orders in the record. The letter x denotes a recorded duration, in minutes. n(x), read as “n of x”, is the number of orders whose recorded duration is exactly x. N and n(x) are counts of orders, not minutes.

    All observations have the same weight. Each card must go into exactly one stack, and all cards must be placed. Describing this record does not require us to assume that the orders are independent or randomly selected; those questions matter if we want to extend the conclusion to other orders.

  2. Sort the values and count their repetitions

    The distinct values are 2, 3, 4 and 5 min. Go through the list, crossing out each card after counting it. You find n(2) = 3, n(3) = 4, n(4) = 2 and n(5) = 1 orders.

    Adding these counts must account for all the cards: N = 3 + 4 + 2 + 1 = 10 orders. There are 4 distinct values but 10 observations: these are two different counts.

  3. Convert a count into a share of the record

    f(x), read as “f of x”, denotes the relative frequency of duration x. Dividing n(x) by N answers the question “what share of all orders has this value?” The counting units cancel: f(x) has no unit. The formula assumes that N is strictly greater than 0.

    f(x)=n(x)N

    For the duration 3 min, we divide the number of orders with this duration by the total in the record:

    f(3)=410=0.40

    To express this share as a percentage, multiply the relative frequency by 100: 0.40 × 100 = 40 %. This is another way of writing the same share, without adding any orders.

  4. Complete every row of the distribution

    Duration 2 min: 3 orders; relative frequency 3/10 = 0.30, or 30 %.

    Duration 3 min: 4 orders; relative frequency 4/10 = 0.40, or 40 %.

    Duration 4 min: 2 orders; relative frequency 2/10 = 0.20, or 20 %.

    Duration 5 min: 1 order; relative frequency 1/10 = 0.10, or 10 %.

    These associations between durations and relative frequencies form the observed distribution. Keep each value beside its share: listing percentages alone would lose what they describe.

  5. Check using both the cards and the shares

    First check: count the original list again directly. Its 10 cards do match the sum of the counts. The stacks of short durations contain 7 cards, and the others contain 3; 7 + 3 = 10.

    Second check: all shares have the same denominator and cover the record without overlap. Their sum must therefore be the whole record:

    0.30+0.40+0.20+0.10=1

    Similarly, 30 % + 40 % + 20 % + 10 % = 100 %. Each relative frequency lies between 0 and 1. Here the decimals are exact; in general, rounded relative frequencies can slightly change the displayed sum. Check using exact fractions first.

    A correct sum does not detect every error: swapping the counts of two durations would preserve the total. Returning to the cards also lets you check that each count is associated with the right value.

  6. State what the shape lets you conclude

    The tallest stack is at 3 min: this recorded duration appears more often than each of the others. Yet it accounts for only 40 % of orders. A majority would require more than 50 % of orders, meaning more than half. “The most frequent” therefore does not mean “the duration of the majority of orders”.

    The diagram shows durations from 2 to 5 min with unequal numbers of repetitions. It describes the current record. It does not guarantee the duration of the next order or an identical distribution at another time.

    Finally, arranging cards by duration removes their order of arrival. Another sequence of the same cards would give the same distribution. To find out whether waiting times increase as work proceeds, you would also need to retain and examine that order. Summaries of centre and spread will come in the next unit.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results, rebuild the stacks from the record, and explain why the denominator counts orders. Then say what you lost by replacing the ordered list with these stacks.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I divide by the number of distinct durations.”

    The 4 distinct values name the stacks. To measure a share of orders, the reference is the full set of 10 cards. For 3 min, the calculation is 4/10 = 0.40, or 40 %.

    Recall the meaning of N and f(x)

  • “The most frequent duration must account for the majority.”

    Compare its share with 50 % of all cards, not just with each other stack. Here 3 min is the most frequent value at 40 %, but orders with the other durations are more numerous when combined.

    Compare the tallest stack with all the others combined

  • “A broad group retains the details of the durations it contains.”

    A group can contain several durations. Its count gives only their total. Write down the group boundaries, then ask whether a card could change duration within the group without changing that total: if so, the detail is lost.

    Examine the intervals in the grouping exercise

Exceeding a given duration

The card with the longest duration has been removed

Hint 1

The number of cards at 3 min stays the same. Which set should the new denominator count?

Hint 2

Subtract the count for the missing duration from the original total. Keep an exact fraction before rounding the percentage.

Compare with the solution

The copy contains 10 − 1 = 9 orders. Its 4 cards at 3 min therefore represent 4/9 of the new total. This is approximately 44.44 %, instead of 40 % in the original record.

To check, count the remaining stacks again: 3 + 4 + 2 = 9. The numerator has not changed; the set of cards described by the copy has become smaller.

The calculation with the new denominator correctly describes this incomplete copy. It does not repair the loss of information. To describe the original record, you must restore the known card and recover the total 10, or explicitly state that you are presenting only a partial copy. Removing a long duration changes the distribution shown.

Check your reasoning

  • You keep 4 as the numerator and obtain 9 as the denominator.
  • You distinguish the exact fraction 4/9 from the rounded percentage 44.44 %.
  • You state which record is being described and why a correct calculation does not reconstruct a lost card.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and limits of information

Are two intervals enough?

Start again from the complete original record. To present it more briefly, replace the exact durations with the groups [2, 4) and [4, 6) min. The notation [a, b) means “at least a and strictly less than b”, where a and b are boundaries in minutes. Calculate the count and relative frequency of each group. Where does the duration 4 min belong? Someone then keeps only these groups: can they recover the exact share of the duration 3 min?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Each card must belong to exactly one group. Read the shared boundary 4 min carefully.

Hint 2

The lower group combines the stacks at 2 and 3 min. Adding their counts is enough to obtain its count.

Hint 3

In the grouped summary, would moving a card from 2 to 3 min change the total of the first group?

Compare with the solution

The group [2, 4) contains the durations 2 and 3 min: 3 + 4 = 7 orders, representing 7/10 of the record, or 70 %.

The group [4, 6) contains the durations 4 and 5 min: 2 + 1 = 3 orders, representing 3/10, or 30 %. The duration 4 min belongs only to the second group: the right boundary of the first is excluded.

Check: 7 + 3 = 10 and 0.70 + 0.30 = 1. You can also add the detailed relative frequencies: 0.70 for the first group and 0.30 for the second.

With only the grouped summary, the share of 3 min can no longer be recovered exactly. The lower group combines this duration with 2 min. Changing the distribution within it would preserve its 7 cards. The original record let us calculate 40 %; that detail comes from the original cards or stacks, not from the groups alone.

Check your reasoning

  • You place the shared boundary 4 min in the second group only.
  • You obtain 7 and 3 orders, with 70 % and 30 %.
  • You check that the groups cover all cards without counting them twice.
  • You explain the loss of detail within a group without claiming that each duration in it has the same relative frequency.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must you retain to describe an observed distribution, beyond the list of distinct values?

You must associate each value with its count or relative frequency. Repetitions are part of the distribution; knowing only the values does not let you recover them.

Recall 2How do you go from n(x) to f(x), and what does the denominator count?

f(x) = n(x)/N, with N strictly positive. N counts all observations in the record being described, not the distinct values. Relative frequency has no unit; multiplying by 100 expresses it as a percentage.

Recall 3What checks should you make on counts and relative frequencies? Is a correct sum enough?

The sum of the counts must equal N, and the sum of the exact relative frequencies must equal 1. Each card is counted once. A correct sum does not detect counts being swapped between values: you must also return to the record.

Recall 4What do you lose by combining several durations into the same interval?

You retain the group total but no longer know how that total is distributed among its durations. The boundaries must place each observation in exactly one group.

Recall 5What can you not infer from the distribution of our orders?

It does not recover the sequence of orders or guarantee the duration of the next order or the distribution of another record. The most frequent value can also account for less than 50 % of orders.
