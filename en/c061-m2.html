Level 2 · C061·M2

MLE

Your goalChoose a maximum likelihood estimate, prove that it gives the maximum, and check that it lies in the allowed domain.

Before you start
c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c059-m6 : deterministic discrete Bellman · c061-m1 : likelihood

✿01 See the idea

A ticket machine is tested under the same conditions. We decided in advance to press the button 4 times. The resulting record is: ticket printed, ticket printed, failure, ticket printed. All these data are fictional; they serve to construct an estimate and do not describe a real machine.

Imagine a dial that represents the probability of printing correctly each time the button is pressed. A setting very close to 0 makes the successes hard to explain; a setting very close to 1 makes the failure hard to explain. We seek the setting that assigns the highest probability to this entire record.

Maximum likelihood estimation, abbreviated MLE in the title, chooses this setting from those in the model. The observations stay fixed during the search. We vary the candidate probability, rather than changing the record to make it look better.

The diagrams distinguish the candidate setting p from the score L of the record. The first quantity is what we seek; the second lets us compare candidates. A large value of L does not give the probability that p is the true value.

The rule to remember

Fix the observations and the model; find the parameter that maximizes their likelihood over the allowed domain; check the maximum and the boundaries.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Estimate the printing probability

The fictional record D = (1, 1, 0, 1) contains n = 4 button presses and s = 3 successes. The allowed domain for p is the closed interval [0 ; 1]. We seek the maximum over this entire interval.

  1. Define the model and each quantity

    Before the test, Xᵢ denotes the random outcome of button press i, where i runs from 1 to n. After the test, xᵢ denotes its observed value: 1 if a ticket is printed correctly, 0 otherwise. D is the sequence of the xᵢ in order. The Bernoulli model is precisely this model with these two outcomes, with probability p of success and 1 − p of failure.

    We assume independent button presses and the same probability p for each. The number of presses is fixed in advance, the outcomes are correctly classified, and no failure is removed. A shared fault or wear during the test would require us to reconsider this model.

    n is the number of button presses; s is the sum of the xᵢ, hence the number of successes; f = n − s is the number of failures. Here f = 4 − 3 = 1. These counts count events. The codes xᵢ, p, its estimate, and the probabilities have no physical unit. p is an unknown parameter; the scene does not supply its true value.

  2. Write down what we maximize

    L(p ; D) is the likelihood: the probability of the record D under the candidate p, considered as a function of p. Independence allows us to multiply the probabilities of the observed outcomes. Each success contributes p; each failure contributes 1 − p.

    L(p;D)=ps(1−p)f=p3(1−p)

    We use the exact sequence, rather than the broader event “obtain s successes in n button presses in any order”. There is therefore no factor counting the possible orders to add to this probability.

    p^(D)∈arg maxp∈[0;1]L(p;D)

    The hat indicates an estimate. “arg max” denotes the set of parameters that give the greatest value; the symbol ∈ means “belongs to”. The rule D ↦ p̂(D) is an estimator; its value for our record will be the estimate. The maximum L(p̂ ; D) is the resulting score, rather than the selected parameter.

  3. Take the logarithm to simplify the product

    Inside the interval, 0 < p < 1, all factors are positive. The natural logarithm, written ln, turns a product into a sum and strictly preserves the order of positive numbers. Maximizing L is therefore equivalent to maximizing ℓ = ln L, called the log-likelihood, in this interior.

    ℓ(p)=sln(p)+fln(1−p)

    ℓ is dimensionless, as the logarithm of this probability. Its derivative ℓ′ gives its slope as p varies. The derivative of ln p is 1/p. That of ln(1 − p) is −1/(1 − p), because the inner factor decreases with p. The counts s and f stay fixed during this operation.

    ℓ′(p)=sp−f1−p=s−npp(1−p)

    For the last equality, the common numerator is s(1 − p) − fp = s − (s + f)p = s − np. This explains why the total number n appears; forgetting the failures would change the function to optimize.

  4. Find the candidate and prove that it gives the maximum

    The denominator p(1 − p) is strictly positive in the interior. The slope is therefore 0 exactly when s − np = 0. Since n is positive, solving this equation gives:

    p^=sn=34=0.75

    For p < 0.75, s − np is positive: ℓ increases. For p > 0.75, it is negative: ℓ decreases. The candidate is therefore the unique interior maximum. This sign analysis is the proof; setting the derivative to 0 alone would not suffice.

    The boundaries remain to be checked. At p = 0, the observed successes have 0 probability; at p = 1, the observed failure is impossible. L is therefore 0 at both boundaries, whereas it is positive at the candidate. We do have the global maximum over the closed interval.

    If all button presses succeeded, there would be no failure factor: L = p to the power n, with its maximum at p = 1. If all failed, L = (1 − p) to the power n, with its maximum at p = 0. These boundaries must be handled directly in L, without taking the logarithm of 0. With no trials, no parameter would be preferred over another.

  5. Calculate the exact score of the selected setting

    Let q = 1 − p name the failure probability. At the selected setting: q = 1 − 3/4 = 1/4 = 0.25, dimensionless. For the successes, multiply p by itself: 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16, then 9/16 × 3/4 = 27/64. The failure factor must still be applied.

    L(p^;D)=34×34×14×34=27/256

    Here the product follows the order of the record. Grouping the successes gives 27/64 × 1/4 = 27/256 ≈ 0.1055, dimensionless. The estimate is 0.75; the score for this exact sequence is approximately 0.1055. They answer different questions.

  6. Check against another candidate and review the conclusion

    The comparison candidate is p = 1/2 = 0.50. Its failure probability is also 1/2. Its successive products give 1/4, then 1/8, then L = 1/16 = 0.0625. The difference 27/256 − 1/16 = 11/256 is positive. This confirms a concrete comparison, supplementing the proof over the whole interval.

    Also check that the derivative is 0 as claimed: np̂ = 4 × 3/4 = 3; s − np̂ = 0; p̂(1 − p̂) = 3/16. Their quotient does give ℓ′(p̂) = 0. This algebraic check does not replace the sign and boundary analysis.

    Conclusion: in our model of independent button presses with a constant probability, the maximum likelihood estimate is the observed frequency 0.75. This agreement comes from the Bernoulli model; an MLE is not always a frequency. We have proved neither the true parameter value nor the quality of future predictions. ✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Maximum likelihood is the probability that my estimate is true.”

    Distinguish p̂ = 0.75, the selected setting, from L(p̂ ; D) = 27/256, the score of the record under that setting. Neither result calculates a probability that the true value of p equals the estimate. A distribution over the parameter would require a different construction.

    Review what arg max, estimate, and score refer to

  • “The larger p is, the better the successes are explained; I therefore take p equal to 1.”

    Use the entire record, including the failure. Its probability is 1 − p and becomes 0 at p = 1. The full product is then 0. If the button presses depend on one another, the factorization must also be reconsidered, rather than multiplying without an assumption.

    Count all the ticket machine outcomes again

  • “A 0 derivative, or the best of my trials, always suffices to prove the MLE.”

    A 0 derivative supplies an interior candidate. Check its domain, whether the function increases or decreases, and the boundaries. The last exercise imposes a bound: the maximum is reached with a positive derivative, because going further is forbidden.

    Try the restricted domain in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Another setting for the same record

Use D = (1, 1, 0, 1) again. Calculate the exact likelihood for p = 0.50, then its difference from that for p̂ = 0.75. Does this comparison alone suffice to prove the maximum over the entire domain?

Hint 1

At p = 0.50, success and failure have the same probability; keep a factor for each button press.

Hint 2

A comparison between candidates says nothing about all the others. Identify what the derivative added to the proof.

Compare with the solution

The product of the 4 factors first gives 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, then 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8, and finally 1/8 × 1/2 = 1/16 = 0.0625, dimensionless.

The score difference is 27/256 − 1/16 = 11/256 > 0. The setting 0.75 is better than 0.50 for this record, but this comparison alone does not rule out another, better candidate. The sign analysis over the interval and the boundary checks do rule this out.

Check your reasoning

  • The product uses the 4 outcomes, including the failure.
  • The exact score is 1/16 and the difference is 11/256.
  • The local comparison is distinguished from a global proof.

Exercise 2 · Reconstructing the calculation

An additional button press fails

We had planned an additional button press on the same ticket machine, under the same conditions. It fails. From the full record, find n, s, and f, write the new likelihood, then prove the new MLE. Do not keep the previous total.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The number of successes does not change; the total and the number of failures each increase by 1.

Hint 2

Use the new n in the numerator s − np of the derivative. Then check the boundaries.

Compare with the solution

The record now has n = 5 button presses, s = 3 successes, and f = 2 failures. The product is L(p ; D) = p to the power 3 × (1 − p) to the power 2. Keeping f = 1 would describe the old record.

The derivative of the log-likelihood has numerator 3 − 5p and positive denominator p(1 − p). It is positive before p = 3/5 = 0.60, 0 at this value, and negative after it. Both boundaries still give a 0 score because both successes and failures are present. The new estimate is therefore 3/5, or 0.60, dimensionless.

Check: 5 × 3/5 = 3, the number of successes; the estimate has decreased after the additional failure. This recalculation still rests on a common probability and independent button presses. If the conditions have changed, combining the trials under the same p requires justification.

Check your reasoning

  • The counts become 5, 3, and 2.
  • The exponents and the denominator of the frequency use the new counts.
  • The maximum 0.60 is justified by the increase and decrease of the function and the boundaries, then interpreted under the assumptions.

Exercise 3 · Transfer under a constraint

The model no longer allows the whole interval

Return to the initial record of 4 button presses and 3 successes. For a mathematical comparison, the candidate model now imposes 0 ≤ p ≤ 0.50. This upper bound is a fictional constraint, rather than a measured property of the ticket machine. Find the MLE in this domain. Must the derivative be 0 at the selected parameter?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First check whether 0.75 is allowed. The observations are unchanged, but the set of candidates has changed.

Hint 2

Over this new interval, compare 3 − 4p with its value at the upper bound; the denominator remains positive for p > 0.

Compare with the solution

The unconstrained candidate 0.75 exceeds the upper bound 0.50: it is forbidden. For 0 < p ≤ 0.50, the numerator satisfies 3 − 4p ≥ 3 − 2 = 1 > 0. The log-likelihood therefore increases over this whole interval; L increases too.

At the left boundary, L is 0; at the upper bound, L = 1/16 > 0. The constrained maximum is therefore reached at p = 0.50. The derivative at the upper bound is not 0: its numerator is 1, its denominator is 1/4, so ℓ′ = 4. This does not contradict the maximum: increasing p would leave the domain.

The same data thus give an unconstrained estimate 0.75 and a constrained estimate 0.50. The domain is part of the definition of the estimate; it is not added after an optimum has been announced.

Check your reasoning

  • The unconstrained candidate is explicitly ruled out because it exceeds the allowed domain.
  • The sign is analyzed over the whole interval, rather than only at the upper bound.
  • The boundary maximum 0.50 and the derivative 4 are compatible and explained.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What stays fixed and what varies when searching for an MLE?

The observed record D and the model stay fixed. We vary the candidate parameter within its domain, then choose a value that gives the maximum of L(p ; D). The selected parameter and the maximum score are distinct.

Recall 2Why does the ticket machine record give p to the power s × (1 − p) to the power f?

Each success contributes a probability p, each failure a probability 1 − p. Assuming independent button presses allows us to multiply these factors. s counts successes, f counts failures; their sum is n. Without independence or a common p, this product must be reconsidered.

Recall 3Why can we seek the maximum of the logarithm, and where is care needed?

The logarithm is strictly increasing over positive numbers: it preserves order and turns the product into a sum. We work in the interior where L is positive, then handle the boundaries directly in L to avoid the logarithm of 0.

Recall 4How do we move from the derivative to a proof of a maximum for the ticket machine?

In the interior, the derivative of ln L has the sign of s − np. With both successes and failures present, it changes from positive to negative at p = s/n. We then check the boundaries and membership in the domain. A constraint can place the maximum at a boundary where the derivative is not 0.

Recall 5What does p̂ = 0.75 mean, and what does this result fail to prove?

This candidate best explains the record in the independent model with a common probability over [0 ; 1]. It proves neither that the true probability is 0.75, nor that the model correctly describes the machine, nor any future performance.

What you understand
A frequency can coincide with a maximum likelihood estimate under a specific model. Calculating this frequency, evaluating a score, and proving an optimum are distinct actions. The ticket machine scene serves only to explain them.
