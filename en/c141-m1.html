Languages

Your goalDefine a command language, decide whether a word belongs to it, and count its words of a given length.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

At the control panel of a demonstration cart, you prepare a tape of letters. A instructs the cart to advance, T to turn, and V marks the end of the command. The panel must accept ATV but reject TVA: a movement letter comes after the announced end. Knowing the available letters is therefore not enough; we also need to specify how they are arranged.

In this fictional scene, a command contains a finite sequence of A and T, possibly empty, followed by V. The letters are uppercase; the tapes contain no spaces or other characters. Repetition is allowed. V alone is an allowed command: it requests no movement. We set no maximum length in this model.

An alphabet is the finite set of available symbols. A word is a finite ordered sequence of these symbols; it can repeat a letter. A language is a set of words chosen over this alphabet. Here, the language contains exactly the tapes that follow our rule for ending a command.

We study the form of commands, called their syntax. Accepting a tape does not show that the cart has moved or that the route is feasible. The letters and counts below belong to an invented example, with no measurements from a real machine.

The rule to remember

Specify the alphabet, then the rule for the whole word. A word made from the right letters can still fall outside the language.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Describe and count the cart commands

We want all allowed tapes of length 3 symbols, then all those whose length does not exceed this bound. The marker V counts towards the length. Movement choices are unrestricted and can repeat.

  1. Distinguish an alphabet, a word and a set of words

    Let Σ, the Greek letter sigma, denote the alphabet {A, T, V}. It contains 3 symbols: 2 movement symbols and 1 final marker. Γ, the Greek letter gamma, denotes the subalphabet {A, T}. Braces denote a set; changing the order in which its elements are written does not change that set. By contrast, the words AT and TA are different because the order of symbols matters.

    For a word w, |w| denotes its length, meaning its number of symbols, counted here in symbols with no physical unit. ε, pronounced epsilon, denotes the empty word: |ε| = 0. ε is notation for the absence of symbols on the tape, not an extra letter in Σ.

    For an integer n greater than or equal to 0, Σⁿ is the set of all words of length n over Σ. Σ* is the set of all finite words over Σ, including ε. The star imposes no bound on length. We define Γⁿ and Γ* in the same way using only the symbols A and T. In particular, Γ to the power 0 contains only ε.

    Γ0={ε}≠∅

    ∅ denotes the empty set, which contains 0 words. The set {ε} contains 1 word, of length 0. We must therefore distinguish a word, its length and a set of words.

  2. Write the rule for the whole word

    A prefix is an initial part of a word; here, u will be the sequence of movements before V. Placing u and V side by side means writing V after u, with no separator. The symbol ∈ means “belongs to” and ⊆ means “is a subset of”. A language over Σ is a subset of Σ*. Our language L is written as follows:

    L={uV|u∈Γ*}⊆Σ*

    Inside the braces, the bar is read as “such that”. This definition says: take any finite word u made of A and T, then append V. Thus, ATV belongs to L. AVV is a word over Σ, but it does not belong to L: after removing the last V, its prefix AV still contains V, which is forbidden in Γ. The symbol ∉ expresses this non-membership.

    The prefix u can be ε. The word V therefore belongs to L. However, ε does not belong to L because the final marker is missing. The rule checks the whole sequence; finding an accepted piece inside a tape is not enough.

  3. Concatenate, then check length and order

    Concatenation joins words while preserving their order. Take u = AT and v = V. Their lengths are 2 and 1 symbols respectively. In uv, we count all the symbols of u, then all those of v:

    uv=ATV;|uv|=|u|+|v|=2+1=3

    The result ATV is in L. Swapping the blocks gives vu = VAT, which keeps the same length but falls outside L: V is placed before the movements. Concatenation is therefore not commutative in general. The length formula remains valid in both orders; it is not enough to test the language rule.

    Joining the empty word changes no symbols: εw = wε = w for any word w. This is why the empty prefix case gives V without adding an epsilon letter to the tape.

  4. Count the words of the requested length

    Ln will denote the set of words in L of length n. N(n) = |Ln| is their number, counted in distinct commands. Bars around a set denote its number of elements; around a word, they denote its length. For n at least equal to 1, the last symbol must be V: this leaves n − 1 free positions, each reserved for A or T.

    With n = 3, remove the place for the marker: 3 − 1 = 2 positions. The first choice is A or T. For each choice, the next is again A or T: multiplication counts all pairs, giving 2 × 2 = 4 prefixes. Since the final V is required, it contributes a factor of 1.

    N(3)=22×1=4 commandes

    Check by another method: the complete list is AAV, ATV, TAV, TTV. Each word has the right length and a single V at the end. There are no omissions or duplicates: removing the last V recovers exactly one of the prefixes AA, AT, TA, TT.

    More generally, N(n) = 2 to the power (n − 1) for n at least equal to 1. Each free position provides the same number of choices, with no dependence between positions. For n = 0, this formula does not apply: N(0) = 0, because ε is rejected. This is a count, not a probability or a performance measurement.

  5. Distinguish a length from a bound

    Counting words of length at most 3 requires adding classes of different lengths. At length 0, no word is accepted. At length 1, only V is accepted, giving 1 word. At length 2, there are AV and TV, giving 2 words. These shorter lengths give 1 + 2 = 3 words, to which we add the 4 words already counted.

    3+4=7 commandes de longueur au plus 3

    Check: V, AV, TV, AAV, ATV, TAV, TTV are all distinct. We add because a word cannot have different lengths at the same time. This total describes a finite part of the language, not the whole language.

    L is infinite in our model: we can always add another A before V and obtain a new accepted word of a different length. All these words remain individually finite. A hardware limit on length would change the model; none has been assumed here.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the lists. Reconstruct the rule for L, explain why V is accepted but ε is rejected, then recover the number of words of the requested length. Justify separately the product of choices and the sum over lengths.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If every letter is allowed, the word is accepted.”

    AVV uses only Σ and has the right length, but its prefix before the last V contains a V. Check the rule for the whole word after checking the symbols.

    Reread the definition of L and the counterexample AVV

  • “The empty word and the empty language are the same thing.”

    ε is a word of length 0. ∅ is a set containing 0 words; {ε} contains 1. In our language, the prefix can be empty, but the command still contains V.

    Review the objects and the empty prefix case

  • “Changing the order does not matter if the total length stays the same.”

    Adding lengths does not record the order of letters. AT and TA have the same length and are different words. With the blocks AT and V, ATV is accepted while VAT is rejected: the same total length, different membership.

    Test the effect of joining commands

Exercise 1 · Identification and justification

Read the whole tape

Classify V, ATV, TVA, AVV and ε by their membership in L. For each rejection, state precisely which rule is violated. Are all these objects words in Σ*?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Remove the last V when it is present. The remaining prefix must contain only A and T.

Hint 2

Σ* contains all finite words made with the available letters, including the empty word. L imposes an additional condition.

Compare with the solution

V belongs to L: its prefix is ε. ATV belongs to L: its prefix AT consists of movements. TVA is rejected because its last symbol is A. AVV is rejected: removing its last V leaves AV, which still contains the final marker. ε is rejected because it has no final V.

All are nevertheless in Σ*: their symbols belong to Σ, and Σ* also contains ε. Belonging to Σ* does not mean belonging to L. The lengths of V, ATV and ε are 1, 3 and 0 symbols respectively.

Check your reasoning

  • You accept V and ATV, then justify each rejection using the ending or the prefix.
  • You distinguish Σ* from L and do not invent an epsilon letter.

Exercise 2 · Counting with a check

Lengthen the tape

Keeping L unchanged, move to a length of 4 symbols. State the number of commands before listing them. Justify each factor, then check the result with a list containing no duplicates.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Reserve the last position for V: this leaves 4 − 1 movement positions.

Hint 2

You can add A or T before each command in the list of length 3. This constructs each new command exactly once.

Compare with the solution

The length increases by 1: 3 + 1 = 4 symbols. The prefix therefore has 4 − 1 = 3 positions, each with 2 choices. The final V still provides 1 choice.

N(4)=23×1=8 commandes

Another calculation: adding A or T before each of the 4 previous commands gives 4 × 2 = 8 commands. The list is AAAV, AATV, ATAV, ATTV, TAAV, TATV, TTAV, TTTV. Removing the first letter of each word recovers a command from the previous list; its first letter distinguishes the constructions.

This count is for the exact length 4. It does not include shorter words and does not count the entire infinite language.

Check your reasoning

  • You reserve the place for V before multiplying the choices.
  • You find 8 commands and check their length, ending and distinctness.
  • You explain why prefixing with A or T creates no omissions or duplicates.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and repair

Is joining commands enough?

Someone claims: “AV and TV are accepted, so joining them gives another accepted command.” Examine AVTV. Then repair the join to preserve the movements A followed by T in a command of L. Does the repair prove that the cart has carried out these movements?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Find the prefix before the last V in AVTV.

Hint 2

In each command, separate the movements from the end marker; construct a new command with a single ending.

Compare with the solution

AV and TV do belong to L. Their concatenation is AVTV, of length 4 symbols. Its prefix AVT contains a V: the result is not in L. This example is enough to refute the claim “always accepted”; adding lengths does not test this property.

To construct a command corresponding to the intended movements, keep the prefix A from AV and the prefix T from TV, join them in that order, then append V: you obtain ATV, of length 3 symbols. This is a new construction. The direct join AVTV remains rejected.

The repaired word satisfies the syntax. No actual movement has been observed. Even a well-formed description leaves its interpretation, route and execution to be checked.

Check your reasoning

  • You provide the counterexample AVTV and locate the forbidden V in its prefix.
  • You construct ATV and explain how you handle the end markers.
  • You distinguish language membership from the execution of a command by the cart.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the difference between Σ, ATV and L?

Σ = {A, T, V} is the alphabet. ATV is a word, an ordered sequence of symbols. L is the set of words whose prefix uses only A and T and that end in V.

Recall 2Why are ε, ∅ and {ε} different objects? Where does ε occur in our example?

ε is a word of length 0, ∅ is a set with no words, and {ε} is a set containing 1 word. ε belongs to Γ* and Σ*, but not to L. It can be the empty prefix of the command V.

Recall 3What does concatenation preserve, and what does swapping AT and V show?

It preserves the order of symbols within each block and adds the lengths. AT followed by V gives ATV; V followed by AT gives VAT. The lengths are equal, but only the first word is in L.

Recall 4Recover the number of commands of length n, then explain the case n = 0.

For n at least equal to 1, the ending V is required and the other n − 1 positions each offer 2 choices: N(n) = 2 to the power (n − 1). At length 0, there are no words in L: the final marker is missing.

Recall 5How can L be infinite when each of its words is finite?

There is no common bound on lengths: adding A before a command produces a longer accepted word. Each tape has a finite length, but this construction gives new distinct words without end.
