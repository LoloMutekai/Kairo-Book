Level 4 · C253·M1

Leverage function

Your goalCalculate, at a given spot and time, the leverage that links Dupire local volatility to the mean stochastic variance, then check that L² × E[v | S] recovers the Dupire variance.

Before you start
c252-m5 : model risk · c239-m5 : calibration and identifiability · c182-m5 : trinomial tree and admissibility · c220-m5 : numerical reproducibility · c198-m5 : independent validation

✿01 See the idea

A fictional foreign exchange desk tracks the euro against the dollar. To price its options, it has two descriptions of volatility. Dupire local volatility, introduced in C182·M5, assigns an instantaneous volatility σl(S, t) to each spot S and time t, constructed to reproduce the surface of vanilla options. A stochastic volatility model, such as those in C239·M5, instead lets the instantaneous variance v evolve randomly.

The local-stochastic volatility model retains both ideas. Its instantaneous volatility is the product of √v, the random factor, and a predetermined function L(S, t): the leverage function. Leverage increases the random factor where the Dupire surface requires it and reduces it elsewhere. It is dimensionless, because it multiplies a volatility to obtain a volatility.

The question in this unit is specific: at a point (S, t), what leverage makes the instantaneous model variance, L² v, equal to the Dupire value σl² on average? The average includes only paths where the spot equals S at time t. This is a conditional expectation, written E[v | S]. Its estimation is covered in the next unit; here it is given, to isolate the role of leverage.

All values on this page are fictional. They serve to calculate a single point carefully; they describe neither an observed market surface nor a validated calibration.

The rule to remember

Leverage is the local volatility divided by the square root of the mean stochastic variance, conditional on the same spot; check it by verifying that L² × E[v | S] recovers σₗ².

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Leverage at a point (S, t)

Fictional values. For the euro against the dollar, the spot is S = 1.10 dollars per euro and the horizon is t = 0.25 years. The Dupire surface gives σₗ = 12 % per year. The stochastic model, whose dynamics are already fixed, gives a square root of the mean variance of m = 15 % per year at this point. What leverage L is needed, and how can it be checked?

  1. Define the quantities, their units and the assumptions

    S is the spot, in dollars per euro; t is time, in years. σl(S, t) is Dupire local volatility, in percent per year; its square σl² is a variance, in percent squared per year. v is the variance factor of the stochastic model, in the same unit. E[v | S] is its mean, calculated only over paths where the spot equals S at time t. m is its square root. L(S, t) is the dimensionless leverage.

    Assumptions: both models describe the same spot; v remains strictly positive, so m does too; L is taken to be positive; the dynamics of v are already calibrated, as in C239·M5, and provide E[v | S]; we work at a single point, rather than across the entire surface. If any of these conditions fails, the formula below must be reconsidered.

  2. Write the consistency condition

    The instantaneous variance of the local-stochastic volatility model is L² v. At the point (S, t), L is a number, so it can be taken outside the average: the conditional mean is L² E[v | S]. Calibration requires it to equal the Dupire variance.

    σl2=L2×E[v|S]

  3. Solve for the leverage

    Dividing by E[v | S] and then taking the positive square root, which requires E[v | S] to be strictly greater than 0, gives leverage as the ratio of two volatilities.

    L=σlE[v|S]

    The denominator is the square root of a variance: it is a volatility, in percent per year. The ratio is therefore dimensionless.

  4. Convert the square root to the mean variance

    The data give m, rather than E[v | S]. We recover the mean variance by squaring m, so that we can later check the result in variance units.

    E[v|S]=m2=152=225 %²

    The Dupire variance is likewise 12² = 144 %².

  5. Calculate the leverage

    L=1215=4/5=0.80

    Both volatilities are in percent per year: division cancels their units. The exact fraction is 4/5; its decimal form is 0.80. This leverage is less than 1.

  6. Check by reconstructing the Dupire variance

    A check must use a different route from the calculation. We square the leverage and multiply it by the mean variance.

    L2×E[v|S]=16/25×225=144=σl2

    The result is 144 %², which is σl²: the leverage correctly reconstructs the Dupire variance.

  7. Check using two variance scenarios

    At the same spot, imagine two equally likely values of v: 125 and 325 %². Their mean is 225 %², equal to the mean variance from the previous step.

    125+3252=225

    Multiply each scenario by L² = 16/25. We obtain 80 and 208 %², with a mean of 144 %², which is σl². Multiplying each scenario by the same factor also multiplies their mean by that factor: this check is algebraic, as the second figure shows. It does not prove that these two scenarios are realistic.

  8. Interpret the result within its limits

    Leverage below 1 means that, at this point, the random factor √v is stronger on average than Dupire volatility: the model attenuates it. Leverage above 1 would do the reverse.

    This value applies to a given point, given dynamics of v and a given conditional mean. It says nothing about the rest of the surface, the cost of calibration or the stability of the calculation.

Explain it in your own words

Why do we average v only over paths where the spot equals S? And why does the check use the square of the leverage rather than the leverage itself?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Leverage is Dupire volatility divided by E[v | S].”

    E[v | S] is a variance, whereas σₗ is a volatility: divide by its square root m. Using the variance would give a quotient of 0.05, and the check would fail, far from σₗ².

    Review the rule to remember

  • “To check, simply multiply the leverage by E[v | S].”

    The condition uses the square of the leverage. The product L × E[v | S] would give 180 %², which is not σₗ² = 144 %². Check with L², then compare with the Dupire variance.

    Repeat the check in the example

  • “Leverage is a market input, the same for every variance model.”

    It depends both on σₗ and on the conditional mean provided by the chosen dynamics of v. For the same σₗ, a different mean changes the leverage, as in the second exercise.

    Go to the exercises

Exercise 1 · Application

A second spot

The same fictional setting, at another point: the spot is 1.05 dollars per euro, at the same time. Dupire volatility is 15 % per year and the square root of the mean stochastic variance is 12 % per year. Calculate the leverage, check it by recovering the Dupire variance, then state whether it is below or above 1.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Divide the volatility by the square root, rather than by the variance.

Hint 2

For the check, square the leverage and multiply by the mean variance, which is the square of the given root.

Compare with the solution

Leverage: 15 / 12 = 5/4 = 1.25. Check: the mean variance is 12² = 144 %² and L² = 25/16. The product is 225 %², equal to 15² = 225 %².

Leverage exceeds 1: here, the square root of the mean stochastic variance is lower than Dupire volatility, so leverage amplifies the random factor. This interpretation applies only to this point.

Check your reasoning

  • Division of two volatilities, giving a dimensionless result.
  • Check using the square of the leverage and the mean variance.
  • Interpretation limited to this point.

Exercise 2 · Diagnostic

The wrong mean

At the first point, a colleague replaces the conditional mean with the mean of v over all paths. The colleague obtains 400 %², with a square root of 20 % per year, and keeps σₗ = 12 % per year. What leverage does this give? Does it pass the check against the conditional mean 225 %² at the first point? What do you conclude?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Apply the same formula as in the example with a different square root.

Hint 2

Check against the conditional mean in the example, rather than the mean the colleague assumed.

Compare with the solution

The colleague obtains leverage of 12 / 20 = 3/5 = 0.60, instead of 4/5 in the example. Against the conditional mean, 9/25 × 225 = 81 %², which is not 144 %²: the check fails.

Note: against the colleague's own mean, 9/25 × 400 = 144 %², so the calculation is internally consistent. A check that reuses the assumption being tested does not detect the error. For the same σl, the chosen mean changes the leverage: it cannot be treated as a market input.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish the conditional mean from the mean over all paths.
  • Compare two leverage values for the same Dupire volatility.
  • Avoid validating a calculation with the assumption it uses.

Exercise 3 · Scenario check

Two scenarios at the second point

At the second spot, the stochastic variance takes two equally likely values conditional on the spot: 100 and 188 %². Show that their mean is the mean variance from the first exercise, then that the mean of L² × v recovers the Dupire variance. These scenarios are fictional; the finite distribution serves only as a check.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The mean of two equally likely values is their sum divided by 2.

Hint 2

Multiply each scenario by L² from the first exercise, then average the results.

Compare with the solution

Mean of v: (100 + 188) / 2 = 288 / 2 = 144 %², equal to 144 %².

With L² = 25/16, the instantaneous variances are 156.25 and 293.75 %². Their mean is 450 / 2 = 225 %², equal to 225 %², the Dupire variance.

Leverage is the same in both scenarios: it changes the scale, and the mean of v then determines the result. This check is algebraic; it does not establish that the distribution of v is realistic at this point.

Check your reasoning

  • Exact mean of the scenarios before applying leverage.
  • Same L² applied to both scenarios.
  • Conclusion limited to an algebraic check.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the leverage function L(S, t) represent?

A dimensionless factor that multiplies √v so that the mean instantaneous variance at spot S and time t recovers the Dupire variance.

Recall 2What relation gives leverage in terms of σₗ and v?

σₗ² = L² E[v | S], so L is σₗ divided by the square root of E[v | S].

Recall 3Why do we average v conditional on the spot being S?

Because the Dupire variance is a variance at this spot: only paths that pass through it count. The mean over all paths gives different leverage.

Recall 4How can calculated leverage be checked?

Square L, multiply by E[v | S] and recover σₗ². This validates the point, rather than the entire calibration.

Recall 5What does the value of leverage at a single point leave unknown?

It says nothing about the rest of the surface, the cost of calibration or numerical stability; E[v | S] comes from assumed dynamics of v.

Reconstruct the reasoning

State the point (S, t) and the units; write σₗ² = L² E[v | S]; solve for L as σₗ divided by the square root of the conditional mean; calculate with volatilities in the same unit; check using the square of the leverage; draw conclusions only for this point and the assumed dynamics of v.
