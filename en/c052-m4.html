Stability

Your goalCalculate what an Euler step does to a deviation, choose a step size that damps it, and distinguish stability, return without oscillation, and accuracy.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You are simulating a cup at 28 °C in a room kept at 20 °C. With no heating or change in the air temperature, your model brings the cup back towards ambient temperature. Yet the calculation can take it below that temperature, then back above it. If these excursions grow, should you blame the cooling model or the way it is calculated?

Euler extends the slope calculated at the start over an entire step. A step that is too long therefore keeps a slope that has become excessive for too long. The numerical cup can overshoot its target even though the cup in the continuous model never crosses it.

The diagram follows the deviation from ambient temperature: a negative deviation means “below ambient temperature”, rather than a negative temperature. The same starting point produces a steady return, an alternation that is damped, or an alternation that grows, depending on the step duration. All the numbers are fictitious; they are used to examine a calculation rule, with no measurements from a real cup.

The stability examined here concerns how a numerical method propagates deviations. It cannot be inferred from the stability of the continuous model. A small difference between two starting values can also be amplified by the calculation. We will derive the factor that determines this behavior.

The rule to remember

For this linear cooling model, calculate the factor remaining after an Euler step. Its absolute value determines damping; its sign determines alternation. Check accuracy separately.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A cup that oscillates in the calculation

Take an ambient temperature A = 20 °C, an initial temperature of 28 °C, and a constant rate λ = 0.50 per minute. Focus on the step h = 3 min, then on the durations that separate damping from amplification. We use exact arithmetic to isolate the effect of the step size.

  1. Define the model, the deviations, and their units

    T(t) is the temperature in °C at time t, measured in minutes from the start. A is the constant ambient temperature. y(t) = T(t) − A is the deviation in °C; initially, it equals 28 − 20 = 8 °C. The rate λ is strictly positive, in inverse minutes. The slope T′, equal to y′, is in °C/min.

    y′(t)=−λy(t)

    Assume a single state, a fixed rate, no heat source, and no change in ambient temperature. Under these assumptions, the continuous solution is an exponential decay:

    y(t)=y0exp(−λt)

    exp is the exponential function. For a finite positive time, its factor is strictly between 0 and 1. Starting above A, this solution therefore stays above it and approaches it. This property belongs to the model; we need to check how much of it Euler preserves.

  2. Derive the factor for an Euler step

    h is the positive, constant step duration, in minutes; n is its integer index, starting at 0. The time of point n is tn = n × h. Tn is the numerical temperature at that time and yn = Tn − A is its numerical deviation. Euler adds the initial slope multiplied by the duration to the state:

    yn+1=yn+h(−λyn)=(1−λh)yn

    Write s = λh for the fraction of the deviation removed by Euler, and R = 1 − s for the remaining factor. s and R are dimensionless: the inverse minutes in λ cancel the minutes in h. A negative factor R makes the deviation change sides at each step. This change of side does not yet tell us whether the amplitude decreases.

  3. Connect the factor to stability

    Compare two calculations using the same h and the same rate, but different starting values. δn denotes their difference in °C. Subtracting their Euler rules gives the same factor R. With no new perturbation between steps:

    δn+1=Rδn,δn=Rnδ0

    The bars |R| denote its absolute value. If |R| < 1, the powers of |R| tend to 0: every initial deviation is damped as the number of steps increases. If |R| > 1, a nonzero initial difference grows without bound. With |R| = 1, it stays bounded but is not damped. In this real scalar test, we therefore reserve the strict condition for an asymptotic return to equilibrium; absolute stability in the broader sense, |R| ≤ 1, includes the boundary without damping.

    |1−λh|<1⇔0<λh<2

    For our positive λ, this gives 0 < h < 4 min. Staying on the same side of ambient temperature also requires R ≥ 0, hence λh ≤ 1. Damping therefore allows steps that cause alternation; faithfully reproducing a return without overshoot requires more.

  4. Calculate the first step that crosses ambient temperature

    With h = 3 min, the fraction removed is s = 0.50 × 3 = 1.50 of the deviation. It exceeds 1: more than the entire initial deviation is removed. The remaining factor is R = 1 − 1.50 = -0.50.

    The deviation becomes -0.50 × 8 = -4 °C. The temperature is therefore 20 + (-4) = 16 °C. The calculation puts the cup below ambient temperature, even though its temperature remains positive.

    Another check: applying Euler directly to T gives 28 + 3 × [−0.50 × (28 − 20)] = 16 °C. This gives the same state without using the recurrence for y.

  5. Continue and track an initial perturbation

    At the next step, the sign changes again: -0.50 × (-4) = 2 °C of deviation. The temperature is 20 + 2 = 22 °C. The amplitudes are 8, then 4, then 2 °C: they decrease, although the signs alternate.

    Check using the power: R squared equals 0.25. After 2 steps, 0.25 × 8 = 2 °C. This is the same result as the successive updates.

    Now add 1 °C only to the starting value of a second calculation. Its difference from the first goes from 1 °C to -0.50 °C, then 0.25 °C. It is damped by the same factor. This experiment tracks an initial perturbation; errors newly introduced at each step would add to this propagation.

  6. Locate the boundary and the divergent case

    With h = 4 min, s = 2 and R = -1. The deviations are 8, -8, 8 °C, then the pattern repeats. They stay bounded, but do not return towards 0. Choosing exactly the bound h = 4 min therefore guarantees no damping.

    With h = 5 min, s = 2.50 and R = -1.50. The deviation becomes -12 °C, then 18 °C: its absolute value increases. The first temperature is 8 °C. An initial difference of 1 °C becomes -1.50 °C, then 2.25 °C. The continuous model is still the same; amplification comes from this choice of step size in Euler.

  7. Separate stability and accuracy

    The step h = 1 min gives R = 0.50: the deviation goes from 8 to 4, then 2 °C, without crossing ambient temperature. This is a damped return on the correct side; its distance from the continuous solution at the same time still needs to be assessed.

    More strikingly, h = 2 min gives s = 1, R = 0, and a deviation of 0 °C from the very first step. The numerical cup is exactly at 20 °C. Yet the continuous exponential factor remains strictly positive at that time: its deviation is nonzero. A calculation with strong damping can therefore have poor fidelity.

    Our step of 3 min also damps deviations, but its first negative deviation has the wrong sign compared with the continuous solution. Stability here controls propagation at a fixed step size; accuracy compares the calculation with a reference. The next unit will organize comparisons at equal physical times and step refinement. The bound obtained applies to Euler on this linear model with a constant rate; another method or different dynamics require their own analysis.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The cooling is stable, so the calculation is stable for any step size.”

    The continuous model and the recurrence do not have the same factor. Calculate R = 1 − λh. With h = 5 min, its magnitude exceeds 1 and amplifies deviations, whereas the continuous exponential damps them. Reduce the step size and justify the criterion for the method.

    Review the factor and the step size boundary

  • “If the cup crosses to the other side, the calculation must diverge.”

    The sign of R explains alternation; its absolute value explains amplitude. With R = -0.50, the alternation is damped. With R = -1.50, it grows. Check the amplitudes before drawing a conclusion, then examine separately the overshoot that is absent from the continuous model.

    Compare the trajectories of the same cup

  • “My calculation stays at equilibrium: this step size must be good.”

    Starting exactly at A gives a 0 deviation that stays 0 even with an amplifying factor. This single trial hides the instability. Compare two nearby starting values and track their difference; also check accuracy, because reaching A artificially in a single step is no proof of exactness.

Hint 1

At the boundary, find R and calculate the next 2 deviations. Is being bounded enough to tend towards equilibrium?

Hint 2

Compare with the solution

At h = 4 min, R = -1: the deviations are 8, -8, 8 °C. They alternate without decreasing. A damped return requires 0 < h < 4 min, with a strict upper bound. Equality remains a bounded case without damping in this test.

At h = 5 min, R = -1.50: -12 °C, then 18 °C of deviation. The amplitude of every nonzero initial perturbation is multiplied by a factor greater than 1 at each step.

0 deviation is a special case: R × 0 = 0, even with this step size. Starting at equilibrium therefore does not test stability with respect to perturbations. A nonzero difference must be tracked; this examines numerical sensitivity without attributing a real oscillation to the cup.

Exercise 3 · Transfer to an accuracy comparison

Two stable calculations at the same time

After 1 min, compare a single step of 1 min with 2 steps of 0.50 min for the same cup. Calculate the final temperature for each path. Do the factors damp deviations? Does their agreement or disagreement allow you to declare the result exact?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For the subdivided path, recalculate R with h = 0.50 min and apply it 2 times.

Hint 2

The total durations must be equal. Then compare the results, keeping their difference distinct from an error measured against the exact solution.

Compare with the solution

The first path gives 24 °C, because its final deviation is 4 °C. For the subdivided path: s = 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25, then R = 0.75. The successive deviations are 6 °C then 4.50 °C; the final temperature is 24.50 °C.

The times are identical: 2 × 0.50 = 1 min. The factors 0.50 and 0.75 are both strictly between 0 and 1: both paths damp deviations without alternation. Yet the temperatures differ by 0.50 °C.

This difference shows that a stability verdict does not determine accuracy. It is not the exact error of either path: we would need to compare with the continuous solution, or establish a justified error estimate. Even agreement between a few step sizes would not suffice to prove exactness. The convergence protocol in the next unit will set out this approach.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you derive the Euler factor for the deviation from ambient temperature?

Set y = T − A, write next y = y + h(−λy), then factor: R = 1 − λh. R is dimensionless and also multiplies the difference between two calculations using the same model.

Recall 2What condition ensures damping, and what happens at the boundary?

For constant positive λ and constant positive h: |R| < 1, or 0 < λh < 2. With R = -1, deviations alternate without being damped: they stay bounded. The boundary does not give an asymptotic return to equilibrium.

Recall 3Does a change of sign at each step imply divergence?

No. A negative R explains alternation; |R| determines amplitude. The factor -0.50 damps deviations, and the factor -1.50 amplifies them. Staying on the same side of ambient temperature also requires R to be positive or 0.

Recall 4Why does a calculation that damps deviations fail to prove accuracy?

Stability describes the propagation of deviations at a fixed step size. Accuracy compares with the continuous model at the same time. Euler can damp deviations while crossing ambient temperature or artificially reach equilibrium; two stable step sizes can also give different temperatures.

Recall 5Why does starting exactly at ambient temperature fail to test the stability of the step size?

0 deviation stays 0 for any R. A nonzero initial difference must be tracked: it is multiplied by R at each step. A single equilibrium trajectory does not reveal this sensitivity.
