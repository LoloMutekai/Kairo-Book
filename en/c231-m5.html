Geodesics

Your goalCalculate a geodesic on a cylinder and check its equation, its parametrization and whether it is globally minimizing as separate questions.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

On the wall of a cylinder, you need to stretch a ribbon between a mark A and a mark B located 5 m higher, directly above it. The cylinder has a circumference of 12 m. The ribbon must stay on the lateral surface; it does not pass through the interior or over a lid. These dimensions and this scene are fictional.

You can go straight up or make 1 turn while going up. To understand the winding path, mentally unroll the wall without stretching it: the ribbon becomes a sloping line segment. When rolled up again, this segment becomes a helix. The curve turns in space, but does not turn within the unrolled surface.

A geodesic extends this idea of a locally straight path. With a suitable parameter, its velocity does not change direction according to the geometry of the surface. Sufficiently short pieces are shortest paths between their endpoints. This does not promise that the whole path is the shortest among all possible detours.

The diagram shows positions on the unrolled wall. B and B′ represent the same physical point after the edges are identified. Choosing B′ rather than B keeps track of the turn made: losing this information would confuse the local shape of the path with its global comparison.

The rule to remember

Fix the metric, find the path whose covariant acceleration vanishes with an affine parameter, then examine the other paths between the same points separately.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A helix can be a geodesic without winning the global comparison

Let us calculate the ribbon making 1 turn. A has unrolled coordinates (0 m ; 0 m), and B′ has coordinates (12 m ; 5 m). We follow the path using a dimensionless parameter t, from 0 to 1. The surface is an ideal circular cylinder with no obstacles.

  1. Fix the coordinates and metric

    u is the distance travelled around the cylinder, in metres; z is the height, in metres. u is not an angle. The values u and u + C represent the same location around the wall, where C = 12 m. We keep u unrolled to count the turns. This unrolling provides local coordinates without distorting lengths; u is not a single global real coordinate on the cylinder.

    Let x denote the pair (u, z). In index notation, x with superscript 1 denotes u and with superscript 2 denotes z: these indices are not powers. g is the matrix of the metric; its entries are dimensionless in these coordinates measured in metres. ds is a length element in metres. The local metric is:

    g=(1001)
    ds2=du2+dz2

    We assume a Riemannian metric, meaning smooth, symmetric and positive definite: every nonzero tangent vector has strictly positive length. The curves considered are regular and have continuous second derivatives. Here, regularity means that the velocity never vanishes.

  2. State precisely what must vanish

    A geodesic with an affine parameter has 0 covariant acceleration. The covariant derivative measures the change in velocity while accounting for the change in the coordinate bases. The rule used is called the Levi-Civita connection of g; for this calculation, it is defined by the coefficients Γ below. They allow this adjustment to be calculated from the metric.

    A dot above x denotes its derivative with respect to t; a double dot denotes its second derivative. For each index k, the equation is:

    x¨k+∑i=12∑j=12Γijk(x)x˙ix˙j=0

    The indices i, j, k and ℓ range over the coordinates from 1 to 2. The sums add the terms for the indicated indices. gᵢⱼ is an entry of g, and g with superscript indices kℓ is an entry of its inverse matrix. ∂ᵢ means differentiating with respect to the coordinate x with index i. The Christoffel symbols are calculated by:

    Γijk=12∑ℓ=12gkℓ(∂igℓj+∂jgiℓ−∂ℓgij)

    The Γ have units of inverse metres. The derivatives of x with respect to t have units of metres per parameter unit, and their second derivatives have units of metres per parameter unit squared. The terms in the equation therefore have the same units. On our unrolled cylinder, all entries of g are constant: their derivatives equal 0, so all Γ equal 0. The equation becomes u″ = 0 and z″ = 0. This does not mean that the acceleration in the ambient space is 0.

  3. Recover this equation by varying the ribbon

    The length L integrates the norm of the velocity. To recover the equation with an affine parameter, we instead vary the geometric energy E, which integrates half the square of this norm. This term does not refer to mechanical energy in joules: here E is in square metres, since t is dimensionless.

    L=∫01u˙2+z˙2dt,E=12∫01(u˙2+z˙2)dt

    Let us move the path slightly by adding εη, without moving its endpoints. ε is a small dimensionless number; η is a displacement in metres, with components ηᵤ and η_z that vanish at the endpoints. δE is the derivative of E with respect to ε at ε = 0. Expanding the squares and then integrating by parts gives:

    δE=−∫01(u¨ηu+z¨ηz)dt

    The boundary term disappears because η vanishes there. For this derivative to vanish for every admissible displacement, we must have u″ = 0 and z″ = 0. If a component of the acceleration remained nonzero near a point, a local displacement of the same sign would produce a nonzero variation there. We thus recover the previous equation, instead of declaring that a ribbon is a geodesic because it looks taut.

    Stationary means that the first-order variation vanishes. A regular geodesic is minimizing on sufficiently short segments, but stationarity does not establish a global minimum. An affine parameter keeps the metric speed constant; a linear change of parameter with nonzero slope preserves the equation.

  4. Solve using the endpoint corresponding to the turn

    A vanishing second derivative gives an affine function of the parameter: u(t) = at + b and z(t) = ct + d. a and c are the displacements per parameter unit; b and d are the initial coordinates, all in metres since t is dimensionless. The starting point requires b = d = 0 m. The endpoint B′ requires a = 12 m and c = 5 m.

    u(t)=12t m,z(t)=5t m

    At t = 0, we recover A; at t = 1, we reach B′. On the cylinder, u = 12 m is identified with u = 0 m: the endpoint is indeed B. When crossing the cut used to unroll the surface, we change coordinates by u → u − C; this translation preserves the derivatives and the equation. Identifying the edges does not create a turn in the path.

  5. Calculate the length and check the square

    The velocity components are constant: 12 m and 5 m per parameter unit. Their metric square is 12 × 12 + 5 × 5 = 144 + 25 = 169 square metres per parameter unit squared.

    L=∫01169dt m=13 m

    To find this square root exactly, let us test the positive number 13: 13 × 13 = 169. Its square is correct, so the speed is 13 m per parameter unit. The parameter interval has width 1: length = 13 × 1 = 13 m. No rounding is involved.

    Independent check: the unrolled path is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with displacements 12 m and 5 m. The Pythagorean theorem gives the same square, 169 square metres. The energy is 169 / 2 = 84.50 square metres; twice this is 169, equal to the square of L for this constant speed on our interval. E and L remain different quantities.

  6. Compare with all paths, including those that are not helices

    With no turn, the vertical segment has u(t) = 0 m and z(t) = 5t m. It also satisfies the geodesic equation and has length 5 m. The helix is therefore 8 m longer between the same physical points.

    Why is the vertical path truly globally minimizing? On every curve on the wall, the norm of the velocity is at least the absolute value of its vertical component. Its length is therefore at least the total vertical variation, which is itself at least the difference in height between A and B, namely 5 m. The vertical path attains this bound. This proof also covers paths that go up, come back down or turn several times.

    More precisely, let k denote the signed integer number of turns. The unrolled representative of the endpoint is (kC, H), where H = 5 m. In this winding class, the sloping segment gives the minimum length Lk:

    Lk=(kC)2+H2

    Here k = 0 gives 5 m, whereas k = 1 gives 13 m. Small continuous displacements of the ribbon with fixed endpoints preserve k: they cannot remove a turn. The helix minimizes length within its winding class and remains a geodesic, while losing the comparison with the vertical path. The local equation and the global choice therefore answer distinct questions.

Explain it in your own words

Explain without formulas how the helix can satisfy the geodesic equation while being longer than the vertical path. Then reconstruct the metric, the endpoint conditions and the proof of the vertical bound.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A geodesic is necessarily the shortest path between its endpoints.”

    The equation concerns local behaviour. Compare the 13 m of the helix with the 5 m of the vertical path: changing the number of turns can win a global comparison without invalidating the equation of the first path.

    Review the comparison of paths and its vertical bound

  • “The helix turns in space, so it must have nonzero covariant acceleration.”

    Covariant acceleration retains only the intrinsic change in velocity. Isometric unrolling makes the helix straight; the curvature visible in space is not enough to decide. Calculate the coefficients in the stated coordinates.

    Return to the wall unrolled without stretching

  • “Since the traced curve is the same, its geodesic equation remains true with any parameter.”

    A varying speed can create tangential acceleration. The same image can represent a geodesic with an affine parameter and no longer satisfy the equation with a 0 right-hand side with another parameter. The reparametrization exercise lets you calculate this.

    Check the derivatives after changing the parameter

Exercise 1 · Application · coordinates and length

Find the midpoint of the winding ribbon

For the helix we solved for, calculate u and z at t = 1/2. Check that the portion travelled measures half the ribbon. The requested coordinates are unrolled, in metres.

Hint 2

Compare with the solution

u = 12 × 1/2 = 6 m ; z = 5 × 1/2 = 2.50 m. Half the displacement in each coordinate has been covered.

The portion measures 13 × 1/2 = 6.50 m. Check using the Pythagorean theorem: the squared displacements are 36 and 6.25 square metres, giving 42.25 square metres in total. The square of 6.50 m is indeed 42.25 square metres. The proportion of length follows from the constant speed, not just the displayed value of the parameter.

Exercise 2 · Reasoning · local and global

Turn in the opposite direction

The ribbon makes k = -1 turn: its unrolled endpoint becomes (-12 m ; 5 m). Write its equation, check that it is a geodesic and decide whether it is globally minimizing. Justify the comparison with all paths.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Changing the sign of the horizontal displacement does not change its square.

Hint 2

For the global minimum, use the height difference as a lower bound.

Compare with the solution

We obtain u(t) = -12t m and z(t) = 5t m. Their second derivatives vanish, and all Γ vanish in these coordinates: the equation is satisfied with this affine parameter.

The squared speed is -12 × -12 + 5 × 5 = 144 + 25 = 169. The length remains 13 m. The vertical path of 5 m is shorter. Every curve must cover at least the height 5 m; the vertical path attains this bound. The ribbon winding in the opposite direction is therefore a geodesic without being globally minimizing.

  • Eliminate the sign by squaring, with a positive length.
  • Prove that the vertical path is minimizing instead of merely citing a shorter competitor.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · same curve, different speed

An identical ribbon traversed differently

Replace t in the helix from the example by φ(t) = (t + t squared) / 2, for t from 0 to 1. φ is dimensionless. Show that the traced curve and length are preserved, then calculate the second derivatives of the coordinates. Is the geodesic equation with a 0 right-hand side still satisfied with t? Also give the unrolled position at t = 1/2.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate φ at the endpoints and calculate φ′. A strictly increasing function covering the same interval preserves the image of the path.

Hint 2

Differentiate φ once more, then multiply by the displacements of the helix.

Compare with the solution

φ(0) = 0, φ(1) = 1 and φ′(t) = 1/2 + t remains strictly positive. The same unrolled segment is therefore traversed in the same direction, at a different speed. Its length remains 13 m: integrating 13φ′(t) amounts to multiplying 13 by φ(1) − φ(0) = 1.

φ″ = 1, so u″ = 12 m per parameter unit squared and z″ = 5 m per parameter unit squared. With the Γ vanishing, these accelerations do not satisfy the equation with a 0 right-hand side. The traced curve remains that of a geodesic; t is no longer its affine parameter. Returning to s = φ(t), where s is a dimensionless parameter, restores constant speed.

At the midpoint of the interval, t squared equals 1/4, t + t squared equals 3/4, so φ(t) = 3/8. The coordinates are u = 4.50 m and z = 1.875 m. They differ from the geometric midpoint found in the first exercise: covering half the parameter time no longer means covering half the ribbon.

Check your reasoning

  • Prove monotonicity and preservation of the endpoints before concluding that the image is the same.
  • Distinguish the unchanged length from the equation that depends on the affine parameter.
  • Calculate the second derivatives and coordinates without confusing t with φ(t).

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does it mean to be a geodesic with an affine parameter?

The covariant acceleration vanishes. The direction of the velocity continues according to the geometry of the surface, and its norm remains constant. This does not guarantee a global minimum between the endpoints.

Recall 2Why do the Christoffel symbols vanish in our unrolling?

The metric in coordinates of distance around the cylinder and height is constant. All derivatives of its entries vanish, so the formula for the symbols gives 0. This conclusion depends on the coordinates used.

Recall 3What data allow the helix on this page to be calculated?

The unrolled metric, the equations u″ = 0 and z″ = 0, an affine parameter from 0 to 1, the starting point and the unrolled representative of the endpoint chosen according to the number of turns. These data give u(t) = 12t m and z(t) = 5t m for 1 turn.

Recall 4What proof distinguishes our helix from a globally shortest path?

The helix measures 13 m and the vertical path 5 m. Every curve joining the marks has length at least equal to their height difference, 5 m; the vertical path attains the bound. The helix remains a geodesic and is minimizing within its winding class.

Recall 5Why can the same ribbon fail to satisfy the geodesic equation after reparametrization?

Length depends on the traced curve, but the equation of 0 covariant acceleration uses an affine parameter. A nonlinear change can add tangential acceleration; recovering the affine parameter restores the equation.
