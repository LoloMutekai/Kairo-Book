Level 2 · C138·M3

Forests

Your goalExplain variable sampling in a random forest and reconstruct its prediction by averaging the outputs of the leaves reached.

Before you start
c099-m5 : probability calibration · c100-m5 : learning curves · c138-m1 : trees · c138-m2 : bagging

✿01 See the idea

A depot wants to predict the duration of the final leg of a parcel’s journey. A tree can ask questions about distance, mass and departure time, then give a duration. Bagging trains several trees on samples drawn from the historical records with replacement: a parcel that has been drawn remains available and can therefore appear several times.

But if distance dominates the other variables, many trees may start with the same question and make similar errors. A random forest adds variable sampling at each split: the tree searches for its question using only the candidate variables at that node. At the next node, sampling starts again.

We will follow 3 small trees. Each receives 4 positions sampled from the same historical records. Of the 3 available variables, we offer only 1 at each split. Variables that are not selected remain available for a later draw. After training, the new parcel passes through all the trees and their predicted durations are averaged.

The parcels, durations and samples on this page are entirely fictional. The scene illustrates a forest for regression: its output is a duration in minutes. It does not measure any improvement in a delivery service.

The rule to remember

Sample parcels with replacement for each tree; sample candidate variables at each node; learn questions using those candidates; average the trees’ predictions for the same new parcel.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the parcel to the average of the leaf outputs

The parcel whose duration we want to predict travels 4 km, weighs 3 kg and leaves at 11 h. Follow the samples and splits provided below. Each tree has a single split followed by terminal leaves: this forest is deliberately small and shallow.

  1. Specify the data and when they are available

    Each historical parcel provides a distance in kilometres, a mass in kilograms, a departure time in hours within the same day, and an observed duration in minutes. Distance, mass and departure time are known at departure; duration is the target to be learned and must not become an input variable.

    A: 2 km; 1 kg; 9 h; duration 10 min.

    B: 4 km; 3 kg; 10 h; duration 14 min.

    C: 8 km; 1 kg; 14 h; duration 18 min.

    D: 10 km; 3 kg; 15 h; duration 22 min.

    x denotes a parcel’s variables, ordered as distance, mass, departure time; y denotes its actual duration. For the new parcel, x is known and y remains hidden during the calculation. A regression tree returns the average duration of the training occurrences that reach its leaf.

  2. Build trees that do not all examine the same candidates

    n = 4 is the number of historical parcels and of positions sampled with replacement for each tree; p = 3 is the number of available variables. T = 3 is the number of trees; m = 1 is the number of candidate variables sampled without replacement at a split. These counts have no physical unit, with 1 ≤ m ≤ p and T ≥ 1.

    For each tree, draw its sample of parcels. At each node where a split is allowed, sample m variables from p, then search their admissible thresholds for the one that most reduces the sum of squared deviations from the leaf averages. Here, an admissible threshold leaves at least 1 occurrence on each side. If no split is admissible, the node remains a leaf. The stopping rule determines whether to continue. Variable sampling starts again at each node; it does not choose a permanent variable for the whole tree.

    Here is a provided fictional outcome: α receives A, B, C, C and distance at its root, with threshold 6 km. β receives A, B, C, D and mass, with threshold 2 kg. γ receives B, B, C, D and departure time, with threshold 12 h. In this small model, each tree stops after this split. Searching for thresholds belongs to the unit on trees; here, they are given.

    The draws for individual trees can be made independently, but they all use the same historical records: this does not make their errors independent. Variable sampling aims to diversify the possible questions, without guaranteeing that all learned questions will differ.

  3. Find the leaves reached

    In α, 4 ≤ 6 km: the parcel joins A and B. Their average duration is (10 + 14) / 2 = 24 / 2 = 12 min. The occurrences of C belong to the other leaf.

    In β, 3 > 2 kg: the parcel joins B and D. The leaf returns (14 + 22) / 2 = 36 / 2 = 18 min.

    In γ, 11 ≤ 12 h: the parcel joins the 2 occurrences of B. The leaf returns (14 + 14) / 2 = 28 / 2 = 14 min. A repetition counts in this tree’s training, but it does not provide a newly observed delivery.

  4. Average the predictions for this same x

    j is a tree’s index, from 1 to T. fⱼ(x) is the duration predicted by that tree for parcel x, in minutes. F(x) is the forest’s prediction, also in minutes. The symbol Σ means to add the outputs of all the trees; the inputs x are identical for every term.

    F(x)=1T∑j=1Tfj(x)

    All trees have the same weight. First add 12 + 18 = 30 min, then 30 + 14 = 44 min. Divide by the number of trees, because each output estimates the same duration.

    F(x)=12+18+143=443≈14.67 min

    The fraction 44/3 min is exact; 14.67 min is a rounded value. Neither distance nor mass is averaged with the minutes.

  5. Check the average another way

    An average with equal weights must remain between the smallest and largest outputs: 12 min ≤ F(x) ≤ 18 min. Our fraction satisfies this bound.

    Also check that the deviations of the outputs from the average balance out. For α: 12 − 44/3 = -8/3 min. For β: 18 − 44/3 = 10/3 min. For γ: 14 − 44/3 = -2/3 min.

    The first deviations add up to 2/3 min; adding the deviation for γ gives 0 min. This check uses the exact fraction, before any rounding. It checks the calculation of the average, rather than the accuracy of the forecast for a future delivery.

  6. Distinguish diversity, correct calculation and predictive quality

    With m less than p, a dominant variable may be absent from a node. The trees then have more opportunities to learn different questions; averaging may reduce their variability. But depriving a node of a useful variable may also weaken its tree. This trade-off must be checked on parcels separate from those used for training.

    Increasing T removes neither a shared offset in the predictions nor future information that has slipped into the inputs. The number of candidate variables and the depth are tuned using separate validation; the test set remains reserved for evaluating the fixed choice. A single delivery allows an error to be calculated, but does not establish general superiority.

    Our forest deliberately uses very short trees. A forest can contain deeper trees: the rule of drawing variables again then applies to their other splits. Averaging the outputs remains the same operation.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I draw the variable once, and this tree will only examine that variable.”

    Candidate variables are sampled at the node. If the tree continues, another draw may offer a different variable or the same one again. Our trees stop at the root only to keep the calculation short.

    Reread the construction and its stopping rule

  • “The forest must choose the duration that receives the most votes.”

    Here, each output is a quantity in minutes: take their average. The average duration may differ from every individual output. Always state whether the target is a quantity or a class before choosing the aggregation.

    Find the average and its units again

  • “Adding more trees is enough to make the forecast good.”

    Copies of the same tree retain exactly its output and its error. Different trees can also share an error. Calculate the case with copies, then distinguish an error on a single parcel from an evaluation on parcels held out.

    Try the predictive quality diagnosis

The parcel becomes lighter

Hint 1

Only the tree that asks a question about mass can change leaves in our small ensemble.

Hint 2

The light parcel leaf in β contains A and C. Their average becomes β’s new output.

Compare with the solution

α stays at 12 min and γ at 14 min. In β, 1 ≤ 2 kg leads to A and C: (10 + 18) / 2 = 28 / 2 = 14 min.

Add 12 + 14 = 26 min, then 26 + 14 = 40 min. The new average is exactly 40/3 min, or approximately 13.33 min.

The exact decrease is 44/3 − 40/3 = 4/3 min. Check: only β has changed; dividing the decrease in its output by T also gives (18 − 14) / 3 = 4/3 min. The new average remains between 12 and 14 min.

Exercise 3 · Reasoned diagnosis

Is a smaller error enough?

Return to the original parcel of 3 kg. After its fictional delivery, its duration is revealed: 16 min. Calculate the squared errors of the forest and of β alone. Someone then replaces the forest with 3 exact copies of β: calculate this average. What do these results establish, and what evidence is missing for choosing a delivery model?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A signed error is the prediction minus the observed duration; its square is expressed in square minutes.

Hint 2

Averaging identical copies does not change their output. A single parcel does not represent the whole distribution of journeys.

Compare with the solution

For the forest, the signed error is 44/3 − 16 = -4/3 min. Its square is 16/9 min². For β: 18 − 16 = 2 min, then 4 min². For this particular parcel, the forest’s squared error is smaller.

The copies of β first give 18 + 18 = 36 min, then 36 + 18 = 54 min. Their average 54 / 3 = 18 min remains β’s output; the squared error remains 4 min².

These calculations establish a local comparison and show that copying a tree adds no diversity. Choosing a model requires comparing errors on several parcels reserved for validation, with inputs that are actually available at departure. Once the settings are fixed, a separate test evaluates that choice. No success rate or general improvement follows from our fictional delivery.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the signed errors before squaring them and state min².
  • Show that the copies have the same average and the same error as β.
  • Limit the verdict to this parcel and distinguish validation for tuning from a separate test.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which sampling operations must we distinguish in our forest?

Sample parcels with replacement for each tree, then sample candidate variables without replacement at each node where a split is attempted. Variable sampling starts again at the next node.

Recall 2What does a regression leaf return, and how should a repeated parcel be treated?

The average duration of the training occurrences that reached that leaf, in minutes. A repeated parcel counts with its multiplicity in that tree; it is not a new independent observation.

Recall 3How can we reconstruct our forest’s output and check its calculation?

Pass the same parcel through all the trees, add their outputs, then divide by T. The output retains the unit of the durations and lies between their extremes. The deviations of the outputs from the exact average must add up to 0 min.

Recall 4Why restrict candidate variables without promising better predictions?

This gives trees opportunities to ask different questions and may reduce their shared variability. But a useful variable may be missing, and the errors remain linked through the same historical records. The trade-off requires separate validation.

Recall 5What is still missing after a smaller error on our fictional parcel?

A comparison on several parcels reserved for validation to fix the settings, followed by evaluation on a separate test. A smaller local error does not prove a general improvement; reading or an assisted answer does not demonstrate an acquired skill either.
