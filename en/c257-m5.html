Sampling frequency selection and MSE

Your goalChoose the sampling frequency of a realized variance estimator by comparing, for each candidate interval, the bias due to noise and the sampling variance through the mean squared error.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

We track a single price during a fictional session lasting 360 minutes. We observe the logarithm of the price, expressed in basis points (bp, labelled pb in the fixed formulas). We want to estimate the integrated variation of this session, denoted by σ²: the variance that the efficient price, the price that would exist without quote errors, accumulates from start to finish. Its unit is bp². This unit leaves a single question open: how often should we record the price?

If we record it rarely, we discard information. The estimate varies greatly from one session to the next, like a survey with too few respondents. If we record it very often, each return also contains quote errors, known as microstructure noise, which are not price movements. These errors accumulate across returns and push the estimate upwards, always in the same direction.

The two problems differ in nature. The second is bias, a systematic difference between the mean of the estimator and the target. The first is variance, the dispersion of the estimator around its own mean. The mean squared error, often abbreviated MSE, is the mean of the squared difference between the estimator and the target. It decomposes exactly into squared bias plus variance, providing a single scale for comparing frequencies.

This unit does not revisit asynchrony or the Fourier estimator: here we have only a single price, a regular grid and a sum of squared returns. All values are fictional. They serve for calculation, rather than to describe a real market or recommend a frequency.

The rule to remember

For each candidate interval: calculate the bias due to noise, the sampling variance, then MSE = bias² + variance; keep the interval that minimizes this MSE under the stated model and parameters.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Choose a sampling interval in a fictional session

A fictional session lasts 360 minutes, with σ² = 100 bp² and ω² = 0.25 bp². There are three candidate intervals: 60, 10 and 1 minutes, giving 6, 36 and 360 returns. Which interval gives the smallest MSE when estimating σ² with RVₙ?

  1. Define the model, symbols and units

    Notation. σ² is the integrated variation of the session, in bp², and equals 100 bp² in this scenario. ω² is the variance of quote noise, in bp², and equals 0.25 bp². n is the number of returns; Δ = 360 / n is the interval in minutes. rk is the kth observed return, ek the efficient price movement component of that return and εk the quote error at the kth time point. RVn, the realized variance, is the sum of the squares of the n returns; it estimates σ², and its unit is bp².

    RVn=∑k=1nrk2

    Model assumptions. Volatility is constant throughout the session. The n components ek are independent Gaussian variables with mean 0 and variance σ²/n each. The observed price is the efficient price plus a Gaussian error εk with mean 0 and variance ω², independent of the efficient price and across time points. The n + 1 time points are equally spaced. A different noise distribution, dependent noise or varying volatility would change the variance formulas below.

  2. Decompose a return and recover the bias

    An observed return contains two quote errors: the error at its final time point and the error at its initial time point, which enters with the opposite sign.

    rk=ek+εk−εk−1

    The three terms are independent. Their variances add: σ²/n for ek, and ω² for each quote error. The variance of a return is therefore v = σ²/n + 2ω², and its square has mean v. Summing n squares gives an expected value of n × v for the estimator.

    E[RVn]=μn=σ2+2nω2

    The bias is therefore μn − σ² = 2nω², in bp². It is proportional to n: doubling the frequency doubles the excess. For n = 1, it would equal 0.50 bp²: this is the contribution d = 2ω² from each return.

  3. Calculate the variance of the estimator

    Two adjacent returns share a quote error with opposite signs: their covariance is −ω². Two returns that are not adjacent share no variable: their covariance is 0.

    Cov(rk,rk+1)=−ω2

    For Gaussian variables X and Y with mean 0, a standard identity gives Cov(X², Y²) = 2 Cov(X, Y)². We accept it here without proving it again; the calculation file for this unit cross-checks it through a separate expansion. Summing over all pairs of returns, the variance of RVn is 2 times the sum of the squares of all covariances: n terms with square v², plus 2(n − 1) terms with square ω⁴ for adjacent pairs.

    Var(RVn)=2nv2+4(n−1)ω4=2μn2n+4(n−1)ω4

    The second expression follows from n × v = μn, giving n v² = μn²/n. The unit of this variance is bp⁴, because RVn is in bp².

  4. Assemble the MSE for an interval

    The MSE is the mean of (RVn − σ²)². Separating the difference into its bias and fluctuation gives a cross term with mean 0. Squared bias plus variance remains.

    MSE(n)=(2nω2)2+2μn2n+4(n−1)ω4

    The first term grows like n²; the second decreases like 1/n while μn stays close to σ². This explains the trough shown in the diagram, under these assumptions only.

  5. Calculate for Δ = 10 minutes, giving n = 36

    Noise contribution per return: d = 2 × 0.25 = 0.50 bp². Bias: 36 × 0.50 = 18.00 bp², giving squared bias 18.00 × 18.00 = 324.00 bp⁴.

    Expected value: μ = 100 + 18.00 = 118.00 bp². Its square is 13924.00. Dividing by 36 gives 386.78, then multiplying by 2 gives 773.56 bp⁴.

    Adjacent pairs: 35 pairs, each contributing 4 × ω⁴ = 0.25, giving 8.75 bp⁴. Variance: 773.56 + 8.75 = 782.31 bp⁴.

    MSE: 782.31 + 324.00 = 1106.31 bp⁴. Each addition combines quantities with the same unit, bp⁴: the bias was squared before being added.

  6. Compare the three intervals

    Applying the same calculation to the other two intervals gives the following table, with bp⁴ as the unit for the last three columns.

    Δ (min)nbiais² (pb⁴)variance (pb⁴)MSE (pb⁴)6069.003537.583546.581036324.00782.311106.31136032400.00525.3132925.31

    At 60 minutes, the squared bias (9.00) is negligible compared with the variance (3537.58): we lack information. At 1 minute, the reverse holds: squared bias (32400.00) accounts for almost all of the MSE (32925.31). Among these three intervals, 10 minutes gives the smallest MSE, 1106.31.

  7. Check the result

    Consistency check: for each interval, bias² + variance gives the MSE, and each grid covers the session, since 36 × 10 = 360 minutes. The calculation file also cross-checks the three MSE values using the explicit covariance of the returns, then, for 6 and 36 returns, through an expansion in independent variables.

    Limiting case without noise (ω² is 0): the formula gives 2σ⁴/n, or 555.56 bp⁴ for 36 returns. This is about half the MSE with noise, and it decreases as n increases; without noise, the highest frequency would be best.

    Grid limitation: an exhaustive search over all integers from 1 to 360 places the minimum at n = 34, outside the grid of whole-minute intervals. Near this trough the curve is flat: moving from 34 to 36 returns barely changes the MSE. This result holds for fictional values and this Gaussian model; it does not indicate a frequency to use with real data, where σ² and ω² would first need to be estimated.

Explain it in your own words

Why does the highest frequency, which uses the most data, fail to give the smallest MSE? Answer using both terms, squared bias and variance, and say what would change if the noise disappeared.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The more often we sample, the more precise the variance estimate becomes.”

    Sampling variance falls, but the bias due to noise grows in proportion to n. At 1 minute, squared bias accounts for 32400.00 bp⁴ out of an MSE of 32925.31 bp⁴. Always compare both terms.

    Review the comparison of the three intervals

  • “Bias disappears when we average over several sessions.”

    In the model, every session has the same expected value, which is too high: μₙ − σ² = 2nω². Averaging reduces the fluctuation, rather than this systematic difference. That is why the decomposition separates the two terms.

    Reread bias, variance and MSE

  • “The MSE is obtained by adding bias and variance.”

    Bias is in bp² and variance is in bp⁴. You must square the bias before adding. For 36 returns: 18.00 × 18.00 = 324.00, then 324.00 + 782.31 = 1106.31 bp⁴.

    Repeat the calculation for Δ = 10 minutes

Exercise 1 · Explained application

Twice as much noise variance

In the fictional scenario, the noise variance ω² is multiplied by 2, with everything else unchanged. For Δ = 10 minutes (36 returns), calculate the new contribution d = 2ω² from a return, then the bias of RVₙ. Compare it with 18.00 bp².

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The contribution d is proportional to ω². What is its new value if ω² doubles?

Hint 2

The bias equals n × d: repeat the multiplication from the example with the new d.

Compare with the solution

The new d is 2 times 0.50, giving 1.00 bp². The bias is 36 × 1.00 = 36.00 bp², twice 18.00 bp². The bias is proportional to ω²: its value depends on the assumed noise, as well as the frequency. You do not need to recalculate the MSE here, because the variance changes too.

Check your reasoning

  • The doubling of ω² is applied to d before multiplication by n.
  • The bias is expressed in bp².
  • The conclusion distinguishes bias from MSE, without recalculating the full MSE.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the MSE of an estimator measure, and how does it decompose?

The mean of the squared difference between the estimator and the target; it equals squared bias plus variance.

Recall 2Why does the bias of realized variance grow with the number of returns in this model?

Each return contributes the same excess 2ω² due to noise; n returns add these contributions, so the bias is 2nω².

Recall 3Which MSE term decreases as n increases, and why?

The sampling variance, of order 2μ²/n: more returns average out more of the fluctuation, as long as the bias remains moderate.

Recall 4What are the units of bias, squared bias and MSE here?

Bias is in bp², while its square and the MSE are in bp⁴: you must square the bias before adding it to the variance.

Recall 5What does the 10 minute interval found in the example fail to establish?

It depends on fictional values and independent Gaussian noise; it is not a recommended frequency for real data.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Set the model, the target σ², the noise assumptions and the units; for each candidate interval, calculate the bias due to noise, the sampling variance, then MSE = bias² + variance; compare on the same scale; keep the minimum while recalling that the result follows from the fictional parameters and stated assumptions.
