Level 2 · C073·M2

Multiple comparisons

Your goalDefine a family of comparisons, calculate the Bonferroni and Holm decisions, then explain the error rate they control and their limitations.

Before you start
c056-m5 : effect size · c065-m5 : prediction interval · c073-m1 : ANOVA

✿01 See the idea

In a workshop, a printer has 3 settings, A, B and C. We want to compare the mean time needed to print the same label. Before taking measurements, we decide to examine every pair: A with B, A with C, B with C. Looking for a difference in several places increases the opportunities to mistake chance for an effect.

The family is the set of hypotheses whose conclusions we want to protect together. Here, it contains 3 comparisons. A false rejection means announcing a difference in means when the corresponding equality is true. The familywise error rate is the probability of making at least 1 of these false rejections in the family.

Bonferroni divides a risk budget among all the comparisons. Holm starts with the smallest p-value, then relaxes the threshold as long as the previous rejections have been made. As soon as a comparison fails, the procedure stops: we cannot skip that failure to obtain a conclusion we like.

The entire scenario and all the p-values on this page are fictional. The p-values are supplied as calculation inputs; no series of printing times is supplied to estimate them. The diagrams show decision rules, rather than actual measurements or an improvement to the printer.

The rule to remember

Set the family, its error rate and the method before the results; then apply the adjustment to the entire family, keeping Holm’s stopping rule.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The same p-values, two ways to protect the family

The fictional protocol includes every pair of settings A, B and C and a familywise error rate α = 5/100 = 0.05. The supplied two-sided tests give p for AB = 0.010, AC = 0.024 and BC = 0.030. Two-sided means that either a shorter or a longer mean time can lead to rejection. Let us compare Bonferroni and Holm to learn their rules; in a study, the method is chosen before the results.

  1. Count the questions and define the symbols

    rkulay vluxzp uahgds nzpgiz mnlziw pbllos dbej zrgxsb lpffjg tnlvkb oeuyvj soygup yoktud vntw The tests concern these equalities; individual times can vary even when the means are equal.

    n is the number of settings and m the number of unordered comparisons; these are counts with no physical unit. Counting each setting with every other setting produces n × (n − 1) ordered pairs. Since AB and BA are the same two-sided question, we divide by 2.

    m=n(n−1)2=3×22=3

    Direct check: AB, AC, BC. The ANOVA in the previous unit asks a different question: whether all the means are equal together. A rejection by ANOVA does not identify which pairs differ and does not allow each pair to be tested without adjustment. Here, the family is declared and can be analysed without making its calculation conditional on an ANOVA result.

  2. State what the p-values must guarantee

    For a given comparison, p is the observed p-value, with no unit. Under its equality hypothesis and the test model, it summarises the probability of a result at least as extreme as the one observed. It is not the probability that the equality is true.

    The multiplicity calculation assumes that every p-value is valid: for every threshold u between 0 and 1, if its null hypothesis is true, Pr(p ≤ u) ≤ u. Pr denotes probability over repetitions of the protocol; u and p have no units. Equality is not required, particularly for a discrete test.

    We therefore assume that the tests suit the measurement design and that their assumptions hold. We do not prove this validity from the p-values alone. The comparisons share batches from the same setting and may depend on one another; the adjustments studied here do not require independent p-values. The family and α are fixed before examining the results.

  3. Divide the risk with Bonferroni

    α is the target maximum familywise error rate, with no unit. Bonferroni rejects an equality if its p-value is less than or equal to α/m. The same threshold is used for every comparison: 0.05 ÷ 3 = 1/60 exactly, or approximately 0.016667.

    Why does this division protect the family? Let I be the set of hypotheses that are actually true, |I| their number, j an index in that set and V the number of false rejections. A false rejection somewhere is a union of events. The probability of a union is at most the sum of their probabilities: any overlap can only be counted more than once.

    Pr(V≥1)≤∑j∈IPr(pj≤αm)≤|I|αm≤α

    The validity of each test gives the second inequality; |I| ≤ m gives the last. If no equality is true, V is 0. This proof also covers situations in which only some equalities are true and assumes no independence between tests.

    With the supplied inputs, 0.010 is less than 1/60: we reject H_AB. 0.024 and 0.030 are greater than this threshold: we reject neither H_AC nor H_BC. Comparisons use the exact fractions, rather than a rounded decimal.

  4. Check Bonferroni using an alternative form

    We can keep the familywise threshold α and adjust each p-value: multiply p by m, then cap it at 1 if necessary. This adjusted p-value is the smaller of 1 and mp. For α strictly between 0 and 1, the decision is identical to p ≤ α/m.

    AB: 3 × 0.010 = 0.030. AC: 3 × 0.024 = 0.072. BC: 3 × 0.030 = 0.090. Every product is less than 1, so the cap changes nothing. Comparing these results to 0.05 again gives only the rejection of H_AB.

    Budget check: 3 × 1/60 = 0.05 = α. By contrast, testing each pair directly at 0.05 would give only a bound of 3 × 0.05 = 0.15 through this proof. This bound is neither a measurement of the actual error rate nor an equality; the observed results alone do not give the probability of a false rejection.

  5. Apply Holm in order, without skipping steps

    Sort the p-values in increasing order, keeping the name of each pair: AB, then AC, then BC. Rank r starts at 1. p_(r) denotes the p-value at that rank, and t_r the corresponding threshold. Their unit is again the abstract unit of probabilities.

    tr=αm−r+1

    At rank 1, the denominator is 3: 0.010 ≤ 1/60. We reject H_AB and continue. At rank 2, 2 hypotheses remain: the threshold is 0.05 ÷ 2 = 0.025. Since 0.024 ≤ 0.025, we reject H_AC and continue.

    At rank 3, 1 hypothesis remains: the threshold is 0.05 ÷ 1 = 0.050. Since 0.030 ≤ 0.050, we reject H_BC. Here, Holm rejects all 3 equalities, compared with 1 for Bonferroni. This does not allow us to choose the method after seeing which conclusion it provides.

    The general rule includes a stop: at the first p_(r) strictly greater than t_r, reject neither that hypothesis nor any that follow. Being below a later threshold does not allow the procedure to resume.

  6. Understand Holm’s protection and interpret the result

    Why does Holm maintain its protection? If any equalities are true, let t be their number and k the rank of their smallest p-value. A false rejection requires rejecting this first true equality. At that step, all t true equalities are still present; the denominator m − k + 1 is therefore at least t. A false rejection thus requires the smallest p-value among the true hypotheses to be at most α/t. The same bound on a union of t events gives a maximum error rate of t × α/t = α. Stopping is essential to this reasoning.

    The methods control the probability of at least 1 false rejection over repetitions of the protocol, rather than the proportion of false conclusions in our list. Failure to reject does not establish equality. A rejection gives neither the direction of the difference, its size in seconds nor its usefulness: the p-values alone cannot support a recommendation for a setting.

    Final check: every hypothesis rejected by Bonferroni is also rejected by Holm, because its first threshold is identical and later thresholds are larger. If the p-values come from unsuitable tests or other trials have been hidden, adjusting this list does not fix the problem. The scope of the family, its assumptions and the calculation still need to be documented.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the decisions and reconstruct the thresholds for the same pairs. Explain why α must not be spent in full on every test, then why Holm must stop at the first failure. Finish by stating what you cannot say about the printing times from these p-values alone.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I keep only the pair with the smallest p-value; my family now contains just one test.”

    The family was defined by the questions examined together, before seeing the results. Restore AB, AC and BC and their count 3. Removing comparisons because they are less appealing does not remove the opportunities for false rejection that they created.

    Review every line in the family

  • “With Holm, I can reject the last comparison even if the preceding one fails.”

    The increasing thresholds are available only as long as the previous rejections succeed. At the first failure, this procedure leaves all remaining comparisons unrejected. Redo the exercise variant on stopping.

    Practise with a blocked step

  • “An adjusted p-value is the probability that my hypothesis is false.”

    A p-value is calculated under a null hypothesis and a test model; adjustment sets a decision rule for a family. Neither the raw nor the adjusted p-value gives the probability that a hypothesis is true. Do not infer an effect size from rejection alone, either.

    Reread the assumptions and the scope of the result

Compare with the solution

There are 4 × 3 ÷ 2 = 6 comparisons. The threshold is 0.05 ÷ 6 = 1/120 exactly, or approximately 0.008333. Counting the pairs given in the hint checks this total.

Since 0.010 > 1/120, Bonferroni does not reject H_AB in this larger family. The same raw p-value can therefore lead to a different decision when the collective question changes. The other p-values are not given; no numerical decision about their hypotheses is possible.

This variant is a protocol choice, rather than permission to redefine the family after the results. Failure to reject AB does not demonstrate that the settings have the same mean printing time.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What defines the workshop’s family of comparisons?

The hypotheses examined and protected together in the protocol, before the results. Every pair of A, B and C gives AB, AC and BC, making 3 comparisons; keeping only the smallest p-value does not honestly redefine this family.

Recall 2What rule does Bonferroni use, and why is independence between tests unnecessary?

Each test is compared to α/m. If every p-value is valid under its own null hypothesis, the probability of any false rejection is at most the sum of the risks for the true hypotheses, and therefore at most α. A union bound does not require independence.

Recall 3How can you reconstruct Holm without forgetting its stopping condition?

Sort the p-values in increasing order, keeping their hypotheses. At rank r, compare to the threshold α/(m − r + 1). Reject and proceed if p ≤ threshold; at the first failure, reject neither that hypothesis nor any that follow. The rank starts at 1.

Recall 4What condition on p-values cannot be created by a multiplicity adjustment?

Under a true null hypothesis, we need Pr(p ≤ u) ≤ u for every threshold u between 0 and 1. This depends on the test and its model. Adjusting a list fixes neither an unsuitable test nor hidden trials outside the declared family.

Recall 5What does the familywise error rate guarantee, and what does the decision leave unknown about the settings?

It bounds the probability of at least 1 false rejection over repetitions of the protocol, under the stated assumptions. It does not give the probability that a particular conclusion is false. Rejection quantifies neither the effect nor its usefulness; failure to reject does not prove equality.
