Level 4 · C230·M5

Regularity and embeddings

Your goalEstablish, on a bounded interval, that finite slope energy controls the changes in a function (embedding of H¹ into Hölder continuous functions with exponent 1/2), then test this control on a family of explicit profiles.

Before you start
c165-m5 : duality · c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c167-m5 : initial and boundary value problem · c230-m1 : test functions · c230-m2 : distributions · c230-m3 : weak derivatives · c230-m4 : Sobolev spaces

✿01 See the idea

A fictional rod is 1 m long. Its temperature is described by a profile u(x), in kelvins, where x is the position in metres. All values on this page are invented so that we can follow a single example through to the end: u(x) is x raised to the power α, with α = 3/4. The profile is 0 K at the left end and 1 K at the right end.

Near the left end, the curve rises almost vertically: its slope has no bound. Yet it has no jump, and the sum of the squared slope over the whole rod, which we will call slope energy, remains finite. The question is therefore: what does finite slope energy require of the curve itself?

Think of energy as a budget. To go from one point to another h metres away, the curve must spend some of this budget on its slope. With a fixed budget, the greatest possible change grows like the square root of h, rather than h. A curve can rise quickly over a very small interval, but the narrower the interval, the more budget it uses. A jump would require an infinite budget.

Embedding one function space into another means two things: every element of the first belongs to the second, and its size in the second is bounded above by a constant times its size in the first. Here, the first space is H¹, the space of functions whose weak derivative is square integrable; the second consists of continuous functions whose changes can be controlled (Hölder continuous functions).

This page considers a single embedding, a single bounded interval, dimension 1, and a family of profiles x to the power α. It does not teach the full theory of Sobolev spaces or what changes in higher dimensions.

The rule to remember

Finite slope energy rules out jumps: the change between two points a distance h apart is at most the square root of h times the square root of the energy.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A profile whose slope grows without bound while its energy stays finite

A fictional rod of length 1 m has profile u(x) = x to the power α with α = 3/4, u in K and x in m. We calculate its slope energy and its norm in H¹, test the estimate for the change between two points, then locate the exponent threshold. The same profile is used at every step.

  1. Define the quantities, spaces, and assumptions

    I is the open interval from 0 to 1 (the rod, in m). u is a real-valued function on I (temperature, in K). A weak derivative of u is an integrable function g on I such that the relation below holds for every test function φ (infinitely differentiable and 0 near the endpoints of I); we denote this function g by u′ (C230·M1 and C230·M3).

    ∫01uφ′dx=−∫01u′φdx

    L² denotes the square-integrable functions on I; H¹ is the set of functions u in L² whose weak derivative is also in L² (C230·M4). The norm is given by the following formula, taking the metre as the unit of length so that the two terms can be added as dimensionless quantities.

    ‖u‖H12=∫01u2dx+∫01(u′)2dx

    For γ between 0 (excluded) and 1, the Hölder seminorm [u]_γ is the smallest constant M such that |u(x) − u(y)| ≤ M |x − y|^γ for all x, y in the closed interval. Its unit is K per m to the power γ; for γ = 1, it is the Lipschitz constant.

    [u]γ=supx≠y|u(x)−u(y)||x−y|γ

    Assumptions for this page: dimension 1, a bounded interval, and real-valued functions. For a general function in H¹, we assume the existence of a continuous representative satisfying u(y) − u(x) = ∫ u′ between x and y (the fundamental theorem for H¹, whose proof is not repeated here). For our profile, this is the usual fundamental theorem on a closed interval contained in the open interval from 0 to 1, extended to x = 0 by continuity. Units c165-m5 to c167-m5, listed as prerequisites in the catalogue, are not used in this calculation.

  2. Check that the weak derivative is the ordinary derivative

    u(x) is x to the power α and is of class C¹ on I. A test function φ vanishes near 0 and 1: the product uφ vanishes at the endpoints, and the integral of its derivative is 0. The product rule then gives exactly the weak derivative relation, with the ordinary derivative.

    Thus u′(x) = α x to the power (α − 1), that is, α times x to the power minus 1/4: the slope is unbounded near 0, but we still need to see whether its square is integrable.

    Check: if φ reached the boundary, a boundary term would appear. The assumption that φ is 0 near the endpoints rules this out.

  3. Calculate the slope energy

    Square u′, integrate, then use the antiderivative of x to the power (δ − 1), valid for strictly positive δ.

    ∫01(αxα−1)2dx=α2∫01x2α−2dx=α22α−1

    The denominator requires 2α − 1 > 0: here 2 × 3/4 = 3/2, so 3/2 − 1 = 1/2. The numerator is 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16. The slope energy is 9/16 ÷ 1/2 = 9/8 K²/m.

    Check by the change of variable x = s to the power 4: the integral becomes 4α² times the integral of s to the power (4 × (2α − 2) + 3), that is, s to the power 1, whose integral is 1/2 between 0 and 1. The product 4 × 9/16 × 1/2 gives 9/8 K²/m again.

  4. Calculate the norm in H¹

    The term without a derivative is the integral of x to the power 2α. Its antiderivative is x to the power (2α + 1) divided by (2α + 1).

    ∫01x2αdx=12α+1

    Here 3/2 + 1 = 5/2, so the integral is 1 ÷ 5/2 = 2/5. The squared norm in H¹ is 2/5 + 9/8 = 61/40, a dimensionless number when the metre is the unit of length. The profile therefore belongs to H¹, as the threshold in the last step will confirm.

  5. Test the estimate for the change between two points

    The estimate follows in two steps. Write u(y) − u(x) as the integral of u′ between x and y, then apply Cauchy–Schwarz to u′ and the constant function 1 on [x, y], whose norm is the square root of y − x.

    u(y)−u(x)=∫xyu′(t)dt
    |u(y)−u(x)|2≤(y−x)∫xy(u′(t))2dt

    Bounding the integral over the smaller interval by the integral over the whole rod gives a bound on the Hölder seminorm with exponent 1/2 by the L² norm of u′.

    [u]12≤‖u′‖L2

    Choose x = 1/16 m and y = 1 m. Since 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4, then 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8 and 1/8 × 1/2 = 1/16, the fourth root of 1/16 is 1/2 and u(1/16) = 1/8 K. Thus u(y) − u(x) = 1 − 1/8 = 7/8 K, whose square is 49/64 K², over a width 1 − 1/16 = 15/16 m.

    The energy between x and y is calculated using the same antiderivative, and the square root of 1/16 is 1/4: 9/8 × (1 − 1/4) = 9/8 × 3/4 = 27/32 K²/m. The upper bound is 15/16 × 27/32 = 405/512. It exceeds 49/64 by 13/512: the inequality holds, with a small margin.

    Check using the whole rod: the squared Hölder quotient is 49/64 ÷ 15/16 = 49/60, below the total energy 9/8 by a margin of 37/120. The bound using all the energy is therefore correct, but less tight.

  6. Show that the slope is unbounded

    The embedding gives the exponent 1/2, but does not give a bounded slope. Measure the slope from 0, first at x = 1/16 m: u(1/16) ÷ 1/16 = 1/8 ÷ 1/16 = 2 K/m.

    Next take x = 1/16 × 1/16 = 1/256 m. Then u is 1/16 × 1/4 = 1/64 K, because the square root of 1/16 is 1/4. The slope from 0 is 1/64 ÷ 1/256 = 4 K/m.

    Every time x is divided by 16, the slope doubles. It therefore exceeds every bound: the profile is not Lipschitz continuous. Yet it is Hölder continuous with exponent 3/4 (accepted here without proof) and belongs to H¹, which guarantees only 1/2. Check: the quotient u(x) ÷ x to the power 1/2 equals x to the power 1/4 and remains below 1 for x below 1.

  7. Locate the exponent threshold

    The same energy is α²/(2α − 1) as long as 2α > 1. The condition for x to the power α to belong to H¹ is therefore α > 1/2.

    For α = 1/2, we have 2 × 1/2 − 1 = 0: the squared derivative is proportional to 1/x, and its integral diverges logarithmically. For α = 1/4, the squared derivative is proportional to x to the power minus 3 over 2, and the energy diverges faster than a logarithm; the exercise on truncated energy calculates some values.

    Check: the square root of x is Hölder continuous with exponent 1/2 and constant 1 because |√x − √y| ≤ √|x − y|, but it does not belong to H¹. The embedding therefore cannot be reversed.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the formulas, explain why the square root of the width appears in the estimate, why x to the power 3/4 satisfies it with a margin, and why the embedding does not give this profile its best exponent.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A function in H¹ has a bounded slope, so it is Lipschitz continuous.”

    Finite energy does not bound the slope at every point. Our profile has slopes from 0 of 2 then 4 K/m, without bound, while remaining in H¹. The embedding promises only the exponent 1/2.

    Review the unbounded slope in the example

  • “A Hölder continuous function with exponent 1/2 belongs to H¹.”

    The inclusion holds in only one direction. The square root of x is Hölder continuous with exponent 1/2 and constant 1, but 2 × 1/2 − 1 = 0 and its energy diverges. Reread the definition of an embedding, which compares two spaces in a fixed order.

    Reread what an embedding means

  • “The energy is finite on every truncated interval, so the function belongs to H¹.”

    Each truncated energy can be finite while growing without bound as we approach the endpoint. For exponent 1/4, we obtain 3/8 then 15/8 K²/m for two truncations approaching the endpoint more closely. We need a uniform bound, that is, a finite integral over the whole rod.

    Redo the exercise on truncated energy

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

Another exponent

On the same fictional rod, consider v(x) = x to the power β with β = 5/4. Show that v belongs to H¹, calculate its slope energy, then test the estimate for the change between x = 0 m and y = 1 m.

∫01(αxα−1)2dx=α2∫01x2α−2dx=α22α−1
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First check that 2β − 1 is strictly positive; the formula in the question can then be written with β in place of α.

Hint 2

Calculate 2 × β, subtract 1, then divide β² by the result. For the change, v(0) = 0 and v(1) = 1.

Compare with the solution

2 × 5/4 = 5/2, so 5/2 − 1 = 3/2 is strictly positive: v belongs to H¹. The numerator is 5/4 × 5/4 = 25/16. The energy is 25/16 ÷ 3/2 = 25/24 K²/m.

The change between 0 and 1 is 1 K over a width 1 m, so its square is 1, and the estimate requires 1 ≤ 1 × 25/24. This holds, with a margin 25/24 − 1 = 1/24.

Independent check: since 2β − 2 = 1/2, the integral of x to the power 1/2 is 2/3, and β² × 2/3 gives 25/24 again.

Check your reasoning

  • Check the sign of 2β − 1 before integrating.
  • Write the energy with its unit K²/m.
  • Compare the squared change with the width times the energy, including the margin.

Exercise 2 · Guided analysis

An energy that diverges

Let w(x) = x to the power 1/4. For ε in the open interval from 0 to 1, accept that the slope energy of w on the interval from ε to 1 is given by the formula shown, valid for α < 1/2. Calculate it for ε = 1/16 then for ε = 1/256, and draw conclusions about whether w belongs to H¹ and what the embedding says about w.

Eα(ε)=α2ε2α−1−11−2α
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Here α = 1/4, so α² = 1/16 and 1 − 2α = 1/2. This leaves ε to the power (2α − 1), that is, ε to the power minus 1/2.

Hint 2

For ε = 1/16, the square root of ε is 1/4 and its reciprocal is 4. For ε = 1/256, the square root is 1/16 and its reciprocal is 16.

Compare with the solution

For ε = 1/16: the reciprocal of the square root of ε is 4, so the numerator is 4 − 1 = 3. Dividing by 1/2 gives 6, then × 1/16 gives 3/8 K²/m.

For ε = 1/256: the reciprocal is 16, the numerator is 16 − 1 = 15, dividing by 1/2 gives 30, then × 1/16 gives 15/8 K²/m.

When ε is divided by 16, the truncated energy is multiplied by 5 and continues to grow: it has no uniform bound. Thus the derivative of w is not square integrable over the whole rod, and w does not belong to H¹. The embedding does not apply and says nothing about w; we only know, by other means, that w is Hölder continuous with exponent 1/4.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate both truncated energies with their units.
  • Identify their unbounded growth, rather than just their finiteness.
  • Do not apply the embedding to a function outside H¹.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

How much energy does a jump cost?

A fictional function in H¹ has slope energy 4 K²/m. (a) Give the greatest possible change between two points a distance 1/16 m apart. (b) A ramp with constant slope rises from 0 to 1 K over a width 1/16 m: calculate its energy and compare it with the budget 4. (c) What is the energy of this ramp for a width 1/256 m, and what does this say about a jump?

|u(y)−u(x)|2≤(y−x)∫xy(u′(t))2dt
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For (a), apply the estimate with energy at most 4: the squared change is at most the width times 4.

Hint 2

For (b), the slope is the height divided by the width; the energy is its square multiplied by the width.

Hint 3

For (c), compare with the quotient of the squared height divided by the width.

Compare with the solution

(a) The squared change is at most 1/16 × 4 = 1/4 K², so the change is at most 1/2 K, because 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. This bound is attained by a ramp with constant slope (the equality case of Cauchy–Schwarz): 1/4 ÷ 1/16 = 4.

(b) The slope is 1 ÷ 1/16 = 16 K/m. The energy is 16 × 16 × 1/16 = 256 × 1/16 = 16 K²/m, well above the budget 4: this ramp is excluded from the class.

(c) For a width 1/256, the energy becomes 1 ÷ 1/256 = 256 K²/m. A change of 1 K over a width h costs 1/h, which grows without bound as h tends to 0: an exact jump would cost infinite energy, consistent with the continuity of functions in H¹.

Check your reasoning

  • Use the estimate with the width and energy, without assuming a constant slope everywhere.
  • Distinguish the bound obtained from the equality case of the ramp.
  • Relate the cost of one over h to the impossibility of a jump in H¹.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which inequality expresses, in dimension 1, that H¹ embeds into the Hölder continuous functions with exponent 1/2?

|u(y) − u(x)|² ≤ |y − x| × (integral of (u′)² between x and y). It follows that the Hölder seminorm with exponent 1/2 is at most the L² norm of u′.

Recall 2Where does the square root of the width in the estimate come from?

From u(y) − u(x) = integral of u′ × 1 between x and y, followed by Cauchy–Schwarz: the L² norm of the constant function 1 on this interval is the square root of y − x.

Recall 3For which exponents α does the profile x to the power α belong to H¹ on the interval from 0 to 1, and what is its energy?

We need 2α − 1 > 0, that is, α > 1/2; the energy is then α² divided by (2α − 1).

Recall 4Why can the inclusion of H¹ into Hölder continuous functions not be reversed?

The square root of x is Hölder continuous with exponent 1/2, but its slope energy diverges: the embedding guarantees an exponent for the whole class H¹; it does not characterize the class.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without support: place H¹ on a bounded interval in dimension 1; write the difference u(y) − u(x) as an integral of u′; apply Cauchy–Schwarz with the constant function; obtain the squared change bounded above by the width times the energy; identify the exponent 1/2; check for x to the power α that the energy is α² divided by (2α − 1) and that the threshold is 1/2; remember the limits: one direction only, energy over the whole rod, dimension 1.
