Level 2 · C074·M3

Confounding

Your goalIdentify a common cause and use calculations to show how group composition can distort a causal interpretation of the difference between group means.

Before you start
c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c073-m5 : interaction and repeated measures · c009-m5 : reading notes · c074-m1 : causal effect · c074-m2 : randomization

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional workshop, everyone assembles the same small cardboard display stand. An instruction sheet is available, but using it is optional. Novices take it more often; experienced people more often do without it and work faster. Can we attribute the longer time taken by people who use the instructions to the instructions themselves?

The trap is to compare groups that already differ before this choice. Prior experience can explain both the use of instructions and the assembly time. It then acts as a confounding variable: a common cause of the choice being studied and the outcome. Confounding means this mixing of the effect we want to study with differences that accompany the choice.

In the diagram, an arrow expresses an assumed causal influence. The path use of instructions ← experience → time allows an association between using instructions and assembly time even if the instructions have no effect. The direct arrow use of instructions → time represents the question being asked, rather than its answer. The arrows come from our assumptions about the scene; the table of times does not prove them.

All counts and times are fictional. To make the calculation checkable, each novice in this table takes 12 min and each experienced person takes 6 min, with or without instructions. This equality is a property of the constructed observations; in a real study, it would not be enough to establish a 0 causal effect.

The rule to remember

Before attributing a difference to an action, ask what could influence both that action and its outcome. Then compare groups with the same experience, keeping the assumptions visible.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Instructions that seem to slow down assembly

With instructions: 8 novices and 2 experienced people. Without instructions: 2 novices and 8 experienced people. Each novice takes 12 min; each experienced person takes 6 min. Let us calculate the difference as “with minus without,” then find where it comes from.

  1. Define the choice, the outcome, and the categories

    A indicates the use of instructions: A = 1 means with instructions and A = 0 means without instructions. Y is an individual’s assembly time, in minutes, including time spent using the instructions. C is experience measured before this choice: N for novice, E for experienced. A and C are categories, with no physical unit.

    The letter a denotes a value of A; na is the total number of people in that group. The subscripts N and E indicate an experience category. Here mN denotes the mean for novices, 12 min, and mE the mean for experienced people, 6 min. We can use the same category means in the groups with and without instructions because they are identical in this table.

    We assume the same object to assemble, the same start and end points for timing, and complete observations. Using the instructions is voluntary, with no random assignment. The assumed causal role of C requires information from before the choice; it does not follow solely from the correlation visible in the data.

  2. Calculate the mean with instructions

    First add the counts: 8 + 2 = 10 people. To recover the sum of the times, multiply each individual time by the number of people concerned: 8 × 12 = 96 min for novices; 2 × 6 = 12 min for experienced people.

    The sum is 96 + 12 = 108 min. Divide it by the count, because a mean distributes this sum across everyone in the group. The bar above Y denotes this observed mean; its subscript indicates the value of A.

    Y¯1=10810=54/5 min=10.8 min

  3. Calculate the mean without instructions, then the raw difference

    Without instructions, the count is 2 + 8 = 10 people. Novices account for 2 × 12 = 24 min; experienced people account for 8 × 6 = 48 min. The sum is 24 + 48 = 72 min.

    The mean without instructions is 72/10 = 36/5 min, or 7.2 min. Keep the exact fractions for subtraction; here the displayed decimals are also exact.

    Δbrut=Y¯1−Y¯0=54/5−36/5=18/5 min

    Δ, the letter delta, denotes a difference here. The raw difference is 3.6 min. The positive sign says only that the group with instructions is slower on average. Each group contains 10 people, but equal counts do not guarantee equal composition.

  4. Show the weights in the mixture

    pa is the proportion of novices in group a: their count divided by na, with no unit. The complement 1 − pa is the proportion of experienced people. A weighted average multiplies each category mean by the share of people it represents.

    Y¯a=pamN+(1−pa)mE

    With instructions, p is 8/10 = 4/5; without instructions, 2/10 = 1/5. The shares of experienced people are 1/5 and 4/5, respectively. Check the weights: 4/5 + 1/5 = 1 and 1/5 + 4/5 = 1.

    The slower category therefore receives more weight with instructions. Taking a simple average of the times 12 and 6 in each group would erase this difference in counts and would not reproduce the observed means.

  5. Compare people with the same experience

    A stratum is a subgroup defined by the same category of C. Among novices, we compare 12 − 12 = 0 min. Among experienced people, 6 − 6 = 0 min. The overall difference therefore disappears within each observed stratum.

    We can locate the exact arithmetic source of the raw difference. Since the category means here are identical with and without instructions, call them mN and mE. Subtracting the weighted averages and collecting terms gives:

    Δbrut=(p1−p0)(mN−mE)

    The differences are 4/5 − 1/5 = 3/5 for the proportions and 12 − 6 = 6 min for the times. Their product is 3/5 × 6 = 18/5 min, or 3.6 min. We recover the raw difference by another route: in this table, it comes entirely from the mixture of categories.

  6. Check the result and limit the conclusion

    The means 10.8 and 7.2 min are indeed between 6 and 12 min. The group with more novices has the higher mean. Summing individual times and calculating with proportions give the same difference; the units remain minutes.

    This table refutes the reasoning “slower with instructions, therefore slowed down by the instructions.” It does not demonstrate that the instructions are useless: the people being compared still differ, even within an experience category. Another unmeasured common cause could remain. Conversely, a 0 raw difference could hide confounding and an effect that cancel each other out.

    The random assignment seen earlier aims to make the choice independent of prior characteristics, without guaranteeing perfect balance in a small sample. Comparing people with the same experience addresses the visible imbalance here; the conditions needed for a causal conclusion will be studied in the unit on identification assumptions.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the means. Recover the difference in minutes from the counts, then explain why equal total counts do not prevent confounding. Finally, distinguish what the calculation demonstrates from what the causal diagram assumes.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The instructions add 3.6 min to assembly.”

    This difference compares people whose experience is distributed differently. Repeat the comparisons within each category and the product “difference in proportions × difference in times.” The observed difference does not automatically measure an effect of the instructions.

    Review comparisons with the same experience

  • “The groups are the same size, so they are comparable.”

    The total group sizes are 10 people, but the proportions of novices are 4/5 and 1/5. Equal sizes do not make the categories interchangeable. Reread the counts in each row of the diagram.

    Reread the group composition

  • “Any variable related to time is a confounding variable.”

    Look for its causal role and its place in time. Here, experience exists before the use of instructions and can influence both the choice and the time. Time spent reading the instructions comes after this choice and may be part of its effect: removing it would change the question being answered. Correlation alone is not enough.

    Review the variables and assumptions

Exercise 1 · Identification and justification

Reconstruct the trap

Without looking at the first diagram, name A, Y, and C in the workshop. Draw the influences that make C a confounding variable. Does the difference between the means alone let you draw these arrows with certainty?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Look for what exists before the choice to use instructions.

Hint 2

A common cause can influence both the use of instructions and the assembly time.

Compare with the solution

A is the use of instructions, Y is time in min, and C is prior experience. The assumed influences are C → A and C → Y. The path A ← C → Y creates an association that is not the effect of A on Y.

The means alone do not prove these influences. In the scene, they are motivated by prior experience and the voluntary use of instructions. Keep this distinction between a causal assumption and an observed relationship.

Check your reasoning

  • You place experience before the use of instructions.
  • You draw the arrowheads from C toward A and toward Y.
  • You do not use the means as proof of the direction of the arrows.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and checking

Give the categories equal weight

Still using the workshop times, construct a comparison that gives each category the weight 1/2 in each of the groups with and without instructions. Calculate the means with these weights and their difference. Explain why this calculation does not change the original observations.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The weights must sum to 1; keep the category times.

Hint 2

With equal weights, add the category times, then divide by 2.

Compare with the solution

With instructions: (12 + 6)/2 = 18/2 = 9 min. Without instructions, the same times receive the same weights, so the mean is also 9 min.

The difference is 9 − 9 = 0 min. We have set a common composition for comparison; we have neither sampled new people nor changed their times. The observed means remain 10.8 and 7.2 min.

This result locates the role of the mixture in this table. Interpreting it as a causal effect would still require justification that experience is enough to make the people comparable. The calculation alone does not provide that guarantee.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain 9 min in each group and a difference of 0 min.
  • You distinguish the comparison weights from the observed counts.
  • You retain the limitation on causal interpretation.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same scene

Would a larger table be convincing?

Someone proposes copying each workshop observation 2 times. They claim: “With more rows, the difference will prove that the instructions slow people down.” Determine what happens to the means and the difference. Then distinguish this duplication from a new study and from random assignment.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In each group, the sum of the times and the count are multiplied by the same factor.

Hint 2

Duplication changes neither the distribution of experience nor how the instructions were chosen.

Compare with the solution

The mean is a quotient. Multiplying its numerator and denominator by 2 preserves its value: 10.8 min with instructions, 7.2 min without instructions, and a difference of 3.6 min. The proportions of novices remain 4/5 and 1/5.

These copies are not new independent observations. Even a larger number of new people, recruited with the same imbalance, would not automatically make the comparison causal. Random assignment changes the rule for assigning the instructions; duplication does not change it.

The remedy starts with a justified comparison design: measure the relevant prior characteristics and, if the context allows it, assign the instructions at random. Adding rows to the table does not close the common cause path.

Check your reasoning

  • You justify the unchanged result using the quotient, without inventing a new difference.
  • You distinguish copies from new observations.
  • You explain why the assignment rule matters for confounding.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What role makes experience a confounding variable in the scene?

It precedes the use of instructions and can influence both that choice and the time: A ← C → Y. This causal role is an assumption about the scene, rather than simply the presence of a correlation.

Recall 2How can 0 differences within categories coexist with an overall difference?

The categories do not receive the same weights in each group. Here the slower novices are more strongly represented with instructions; the overall means therefore mix time and composition.

Recall 3How else can you check the raw difference in the workshop?

Since category times are identical with and without instructions, multiply the difference in the proportions of novices by the difference in times: 3/5 × 6 min = 18/5 min. This identity depends on the equalities specific to this table.

Recall 4Does a 0 difference among people with the same experience prove that the instructions are useless?

No. It describes the people observed within each category. Other common causes may remain; the conditions that make the comparison causal still need justification.

Recall 5Why do equal counts or more rows fail to resolve confounding?

Size does not guarantee the same composition or change the assignment rule. Copies even preserve the means exactly; they provide no new independent observations.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the reasoning from memory: name the choice and the outcome; look for a prior common cause; calculate the means with their actual weights; compare people with the same experience; explain the difference between an observed difference and a causal effect. Finish by naming what the table still does not allow you to claim.
