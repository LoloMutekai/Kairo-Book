Normalization

Your goalNormalize the pre-activations of a unit over a small batch: center them, divide by the square root of σ² + ε, then restore scale and shift with γ and β, while checking the mean and variance.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A unit in a dense layer computes, for each example, a weighted sum of its inputs plus a bias. We call this number the pre-activation z: it is the input to the activation function. Take a fictional unit and a small batch of 3 examples processed together. Its pre-activations are z1 = 1, z2 = 4 and z3 = 10, in arbitrary units (a.u.).

During training, the weights of the preceding layers change at each step. The z values of a given unit can therefore shift or spread out from one batch to the next, and the activation function receiving them may encounter a different scale each time. Batch normalization acts inside the network: it centers the batch z values around 0, brings their spread to a comparable scale, then lets the network choose the final scale.

There are three successive steps. Center: subtract the batch mean μ. Scale: divide by a batch standard deviation, computed with a small quantity ε added to the variance to prevent division by 0. Restore: multiply by a factor γ and add a shift β. Like the weights, γ and β are parameters that the optimizer can adjust; here we fix them and train nothing. Without this last step, each unit would be restricted to a mean of 0, which could deprive the next layer of a useful adjustment.

This calculation resembles standardizing a data column in a pipeline (c067-m5). The difference is where and when it happens: here the mean and variance come from the current batch, inside the network, and are recalculated for each batch.

The values are fictional. They are chosen to give exact square roots; they do not come from any trained network. This page teaches the calculation, rather than its effect: it measures no improvement in training.

The rule to remember

Center using μ, scale by dividing by √(σ² + ε), then restore the scale with γ and the shift with β; the mean and variance come from the batch, one unit at a time.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Normalize three pre-activations of a unit

A batch of 3 examples gives a fictional unit the pre-activations z₁ = 1, z₂ = 4 and z₃ = 10 a.u. We fix ε = 1/16 a.u.² (0.0625 in decimal form), γ = 2 and β = 1. Let us calculate the output y for each example, then check its mean and variance.

  1. Define the quantities and assumptions

    n is the number of examples in the batch, here 3. For example i, with i from 1 to n, the pre-activation of a single unit is written z, indexed by i, in a.u. μ is the batch mean, in a.u. σ² is the batch variance, in a.u.²: the mean of the squared deviations from μ, dividing by n. ε is a strictly positive real number, in the same units as σ². ẑ is the standardized value, which is dimensionless. γ, dimensionless, and β, in a.u., are the output scale and shift; y is the output.

    μ=1n∑i=1nzi
    σ2=1n∑i=1n(zi−μ)2

    Assumptions: only one unit is processed at a time; the statistics come from the entire batch itself; ε is the same for every example. Dividing by n, rather than n − 1, is a convention here; the third exercise compares the two.

  2. Center: mean and deviations

    We add the three values: 1 + 4 = 5, then 5 + 10 = 15. Dividing by n gives the mean: μ = 15 / 3 = 5 a.u. We then subtract μ from each value: d1 = 1 − 5 = -4, d2 = 4 − 5 = -1 and d3 = 10 − 5 = 5.

    Check: a mean is a balance point, so the deviations cancel out. Here -4 + (-1) + 5 = 0. If this sum is not 0, the error is in μ or in a subtraction.

  3. Measure the spread: batch variance

    Deviations with opposite signs would cancel in a simple sum, so we square them: the square of -4 is 16, that of -1 is 1 and that of 5 is 25, in a.u.² Their sum is 16 + 1 + 25 = 42. We divide by n: σ² = 42 / 3 = 14 a.u.²

    Check by another method: the mean of the squared z values, minus the square of the mean, gives the same variance. This second method, recalculated separately in the unit checking file, also gives 14.

  4. Prepare the denominator with ε

    We add ε to the variance: σ² + ε = 14 + 1/16 = 225/16 a.u.² We still need to find a positive r such that r² = 225/16. We look for a numerator and a denominator whose squares match those of the fraction: 15 and 4 work. So r = 15 over 4, or 15/4 a.u. (3.75 in decimal form).

    σ2+ε=225/16=15/4

    Check: 15/4 × 15/4 = 225/16, which is indeed σ² + ε. The values were chosen to make this square root exact; in general, it is irrational and is computed numerically. ε acts as a safeguard: if σ² were 0, the denominator would remain strictly positive.

  5. Scale: divide each deviation

    z^i=zi−μσ2+ε

    Each deviation is divided by r = 15/4, which makes the value dimensionless: -4 divided by 15/4 gives ẑ1 = -16/15 (≈ -1.07); -1 divided by 15/4 gives ẑ2 = -4/15 (≈ -0.27); 5 divided by 15/4 gives ẑ3 = 4/3 (≈ 1.33).

    Check: their sum remains 0, so their mean does too. Their variance is σ² / (σ² + ε), or 14 divided by 225/16, giving 224/225, approximately 0.996. It approaches 1 without reaching it as long as ε is positive: stating “variance equal to one” would be inaccurate.

  6. Restore the scale: γ and β

    yi=γz^i+β

    With γ = 2, we first multiply: 2 × (-16/15) = -32/15, 2 × (-4/15) = -8/15 and 2 × 4/3 = 8/3. Then we add β = 1: y1 = -17/15 (≈ -1.13), y2 = 7/15 (≈ 0.47) and y3 = 11/3 (≈ 3.67).

    Check: since the mean of ẑ is 0, the mean of y is β. Here the sum of the three outputs is n × β = 3 × 1, so the mean is 1. The variance of y is γ² times that of ẑ: 4 × 224/225 = 896/225, or approximately 3.98 a.u.²

  7. Interpret the result without overstating it

    The same z placed in another batch would receive a different value of ẑ, because μ and σ² depend on the entire batch. This dependence follows from the calculation; it is not a data entry error.

    This unit establishes neither that such normalization speeds up real training nor that ε = 1/16 is a good setting: ε is large here to keep the fractions readable. It also does not cover backpropagation through μ and σ², or the use of stored statistics to process an isolated example after training.

Explain it in your own words

Explain why ε is placed under the square root, alongside σ², and why the variance of ẑ stays slightly below one as long as ε is positive. Also say how γ and β change the mean and variance of the output.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “After normalization, the output always has a mean of 0 and a variance of one.”

    Only the standardized value ẑ is centered, and its variance is σ² / (σ² + ε), so slightly less than 1. The output y has mean β = 1 and variance γ² times that of ẑ, or 3.98 a.u.² in the example.

    Review the step that restores the scale

  • “To scale, divide the deviation by the variance.”

    The variance is in a.u.² and the deviation in a.u.: the quotient would retain inverse units. You must divide by the square root. In the example, dividing d₁ = -4 by 225/16 would give -0.28, instead of ẑ₁ = -1.07.

    Review the preparation of the denominator

  • “A batch containing a single example is normalized like any other.”

    With a single example, the mean is that example, the deviation is 0 and so is the variance: ẑ is 0 regardless of z, and the output is β. Without ε, we would divide 0 by 0. The second exercise covers this limiting case with a constant batch.

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

A different scale and shift

Use the standardized values ẑ₁, ẑ₂ and ẑ₃ from the example again. This time set γ = 3 and β = 5. Calculate the three outputs and check that their sum is n × β.

Hint 1

There is nothing to recalculate before multiplying: ẑ depends on neither γ nor β.

Hint 2

Multiply each ẑ by γ, then add β. To check, compare the sum of the outputs with 3 × 5.

Compare with the solution

Multiplications: 3 × (-16/15) = -16/5, 3 × (-4/15) = -4/5 and 3 × 4/3 = 4.

Adding β = 5: y1 = 9/5 (≈ 1.80), y2 = 21/5 (≈ 4.20) and y3 = 9.

Check your reasoning

  • ẑ reused without computing a new mean or variance.
  • Multiplication by γ before adding β.
  • Output mean equal to β, checked using the sum.

Exercise 2 · Limiting case

A batch in which all values are equal

The three pre-activations in a batch are all 5 a.u. With ε = 1/16, γ = 2 and β = 1, calculate μ, σ², the denominator, ẑ and y. What would happen if ε were 0?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate the deviations from the mean: all the numbers are equal.

Hint 2

For the denominator, find a positive r such that r² = ε, since σ² is 0.

Compare with the solution

The mean of three values equal to 5 is 5. Each deviation is 0, so σ² = 0.

The denominator is √(0 + 1/16) = 1/4, because 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16. Then ẑ = 0 divided by 1/4, giving 0, for each example, and y = 2 × 0 + 1 = 1.

With ε equal to 0, we would have 0 / 0: the value is undefined. With positive ε, the output exists, but it no longer depends on z: the batch passes on no information about the inputs, only β.

Check your reasoning

  • 0 deviations, hence 0 variance.
  • Positive square root of ε calculated and checked.
  • Conclusion that the output is β for every example.
  • Case of 0 ε identified as undefined division.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

Divide by n or by n − one

The code reviewed in the milestone standardizes a column using the sample standard deviation, which divides the sum of squared deviations by n − 1. For the batch in the example, calculate this variance, compare it with σ² computed by dividing by n, and give their ratio.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

You already know the sum of squared deviations from the variance step.

Hint 2

Divide it by n − 1, then divide this result by the batch variance.

Compare with the solution

We have n − 1 = 2. The sample variance is 42 / 2 = 21 a.u.², compared with 14 a.u.² when dividing by n.

The ratio is 21 / 14 = 3/2, that is, n / (n − 1): the same sum is divided by a smaller number. The standard deviation, and therefore the standardized values, also change.

Neither convention is “the true one”: you must choose and remain consistent. The difference decreases as n grows, since n / (n − 1) approaches 1. For a batch of 3 examples, it is still substantial.

Check your reasoning

  • Same sum of squares reused.
  • Ratio equal to n / (n − one).
  • Neither convention presented as the only correct one.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which statistics are used to normalize a unit, and what are they calculated over?

The mean μ and variance σ² of that unit’s pre-activations, calculated over the examples in the current batch. Each unit has its own μ and σ².

Recall 2Why divide by √(σ² + ε) rather than by σ?

ε, strictly positive and in the same units as σ², prevents division by 0 when the batch is constant. It also makes the variance of ẑ slightly less than 1.

Recall 3What are the mean and variance of y = γẑ + β?

The mean is β, because the mean of ẑ is 0. The variance is γ² times the variance of ẑ, or γ² σ² / (σ² + ε).

Recall 4Why does the output for one example depend on the other examples in the batch?

Because μ and σ² come from the entire batch: the same z, in another batch, receives a different ẑ. In a batch containing a single example, ẑ is always 0.

Reconstruct the reasoning

One unit, one batch: calculate μ, then σ² by dividing by n; form √(σ² + ε), with ε positive and in the units of σ²; divide each deviation by this square root to obtain ẑ, with mean 0 and variance σ² / (σ² + ε); apply γ, then β to obtain y, with mean β. Keep the assumptions in mind: batch statistics, fictional values, no measured improvement in training.
