Level 2 · C092·M5

Comparison with a baseline and ablation

Your goalCompare forecasts against a common baseline, then isolate the effect of removing a piece of information without extending the conclusion beyond the trial.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

Before opening a greenhouse, you want to forecast whether it will rain in the next 6 hours. A model uses recent humidity, among other information. It sometimes makes a good forecast, sometimes a bad one. How can you tell whether it adds anything compared with a much simpler rule?

A baseline is a comparison rule chosen before seeing the results. Here, R always forecasts the rain frequency estimated over a separate earlier period: 0.50. It provides the starting point to beat. It does not represent the true outcome: that will be observed later.

The full model, called M, receives the planned information, including recent humidity. Its version A removes only that information and uses the missing data procedure fixed before the trial. This controlled removal is an ablation. The remaining rules and their settings are kept, with no retraining. Comparing M with R assesses relative performance; comparing M with A examines this component's contribution in this setup.

The entire scene is fictional: the forecasts, past frequency and outcomes are exercise data. We are evaluating forecasts already supplied; we are not building a weather model. A good average over these days promises neither good future weather forecasts nor better greenhouse management.

The rule to remember

Keep the same cases, the same horizon, the same information available at the time of the forecast and the same score; for an ablation, change only the stated component.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Does the greenhouse model add anything?

Over 4 consecutive days with no exclusions, we keep the forecasts in the diagram, then the outcomes y = (1, 0, 1, 0). R forecasts 1/2 throughout. M forecasts (3/4, 1/4, 1/4, 1/4); A forecasts (1/2, 1/2, 1/2, 1/4). Let us calculate their scores and the differences specified before the trial.

  1. Fix what makes the comparison fair

    Each forecast concerns whether it will rain at least once at the greenhouse during the 6 hours after opening. The rain criterion and the instrument used to record it are identical. The forecast is saved before this window; the information for the day is what is available at that moment. The baseline uses only the past period, never the trial outcomes.

    Before the outcomes, we fix the days, horizon, score, baseline and removal of humidity. An ablation protocol must also say whether it retrains the model: here, it does not. Retraining would have posed a different question, about the best model rebuilt without this information. The outputs are supplied, not adjusted to obtain the results in the diagram.

  2. Name the score and the two questions

    We return to the Brier score for a binary event. i is the day index, from 1 to n = 4; n counts the cases. yᵢ is the unitless indicator equal to 1 if the event occurs, 0 otherwise. pᵢ is the forecast probability of that event, between 0 and 1, with no units. The individual score ℓᵢ is the squared difference between pᵢ and yᵢ; B is its average over the same n cases. Both scores have no units and are smaller when the forecast is better according to this criterion.

    ℓi=(pi−yi)2;B=1n∑i=1nℓi

    The symbol ∑ asks us to add over all the days, including the endpoints. On B, the superscript letters M, R and A name the method: they are not powers. Δ denotes a difference in scores: Δref = Bᴿ − Bᴹ and Δabl = Bᴬ − Bᴹ. A positive difference favors M. These are absolute score differences, not percentages or success rates.

    The descriptive calculation does not assume that the days are independent. To generalize to a future season, their dependence and changes in regime will need to be examined; these fictional cases provide no such evidence.

  3. Calculate M's losses day by day

    Squaring penalizes the difference from what actually happens. Day 1: (3/4 − 1) = -1/4, then (-1/4) squared = 1/16. Day 2: 1/4 − 0 = 1/4, whose square is 1/16.

    Day 3: 1/4 − 1 = -3/4, whose square is 9/16. This is the day when M forecast a low probability of rain, yet it rained. Day 4: 1/4 − 0 = 1/4, whose square is 1/16.

    BM=1/16+1/16+9/16+1/164=3/16

    To spell out the addition: the first terms give 1/8, the next ones 5/8, making 3/4 in total. Dividing by 4 gives 3/16, exactly 0.1875 with no units. We keep fractions until the end.

  4. Calculate R and A on the same outcomes

    For R, the differences are -1/2, 1/2, -1/2 and 1/2. Their squares are respectively 1/4, 1/4, 1/4 and 1/4: forecasting 1/2 gives the same square for either binary outcome. The pairwise sums are 1/2 and 1/2, making 1 in total; the average is Bᴿ = 1 ÷ 4 = 0.2500.

    For A, the first days use 1/2, like R. Their scores are therefore 1/4, 1/4, 1/4. The last uses 1/4, like M, and gives 1/16. We add 1/2 and 5/16, making 13/16; dividing by 4 gives Bᴬ = 13/64 ≈ 0.2031. The symbol ≈ indicates only rounding for display.

    The cases, outcomes and denominator are identical. A does not have to match R: the baseline is an external rule; the ablation keeps the other information used by M.

  5. Compare without promising more than the calculation shows

    Against R: Δref = 1/4 − 3/16 = 1/16, exactly 0.0625 in score in favor of M. Against A: Δabl = 13/64 − 3/16 = 1/64, exactly 0.015625. Removing humidity therefore worsens the average score of the fixed version here. The difference against the baseline does not by itself measure the effect of humidity.

    M has the best average score, followed by A, then R. Yet on day 3, the loss for M exceeds the loss for R by 9/16 − 1/4 = 5/16. A better average does not mean winning on every case.

    This assessment establishes neither correct calibration nor a useful greenhouse response. Calibration compares forecast probabilities with observed frequencies over enough comparable cases. A response requires its own assessment of consequences and costs: a good forecast does not prove that an action was taken or that it helped.

  6. Check by another route

    If y = 0, the score is p squared; if y = 1, it is (1 − p) squared. This approach recovers the same losses without repeating the signed differences. All scores lie between 0 and 1: a negative square or an average outside this interval would indicate an error.

    Also check the paired differences, meaning those calculated for each same day. R minus M gives (1/4 − 1/16), (1/4 − 1/16), (1/4 − 9/16), (1/4 − 1/16). Their average again gives 1/16. A minus M gives the same differences on the first days, then 1/16 − 1/16 on the last; the average again gives 1/64.

    Both interpretations still need to be defended: M wins on this score and these cases; its advantage may change with the weather regime, the chosen baseline or the handling of missing information. The ablation describes removal in this fixed system, not a universal cause of good forecasts.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain to someone who wants to keep M and to someone else who prefers R which comparison answers their question. Then reconstruct the loss on the rainy day with a poor forecast and explain why you must keep it.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The baseline is the observed truth.”

    Separate R, a forecast available before the outcome, from y, the later outcome. The score compares each method with y; the comparison then compares their scores. A baseline adjusted using the outcomes of the days to be evaluated receives forbidden information.

    Review the forecasts before the outcome

  • “M beats R: humidity explains the entire gain.”

    R and M differ in more than humidity. To isolate its removal in this setup, compare M with A, keeping the other rules fixed. The difference Δabl remains specific to these cases and this handling of missing information; it is not the difference Δref.

    Revisit the two score differences

  • “An odd day can be removed from the assessment after the calculation.”

    If it is excluded because M loses on it, the selection uses the outcome. Keep all the planned cases. A real measurement defect requires a documented rule that is independent of which method wins and is applied to all three methods.

    Audit the day someone wants to remove

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · local calculation

The day that challenges the average

On day 3, y = 1, M forecasts 1/4 and R forecasts 1/2. Calculate their scores and the additional loss for M. What happens to the claim “M wins every day”?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate each difference from the outcome, then square it.

Hint 2

The smaller score is better. For the additional loss for M, subtract the score for R from the score for M.

Compare with the solution

M: (1/4 − 1) squared = 9/16. R: (1/2 − 1) squared = 1/4. The additional loss is 9/16 − 1/4 = 5/16, or 0.3125 with no units.

M therefore loses to R on this day. This refutes “every day” without contradicting its better average score overall. An average also includes the cases where the ranking reverses.

Check your reasoning

  • You show the differences and then the squares, with no negative score.
  • You obtain 5/16 in favor of R on that day.
  • You distinguish this day from the trial average.

Exercise 2 · Application · isolate the comparison

Two differences, two claims

Reconstruct Bᴬ from the forecasts made by A. Then calculate Bᴿ − Bᴹ and Bᴬ − Bᴹ. Which difference answers “does humidity help in this fixed version?” Give one limitation of your answer.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

On the first days, A and R forecast the same thing; on the last, A and M match.

Hint 2

Under each difference, write what changes between its two terms before interpreting its sign.

Compare with the solution

The losses for A are 1/4, 1/4, 1/4, 1/16. Their sum is 13/16; Bᴬ = 13/16 ÷ 4 = 13/64 ≈ 0.2031.

The comparison with the baseline gives Δref = 1/16. The ablation gives Δabl = 1/64: removing humidity makes the score higher. This latter difference answers the question about the component, in the fixed system and on these cases.

The conclusion does not guarantee the same advantage after retraining, on other days or with a different way of handling missing data. A variable related to humidity may also replace part of its contribution; the difference does not measure a universal value of this information.

Check your reasoning

  • You recover the losses for A and their common denominator.
  • You distinguish 1/16 from 1/64 without converting them into success rates.
  • You connect the conclusion to the specific removal and cite a relevant limitation.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · audit an assessment

The temptation to erase the inconvenient rain

After seeing the scores, someone excludes day 3 because M loses on it. Recalculate the scores for M and R and their difference on the remaining days. Can this new result replace the planned assessment? Propose a repair that allows a new fair evaluation.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Remove the score for that day from each sum; the number of remaining cases also changes.

Hint 2

Compare the new result with the complete assessment, then ask when the exclusion rule was decided.

Compare with the solution

For M, the sum becomes 3/4 − 9/16 = 3/16. For R, it becomes 1 − 1/4 = 3/4. There are 3 days left: truncated Bᴹ = 1/16, truncated Bᴿ = 1/4.

The difference becomes 3/16, or 0.1875, compared with 0.0625 for the complete assessment. This is 3 times the initial difference, simply because the unfavorable day was removed. The arithmetic is correct; replacing the planned assessment is biased by selection after the outcome.

Keep the complete assessment as the planned result and label the truncated assessment as exploratory. If a measurement defect criterion is justified independently of the scores, write it before a new series, apply it equally to the methods and evaluate on new cases without adjusting the rule after their outcomes. The current assessment does not become preregistered because its rule is written now.

Check your reasoning

  • You subtract the same day in both sums and divide by 3.
  • You obtain 3/16 and explain why its correct calculation does not validate the protocol.
  • You keep the planned assessment and propose a new evaluation with rules fixed before the outcomes.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the difference between baseline R and outcome y?

R is a rule stated before the outcome, chosen to compare performance. y is what happens afterwards and is used to calculate the losses for all the methods.

Recall 2What must be kept the same when comparing the greenhouse forecasts?

The same days, the same target and horizon, the information available at the time of the forecast, the same score and the same inclusion rules, fixed before the outcomes.

Recall 3What question does M versus A ask, and what must be specified?

It asks what removing humidity changes in the setup being evaluated. The handling of missing information and whether the other settings are fixed or retrained must be specified; here they are fixed.

Recall 4Why does a positive Bᴬ − Bᴹ favor M without proving a general gain?

The score is a loss to minimize: A loses more on average. The difference describes the cases and removal evaluated; it demonstrates neither calibration, nor the usefulness of a response, nor that the advantage persists elsewhere.

Recall 5Why does removing the unfavorable day after its outcome distort the assessment?

The rule selects cases according to observed performance. It favors the model in the very evaluation meant to assess it. Keep the planned assessment and test any new rule on a new series.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct this sequence using the greenhouse: target and horizon → baseline fixed before the outcome → same cases and same score → overall comparison → removal of a piece of information → paired difference → scope and limits. Justify the direction of each difference and explain why the unfavorable day remains in the assessment.
