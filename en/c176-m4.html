Level 3 · C176·M4

Control variates

Your goalBuild a control variate estimator, justify its centering, and choose the coefficient that minimizes its variance.

Before you start
c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c051-m5 : error diagnostics · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c017-m5 : reproducibility protocol · c176-m1 : generators and seeds · c176-m2 : Monte Carlo error · c176-m3 : antithetic variates

✿01 See the idea

A simulator processes batches of files. You want to estimate the mean time for a batch. Some runs contain mostly large batches: their mean time then suggests slower processing, even when the simulator follows its model exactly. But you already know the expected mean batch size. This information can help adjust for a fluctuation in the mix of batch sizes.

For each simulated batch, its time and size are kept together. If larger batches take longer, an excess of files in the run suggests subtracting part of the mean time. A run containing mostly small batches receives the opposite adjustment. The times actually calculated stay the same: we build another way to estimate their mean.

Size acts as a control variate: a quantity related to time whose expectation is known under the same simulation distribution. It is not a command that controls the program. We subtract its expectation, then use this deviation to offset part of the sampling noise.

The entire scene is fictional. The sizes, the time rule, and the draws below form a teaching model that can be checked with pencil and paper. They describe no measured performance. The small model also gives the true mean time: we can check the estimator against it, although that mean would normally be the quantity to discover.

The rule to remember

Pair each time with its control variate, center the control variate on its known expectation, then subtract its deviation multiplied by a coefficient fixed before the draws. Check that the mean is preserved and calculate the resulting variance.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A mean time less sensitive to random batch sizes

The batch contains X = 1 or 3 files, with equal probabilities. Its time Y equals a + bX + ε, with a = 10 s and b = 2 s per file. The noise ε takes the value −1 or +1 s, with equal probabilities, independently of X. We will compare means over n = 4 independent batches with the same distribution.

  1. Define the target and what is already known

    X is the random size of a batch, in files; Y is its time, in seconds. a is the base time; b converts files into seconds; ε represents the remaining noise. Each combination of size and noise has probability 1/(2 × 2) = 1/4. This denominator counts the possible outcomes for a batch, independently of the number n of batches drawn. Successive batches are sampled with replacement: their pairs (X, Y) are independent and have the same distribution.

    E denotes expectation, the weighted average using the probabilities of the distribution. The target μ = E[Y], in seconds, is the expected time. The known control variate mean is m = E[X], in files: m = (1 + 3)/2 = 2 files. Knowing this expectation does not force a finite run to have exactly this mean size.

    Var denotes variance, the expectation of the squared deviation from the expectation. Let V = Var(X), in squared files, and C = Cov(Y, X), in seconds times files. The covariance is C = E[(Y − μ)(X − m)]: here it indicates whether times and sizes deviate together from their means. These quantities are finite in our distribution; we will assume that V is strictly positive so that we can choose a coefficient by division.

  2. Create an adjustment that does not shift the target

    β is a coefficient fixed before the run, in seconds per file. The adjusted contribution Zβ, in seconds, subtracts the term β(X − m) from Y. The pairs stay together: the X used belongs to the same batch as Y.

    Zβ=Y−β(X−m)
    E[Zβ]=μ−β(m−m)=μ

    The deviation X − m has expectation 0, so the adjustment preserves the target for any fixed β. This is what “unbiased” means: the mean over possible runs is correct, though each individual result need not be. Centering on the observed mean of the run would instead cancel any adjustment to its mean.

    Write Xᵢ and Yᵢ for the values of batch i, with i ranging from 1 to n; n is the number of batches and has no unit. A bar denotes their arithmetic mean. The hat indicates an estimate of μ. The mean of the adjusted contributions is written as follows:

    μ^β=Y¯−β(X¯−m)

  3. Calculate the moments of the fictional distribution

    The noise is centered: E[ε] = 0 s, with Var(ε) = 1 s². The sizes differ from m by −1 or +1 file. Thus V = ((1 − 2) squared + (3 − 2) squared)/2 = 1 squared file. These calculations use the probabilities of the distribution, not the frequencies of a particular sample.

    The mean time is μ = 10 + 2 × 2 = 10 + 4 = 14 s. Independence and centering of the noise give Cov(ε, X) = 0, so C = bV = 2 × 1 = 2 seconds times files.

    Var(Y)=b2V+Var(ε)

    The factor b squared equals 4; its variance contribution is 4 s². The raw variance is therefore 4 + 1 = 5 s². We can check this without the decomposition: the possible times are 11, 13, 15, and 17 s, each with probability 1/4. The mean of their squared deviations from 14 s gives the same result.

  4. Choose the coefficient that removes the most variance

    Expanding the square of (Y − μ) − β(X − m), then taking its expectation, gives the variance of Zβ. The cross term contains the covariance C. X and Y must therefore be related within the same batch; assuming they are independent would remove precisely the useful information.

    Var(Zβ)=Var(Y)+β2V−2βC

    To find the minimum without differentiation, we collect the terms into a square. The star in β* denotes the optimal coefficient:

    β∗=CV;Var(Zβ)=Var(Y)−C2V+V(β−β∗)2

    Since V is positive, the last term is minimized when β = β*. Here β* = 2/1 = 2 s per file. The minimum variance is 5 − 4 = 1 s². All terms in the variance formula are in squared seconds; C/V is indeed in seconds per file.

    A large covariance therefore does not justify an arbitrary coefficient. If C is 0, the best coefficient is 0. If V is 0, the control variate is constant almost surely: its centered version adjusts nothing, and the quotient C/V must not be calculated.

  5. Check the distribution after adjustment

    With β* = b, substitute Y = a + bX + ε: Zβ* = a + bX + ε − b(X − m) = a + bm + ε. The term that depends on size disappears. Zβ* therefore equals 14 − 1 = 13 s or 14 + 1 = 15 s, with the same probabilities. Its mean remains 14 s and its variance is 1 s²: only the noise ε remains.

    The n batches are mutually independent and β is fixed. The variance of their mean is the variance of a contribution divided by n: for n = 4, it is 5/4 = 5/4 s² ≈ 1.25 s² without adjustment, compared with 1/4 = 1/4 s² ≈ 0.25 s² after adjustment. The standard error is the square root of this variance of the mean, in seconds.

    This comparison is exact under our model. It assumes no normal distribution. It describes neither the variance of physical times transformed by a program nor a saving in computation time: it concerns two estimators using the same number of batches.

  6. Work through an unbalanced run

    Here is a fixed fictional run of pairs (files; seconds): (1; 11), (3; 15), (3; 17), (3; 15). The same outcome can recur because sampling is with replacement.

    The sizes total 1 + 3 × 3 = 10 files; their mean is 10/4 = 2.5 files per batch. The times total 11 + 2 × 15 + 17 = 58 s; their raw mean is 58/4 = 14.5 s.

    The run contains, on average, 2.5 − 2 = 0.5 more file per batch than expected. The coefficient converts this excess into 2 × 0.5 = 1 s. We subtract this quantity:

    μ^β∗=14.5−2×(2.5−2)=13.5 s

    Check batch by batch: the adjusted contributions are 13, 13, 15, 13 s. Their sum is 54 s and 54/4 = 13.5 s. This gives the same result as the calculation using the means.

    The true mean of the model is 14 s. In this particular run, 14.5 s and 13.5 s are equally far from it, on opposite sides. A lower variance over possible runs does not promise a smaller error in every run.

  7. Distinguish the exact proof from practical tuning

    We knew C and V exactly because the fictional distribution is tiny. In a more complex simulation, we can estimate their ratio on an independent pilot run, then fix that coefficient for the run used in the final estimation. Centering and preservation of the mean are then still justified conditional on the chosen coefficient. Its actual optimality still needs to be checked.

    Estimating β and the mean from the same draws does not let us automatically reuse our proof for a fixed β: the coefficient then depends on the deviations it multiplies. Bias at a finite sample size and a different uncertainty analysis are possible. This does not make the practice universally wrong; the guarantee proved here does not cover it.

    Before using a control variate, document its distribution, the source of m, the pairing, the choice of coefficient, and the cost of calculating the control variate. Replacing an expectation with a merely plausible value can reduce spread while shifting the target, as the last exercise will show.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I subtract the observed mean of the control variate: surely it should be the reference.”

    To adjust the mean, the reference is m = E[X], known under the simulated distribution. Centering each control variate on the mean of the run makes the mean deviation exactly 0: the estimate remains the raw mean. Find the difference between 2.5 and 2 files in the example.

    Review the centering and the unbalanced run

  • “The control variate is related to time, so any adjustment reduces the variance.”

    The sign and magnitude of the coefficient matter. The variance contains a positive term in β squared and a cross term involving covariance. The coefficient β* minimizes their sum; the coefficient with the opposite sign in the exercise increases the spread.

    Test the wrong coefficient

  • “The variance falls, so this estimate is more accurate and unbiased.”

    Preservation of the expectation comes from correct centering and a fixed coefficient, not from the variance alone. Incorrect centering can keep the spread low while creating bias. Even with correct centering, the calculated run is no closer to the true mean than the raw run.

    Review the numerical check and its limits

Exercise 1 · Application · signs and units

Two sizes, the same positive noise

In the same model, consider the small batch (1 file; 13 s) and the large batch (3 files; 17 s). With m = 2 files and β = 2 s per file, predict the direction of each adjustment, then calculate the adjusted contributions. Why can they be equal?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write X − m for each batch before multiplying by β.

Hint 2

Subtracting a negative number increases the time that contributes to the estimator.

Compare with the solution

For the small batch, X − m = 1 − 2 = −1 file. Its contribution is 13 − 2 × (−1) = 15 s: we add time to bring its size to the reference.

For the large batch, X − m = 3 − 2 = 1 file. Its contribution is 17 − 2 × 1 = 15 s: we subtract the time attributed to its extra size.

Both batches have the same noise ε = +1 s. After exactly removing the size component, they therefore contribute the same value. This does not mean that their raw times become equal.

Check your reasoning

  • The small batch receives an increase and the large batch a decrease, with reasons given before the results.
  • The product seconds per file × files gives seconds.
  • The equality of the contributions is explained by the noise shared by the two cases, without changing the raw times.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis · variance and coefficient

The sign is reversed

We keep the correct expectation m, but fix β = -2 s per file. Use V = 1 squared file, C = 2 seconds times files, and Var(Y) = 5 s². Calculate the variance of an adjusted contribution, then that of the mean of 4 batches. Does the estimator remain unbiased?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In the term −2βC, the sign of β changes the sign of the entire term.

Hint 2

You can check using Var(Zβ*) + V(β − β*) squared.

Compare with the solution
Var(Zβ)=5+(-2)2×1−2×(-2)×2=17 s²

Check using the square: β − β* = -2 − 2 = -4 s per file. The square equals 16; multiplied by V, it adds 16 s² to the minimum variance 1 s². We again obtain 17 s², greater than the raw variance 5 s².

The variance of the mean is 17/4 = 17/4 s² ≈ 4.25 s². Correct centering and a fixed coefficient nevertheless preserve E[Zβ] = μ: unbiasedness and low variance are different properties.

Check your reasoning

  • The cross term increases the variance when the sign of the coefficient is reversed here.
  • The variance per batch and the variance of the mean are distinguished by division by n.
  • Unbiasedness is justified by the expectation of X − m, regardless of the poor variance result.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · detect a shifted target

A precise control variate, a wrong reference

We return to β = 2 s per file, but someone replaces m with a fixed reference m̃ = 3 files. Find the estimate obtained for our run and the bias over possible runs. Does its variance per batch change? Respond to the claim: “The spread is low, so the reference is suitable.”

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare Y − β(X − m̃) with Y − β(X − m) before substituting numbers.

Hint 2

Their difference is a constant: β(m̃ − m). A constant shifts the expectation but not the variance.

Compare with the solution

m̃ is read “m tilde” and denotes the incorrect fixed reference. The reference deviation is 3 − 2 = 1 file. The contribution is therefore shifted by β(m̃ − m) = 2 × 1 = 2 s. The bias, defined as the expectation of the estimate minus μ, is positive and equals 2 s.

In our run, the estimate becomes 14.5 − 2 × (2.5 − 3) = 15.5 s. Shifting the correctly centered estimate gives the same result: 13.5 + 2 = 15.5 s.

The variance per batch remains 1 s²: adding a constant does not change the deviations from the new expectation. A low variance therefore does not validate the reference. We must restore m = E[X] under the distribution being used, then redo the estimation and its check.

Check your reasoning

  • The shift β(m̃ − m) is obtained symbolically, with the correct sign.
  • The estimate for a run and the bias defined over possible runs are distinguished.
  • The unchanged variance is not presented as proof that the reference is correct.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What property of the control variate preserves the target mean?

Its expectation m is known under the same distribution as the simulation. For fixed β, E[β(X − m)] is 0; subtracting this term from Y preserves E[Y]. Centering on the mean of the run simply cancels the adjustment to that mean.

Recall 2How can you recover the optimal coefficient and its unit?

With the convention Zβ = Y − β(X − m), β* = Cov(Y, X)/Var(X), if Var(X) is positive. Here the coefficient is in seconds per file. Completing the square in the variance gives a nonnegative square term and shows the minimum.

Recall 3What must be paired, and what is independent in our proof?

The control variate and the time belong to the same batch; their covariance provides the useful information. Pairs from different batches are independent and have the same distribution: with fixed β, the variance of the mean is then Var(Zβ)/n.

Recall 4Why fix a coefficient estimated from an independent pilot run?

Conditional on this coefficient, the draws in the final run remain independent of its estimation: the proof for a fixed coefficient applies. Estimating the coefficient on the same run requires another justification; optimality is not guaranteed merely by having a covariance estimate.

Recall 5What does a smaller variance fail to prove?

It proves neither a better result in every run, nor unbiasedness if the centering is wrong, nor a saving in computation time. In the model, these points must be checked separately.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the steps: choose a quantity associated with Y whose expectation m is known; form X − m; justify that Y − β(X − m) preserves the expectation for fixed β; expand the variance and recover β*; calculate the adjusted mean; check centering, units, and independence between batches. Finally, explain how a wrong reference can keep variance low while creating bias.
