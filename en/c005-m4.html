Pythagoras and the square root

Your goalCalculate the diagonal of a rectangular frame using the Pythagorean theorem, then use the square root to find its length and check it.

Before you start
C004·M5 : rational equations · C005·M1 : angles · C005·M2 : triangles · C005·M3 : similarity

✿01 See the idea

You are holding a small rectangular photo frame 6 cm high and 8 cm wide. These dimensions are fictional, chosen to make the calculations work out exactly. You want to stretch a thread from one corner to the opposite corner, across the inside of the frame. What length of thread should you cut?

Following the edges would take 6 + 8 = 14 cm. The thread takes the shortest route, so it must be shorter. But by how much? Adding the edges does not answer the question, because it measures a different route.

The corner of the frame is a right angle (C005·M1). The vertical edge, the horizontal edge and the thread therefore form a right triangle (C005·M2). The thread is opposite the right angle: it is called the hypotenuse. It is the longest of the three sides.

The idea behind the Pythagorean theorem: compare the squares of the lengths rather than the lengths themselves. Build a square on each side, tiled with squares one centimetre on each side. The square built on the 6 cm edge contains 6 rows of 6 tiles, making 36 tiles. The square on the 8 cm edge contains 64. The theorem states that the square built on the thread contains as many tiles as the other two combined: 100.

One question remains, working backwards: what side length gives a square containing 100 tiles? This is what the square root finds. It undoes squaring, just as division undoes multiplication.

Everything depends on two assumptions: a true right angle at the corner, and both edges measured in the same unit. Without them, the arithmetic can be correct while the result is wrong for the frame.

The rule to remember

In a right triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides; the positive square root of this sum gives the length of the hypotenuse.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The length of the thread, step by step

Fictional data: a rectangular frame with height a = 6 cm and width b = 8 cm. Find the length d, in centimetres, of a thread stretched between two opposite corners.

  1. Name the quantities and assumptions

    a and b are the lengths of the two edges that meet at the corner, in centimetres (cm). d is the length of the thread, in centimetres, currently unknown. The square of a length, for example a × a, is measured in square centimetres (cm²): it counts tiles one centimetre on each side.

    Assumptions: the corner is a right angle, since the frame is a rectangle; a, b and d are positive lengths expressed in the same unit. If the angle were not a right angle, the following steps would not apply.

  2. Identify the right triangle

    The thread divides the frame into two triangles that fit exactly on top of each other. In the lower triangle, the right angle is at the bottom left corner. The side that does not touch this corner is the thread: it is the hypotenuse. Edges a and b are the two sides that meet at the right angle.

  3. Write the Pythagorean relation

    For a right triangle with hypotenuse d and sides a and b meeting at the right angle, the Pythagorean theorem states:

    d2=a2+b2

    The exponent 2 means “multiplied by itself”. We accept this theorem here: counting the tiles in the second diagram checks it for this frame, without proving it for all right triangles.

  4. Square each side

    The relation concerns squares of lengths, rather than lengths: calculate each square separately before adding anything. Centimetres multiplied by centimetres give square centimetres.

    a2=6×6=36 cm²b2=8×8=64 cm²

  5. Add the squares

    d2=36+64=100 cm²

    This number is not yet the length of the thread. It is the number of tiles in the square built on the thread, expressed in square centimetres.

  6. Take the square root

    Definition: for a positive number S or 0, the square root of S, written √S, is the nonnegative number whose square equals S. We therefore seek a length d such that d × d = 100.

    Try whole-number lengths. 9 × 9 = 81: too small. 10 × 10 = 100: exactly right. 11 × 11 = 121: too large. A greater length always has a greater square, so no other positive length works.

    d=100=10 cm

    The unit follows the same process: the square root of a quantity in cm² gives cm again. The number −10 also has square 100; it is excluded because a length is never negative.

  7. Check in three ways

    Square again: 10 × 10 = 100, equal to the sum 100 found above.

    Plausibility: the thread (10 cm) is longer than the longest edge (8 cm) and shorter than the route around the frame (14 cm). It saves 14 − 10 = 4 cm of thread.

    Similarity (C005·M3): a right triangle with sides 3 cm and 4 cm has hypotenuse 5 cm, because 9 + 16 = 25 = 25, the square of 5. Our triangle has the same right angle and proportional sides: 6 ÷ 3 = 2 and 8 ÷ 4 = 2. It is an enlargement with scale factor 2, so the thread measures 5 × 2 = 10 cm. This gives the same value using smaller numbers.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the page, explain why 100 is not the length of the thread, and what taking the square root does to the unit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The Pythagorean theorem works in any triangle.”

    The relation requires a right angle. Imagine the corner of the frame changing shape. If the angle opens until it is a straight angle, the third side measures 6 + 8 = 14 cm; if it closes completely, the third side measures 8 − 6 = 2 cm. Between these extremes, the length changes with the angle; it equals 10 cm only at a right angle. Check the angle first, then calculate.

    Review the assumptions in the example

  • “The sum of the squares is the length we want.”

    100 counts tiles, in cm²: it is the square of the length of the thread. A thread 100 cm long would be much longer than the 14 cm route around the frame, which makes no sense. Take the positive square root, then square again to check.

    Review the definition of the square root

  • “The diagonal is the sum of the edges.”

    6 + 8 = 14 cm measures the route around the frame. The thread crosses in a straight line, so it is shorter. Checking the squares confirms this: 14 × 14 = 196, which is 96 more than the sum of the squares 100.

Exercise 1 · Direct application

A more elongated frame

Another fictional frame measures 5 cm by 12 cm, with right angles at the corners. What length of thread is needed to join two opposite corners? Give the result in centimetres, then check it.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Identify the hypotenuse, then calculate the two squares separately.

Hint 2

Their sum is 169 cm². What whole number, multiplied by itself, gives 169?

Compare with the solution

The right angle at the corner makes the thread the hypotenuse of the triangle formed with the two edges. Squares: 5 × 5 = 25 cm² and 12 × 12 = 144 cm². Sum: 25 + 144 = 169 cm².

Square root: 13 × 13 = 169, so the thread measures 13 cm. Plausibility: 13 cm is longer than the longest edge (12 cm) and shorter than the route around the frame, 5 + 12 = 17 cm.

Check your reasoning

  • The right angle is identified before calculating.
  • The squares are calculated separately, then added.
  • The positive square root is given in cm and checked by squaring again.

Exercise 2 · Inverse problem

Find the height of a frame

A thread 15 cm long joins two opposite corners of a fictional rectangular frame 12 cm wide. What is the height of the frame?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The thread is still the hypotenuse. Write the Pythagorean relation with the height h as the unknown.

Hint 2

Isolate the square of h, as in an equation: subtract the square of the width from the square of the length of the thread.

Compare with the solution

With h as the height in cm: h × h + 12 × 12 = 15 × 15. Subtract the same number from both sides: h × h = 225 − 144 = 81 cm².

The positive square root of 81 is 9, because 9 × 9 = 81. The height is 9 cm. Check: 81 + 144 = 225, which is indeed the square of the length of the thread; and 9 cm is shorter than the thread.

Check your reasoning

  • The thread, rather than an edge, has its squared length alone on one side of the equation.
  • Squares are subtracted, never the lengths themselves.
  • The result is in cm, shorter than the thread, and checked in the original relation.

Exercise 3 · Challenge · a square root that is not an integer

The square frame

A fictional square frame measures 5 cm on each side. Bound the length of the diagonal thread between two values given to one decimal place in centimetres, then explain why 10 cm does not work.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The sum of the squares is 50 cm². Find two consecutive integers whose squares lie on either side of it.

Hint 2

Then try 7.1: is its square already greater than 50?

Hint 3

Compare the proposed length with the route along two edges.

Compare with the solution

The corner is a right angle: d × d = 5 × 5 + 5 × 5 = 25 + 25 = 50 cm². Now 7 × 7 = 49 is too small and 8 × 8 = 64 is too large: d is between 7 cm and 8 cm.

At one decimal place: 7.1 × 7.1 = 50.41, already greater than 50. So 7 cm < d < 7.1 cm. We accept here that no terminating decimal gives the exact result: keep √50 cm as the exact value and round only for readability, with the unit.

10 cm equals 5 + 5, the route along two edges. Its square, 100, exceeds 50: it is a different path from the thread.

Check your reasoning

  • The sum of the squares is calculated before taking any square root.
  • The bounds are justified by two squares, one below and the other above.
  • The exact value and the rounded value are distinguished, with the unit cm.
  • The route around the frame is ruled out by comparing squares.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must you check before using the Pythagorean theorem?

Check that the triangle has a right angle; then identify the hypotenuse, the side opposite the right angle. The lengths must be in the same unit.

Recall 2For the frame measuring 6 cm by 8 cm, what is d × d, and why?

36 + 64 = 100 cm²: the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides.

Recall 3What does the square root of 100 give?

It gives the positive number whose square equals 100: 10, because 10 × 10 = 100. It takes us from cm² back to cm.

Recall 4How can you check a calculated diagonal?

Square it again to recover the sum of the squares; check that it is longer than the longest side and shorter than the route along the two edges.

Recall 5Why is the length of the thread not 6 + 8 cm?

This sum measures the route along the edges, 14 cm. The thread takes the shortest route: 10 cm, which is 4 cm shorter.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Identify the right angle and the hypotenuse opposite it. Square the other two sides and add: this gives the square of the hypotenuse, in square units. The positive square root gives a length again; square it again to check it, and do not confuse it with the route along the edges.

What you understand
A length between two landmarks can be calculated using the Pythagorean theorem when the two measurement directions are perpendicular and expressed in the same unit. This exact calculation does not yet tell us what the length means in the real world: we need to know what the landmarks represent and in which space they are measured.
