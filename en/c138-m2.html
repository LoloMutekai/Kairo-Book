Bagging

Your goalBuild a prediction using bagging: draw samples with replacement, fit a tree to each sample, average the predictions, then explain what this mean removes from the error and what it leaves unchanged.

Before you start
c099-m5 : probability calibration · c100-m5 : learning curves · c138-m1 : trees

✿01 See the idea

A fictional bicycle workshop wants to predict the time needed for a service, in minutes, from the distance travelled, in hundreds of kilometres. It has kept records of 6 past services. All values in this lesson are fictional: they are used for calculations and do not describe a real workshop.

A tree with a single split acts like a sorting desk. An observation whose distance is less than or equal to the split receives the mean service time of the left leaf; the others receive that of the right leaf. With few observations, a small change in the sample moves the split, and a bicycle near the boundary then changes leaf and therefore prediction. We call this sensitivity the instability of the tree.

Bagging addresses this instability without changing the rule used by the tree. We repeat the experiment on variations of the sample: each variation draws 6 observations with replacement from the 6 available. The same observation may appear several times, while another may never be drawn. We fit a tree to each variation, then average their predictions.

The mean does not discover the truth: it offsets deviations in opposite directions between trees. If all the trees made the same mistake, the mean would make it too. We must therefore always ask how much the trees differ and in which direction they are wrong together. A large gap between training error and validation error, seen on a learning curve, may indicate high variance; it does not prove it, and probability calibration remains a separate check.

The rule to remember

Draw B samples with replacement, fit the same tree to each, and average the predictions. The mean squared error of the trees equals the squared error of the mean plus the dispersion of the predictions around that mean.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Predict a service time using three randomly sampled trees

A bicycle at x₀ = 4 hundreds of kilometres arrives at the fictional workshop. Its service time, recorded later and also fictional, is y₀ = 22 min and is used only to measure error. We predict its service time using bagging with B = 3 trees.

  1. Set out the data, symbols and assumptions

    Each observation is a pair (x ; y): x is the distance in hundreds of kilometres, and y is the time in minutes. The 6 pairs are (1 ; 10), (2 ; 14), (3 ; 18), (5 ; 26), (6 ; 30) and (7 ; 34). The distances are all different, so an observation can be identified by its distance.

    Here a tree is a stump: a single split on x. An observation goes left if x is less than or equal to the split, and right otherwise. The prediction of a leaf is the mean time of its observations, in minutes. Among the possible splits, we keep the one that minimises the sum of squared deviations from the leaf means, in squared minutes. We accept only samples for which this minimum is unique.

    B is the number of trees, n the number of observations, p_b the prediction of tree b for x0 in minutes, and p̄ the mean of the p_b. Assumptions: each draw selects each observation with equal probability, independently of the other draws, with replacement; all trees follow the same rule; aggregation uses the simple mean, without weights.

  2. Draw three samples with replacement

    To make every line checkable, the samples are fixed and fictional; actual random sampling would produce others. We write them as the distances drawn.

    Sample A: 1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 6. Sample B: 1, 1, 1, 1, 3, 5. Sample C: 3, 5, 5, 5, 7, 7.

    Each sample contains 6 draws, but fewer than 6 distinct observations: this is a feature of sampling with replacement.

  3. Fit a tree to each sample and read the leaf for x₀

    Sample A: the selected split is 3. Since 4 exceeds 3, the bicycle goes right. This leaf contains the distances 5 and 6, with service times 26 and 30 min: a total of 56 min for 2 observations.

    pA=562=28 min

    Sample B: the selected split is 1, so the bicycle again goes right. The leaf contains the distances 3 and 5, with service times 18 and 26 min: a total of 44 min for 2 observations.

    pB=442=22 min

    Sample C: the selected split is 5. This time 4 is less than the split: the bicycle goes left. The leaf contains the distance 3 once and the distance 5 three times, giving the time 18 followed by 26 repeated: a total of 96 min for 4 observations.

    pC=964=24 min

    The split changes from sample to sample (3, 1, 5) and the bicycle changes sides: the instability is visible. For comparison, the tree fitted to the 6 observations splits at 3 and predicts 90 / 3 = 30 min.

  4. Aggregate by averaging

    Each tree has the same weight as the others, so we take the simple mean of the predictions.

    p¯=1B∑b=1Bpb=28+22+243=743≈24.67 min

    The exact fraction is 74/3 min. For classification, we could vote or average probabilities; this averaging does not make the probabilities calibrated, as c099-m5 teaches us to check.

  5. Measure the errors at x₀

    The error of a tree is p_b − y0, in minutes; we square it to obtain squared minutes. Tree A: 6 min, giving 36 min². Tree B: 0 min, giving 0 min². Tree C: 2 min, giving 4 min².

    36+0+43=403≈13.33 min²

    For the mean, the error is 24.67 − 22 = 2.67 min, giving 7.11 min²: this is less than the mean of the squared errors of the trees (13.33 min²) and much less than the single tree (8 min, giving 64 min²). But tree B and tree C have smaller errors than the mean. A single point cannot rank methods.

  6. Decompose the error: what the mean removes

    Write p_b − y0 = (p_b − p̄) + (p̄ − y0). When we square this, the cross term contains the sum of the (p_b − p̄), which is 0 by the definition of the mean. The following equality remains, valid for any list of predictions.

    1B∑b=1B(pb−y0)2=(p¯−y0)2+1B∑b=1B(pb−p¯)2

    The last term is the dispersion of the predictions, in squared minutes. Deviations from the mean: 3.33, -2.67 and -0.67 min, summing to 0. Their squares are 11.11, 7.11 and 0.44 min², totalling 18.67 min². Dividing by 3 gives the dispersion 56/9 min², or approximately 6.22 min².

    7.11+6.22=13.33=13.33 min²

    Check: the squared error of the mean plus the dispersion gives exactly the mean of the squared errors, 40/3 = 40/3 min². The mean has therefore removed the dispersion. What remains, 7.11 min², comes from the fact that no tree predicts below the reference: no mean corrects a shared deviation.

  7. Count the observations absent from each sample

    A draw misses a given observation with probability 1 − 1 / n. The n draws are independent, so the observation is absent from all of them with this probability raised to the power n.

    (1−1n)n=(56)6=15625/46656≈0.335

    In our samples, sample A leaves out 1 observation out of 6 (distance 7), a proportion of 0.167. Sample B leaves out 3 (2, 6 and 7), a proportion of 0.500, and sample C leaves out 3 (1, 2 and 6), a proportion of 0.500. An individual sample fluctuates around 0.335. These absent observations, called out-of-bag observations, could be used to evaluate each tree on cases it has not seen; we do not do that here.

Explain it in your own words

Explain why the mean squared error of the trees equals the squared error of the mean plus the dispersion, then why this equality does not guarantee that the mean beats every tree.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The mean of several trees always beats each of them.”

    It does at most as badly as the mean of the squared errors of the trees, rather than necessarily better than each tree. In the example, tree B (0 min) and tree C (2 min) have smaller errors than the mean (2.67 min) at this point.

    Review the error comparison

  • “Bagging corrects the bias of a tree.”

    It removes the dispersion of the predictions, rather than a deviation shared by all the trees. Here, the dispersion (6.22 min²) disappears in the mean, but the error of the mean (7.11 min²) remains, because no tree predicts below the reference.

    Review the error decomposition

  • “Even with almost identical trees, averaging improves the result.”

    The gain comes from differences between trees. If the predictions are identical, the dispersion is 0 and the mean makes the same error as each tree. Repeat the exercise on identical trees.

Exercise 1 · Explained application

Add a tree

Another fictional sample produces a tree that predicts 20 min for the same bicycle. Recalculate the mean of the 4 trees and its error relative to y₀ = 22 min.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The sum of the first 3 predictions is already known: 74 min. Then change the denominator.

Compare with the solution

New sum: 74 + 20 = 94 min. New number of trees: 4. Mean: 94 / 4 = 23.5 min. Error: 23.5 − 22 = 1.5 min.

This error is smaller than that of the 3 trees (2.67 min), because this new tree predicts below the others. Adding a tree does not guarantee such a change: it depends on its prediction.

Check your reasoning

  • Use 4 as the denominator, rather than 3.
  • Give the mean and the error in minutes.
  • Do not present 20 min as a real measurement: the value is fictional.

Exercise 2 · Application with comparison

Read an out-of-bag sample

In sample B, what proportion of the 6 observations is absent? Compare it with the expected proportion for 6 draws. Can you conclude that the sampling is faulty?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

List the distinct distances present in B, then count those in the full set that are missing.

Hint 2

The expected proportion for 6 draws was calculated in the last step of the example.

Compare with the solution

B contains only the distances 1, 3 and 5. The observations 2, 6 and 7 are therefore absent: 3 out of 6, a proportion of 0.500.

The expected proportion is 0.335. The gap is clear, but a single sample of 6 draws cannot be judged against an expected value alone: it fluctuates. Suspecting a sampling fault would require observing many samples, rather than a single one.

Check your reasoning

  • Count the absent observations, rather than the duplicates.
  • Write the proportion using the decimal comma retained in this edition.
  • Do not draw a conclusion from a single sample.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis

Trees that are too similar

A colleague draws 3 samples but obtains the same prediction for each, 24 min, with y₀ = 22 min. What are the dispersion and the error of the mean? What does she gain by averaging? What would a nonzero dispersion, like the one in the example, have changed?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

If the predictions are equal, what is their mean, and then each deviation from that mean?

Hint 2

Use the equality from the example: error of the trees = error of the mean + dispersion.

Compare with the solution

The mean is 24 min, like each prediction. Each deviation from the mean is 0, so the dispersion is 0 min². The error of each tree is 2 min, giving 4 min², and the mean has the same error: 4 = 4 + 0.

She gains nothing at this point: averaging copies removes no dispersion. In the example, the dispersion of 6.22 min² is the part that the mean could remove. This gain concerns this point and these samples; it cannot be generalised without further data.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify that 0 dispersion eliminates the gain.
  • Use the decomposition equality with its units in squared minutes.
  • Do not conclude that bagging fails in general.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does bootstrap sampling do in bagging?

It draws as many observations as the original dataset, with replacement: some appear several times, while others are absent.

Recall 2How are trees aggregated in regression, and what does this mean remove?

Their predictions are averaged. The mean squared error of the trees equals the squared error of the mean plus the dispersion: the mean removes the dispersion.

Recall 3What does bagging fail to correct?

A deviation shared by all the trees. Without differences between the trees, the dispersion is 0 and so is the gain.

Recall 4What are out-of-bag observations, and what is the probability that a given observation is absent from a sample?

They are the observations that the sample did not draw. The probability is one minus the reciprocal of n, all raised to the power n: approximately 0.335 for 6 observations.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Draw as many observations as the original dataset, with replacement; fit the same tree to each sample; read its prediction for the new case; average; check that the mean error of the trees equals the error of the mean plus the dispersion; conclude that only the dispersion decreases, with no guarantee against a shared deviation or of beating the best tree.
