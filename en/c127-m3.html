Level 2 · C127·M3

Circulation

Your goalCalculate the circulation of a force along an oriented path, then distinguish a reversal of direction, a change of parameter and a change of path.

Before you start
c058-m5 : change of variables · c057-m5 : Hessian · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c127-m1 : parametric curves · c127-m2 : fields

✿01 See the idea

A small cart follows a rail: it moves up from A to B, then to the right from B to D. A force always pushes to the right and becomes stronger as the cart moves higher. During the climb, it pushes sideways; on the horizontal section, it acts in the direction of motion. Before calculating, predict on which part of the rail this force does work.

Circulation, the line integral of a vector field along an oriented path, adds up the contribution of the field in the direction of each small displacement. For a force field, this sum is the work done by that force. A push perpendicular to the displacement contributes nothing; a push in the opposite direction makes a negative contribution. We therefore keep the direction and orientation of the path, as well as its length.

The scene and its values are fictional. The rail constrains the path, and we calculate only the work done by the force described. We calculate neither the work done by all the forces nor the final speed of the cart. The drawing shows directions; the force arrows are not drawn to scale.

The rule to remember

At each point on the path, evaluate the field, take its dot product with the oriented displacement, then add the contributions by integration.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Work along the bent rail

In an orthonormal Cartesian coordinate system, the coordinates x and y are in meters. The rail runs from A = (0 ; 0) to B = (0 ; 2), then to D = (3 ; 2). Let L = 3 m, H = 2 m and κ = 2 N/m. The field is F(x, y) = (κy ; 0), in newtons. Calculate its circulation along this oriented path.

  1. Identify what is being integrated

    F is the force vector; Fx and Fᵧ are its horizontal and vertical components. The coefficient κ converts the height y into a horizontal force: (N/m) × m = N. It depends on neither the coordinate x nor time. L and H are the dimensions of the rail. C denotes the path with its direction A → B → D; W denotes the work to be found, in joules, with 1 J = 1 N·m.

    For a small displacement dr = (dx ; dy), dx and dy are the changes in coordinates, in meters. The dot product F · dr is Fx dx + Fᵧ dy. The dot · denotes this sum of products, rather than componentwise multiplication that would leave a vector. Here Fᵧ = 0 N: only the horizontal change dx can contribute.

    The field is continuous on the rail. The path is regular and continuously differentiable on each segment; we handle the bend by treating these segments separately. No theorem involving a surface is needed.

  2. Turn the path into an ordinary integral

    A parametrization r(t) = (x(t) ; y(t)) gives the position on C, between the bounds a and b, with a < b. The parameter t and its bounds are dimensionless: t tracks progress along the path, rather than a duration in seconds. The prime denotes differentiation with respect to t. Thus r′(t) = (x′(t) ; y′(t)) is in meters per unit of parameter, and dr = r′(t) dt.

    WC=∫CF·dr=∫abF(r(t))·r′(t)dt

    The symbol ∫ adds up infinitesimal contributions. We evaluate F at the position r(t), then take its dot product with r′(t). In this scene, the expression to integrate is κ y(t) x′(t): its unit is N·m, since t is dimensionless. To calculate the work along the bent rail, we parametrize AB and BD separately, each from 0 to 1, then add their integrals.

  3. Climb along AB: the force remains sideways

    On AB, choose r(t) = (0 ; Ht). The position does start at A and end at B. Its derivative is (0 ; H): the horizontal coordinate of the cart does not change. On this segment, F(r(t)) = (κHt ; 0). Even though the force grows, its vertical component remains 0.

    F·r′=κHt×0+0×H=0

    Integrating a contribution that is identically 0 gives work along AB of 0 J. The rail can guide the cart upward; this does not create a vertical component of the force we are studying.

  4. Go from B to D: the force acts along the motion

    On BD, choose r(t) = (Lt ; H). Its derivative is (L ; 0). The height is constant: F(r(t)) = (κH ; 0), so the horizontal force is 2 N/m × 2 m = 4 N. The dot product is κHL, constant and positive. In the following formula, the subscript BD denotes this segment.

    WBD=∫01κHLdt=4 N×3 m=12 J

    The parameter interval has length 1 − 0 = 1. No duration factor is missing: r′ already contains the displacement per unit of parameter.

  5. Add the contributions and check another way

    The circulation along the complete path is the sum of the work along AB and BD: 0 + 12 = 12 J. The bend adds no extra displacement: we do not add a point contribution to the work at the join.

    Check without a parameter: on AB, dx = 0, so κy dx is 0. On BD, the force is constant at 4 N and the horizontal displacement is 3 m; we again obtain 12 J. The positive sign is consistent with a force acting along the segment that contributes to the work.

    The total length of the rail is 2 + 3 = 5 m. Multiplying the maximum force 4 N by this length would give 20 J. This is not the work we are calculating: that operation would assign the maximum push to the whole path and ignore the perpendicular directions during the climb.

  6. Check that the parameter does not create work

    On the same segment BD, use another dimensionless parameter u, between 0 and 0.5, with t = 2u. The position is now (2Lu ; H). Its derivative with respect to u is (2L ; 0), so its horizontal component is 6 m per unit of u.

    The dot product is then 4 × 6 = 24 J, but the interval is 1/2 times as long. Its integral gives 24 × 1/2 = 12 J, as before. The extra factor in the derivative is compensated for by the change in bounds.

    More generally, a regular, increasing change of parameter preserves the circulation along the same oriented path: this is a change of variable in the integral. Reversing the direction of the path changes its sign. Replacing the rail with another path is a further, different operation: the endpoints alone are not enough to predict the work. The exercises ask you to distinguish these cases.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the solution, draw the rail and its arrows. Explain why the nonzero force during the climb can do 0 work, then reconstruct the integral and its unit. Why is changing the parameter different from changing the path?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I use the magnitude of the force and the full length of the rail.”

    Start with the directions. On AB, the force is horizontal and the displacement is vertical: their dot product is 0. Adding positive magnitudes removes this information. Only on BD is the force constant and parallel to the displacement; the product force × length is appropriate there.

    Review the diagram of perpendicular directions

  • “To calculate, I integrate F(r(t)) without the derivative of the path.”

    Write dr = r′(t) dt before substituting values. F(r(t)) says where the force is evaluated; r′(t) carries the displacement, its direction and its scale. Without this factor, changing the parameter would artificially change the result and lose the unit N·m.

    Revisit the integral and the check using another parameter

  • “The endpoints are the same: every path gives the same work.”

    Identical endpoints do not guarantee this property for an arbitrary field. Evaluate the field along the new path. On the bent rail, all horizontal progress takes place at the maximum height; on the diagonal, it starts lower down. The final exercise calculates the difference.

    Compare the rail and the diagonal in the final exercise

Exercise 1 · Local application

A nonzero push

On AB, keep r(t) = (0 ; Ht). At the parameter value t = 0.5, calculate the height, the force vector and the dot product with r′(t). Does a nonzero force require a nonzero contribution to the work?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First substitute t into Ht; the field depends on the resulting height.

Hint 2

Write the components of the derivative of the path before multiplying those in the same direction.

Compare with the solution

The height is Ht = 2 × 1/2 = 1 m. The field is (κy ; 0) = (2 ; 0) N. It is therefore nonzero.

The derivative remains (0 ; 2) m per unit of parameter. The dot product is 2 × 0 + 0 × 2 = 0 J. The absence of horizontal displacement explains this 0 contribution.

This local value alone would not be enough to establish the work along all of AB. Here the same product is 0 at every parameter value; this property over the whole segment makes the integral 0.

Check your reasoning

  • I evaluate the force at the point reached and give its components in newtons.
  • I distinguish a nonzero force from a 0 dot product.
  • I do not turn a single local evaluation into a proof about an entire integral.

Exercise 2 · Reversed path

Return along the same rail

The cart now follows D → B → A in the same field. Parametrize DB by r(t) = (L(1 − t) ; H), for t between 0 and 1. Calculate the work along DB, then along the entire return path. What do you get when you add the work on the outward and return journeys?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

On DB the force does not change direction, but the horizontal derivative of the path becomes negative.

Hint 2

On BA the displacement remains vertical. Then add the contributions with their signs.

Compare with the solution

The derivative of r on DB is (−L ; 0), with −L = -3 m per unit of parameter. The field on DB remains (4 ; 0) N. Their dot product is 4 × (-3) = -12 J, constant over the interval of length 1.

On BA, the force is still perpendicular to the displacement, so the work is 0 J. The work on the return journey is -12 J, the opposite of 12 J. The outward journey followed by the exact return journey gives 12 + (-12) = 0 J.

The field has not been replaced by its opposite: the direction of displacement has changed. This cancellation applies to a path followed by that same path in reverse; it does not establish that the work along every closed loop would be 0.

Check your reasoning

  • I obtain a negative horizontal derivative and keep the force pointing to the right.
  • I give the result in joules with its sign.
  • I limit the cancellation to the path followed by its exact return path.

Exercise 3 · Transfer to a new path

Replace the bend with a diagonal

In the same fictional scene, the rail is replaced by the straight segment A → D, with the field unchanged. Use r(t) = (Lt ; Ht), for t between 0 and 1. Calculate the work and compare it with the work along the bent rail. Check your result using the average of the horizontal forces at the start and end, and justify why it is appropriate here.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The derivative is (L ; H), but the force has only a horizontal component. Also replace y with Ht in the field.

Hint 2

The dot product is κHLt. An antiderivative of t is its square divided by 2; evaluate it at the bounds.

Hint 3

On this diagonal, the horizontal force increases linearly with x. Its average value is therefore the average of its endpoint values.

Compare with the solution

The field along the new path is (κHt ; 0). With the derivative (L ; H), its dot product is κHLt = 12t J. You must integrate this variable expression, rather than use the force at the endpoint along the whole diagonal.

Wdiag=κHL∫01tdt=κHL12=6 J
∫01tdt=12−022=12=0.5

The subscript “diag” denotes the diagonal segment. Independent check: Fx increases linearly from 0 N to 4 N as x goes from 0 to 3 m. The average horizontal force is (0 + 4)/2 = 2 N. Multiplying it by the horizontal progress gives 2 × 3 = 6 J. We use horizontal progress because it is what appears in Fx dx.

The diagonal gives 6 J, which is 6 J less than the 12 J for the bent rail. The points A and D are the same; the path has changed, and so has the work. Averaging only the endpoint forces is exact here because the variation is linear; it is not exact for an arbitrary field.

Check your reasoning

  • I replace y with Ht in the field before taking the dot product.
  • I integrate the factor t and keep the exact fraction until the result.
  • I justify the check by the linear variation of Fₓ with x.
  • I explain the difference from the bent rail without relying on the endpoints alone.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you reconstruct circulation from a parametric curve?

Evaluate F at the position r(t), take the dot product F(r(t)) · r′(t), then integrate between the bounds of the path. Add the integrals if the path has several pieces. For a force in N and positions in m, the result is in J.

Recall 2Why does the force that grows during the climb do no work along AB?

It remains horizontal while every displacement is vertical. The dot product is 0 everywhere on AB; its integral is therefore 0 J, even when the force is nonzero.

Recall 3What changes when you use a different increasing parameter or reverse the direction of travel?

A regular, increasing change of parameter preserves the same oriented path and the same circulation: the derivative and the bounds compensate for each other. Reversing the direction changes the sign of the displacement and the work, in the same field.

Recall 4How does the cart disprove the idea that the endpoints are always enough?

In the same field, the rail A → B → D gives 12 J and the diagonal A → D gives 6 J. On the rail, all horizontal progress takes place at the maximum force; on the diagonal, it starts at a weaker force.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct from memory a drawing of the rail, the assumptions and the units, then the passage from a small displacement to the integral. Recover why AB contributes nothing and why BD gives 12 J. Finish by distinguishing the effects of another parameter, the reverse direction and another path.
