Level 3 · C200·M2

Cointegration

Your goalRecognize a stationary combination of nonstationary series, calculate its spread, and distinguish a mathematical construction from evidence based on observations.

Before you start
c101-m5 : rolling-origin forecasting · c145-m5 : policy evaluation · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c200-m1 : ADF and KPSS

✿01 See the idea

A workshop tracks two cost indices each week: X for raw material and Y for a processed product. Their levels can shift for a long time after a rise in raw material costs. Yet once the raw material index is multiplied by the right coefficient and the fixed offset is removed, the remainder can fluctuate around the same center.

In our fictional model, Y contains 2 times X, an offset of 5 points, and a small local spread. At the first date, X is 100 points and Y is 206 points. Comparing these levels directly hides their relationship. The useful calculation is Y − 5 − 2X: it removes exactly the component that drifts in common.

Cointegration describes this possibility: nonstationary series have a combination with constant coefficients that is stationary. A visual correlation between the levels is insufficient. The question concerns how the spread behaves over time.

These indices, their values, and their law of evolution are invented for this page. Points are a common index unit, with no conversion to money. The model idealizes the evolution and imposes no bound on the levels. The short sample path in the diagram cannot reveal its law on its own.

The rule to remember

Check the series separately, choose a fixed combination that cancels their common drift, then justify the stationarity of the spread. A small difference over a few dates is insufficient.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Two indices and a drift to cancel

At weeks t = 0, 1, 2, and 3, X is successively 100, 102, 104, and 102 points. The local spreads chosen for this realization are 1, -1, 1, and -1 point. With α = 5 points and dimensionless β = 2, let us construct Y, then the spread e. These parameters are given, rather than estimated from the sample path.

  1. Define the target property precisely

    t is the week number; a lag h measures a number of weeks. Xt and Yt are the indices at the same date, in points. For this unit, an I(0) process is weakly stationary with short memory: a constant mean and constant finite variance; covariance depending only on the lag h, with a finite sum of absolute covariances. Covariance measures how two deviations from their respective means vary together. Here its unit is squared points.

    An I(1) series is nonstationary in levels, and its difference ΔXt = Xt − previous X is I(0). The symbol Δ means a change between consecutive dates, in points; we do not divide by a duration. In the bivariate case studied here, X and Y are cointegrated if each is I(1) and there is a constant, nonzero coefficient β such that the spread e below is I(0).

    et=Yt−α−βXt

    α is a fixed offset in points; β is the coefficient of X, dimensionless because the indices use the same unit. et is the centered spread, in points. Normalization sets the coefficient of Y to 1; it only fixes how the combination is written. A constant offset does not change stationarity.

  2. Set up a construction with known assumptions

    X starts at initial X = 100 points, a deterministic value. Each following week, a shock εt of +2 or -1 × 2 points is added to X, with probability 0.5 for each possibility. The shocks are independent and identically distributed across weeks. Their mean is 0 point and their variance is 4 squared points.

    Xt=Xt−1+εt
    Yt=α+βXt+ut

    At every date, including the start, ut equals 1 or -1 point, each with probability 0.5. The u values are independent of each other and of all the ε shocks. Their mean is therefore 0 point and their variance is 1 squared point. All these draws are assumptions, rather than conclusions drawn from the diagram. α = 5 points and β = 2 remain fixed.

  3. Check that both levels are I(1)

    E denotes the mean under the model distribution, and Var its variance, in squared points. Successive shocks are independent: their variances add. After t steps, E(Xt) = 100 points and Var(Xt) = t × 4 squared points. The variance depends on t: X is nonstationary. However, ΔXt = εt is an independent, identically distributed sequence, hence I(0). X is therefore I(1).

    Independence of X and u gives Var(Yt) = β squared × Var(Xt) + Var(ut) = 4 × t × 4 + 1, in squared points. This variance also grows with t. Y is therefore nonstationary. Its difference is:

    ΔYt=βεt+ut−ut−1

    This combination of shocks whose distributions stay the same over time has a stable mean and covariance. Two differences more than 1 step apart share no shock and are independent; its memory is short. ΔY is I(0), and Y is I(1). We therefore do not require the two levels to be stationary already.

  4. Calculate the sample path of both indices

    The realized shocks for X are +2, +2, then −2 points. This gives 100 + 2 = 102, then 102 + 2 = 104, and 104 − 2 = 102 points. The particular signs in this sample path do not change the assumed law.

    At t = 0: βX = 2 × 100 = 200 points; the level α + βX is 205 points. Adding u = 1 point gives Y = 206 points.

    At t = 1: βX = 2 × 102 = 204 points; the level is 209 points. Adding u = -1 point gives Y = 208 points.

    At t = 2: βX = 2 × 104 = 208 points; the level is 213 points. Adding u = 1 point gives Y = 214 points.

    At t = 3: βX = 2 × 102 = 204 points; the level is 209 points. Adding u = -1 point gives Y = 208 points.

  5. Remove the common part, operation by operation

    At each date, we subtract the level α + βX because it contains the random walk. The remainder is successively: 206 − 205 = 1 point; 208 − 209 = -1 point; 214 − 213 = 1 point; 208 − 209 = -1 point.

    et=(α+βXt+ut)−α−βXt=ut

    The algebraic cancellation holds at every date in the model, rather than only in the 4 observations. Since u is independent and identically distributed over time, its mean and variance remain fixed, and its covariances at nonzero lags are 0. The spread e is therefore I(0): X and Y are cointegrated under the stated assumptions.

  6. Check by reconstruction and dispersion

    Reverse check: adding the residual to the level must recover Y. We get 205 + 1 = 206, 209 + (-1) = 208, 213 + 1 = 214, and 209 + (-1) = 208 points. This check helps detect an inconsistent sign; also check that each X, Y pair has the same date.

    Check the property: after 2 steps, Var(X) = 2 × 4 = 8 squared points. The common part of Var(Y) is 4 × 8 = 32, then the spread adds 1, giving 33 squared points. After 4 steps, Var(X) = 16 and Var(Y) = 64 + 1 = 65 squared points. Var(e) remains 1 squared point in both cases.

    These variances describe all possible draws at a given date, rather than the empirical variance of the short sample path. That path does have a sum of spreads 0 + 0 = 0 point and a mean 0 ÷ 4 = 0 point; a 0 empirical mean does not demonstrate stationarity.

  7. State the conclusion and its exact scope

    Cointegration is proved for our construction because we specified the laws of X and u, then canceled the nonstationary component. With observations alone, these laws are unknown. We would need to examine the integration order of the series and of a spread with fixed coefficients, taking into account how those coefficients were estimated. Engle–Granger, in the next unit, addresses this step; the thresholds of an ordinary test do not automatically carry over to estimated residuals.

    Here, u is independent across weeks: the predicted mean of the next spread is therefore 0 point, whatever its current value. This is an additional property of our law, rather than a speed of return supplied by the word cointegration alone. No particular path is forced to cross its center at a promised date. A general stationary spread can even extend far beyond the values in the diagram: being bounded is not its definition.

    The relationship alone does not prove causation between the indices or the future stability of estimated coefficients. Omitting α only shifts the center: Y − βX = α + u remains stationary. Changing β, however, can leave part of X in the spread, as the last exercise will show.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The two indices rise together: they are cointegrated.”

    An apparent common movement or a high correlation does not show that the right combination is stationary. Check the series in levels, then the spread with fixed coefficients. In our example, the conclusion follows from the exact cancellation of X together with an explicit assumption about u.

    Review the check of the levels and the cancellation

  • “The mean of the spreads is 0, so they are stationary.”

    The mean of our sample path is 0 point, but subtracting a mean can center any short list. Stationarity requires a stable mean and covariance over time. Here it comes from the law of the u values, rather than empirical centering.

    Reread what the diagram only illustrates

  • “A stationary spread must return exactly to the center at the next step.”

    The predicted mean and a realization are different. Our spread can only take the values 1 or -1 point: its center is 0 point, a value it never even reaches. Stationarity imposes neither an exact crossing nor a time limit for returning.

    Review the conclusion and its assumptions

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

A wrong coefficient leaves the drift

Someone replaces β = 2 with b = 1 and constructs r = Y − α − bX. b is dimensionless and r is in points. Calculate r at weeks 0 and 2, then prove what happens to its variance. Can we conclude that the pair X, Y is no longer cointegrated? Would a short sample path suffice to prove the same verdict for real indices?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Expand r using Y = α + βX + u: what coefficient remains in front of X?

Hint 2

Distinguish the failure of the combination using b from the existence of a combination using β.

Compare with the solution

At the start: Y − α = 206 − 5 = 201 points; r = 201 − 100 = 101 points. At t = 2: Y − α = 214 − 5 = 209 points; r = 209 − 104 = 105 points.

Algebraically, r = (β − b)X + u. Here β − b = 2 − 1 = 1, so r = X + u. X and u are independent: Var(rt) = t × 4 + 1, in squared points. This variance grows with t; r is nonstationary. The conclusion follows from the model, rather than the rise between the two calculated values.

The pair remains cointegrated because the correct coefficient β = 2 still gives e = u. It is the choice b = 1 that fails. For real indices, neither this short sample path nor a centered spread would demonstrate the assumed laws; we would need to estimate the relationship, then examine stationarity using an appropriate procedure.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover 101 and 105 points.
  • Obtain r = X + u and a variance that depends on t.
  • Distinguish a wrong coefficient from the absence of any stationary combination.
  • Distinguish a proof conditional on the model from a conclusion about observations.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be checked to speak of cointegration between X and Y in this unit?

Each is I(1) in levels, and a combination with constant coefficients Y − α − βX is I(0). The levels are nonstationary; their combination is stationary.

Recall 2Why is the spread in our workshop stationary?

Substituting Y = α + βX + u gives e = u. The u values are independent and identically distributed, with mean 0 and variance 1 squared point. The component that accumulates shocks has disappeared.

Recall 3Why do omitting α and changing β have different consequences?

Omitting α adds a constant to the spread: its center shifts. Changing β can leave a nonzero multiple of X, whose variance grows with time. In our model, b = 1 leaves X + u.

Recall 4Why do a 0 empirical mean and two nearby curves prove nothing on their own?

They describe a finite sample path. Stationarity concerns the moments and temporal dependence of the process. Here, the laws are imposed; for observations, they still need to be examined using an appropriate procedure.

Recall 5Does a center of 0 point promise an exact crossing of that center?

No. Our spread is stationary but only takes the values 1 or -1 point. The center is a model mean, rather than a destination guaranteed at a given date.
