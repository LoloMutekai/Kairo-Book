Level 4 · C246·M1

Pathwise differentiation under conditions

Your goalCalculate a sensitivity by differentiating each path with the randomness held fixed, then justify when the average of these derivatives estimates the derivative of the expectation.

Before you start
c176-m5 : Correlated simulation · c240-m5 : Forward smile and VIX · c186-m5 : Risk near the barrier · c182-m5 : Trinomial tree and admissibility

✿01 See the idea

Imagine an option that pays only the amount by which a final price exceeds a threshold of 100 €. If the final price stays below this threshold, a small change brings no payoff. If it is already above it, each additional euro in the final price adds 1 € to the payoff. At the exact threshold, the slope changes abruptly.

We want the sensitivity of the average value of this option to the initial price. The pathwise method holds the randomness fixed and follows how a slight increase in the initial price passes through the final price calculation and then the payoff calculation. We then average these small slopes. “Pathwise”, in the canonical title, refers to this differentiation with the randomness held fixed.

In our fictional example, the final price equals the initial price multiplied by a random factor. With the factor 1.1, increasing the initial price by 1 € increases the final price by 1.1 €. If the payoff is active, its sensitivity to the initial price is therefore 1.1 €/€, rather than simply 1 €/€.

The data, option and model are fictional and intended for teaching. The diagram shows a local change, rather than financial performance. The difficult question is whether differentiating before averaging really gives the derivative of the average: this requires assumptions, even with many paths.

The rule to remember

Hold the randomness fixed; differentiate the final price and then the payoff; check the conditions for interchanging differentiation and expectation; average the contributions from all paths, including those with 0 contributions.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The sensitivity of a call option in a model with a uniform factor

The initial price is s = 100 €, and the contractual threshold is K = 100 €. We consider a single fixed time horizon, with a 0 interest rate: no discounting changes the payoff. The factor U is uniform between a = 0.5 and b = 1.5, and has no units. Its distribution stays the same when s changes. The 4 fictional realizations A, B, C and D are 0.8, 0.9, 1.1 and 1.2; they allow us to recalculate an average by hand.

  1. Define the price, the payoff and the target quantity

    X(s,U) is the final price, in euros. The symbol φ denotes the payoff function of a call option: it takes the positive excess over K, in euros. F(s,U) is the payoff obtained for a realization of the factor; V(s) is its expectation, meaning the average over the entire distribution of U. The symbol E denotes this expectation. The sensitivity we seek is the derivative V′(s), in €/€.

    X(s,U)=sU;φ(x)=max(x−K,0)
    F(s,U)=φ(sU);V(s)=E[F(s,U)]

    Here x is a possible final price, s stays strictly positive and K stays fixed. The model describes a single maturity; it does not claim to calibrate market dynamics or handle every sensitivity.

  2. Differentiate with the realization held fixed

    J denotes the derivative of X with respect to s, with U held fixed: J = U, in €/€, so it is dimensionless. The symbol ∂ denotes a partial derivative: we vary s while holding the other argument U constant. Away from the threshold, the derivative of φ is 0 below K and 1 above it. The chain rule multiplies these two derivatives.

    ∂F∂s=φ′(sU)J=UIsU>K

    I with a condition as its subscript is the indicator of that condition: it equals 1 when the condition is true and 0 otherwise. The formula applies when sU differs from K. Since U has a continuous distribution, the probability of sU = K is 0 for each fixed s: the derivative exists almost surely, meaning except on an event of 0 probability. This is still insufficient to move the derivative inside E.

  3. Justify the interchange rather than assuming it

    h is a nonzero change in the initial price, in euros, small enough to keep s + h positive. The quotient Hh measures the change in payoff divided by h, in €/€. A sufficient criterion is as follows: these quotients converge almost surely to the derivative, and their absolute values are bounded, for all small h, by the same variable G with finite expectation. This is domination of the difference quotients, rather than just a bound on the resulting derivative.

    Hh=F(s+h,U)−F(s,U)h;|Hh|≤|U|≤b

    Why does this bound hold? Taking the positive part cannot enlarge a difference: |φ(x) − φ(y)| ≤ |x − y| for two prices x and y. The final price changes by hU, giving the bound |U|. Here G can be the constant b = 1.5, and E[G] = 1.5 is finite. This domination lets us apply dominated convergence, the theorem that permits the interchange of limit and expectation here. The distribution of U is fixed; no derivative of this distribution is omitted.

    V′(s)=limh→0E[Hh]=E[UIsU>K]

  4. Calculate the final prices

    For A: 100 × 0.8 = 80 €. For B: 100 × 0.9 = 90 €. For C: 100 × 1.1 = 110 €. For D: 100 × 1.2 = 120 €. These multiplications propagate the same initial price through the supplied realizations.

    A and B are below K; C and D are above it. None of these realizations falls exactly on the kink, so their local derivatives are defined.

  5. Calculate the payoffs and then their average

    A and B pay 0 €. C pays 110 − 100 = 10 €; D pays 120 − 100 = 20 €. The sum is 30 €; we divide by the total number of realizations, n = 4, to obtain the average V̂n. The hat indicates an estimate from a sample; the subscript n indicates its size.

    V^n=0+0+10+204=7.50 €

    This average of payoffs is a value, rather than a sensitivity. We need another average, this time of the derivatives.

  6. Average the sensitivity contributions

    The contributions from A and B are 0: their payoffs remain locally 0, even if their final prices increase. C contributes 1.1 €/€ and D contributes 1.2 €/€. We add these contributions and again divide by n = 4. Δ̂n, read as “estimated delta”, denotes this average.

    Δ^n=1n∑i=1nUiIsUi>K

    The sign ∑ adds the contributions: i is the realization number, from 1 to n, and U with the subscript i is its factor. All these indices are labels with no units. Δ̂n has units of €/€; the sum is not over payoffs in euros.

    Δ^n=0+0+1.1+1.24=23/40=0.575 €/€

    An inactive realization counts towards the sample size. Dividing only by the number of positive payoffs would change the question: it would give an average conditional on an active payoff.

  7. Check with a change, keeping the randomness the same

    For C, let us increase s by h = 1 € without changing U. The initial price becomes 101 €, the final price 111.1 € and the payoff 11.1 €. The payoff has increased by 1.1 € for 1 € of initial price: this recovers the contribution 1.1 €/€ from the first diagram.

    The check also covers the bounds: each contribution lies between 0 and b = 1.5 €/€. The average 0.575 €/€ lies in this interval. These checks would detect, among other things, an omitted factor U or confusion between payoff and derivative. The exercise on changing the initial price repeats the check for the entire average.

  8. Separate the theoretical identity from the estimate

    The interchange with expectation is justified for the uniform distribution and continuous payoff studied here. For a sample of independent realizations from this same distribution, the average contribution is an unbiased estimator of V′(s): its expectation is V′(s). This does not mean that the particular average 0.575 is the exact derivative of the model.

    Our realizations are a fictional set supplied for the calculation, rather than the output of an actual simulation run. No statistical uncertainty is quantified here. If the payoff changes, if the distribution of the factor depends on s or if the numerical simulation replaces other dynamics, we must reexamine, respectively, the interchange, the parametrization of the randomness or the discretization error. An exactly calculated average does not settle these questions.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the equations, describe how the small change in s reaches the payoff. Explain why the kink is acceptable here, where domination enters the argument and why realizations with no payoff remain in the denominator.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The derivative of the payoff is an indicator, so my contribution is just that indicator.”

    The indicator gives the slope with respect to the final price. For the slope with respect to the initial price, multiply again by J = U. For C, the contribution is 1.1 €/€; the first diagram shows exactly this propagation factor.

    Review differentiation with the realization held fixed

  • “I can differentiate almost surely, so I can always differentiate the expectation.”

    A justification for the interchange is missing. Check that an integrable variable dominates the difference quotients for all small changes. The discontinuous payoff in the last exercise has a local derivative of 0 almost surely, while its expectation varies.

    Test the condition on the discontinuous payoff

  • “I only average the paths that pay.”

    The average concerns the entire distribution, so it includes all simulated realizations. 0 contributions do not disappear from the denominator: here we divide the sum 2.3 by 4.

    Review the average of the contributions

Exercise 1 · Local application

A price that rises, a payoff that stays 0

In the initial example, compare B and C. Give the derivative of the final price, the derivative of the payoff with respect to the final price, and then the contribution of each realization to Δ̂ₙ after division by n. Explain why B must remain in the sample size.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Hold U fixed. The final price X = sU has derivative U, even if the payoff is 0.

Hint 2

Multiply this derivative by the slope of the payoff, then divide by the total sample size.

Compare with the solution

For B, J = 0.9 €/€, but the final price 90 € stays below K = 100 € for small changes. The slope of the payoff with respect to the final price is 0, so its contribution both before and after division by n is 0.

For C, J = 1.1 €/€ and the slope of the payoff is 1. The contribution before division is 1.1 €/€; its share of the average is 1.1/4 = 11/40 = 0.275 €/€. D contributes 0.300 €/€, giving the sum 0.575 €/€ when the 0 contributions are also included.

Removing B from the sample size would discard a real region of the distribution. A sensitive final price can lead to a locally insensitive payoff.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish the two derivatives and then their product.
  • Give 0 for B and 0.275 €/€ for the share from C.
  • Justify the total sample size without discarding 0 payoffs.

Exercise 2 · Complete check on paper

Recover the average slope by applying the same change

Use the same factors A to D and replace s by s + h = 101 €, with h = 1 €. Calculate the new payoffs, their average, and then the difference between the averages divided by h. Why does the result coincide exactly with Δ̂ₙ here? Does this equality certify the exact derivative of V?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A and B stay below K; C and D stay above it. Do not draw any new randomness.

Hint 2

For C and D, start with 101 × U − 100, then divide the sum of all payoffs by 4.

Compare with the solution

Payoffs A and B remain 0 €. For C: 101 × 1.1 − 100 = 11.1 €. For D: 101 × 1.2 − 100 = 21.2 €. The sum is 32.3 €, so V̂n(s + h) = 8.075 €.

The difference between the averages is 8.075 − 7.500 = 0.575 €. Dividing by 1 € gives 0.575 €/€, exactly 23/40 €/€. Each payoff is affine over this change, and no factor causes a threshold crossing.

This equality checks the derivative of the average for this sample. With a change that crosses the kink, a finite difference can differ from the local slope. Even the exact equality in this exercise does not remove the sampling error relative to V′. Drawing different factors would also mix the effect of the price change with the effect of changing the sample.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the same realizations and the sample size 4.
  • Obtain 8.075 € and then 0.575 €/€, showing the subtraction.
  • Relate the equality to the absence of threshold crossings and distinguish the empirical average from the expectation.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosing the assumptions

The same threshold, but a payoff that jumps

In the same model, replace only the payoff by L euros if sU > K, and 0 otherwise, with L = 10 €. For s near 100 €, K/s stays between a and b. Calculate its average value using the uniform distribution, then its derivative at s = 100 €. Compare it with the pathwise derivatives. Which earlier condition fails?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The factor must exceed K/s. For a uniform distribution, the probability is the length of the favorable interval divided by the total length.

Hint 2

With the factor held fixed and away from the threshold, a piecewise constant payoff has a derivative of 0. Yet changing s moves the boundary K/s of the favorable interval.

Compare with the solution

Let V_d denote the value of this discontinuous payoff; the subscript d stands for “discontinuous”. The total width of the interval is b − a = 1. At s = 100 €, K/s = 1; the favorable length is b − K/s = 0.5. The probability is 0.5, giving V_d(s) = 5 €.

Vd(s)=Lb−K/sb−a;Vd′(s)=LKs2(b−a)

The derivative is 1000/10000 = 0.1 €/€. Yet for each fixed factor away from the threshold, the payoff derivative is 0: the average of these derivatives is 0, rather than 0.1.

The continuous kink of the call option has become a jump of height L. Factors in an increasingly narrow band cross the threshold; their difference quotient has magnitude L/|h|. There is no common integrable variable dominating these quotients here: if there were, the theorem would give a 0 average derivative, contradicting the exact calculation. The rarity of the exact threshold therefore does not control what happens nearby. This exercise identifies a limitation of the method; it does not yet ask for a replacement method.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover 5 € using a probability calculated from lengths.
  • Obtain 0.1 €/€ and contrast it with the 0 average of the local derivatives.
  • Identify domination of the difference quotients as the missing condition, without concluding that every isolated point of nondifferentiability is forbidden.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do we hold fixed when differentiating path by path?

We hold fixed the realization of the underlying randomness U and its distribution, which is independent of the parameter. The final price X(s,U) changes with s; its derivative must be propagated through to the payoff.

Recall 2Why does the indicator alone fail to give the contribution of the call option?

It is the derivative of the payoff with respect to the final price. The chain rule multiplies it by the derivative J of the final price with respect to the initial price; in our model J = U.

Recall 3What sufficient criterion allows us to interchange differentiation and expectation here?

Almost sure convergence of the difference quotients to the derivative, with their absolute values dominated by the same variable with finite expectation for all small changes. The call payoff is Lipschitz and |Hₕ| ≤ |U| ≤ b.

Recall 4Why is a threshold with 0 probability insufficient to justify the method for a discontinuous payoff?

Realizations near the threshold cross the boundary when s varies. The difference quotients there become large and have no common integrable dominating variable here. The 0 local derivatives miss this boundary effect.

Recall 5What does the result 0.575 €/€ from our sample prove?

It is the exact average of its contributions, counting the inactive realizations. It does not give the exact sensitivity of the entire distribution, statistical uncertainty or proof of actual use of the mechanism.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without reading: start from randomness with a fixed distribution, propagate the derivative from the final price to the payoff, justify the interchange with expectation using dominated difference quotients, then average all contributions. Next explain why a continuous kink can be admissible and a jump can make this reasoning fail.
