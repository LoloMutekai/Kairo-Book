Level 2 · C050·M2

SVD

Your goalDecompose a plane transformation into changes of coordinate frame and stretches, calculate its singular values, and check the reconstruction of its matrix.

Before you start
c048-m5 : positivity · c040-m5 : least squares · c050-m1 : Cholesky

✿01 See the idea

A drawing machine transforms the movements of its stylus before tracing them on a sheet of paper. A horizontal movement can produce a longer, slanted mark. To understand the distortion of the whole drawing, let us find the directions that become the principal axes of the traced shape.

Imagine a circle of radius 1 mm around the starting position. Our transformation turns it into an ellipse: certain directions are stretched by 10, and the corresponding perpendicular directions by 5. These factors have no units and are the singular values.

Singular value decomposition, abbreviated SVD, describes this transformation in simple steps: express the movement in a coordinate frame suited to the input, stretch its components, then express them in the output coordinate frame. A change between orthonormal coordinate frames preserves lengths; it can include a reversal of orientation.

The machine, its matrix, and all the numbers in this scene are fictional. We assume a fixed linear transformation of movements, with no translation or noise, and the same scale in millimetres on each axis. Here we are seeking neither to invert the transformation nor to remove a direction.

The rule to remember

The columns of V give the input directions, the columns of U give their output directions, and each singular value gives the associated stretch factor. To act on a vector, read UΣVᵀ from right to left.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Find the stretch factors of the drawing machine

The input movement x and the traced movement y are columns of 2 components in mm. The machine applies y = Ax, with the rows of A equal to (6, 8) and (-4, 3). Let us calculate an exact SVD and check it on a horizontal movement.

  1. Read the factors and their dimensions

    y=Ax;A=[68-43]

    The entries of A have no units: they multiply millimetres to produce millimetres. The norm is the Euclidean length, hence the square root of the sum of the squared components. A superscript T transposes a matrix by interchanging its rows and columns.

    A=UΣVT;UTU=I;VTV=I

    I is the identity, which leaves every vector unchanged. U and V are orthogonal: their columns are perpendicular and have norm 1. Σ, the capital letter sigma, is diagonal; its entries σ1 and σ2 are the singular values, ordered with σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ 0. The subscripts distinguish the directions. All these entries have no units. Here A, U, V, and Σ each have 2 rows and 2 columns.

    An SVD exists for every real matrix, including rectangular matrices. In its full form, for A with m rows and n columns, U has m rows and m columns, V has n rows and n columns, and Σ has m rows and n columns. m and n are the dimensions; any 0 singular values describe directions that are collapsed. The following calculation stays in the plane, and its singular values will be strictly positive.

  2. Compare directions using AᵀA

    Call the matrix AᵀA G. The product xᵀGx equals the squared length of Ax. G therefore lets us find the directions whose images are stretched the most or the least, while working in the input coordinate frame. G is symmetric and positive semidefinite.

    Its first diagonal entry comes from the dot product of the first column of A with itself: 6 × 6 + (-4) × (-4) = 36 + 16 = 52. Off the diagonal: 6 × 8 + (-4) × 3 = 48 + (-12) = 36. The other diagonal entry is 8 × 8 + 3 × 3 = 64 + 9 = 73.

    G=ATA=[52363673]

  3. Find the squared stretch factors

    An eigendirection v of G satisfies Gv = λv, with v nonzero; λ is its eigenvalue. For v of norm 1, the squared length of Av equals λ. We must therefore take the nonnegative square root of λ to obtain the singular value σ, rather than keeping λ.

    We solve det(G − λI) = 0, where det is the determinant. For our matrix, the polynomial has leading coefficient 1, the coefficient of λ is the negative of 52 + 73, and the constant term is 52 × 73 − 36 × 36. The sum of the diagonal entries is 125 and the constant term is 3796 − 1296 = 2500. To factor the polynomial, let us find numbers with sum 125 and product 2500: 100 and 25 work.

    λ2−125λ+2500=(λ−100)(λ−25)

    Expanding this factorization gives exactly the original coefficients: 100 + 25 = 125 and 100 × 25 = 2500. The roots are therefore λ1 = 100 and λ2 = 25. Their square roots give σ1 = 10 and σ2 = 5; check: 10 × 10 = 100 and 5 × 5 = 25.

  4. Normalize the input directions

    For λ1 = 100, the first equation of (G − λ1I)v = 0 is (52 − 100)vx + 36vᵧ = 0. vx and vᵧ are the horizontal and vertical components of the vector we seek. The vector (3, 2 + 2) satisfies this equation; the other row also checks out in Gv = λ1v. Its norm is 5, since 3 × 3 + (2 + 2) × (2 + 2) = 25 = 5 × 5.

    We therefore divide its components by 5: v1 = (3/5, 4/5)ᵀ, or (0.60, 0.80)ᵀ in decimal notation using the French decimal comma, with no units. A perpendicular unit direction is v2 = (-4/5, 3/5)ᵀ. It satisfies Gv2 = 25v2; always pair the correct direction with its own singular value.

    V=[3/5-4/54/53/5]

    Length check: 9/25 + 16/25 = 1 for each column. Their dot product is (3/5) × (-4/5) + (4/5) × (3/5) = 0. We do indeed have an orthonormal basis.

  5. Calculate the output directions

    For each positive singular value, calculate uᵢ = Avᵢ/σᵢ; i denotes one of the directions. Division removes the stretch and leaves a vector of norm 1. Here the components of Avᵢ have no units; the physical movement associated with a step of 1 mm is 1 mm times Avᵢ.

    For v1, the horizontal component is 6 × 3/5 + 8 × 4/5 = 18/5 + 32/5 = 10. The vertical component is (-4) × 3/5 + 3 × 4/5 = -12/5 + 12/5 = 0. Dividing by σ1 = 10 gives u1 = (1, 0)ᵀ.

    For v2, the horizontal component is 6 × (-4/5) + 8 × 3/5 = -24/5 + 24/5 = 0. The vertical component is (-4) × (-4/5) + 3 × 3/5 = 16/5 + 9/5 = 5. Dividing by σ2 = 5 gives u2 = (0, 1)ᵀ.

    U=[1001];Σ=[10005]

    The output directions are therefore the axes of the sheet. For a 0 singular value, this division would be impossible; we would have to complete an orthonormal basis by making another choice. That case does not occur in our calculation.

  6. Reconstruct every entry of A

    The transpose Vᵀ places the input directions in rows: their product with x calculates the components of x in this basis. Σ multiplies the first row by 10 and the second by 5. Since U is the identity, it leaves this product unchanged.

    UΣVT=[10005][3/54/5-4/53/5]=[68-43]=A

    The products are 10 × 3/5 = 6, 10 × 4/5 = 8, 5 × (-4/5) = -4, and 5 × 3/5 = 3. Every entry is recovered, which checks the entire transformation rather than just one direction.

  7. Follow a movement and check its unit

    Take x = (1, 0)ᵀ mm, a horizontal movement of the stylus. Vᵀx gives (3/5, -4/5)ᵀ mm. After stretching, the components become (6, -4)ᵀ mm; U preserves them. The traced movement is therefore y = (6, -4)ᵀ mm.

    Direct check: Ax = (6 × 1 + 8 × 0, (-4) × 1 + 3 × 0)ᵀ mm = (6, -4)ᵀ mm. Length check: its squared norm is 52 square millimetres, identical to 100 × 9/25 + 25 × 16/25 = 52 square millimetres in the singular coordinate frame.

    The horizontal movement is not a singular direction: its stretch combines the factors 10 and 5. The extreme factors apply to the directions v1 and v2; they are not multipliers to apply blindly to the original axes.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The singular values are 100 and 25.”

    These numbers are the eigenvalues of G = AᵀA. G describes squared lengths. Take their nonnegative square roots: the stretch factors are 10 and 5. Check them using Avᵢ = σᵢuᵢ.

    Revisit the step from squares to lengths

  • “V contains the directions, so I calculate UΣVx.”

    The directions are the columns of V. To obtain their dot products with x, we need them in rows: use Vᵀ. Redo the horizontal movement; replacing Vᵀ with V gives an incorrect vertical component.

    Check the order of the factors on the vertical movement

  • “I can change the sign of just one column of V without changing anything.”

    A sign change must be applied to both associated vectors, uᵢ and vᵢ. Changing only vᵢ reverses its contribution to the product. The singular value remains nonnegative; it does not compensate for this isolated change.

    Test the signs using the same drawing machine

Exercise 1 · Warm-up

From the square to the length

For the same machine, Gv₁ = 100v₁ and the norm of v₁ is 1. Give σ₁ and the traced movement produced by x = 1 mm × v₁. Why would 100 mm be incorrect for its length?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Relate xᵀGx to the square of the traced length.

Hint 2

Av₁ has been calculated: remove or restore the factor of 1 mm according to whether you are discussing a vector with no units or the movement.

Compare with the solution

The square of the stretch factor is 100. Its nonnegative square root is σ1 = 10, with no units. Since Av1 = (10, 0)ᵀ, the traced movement is (10, 0)ᵀ mm and its length is 10 mm.

Using 100 mm confuses a squared factor with a length factor. For the chosen step of 1 mm, the squared length is 100 square millimetres.

Check your reasoning

  • You distinguish an eigenvalue of G from a singular value of A.
  • You obtain 10 mm and express the squared length in square millimetres.

Exercise 2 · Application

Trace a vertical movement

Using the same factors, apply Vᵀ, Σ, and U in succession to x = (0, 1)ᵀ mm. Compare with Ax and explain why the factor 5 alone is not enough to trace this movement.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Multiplying a matrix by this vector selects its second column.

Hint 2

In Vᵀ, the components of this column are 4/5 and 3/5.

Compare with the solution

Vᵀx = (4/5, 3/5)ᵀ mm. Σ gives (10 × 4/5, 5 × 3/5)ᵀ mm = (8, 3)ᵀ mm. U is the identity, so y = (8, 3)ᵀ mm.

The direct product Ax selects the second column of A: (8, 3)ᵀ mm. The vertical movement decomposes along both singular input directions. The factor 5 applies to v2, which is slanted in the original coordinate frame.

Check your reasoning

  • You preserve the order Vᵀ then Σ then U, and the units.
  • You recover exactly the second column of A.
  • You distinguish an original axis from a singular direction.

Exercise 3 · Justification

Two coordinate frame diagrams for the same transformation

One person replaces v₁ with −v₁ and keeps all the other columns and Σ. Another simultaneously replaces v₁ with −v₁ and u₁ with −u₁. Which person preserves A? Justify your answer by following the movement x = 1 mm × v₁, where v₁ denotes the original direction.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For this movement, the component along v₂ is 0. What happens to the dot product with −v₁?

Hint 2

After the change of input coordinate frame, the first component is −1 mm. Then follow the stretch and the chosen output direction.

Compare with the solution

Changing only v1 changes its component from 1 mm to −1 mm. With u1 unchanged, the image becomes −10 mm × u1, instead of 10 mm × u1. The transformation has changed; this is a sufficient counterexample.

When u1 is also changed, the same negative component is multiplied by the opposite output direction: (−10 mm)(−u1) = 10 mm × u1. For every x, the signs cancel in the contribution u1σ1v1ᵀ; the other contribution stays the same. The full product A is preserved.

The vectors in an SVD therefore do not have uniquely determined signs. However, the factors must stay paired; changing a sign does not turn σ1 into a negative value.

Check your reasoning

  • You give a movement whose image changes in the first case.
  • You justify why the signs cancel for every x in the second case.
  • You keep the singular values nonnegative.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do Vᵀ, Σ, and U do, in that order?

Vᵀ calculates the components in the input directions; Σ stretches them by nonnegative factors; U assembles them in the output directions. U and V are orthogonal.

Recall 2Why take the square roots of the eigenvalues of AᵀA?

For a unit eigenvector v, the squared length of Av equals the eigenvalue λ. The length factor is therefore σ = √λ, chosen to be nonnegative.

Recall 3How can we recover uᵢ when σᵢ is strictly positive?

Calculate Avᵢ, then divide by σᵢ: uᵢ = Avᵢ/σᵢ. This unit vector gives the output direction associated with vᵢ. This division cannot be used for a 0 singular value.

Recall 4What should we check before accepting the factors of an SVD?

Check the dimensions, the orthonormality of the columns, the nonnegative and ordered singular values, the relations Avᵢ = σᵢuᵢ, then the reconstruction A = UΣVᵀ.

Recall 5Why can vectors with different signs describe the same SVD?

Simultaneously reversing uᵢ and vᵢ preserves their contribution uᵢσᵢvᵢᵀ. Reversing just one of them changes this contribution. The singular value remains nonnegative.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the factors, reconstruct the reasoning: use AᵀA to measure squared lengths; extract orthonormal eigendirections from it; take nonnegative square roots for the stretch factors; recover the output directions; check each factor and then their product. Finally, explain the path of the horizontal movement with its units.
