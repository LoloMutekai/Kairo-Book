Level 2 · C064·M3

Innovation and scalar gain

Your goalCalculate the difference between a reading and its prediction, then the gain that sets the weight of this reading, while distinguishing the observed value from the uncertainties in the model.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

The room’s sensor has finally returned a reading. Before this reading, we had predicted 20 °C, with an error variance of 5 °C². The sensor reports 23 °C. The disagreement is visible; its cause is not. The room may have warmed up, the sensor may have drifted, or both may have happened.

We call the signed difference between the reading and the prediction the innovation. It indicates the direction in which the reading suggests moving the estimate. It does not give the model’s actual error: to know that, we would need to know the actual temperature.

The scalar gain sets the fraction of this difference that the update will use. It compares the uncertainties specified for the prediction and the sensor. A reading further from the prediction changes the difference; with fixed variances, it does not change this gain.

All the data for this room are fictional. We take the predicted pair from the previous unit and compare it with a reading for the same instant. This page prepares for the update; it stops at calculating the innovation, its variance and the gain.

The rule to remember

Subtract the values to measure the disagreement; add the independent variances to describe its uncertainty; divide the prediction variance by this sum to obtain the gain.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What does the reading of 23 °C tell us?

At the current instant, the prediction is x̂⁻ = 20 °C and its variance is P⁻ = 5 °C². The reading is z = 23 °C; the sensor noise has variance R = 20 °C². Let us calculate the quantities needed to set its influence.

  1. Define the errors before combining the numbers

    x is the unknown actual temperature; x̂⁻, with a hat, is its prediction before the reading; z is the value read. All are in °C. The superscript − means “before the reading”, not “negative”. The measurement model is z = x + v, where v is the sensor error, in °C.

    The prediction error is e = x − x̂⁻. We assume that e and v have mean 0 °C, are independent and have known finite variances P⁻ and R, in °C². These variances describe the expected spread of the errors given the information available before this reading; they are not the squares of errors observed here.

    A variance can be 0; it cannot be negative. To calculate the gain, their sum must be strictly positive. The relationship z = x + v assumes that the sensor directly measures the same temperature, in the same unit. A systematic bias or dependence between the errors would require revising this model.

  2. Subtract towards the reading

    We denote the innovation by y, in °C of difference. Subtracting the prediction from the reading gives the signed change from what was expected to what arrives.

    y=z−x^−=23−20=3 °C

    The positive sign suggests moving towards a warmer value. We know that z differs from x̂⁻ by 3 °C; we do not know whether x differs from x̂⁻ by this amount. Confusing y with the error e would amount to declaring the sensor perfect.

  3. Add the uncertainties that produce the difference

    Substituting x + v for z gives y = (x − x̂⁻) + v = e + v. The disagreement therefore combines the prediction error and the sensor error. Their independence and 0 means allow us to add their variances. We call the innovation variance S, in °C².

    S=P−+R=5+20=25 °C²

    S describes the possible differences under the model before seeing this reading. It is not y squared, and it is not recalculated by squaring our observed disagreement. We add degrees squared, never a temperature and a variance.

  4. Form the gain with the prediction variance in the numerator

    K denotes the scalar Kalman gain. In our direct measurement model, it is the weight that the update will give to the reading: the fraction of the innovation to use. It equals the prediction variance divided by the total variance of the disagreement.

    K=P−S=525=15=0.20

    The °C² units cancel: K has no unit. The more uncertain the prediction is relative to the sensor, the more weight the sensor receives. Swapping the roles of P⁻ and R in the numerator would give the weight of the prediction, not that of the reading.

    Here, K is between 0 and 1. This does not mean that the sensor has a probability of 0.20 of being correct: a combination coefficient is not a probability of truth.

  5. Check the result using another ratio

    The measurement variance is 4 times the prediction variance: R/P⁻ = 20/5 = 4. Since P⁻ is strictly positive here, divide the numerator and denominator of K by P⁻.

    K=11+RP−=11+4=0.20

    This second approach gives the same gain. It also explains its meaning: with P⁻ fixed, increasing R increases the denominator and reduces K. Check by multiplying back: K × S = 0.20 × 25 = 5 °C², matching P⁻.

    We therefore keep y = 3 °C, S = 25 °C² and K = 0.20 with no unit. The update of the estimate and its variance will be worked through in the next unit; these calculations have not revealed any actual temperature.

  6. Check what happens at the limits

    If P⁻ is 0 and R remains positive, the gain is 0: the model assigns all certainty to the prediction. If R is 0 and P⁻ remains positive, the gain is 1: the model treats the reading as exact. These statements describe the chosen assumptions, not a guarantee about a real sensor.

    If both variances are 0, S is 0 and the quotient is undefined. We do not choose a gain arbitrarily: this case must be handled separately. With strictly positive variances, the gain stays strictly between 0 and 1.

    Finally, replacing only z leaves P⁻, R, S and K unchanged in this calculation. Only the innovation changes. A surprising reading may prompt us to examine the model, but its size does not automatically reduce the gain calculated here.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The room must have warmed up by 3 °C.”

    This number is z − x̂⁻, not x − x̂⁻. It combines the prediction error and the sensor error. Rewrite y = e + v and keep the actual temperature unknown.

    Review the definition of the innovation and its errors

  • “The specified standard deviation can replace R in the sum.”

    R is a variance in °C²; the standard deviation is in °C. First square the standard deviation, then add variances. The exercise with the more precise sensor makes this conversion.

    Convert the standard deviation in the precision exercise

  • “A reading very far from the prediction must give a larger gain.”

    With P⁻ and R fixed, z does not enter K = P⁻/(P⁻ + R). The reading changes the innovation and its sign. The gain remains a weight calculated from the variances, not a measure of surprise or a probability that the sensor is correct.

    Review the gain checks and the case where only z changes

Exercise 1 · Direct application

The sensor suggests moving down

At the same fictional instant, replace only the reading with 18 °C. Keep the prediction 20 °C, P⁻ = 5 °C² and R = 20 °C². Calculate y and K, then state the suggested direction for the update. What would y be if the sensor read exactly 20 °C?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Always subtract the prediction from the new reading, in that order.

Hint 2

For the gain, look for what has changed in P⁻/(P⁻ + R).

Compare with the solution

The innovation is 18 − 20 = -2 °C. Its negative sign suggests moving the estimate towards a lower temperature; it does not make the gain negative.

S remains 25 °C² and K remains 1/5 = 0.20, because the variances have not changed. The gain sets the fraction of the difference; the sign of y indicates its direction.

If z = 20 °C, y = 20 − 20 = 0 °C. Agreement with the prediction makes neither P⁻ nor R 0: a 0 innovation is not proof of an exact temperature.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain an innovation of -2 °C by subtracting in the correct direction.
  • You justify the unchanged, positive gain using the variances.
  • You distinguish agreement between values from certainty about the actual temperature.

Exercise 2 · Conversion and comparison

A sensor with a known standard deviation

Start again from the initial prediction and the reading 23 °C. Replace the assumption R = 20 °C² with independent sensor noise of 0 mean and standard deviation 2 °C. Before calculating, state whether the reading will receive more or less weight. Then calculate R, S and K, keeping the exact fraction. This variant replaces the observation in the example; it is not added after an update.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Squaring a standard deviation gives a variance; check the unit before adding.

Hint 2

The variance P⁻ remains 5 °C². Compare the new measurement variance with 20 °C².

Compare with the solution

The new sensor receives more weight: its variance is lower. R = 2 × 2 = 4 °C². Putting 2 directly into the sum would mix degrees and degrees squared.

S = 5 + 4 = 9 °C². Dividing P⁻ by this sum gives K = 5/9, or approximately 0.5556 with no unit. Keep the fraction for the following calculations.

The gain exceeds 0.20. Since the prediction and the reading remain the same, y is still 3 °C. We have changed the weight assigned to the disagreement, not the disagreement itself.

Check your reasoning

  • You predict the direction of the change before consulting the answer.
  • You obtain R = 4 °C² and K = 5/9 without intermediate rounding.
  • You explain why the innovation remains 3 °C.

Exercise 3 · Inverse reasoning

Which assumption would give this gain?

Still considering the same room, keep P⁻ = 5 °C², the prediction 20 °C and the reading 23 °C. What R would give K = 2/5 = 0.40? Isolate R in the formula, then check by substitution. Does this calculation allow you to claim that the real sensor has this variance?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Multiply K = P⁻/(P⁻ + R) by the denominator, then subtract K × P⁻ from both sides.

Hint 2

You obtain K × R = P⁻ × (1 − K). The requested gain is strictly positive: you can divide by K.

Compare with the solution

From K × (P⁻ + R) = P⁻, we deduce K × R = P⁻ × (1 − K). Therefore R = P⁻ × (1 − K)/K. This reasoning uses the requested gain, which is nonzero.

R=5×(1−25)25=325=7.5 °C²

The complement of the gain is 3/5; the product in the numerator is 3 °C². The required variance is therefore 15/2 °C², or 7.5 °C². Check: S = 5 + 7.5 = 12.5 °C², then 5/12.5 = 0.40 with no unit.

The innovation remains 3 °C. The calculation finds an assumption compatible with the desired gain; it does not measure the noise of a real sensor. Choosing a gain and deducing R does not replace an independent justification of this variance.

Check your reasoning

  • You isolate R using a justified equality and check that its value is positive.
  • You recover 0.40 by putting 7.5 °C² back into the original formula.
  • You distinguish algebraic consistency from a sensor variance established through observations.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the innovation, and what are its sign and unit?

y = z − x̂⁻: reading minus prediction at the same instant. Its unit is °C in the room. Its sign gives the suggested direction for the update; it does not give the actual error, since x remains unknown.

Recall 2Why does S equal P⁻ + R, and under which assumptions?

y = e + v combines the prediction error and the sensor noise. With the independent errors of 0 mean in the model, the variances add. S is in °C²; it is not the square of the observed innovation.

Recall 3Reconstruct the gain formula and explain the role of each variance.

K = P⁻/(P⁻ + R), with no unit for a direct measurement in the same unit. With P⁻ fixed, increasing R reduces the weight of the reading; with positive R fixed, increasing P⁻ increases this weight. The sum must be strictly positive.

Recall 4If only z changes, what happens to y, S and K?

y changes with the reading; S and K remain identical as long as the variances are fixed. K quantifies neither the surprise of this reading nor its probability of being correct.

Recall 5What happens if one variance is 0, then if both are?

0 P⁻ and positive R give K = 0; 0 R and positive P⁻ give K = 1. If both are 0, the denominator is 0: the formula gives no gain. These are cases in the model, with no guarantee about a real sensor.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without consulting the example, reconstruct the steps: name the quantities and their units, derive y from the values, then S from the errors, and form K. Then explain why a different reading changes y and why a different variance R changes K. Finish with the case where the denominator becomes 0.
