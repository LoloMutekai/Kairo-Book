Level 2 · C137·M4

SVD and PCA

Your goalConnect the singular value decomposition of a centered matrix to principal component analysis (PCA): read the axes, variances, coordinates and proportion of variance, then check this connection using four fictional observations.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

Two sensors, A and B, record the temperatures of four rooms in a fictional building: Living Room, Kitchen, Office and Bedroom. For each room, we keep the deviation of each sensor from its own mean across the four rooms, in tenths of a degree Celsius (d°C). The values are invented to make the calculations work out exactly; they do not describe any real building.

When a room is warmer than the mean for sensor A, it almost always is for sensor B too: the four points form an elongated cloud rather than a disk. PCA seeks the direction of elongation and its size, meaning the direction of maximum variance in the cloud. There are two ways to find it: diagonalize the covariance matrix, or apply SVD directly to the matrix of deviations. This unit connects them.

The picture to keep in mind: SVD arranges the cloud into ordered layers. Each layer has a size σ, in the unit of the readings (d°C), a direction v, which is an axis with no unit, and a number for each room that tells us where it lies on that axis. The first layer is the most stretched axis; the second is perpendicular to it.

Three things are taken as given. The columns are already centered (see the unit on centering). The covariance uses the divisor n − 1: another convention would multiply the variances by a constant factor without changing either the axes or the proportions of variance. Finally, the SVD comes from a computational routine whose result we check instead of trusting it blindly.

What PCA does not tell us: what its axes mean in the building, or how many axes to keep. That choice belongs to the next unit.

The rule to remember

Center the matrix X and decompose it as X = U Σ Vᵀ: the columns of V are the axes, σ² ÷ (n − 1) gives the variance along each axis, and X V gives the coordinates of each observation on those axes.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Read PCA from the SVD for four rooms

We start with the deviations in matrix X (four rooms, two sensors, in d°C) and the SVD supplied by a routine: σ₁ = 20 d°C, σ₂ = 10 d°C. We check that the columns of V are the PCA axes, then derive variances, coordinates and the proportion of variance. All values are fictional.

  1. Set out the quantities, units and assumptions

    X is the matrix of deviations: n = 4 rows (Living Room, Kitchen, Office, Bedroom) and p = 2 columns (sensors A then B), in d°C. The Office is the negative of the Kitchen and the Bedroom is the negative of the Living Room: each column sums to 0, so X is centered. This construction gives exact numbers and short calculations.

    X=(211105−10−5−2−11) d°C

    SVD writes X as a product of three matrices. U has 4 rows and 2 columns; V has 2 rows and 2 columns; both have columns of length one that are pairwise perpendicular, and neither has a unit. Σ is diagonal: its values σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ 0 are the singular values, in d°C.

    X=UΣVT

    For this fictional example, the routine returns σ1 = 20 d°C, σ2 = 10 d°C and the axes v1 = (0.6 ; 0.8) and v2 = (−0.8 ; 0.6), the columns of V. The assumptions: centered columns, the divisor n − 1 = 3 for the covariance matrix S, and axes defined up to a sign.

  2. Form XᵀX, which contains the axes and the σ² values

    Since UᵀU = I (I is the identity matrix), we obtain XᵀX = V Σ² Vᵀ. The square matrix XᵀX, of size 2 by 2, therefore has the columns of V as eigenvectors and the σ² values as eigenvalues. It also equals n − 1 times the covariance matrix S.

    XTX=VΣ2VT=(n−1)S

    Its entries are sums of squares and products across the four rooms. The Office and Bedroom are the negatives of the Kitchen and Living Room: their squares and products are identical, so each sum is counted twice.

    g₁₁=2×(22+102)=208 d°C²
    g₂₂=2×(112+52)=292 d°C²
    g₁₂=2×(2×11+10×5)=144 d°C²

    Unit check: d°C × d°C = d°C². The matrix is symmetric: g12 appears twice.

  3. Check that an axis is an eigenvector

    If v1 is indeed the axis associated with σ1, then XᵀX v1 = σ1² v1. Here σ1² = 20 × 20 = 400 d°C². Let us calculate the left side row by row, then the right side.

    (XTX)v₁=(208×0.6+144×0.8;144×0.6+292×0.8)=(124.8+115.2;86.4+233.6)=(240;320)
    σ₁2v₁=(400×0.6;400×0.8)=(240;320)

    The two vectors agree: v1 is an eigenvector of XᵀX for σ1². Since XᵀX is symmetric, the axis v2, perpendicular to v1, is also an eigenvector; its eigenvalue is the trace minus σ1². Check: 208 + 292 = 500 and 500 − 400 = 100, which does equal σ2² = 10 × 10. The sum of the eigenvalues, 400 + 100 = 500, equals the trace.

  4. Convert σ² to variance along an axis

    The covariance matrix is S = XᵀX ÷ (n − 1). Its eigenvalues are therefore the variances along the axes, μ = σ² ÷ (n − 1), in d°C². For the first axis, μ1 equals 400 ÷ 3, or approximately 133.33 d°C² (exact value: the fraction 400/3).

    μ₁=σ₁2n−1=4003≈133.33 d°C²

    Check: the total variance, the sum of the variances of the two sensors, is the trace of S: 500 ÷ 3, or approximately 166.67 d°C². SVD redistributes it among the axes without loss or gain. The variance along the second axis is left to the first exercise. With the divisor n in place of n − 1, all variances would change by the same factor.

  5. Read the coordinates of the Living Room

    The coordinate of a room on an axis is the product of its row and that axis, component by component. Taken together, these are the rows of X V, and X V = U Σ since VᵀV = I. For the Living Room, with row (2 ; 11):

    z₁₁=2×0.6+11×0.8=1.2+8.8=10 d°C
    z₁₂=2×(−0.8)+11×0.6=−1.6+6.6=5 d°C

    The Living Room lies at 10 d°C on the first axis and at 5 d°C on the second, as the first diagram shows. The rows for the Office and Bedroom are the negatives of those for the Kitchen and Living Room, so their coordinates are too: each column of X V sums to 0.

  6. Measure what a single axis retains

    The proportion of variance along the first axis is σ1² ÷ (σ1² + σ2²). The divisor n − 1 cancels: this ratio does not depend on the chosen convention.

    p₁=σ₁2σ₁2+σ₂2=400500=0.80=80 %

    Compression means replacing each room with its projection onto the first axis. For the Living Room, this gives:

    x^=z₁₁v₁=10×(0.6;0.8)=(6;8) d°C

    The reconstructed point differs from the measured point, whose row is (2 ; 11): we have retained part of the variance rather than all of it. The exact cost of this loss is measured in the last exercise.

  7. What the checks show and what they do not show

    Passed: the axis v1 satisfies XᵀX v1 = σ1² v1; the trace of XᵀX equals the sum of the σ² values; the units are consistent (σ in d°C, variances in d°C², proportions with no unit).

    Not shown: the axis v2 was not recalculated directly; it was deduced from symmetry. The cloud is symmetric by construction, with only two sensors. The physical meaning of the axes has not been established. Finally, (u, v) and (−u, −v) give the same product σ u vᵀ: the sign of an axis is a convention.

Explain it in your own words

Why is the variance along an axis σ² ÷ (n − 1) rather than σ, and why does the proportion of variance stay the same if we divide by n instead? Also explain why centering must come before SVD.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A singular value is the variance along the axis.”

    σ is in the unit of the data (d°C); the variance is σ² ÷ (n − 1), in d°C². Compare σ₁ = 20 d°C with the variance along the first axis, approximately 133.33 d°C²: neither the unit nor the number agrees.

    Review the conversion to variances in the example

  • “I can decompose the raw data: it amounts to the same thing.”

    Without centering, the mean becomes the dominant direction and inflates σ₁; the σ² values then mix mean and variance and are no longer variances along the axes. Center each column first, as in X here, where each column sums to 0.

    Reread the starting assumptions

  • “If my routine returns −v₁ instead of v₁, my PCA is wrong.”

    The pairs (u, v) and (−u, −v) give the same σ u vᵀ: the same σ values, the same reconstruction, and coordinates with opposite signs. Compare the axes up to a sign. In the second exercise, the negative coordinate of the Kitchen on the second axis would change sign with the other choice.

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Variance and proportion along the second axis

Using the same example (σ₂ = 10 d°C, n − 1 = 3), calculate the variance along the second axis in d°C², then its proportion of variance as a percentage. Check that the two proportions total 100 %.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Proceed as for the first axis: first σ₂², then division by n − 1.

Hint 2

The proportion is σ₂² divided by the trace 500; it does not depend on the divisor n − 1.

Compare with the solution

σ2² = 10 × 10 = 100 d°C². Variance along the second axis: 100 ÷ 3 = 33.33 d°C² (exact value: the fraction 100/3).

Proportion: 100 ÷ 500, or 20 %. Check: 80 % + 20 % = 100 %. The variances also add up: 133.33 + 33.33 gives 166.67 d°C² up to rounding, with exact values 400/3, 100/3 and 500/3.

Check your reasoning

  • Use σ² rather than σ, with the unit d°C².
  • Divide by n − 1 for the variance, but not for the proportion.
  • Check that the two proportions total one hundred percent.

Exercise 2 · Guided application

The coordinates of the Kitchen

The Kitchen has row (10 ; 5) d°C. Calculate its coordinates on v₁ = (0.6 ; 0.8) and on v₂ = (−0.8 ; 0.6). Then check that, on each axis, the sum of the squares of the four coordinates gives σ².

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A coordinate is a dot product: row × axis, component by component, then sum.

Hint 2

For the second axis, the first product carries the minus sign from v₂: write the sign before calculating.

Hint 3

The Office and Bedroom are the negatives of the Kitchen and Living Room: their coordinates are opposite, so each square is counted twice.

Compare with the solution

First axis: 10 × 0.6 + 5 × 0.8 = 6 + 4 = 10 d°C. Second axis: 10 × (−0.8) + 5 × 0.6 = −8 + 3 = −5 d°C.

Check for the first axis: 2 × (10² + 10²) = 400 d°C², which is σ1². Check for the second: 2 × (5² + 5²) = 100 d°C², which is σ2².

The Kitchen lies on the opposite side from the Living Room on the second axis. With the other sign for the axis, all signs in this column would reverse without changing the squares.

Check your reasoning

  • Two dot products, with the correct signs.
  • Check the sums of squares against σ², with unit d°C².
  • Mention the sign convention for an axis.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

The cost of using a single axis

Reconstruct the Living Room using only the first axis, then measure its residual: the difference between its row and its reconstruction. Here the four rooms have residuals of the same size; use this to deduce the sum of squared residuals and compare it with σ₂². State what this check establishes and what it does not establish.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Reconstruction = coordinate on the first axis × v₁; the residual is the row minus this reconstruction.

Hint 2

The residual for the Living Room contains only the second axis: its squared size must equal the square of the corresponding coordinate.

Hint 3

For the Kitchen, the residual is (10 − 6 ; 5 − 8); the Office and Bedroom are the negatives. Then multiply by n.

Compare with the solution

Reconstruction of the Living Room: (6 ; 8). Residual: (2 − 6 ; 11 − 8) = (−4 ; 3) d°C.

Squared size: 4² + 3² = 16 + 9 = 25 d°C², which is the square of the Living Room coordinate 5 on the second axis.

The Kitchen has reconstruction 10 × v1 = (6 ; 8) and residual (4 ; −3): the same size. The Office and Bedroom are the negatives. Total: 4 × 25 = 100 d°C², which is σ2² = 100 d°C²; the loss is therefore 20 % of the total variance.

What is established: in this example, retaining one axis leaves a residual sum of squares of exactly σ2². The general result on truncated SVD is taken as given in this unit; a single symmetric example does not prove it, nor does it tell us how many axes to keep.

Check your reasoning

  • Reconstruct using only the coordinate on the first axis.
  • Measure the residual in d°C² using squares.
  • Connect the total to σ₂² and distinguish a check on the example from a result taken as given.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do the columns of V in X = U Σ Vᵀ contain, for a centered matrix X?

The principal axes: vectors of length one, pairwise perpendicular, that are eigenvectors of XᵀX and therefore of the covariance matrix. They have no unit and are defined up to a sign.

Recall 2How do you go from a singular value σ to the variance along the axis, and in what unit?

Variance = σ² ÷ (n − 1), in the squared unit of the data. Here σ₁ = 20 d°C gives approximately 133.33 d°C².

Recall 3What does the product X V represent?

The coordinates of the observations on the axes, equal to U Σ. Each column sums to 0 and its variance is that of the axis. The Living Room lies at (10 ; 5) d°C.

Recall 4What step must come before SVD for it to give PCA?

Centering the columns. Without it, the mean contributes to the σ² values, which are no longer variances along the axes.

Recall 5What proportion of variance does the first axis retain here, and what do we lose by retaining only that axis?

σ₁² ÷ (σ₁² + σ₂²) = 80 %. In this example, the sum of squared reconstruction residuals is exactly σ₂² = 100 d°C², or 20 % of the total variance.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Center X; decompose X = U Σ Vᵀ; read the columns of V as axes; calculate σ² ÷ (n − 1) as the variance along each axis, in the squared unit; read X V = U Σ as coordinates; measure the proportion as σ² divided by the sum of the σ² values; check using XᵀX v = σ² v and the trace; keep in mind the arbitrary sign of the axes, centering and the fictional nature of the example.
