Level 3 · C183·M1

Meshing

Your goalBuild a uniform asset price–time grid, identify its supplied data and the values to calculate, then distinguish refinement from extending the domain.

Before you start
c167-m5 : initial and boundary value problem · c051-m5 : error diagnostics · c175-m5 : model limitations · c050-m5 : conditioning

✿01 See the idea

At maturity, a fictional contract pays the positive difference between an asset price and a threshold of 80 €; if the price is below the threshold, it pays nothing. To prepare to calculate its value before maturity, imagine a sheet of graph paper: possible asset prices run horizontally, and times remaining run vertically. Each intersection will be an address at which to store a contract value.

The sheet cannot contain every asset price and every instant. We choose prices between 0 € and 160 €, spaced 40 € apart, and times remaining between 0 and 1 year, spaced 0.25 year apart. This choice of points is called a mesh. It determines where the calculation will work; it does not yet give the contract prices.

When the time remaining is 0, the contractual payoff is known. Boundary values must also be supplied along the sides. The other intersections await a calculation rule. Do not fill their entries with the payoffs at maturity: that would confuse different instants.

All data are fictional. The grid is deliberately coarse so that you can reconstruct it by hand. We are preparing only the coordinates, storage arrangement and boundaries; this provides neither financial advice nor a market price.

The rule to remember

Set the domain and units; count the intervals; use them to find the step sizes and nodes; distinguish supplied values from those still to be calculated.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Prepare all the addresses for the same contract

The contract covers one unit of the asset and settles only at maturity: it is a European call option. Its threshold, called the strike price, is K = 80 €. Maturity is at T = 1 year from today. Choose Smax = 160 €, M = 4 asset price intervals and N = 4 time intervals. The aim is to prepare the grid, before propagating any contract prices.

  1. Separate the coordinates from the stored value

    S is the asset price in euros. t is the time elapsed since today, in years; T is the total time until maturity. V(S,t) denotes the contract value, in euros for our unit of the asset. We use τ = T − t, the time remaining: today τ = T; at maturity τ = 0. Moving forwards in τ therefore means moving backwards in calendar time t.

    The asset price domain of the model is unbounded above. Truncating it means replacing it, for calculation, with the interval from 0 to Smax. This numerical upper limit does not claim that the asset price can never exceed it. M and N are strictly positive, dimensionless integers that count intervals; our grid is uniform in each direction.

    We assume a 0 interest rate, no dividends and an absorbing asset price of 0: in the model, it stays at 0. These assumptions will let us describe simple boundaries. No diffusion coefficient is needed to position the nodes; one will have to be specified to solve a pricing equation.

  2. Divide lengths by numbers of intervals

    The step size is the distance between two consecutive coordinates. Dividing the domain length by its number of intervals gives the step size; the quotient retains the unit of that length.

    ΔS=SmaxM=1604=40 €
    Δτ=TN=14=1/4 an

    The fraction 1/4 year can be written as 0.25 year. Reverse check: 4 × 40 € = 160 € and 4 × 0.25 year = 1 year. Dividing by the number of nodes would give a different spacing and miss the required endpoint.

  3. List the nodes and their storage arrangement

    i is the integer index of a column, from 0 to M inclusive; n is the integer index of a time row, from 0 to N inclusive. Lowercase n identifies the current row; uppercase N counts all the time intervals. The indices are dimensionless.

    Si=iΔS,τn=nΔτ

    The asset prices are 0, 40, 80, 120 and 160 €, giving 5 nodes. The times remaining are 0.00, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75 and 1.00 year, giving 5 rows. The threshold K = 80 € aligns with the node of index 2; this alignment is a convenient choice here, rather than a universal condition.

    The complete array has (M + 1) × (N + 1) = 5 × 5 = 25 entries. The 4 × 4 = 16 small rectangles are the cells between the nodes; they are not the locations where we store values. In an array arranged by time rows and then asset price columns, row n and column i correspond to the pair (Sᵢ, τn).

  4. Associate an address with the correct instant

    Uin≈V(Si,T−τn)

    Uᵢⁿ is the numerical value at this address, in euros; its superscript n is a row label, not a power. The sign ≈ reminds us that a future grid solution will be an approximation. Knowing i and n lets you find the coordinates; it does not yet let you find Uᵢⁿ.

    At i = 3 and n = 2, the asset price is 3 × 40 = 120 €; the time remaining is 2 × 0.25 = 0.50 year. The corresponding calendar time is 1 − 0.50 = 0.50 year. This address is in the interior and off the maturity row: its value still has to be calculated.

  5. Fill only the maturity row

    g(S) is the contract payoff at maturity, in euros. max(a,b) means keeping the larger of a and b. For our option, the payoff is the difference S − K when it is positive, otherwise 0 €.

    Ui0=g(Si)=max(Si−K,0)

    At asset prices 0, 40 and 80 €, there is no positive excess over the threshold of 80 €: all the payoffs are 0 €. At 120 €, subtract the threshold: 120 − 80 = 40 €. At the last node: 160 − 80 = 80 €. The maturity row therefore contains, from left to right: 0, 0, 0, 40, 80 €.

    This row is terminal data in t, but a starting row in τ. Copying it onto the other rows would not solve any pricing equation.

  6. Specify the boundaries and check the corners

    On the left, the absorbing asset price of 0 gives a 0 payoff and value for this option. We therefore impose U at spatial index 0 equal to 0 € on every row. On the right, we choose the large asset price approximation: the contract value is close to the asset price minus the strike price. With our 0 interest rate and dividends, this supplies the constant boundary value 160 − 80 = 80 €.

    In the truncated numerical problem, the entries on the right boundary are set to exactly 80 €. Relative to the model price on the unbounded domain, this is an approximation whenever τ is positive: at a finite price, the asset can still finish below the threshold. We have not shown that Smax = 160 € makes this error negligible.

    At the corners with 0 time remaining, the boundaries match the payoffs already entered: 0 € on the left and 80 € on the right. This check avoids contradictory data; it does not validate the accuracy of the upper asset price boundary at other times.

  7. Count what remains to be calculated and check the grid specification

    Each row after the maturity row has M − 1 = 4 − 1 = 3 interior nodes. There are N = 4 such rows, so 3 × 4 = 12 values must be produced during the future solution process. This does not mean that they will be solved for simultaneously: the chosen scheme will determine the order of operations.

    Another count: the maturity row supplies 5 values. The sides add 2 × 4 = 8 values, excluding the corners already counted. Total supplied: 5 + 8 = 13; then 13 + 12 = 25, the number of all intersections. A boundary value can be approximate while still being supplied.

    Final check: the asset price 100 € would correspond to index 100/40 = 5/2, or 2.5. This index is not an integer: this price is not a node of our grid. Rounding the index would change the question being asked. An interpolation rule or a different mesh will be needed, without inventing a contract value today.

    Our grid specification is now complete: truncated domain, units, step sizes, coordinates, storage arrangement, starting row and declared boundaries. The explicit scheme comes in the next unit. Neither its stability nor its convergence follows from this mesh alone.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Redraw the grid, then explain why the row with 0 time remaining is known and why an interior entry at the same asset price remains unknown. Then recover the total using the supplied values and the values to be produced.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “There are 5 points, so I divide 160 € by 5.”

    A step connects two consecutive points. With the endpoints included, 5 points delimit 4 intervals: ΔS = 40 €. The proposed division would give 32 €; after 4 intervals, you would no longer reach the stated upper limit. Count the gaps on the line again.

    Count the gaps and endpoints again

  • “The superscript n gives the time elapsed since today.”

    Here n counts steps in time remaining: τₙ = nΔτ. Then find t = T − τₙ. Row n = 0 corresponds to maturity, whose payoff is known, and row n = N corresponds to today. Writing down the convention prevents you from reversing the data.

    Reread the coordinates and direction of time

  • “A supplied boundary value must be exact, and more points are enough to fix everything.”

    The right boundary value is imposed in the calculation but approximate for the unbounded model. Reducing ΔS at a fixed upper limit does not move this boundary further away; increasing the upper limit with a fixed number of intervals increases ΔS. Separate local resolution from domain truncation before concluding that there is an improvement.

    Compare the two grid modifications

Exercise 1 · Locate and explain

An address is not yet a price

In the original grid, find the coordinates for i = 3, n = 2, then the associated calendar time. Someone enters 40 € in this entry by subtracting K from the asset price. Can they justify this entry using only the maturity data?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Multiply the spatial index by ΔS and the time index by Δτ separately.

Hint 2

The payoff g is given on the row with 0 time remaining. Check whether the requested address lies on it.

Compare with the solution

S = 3 × 40 = 120 € and τ = 2 × 0.25 = 0.50 year. So t = 1 − 0.50 = 0.50 year from today.

The subtraction 120 − 80 = 40 € gives the payoff for this asset price at maturity. The requested entry has positive time remaining and is not on any boundary: its price does not follow from this subtraction alone. It still has to be calculated with a model and a scheme.

Check your reasoning

  • Give the coordinates in euros and years, then convert τ to t.
  • Distinguish the payoff at maturity from the interior value that is still unknown.
  • Do not interpret the superscript n as a power or directly as a duration.

Exercise 2 · Counting on paper

Complete the count without counting corners twice

Redraw the complete grid. First count the values to be produced, then the data supplied by maturity and the sides. Someone proposes counting the sides on every row and then adding the whole maturity row. Where does their count overlap?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

On a row other than the maturity row, remove both endpoints to count the interior.

Hint 2

Mark the corners of the maturity row: do they also belong to the sides?

Compare with the solution

There are 3 interior nodes on each of the 4 rows other than the maturity row, so 12 values must be produced. The maturity row supplies 5 data values, then the sides supply 8 additional data values: 13 distinct data values.

The check recovers 13 + 12 = 25 nodes. Counting the sides on every row before adding the maturity row counts each of the 2 bottom corners a second time. Excluding these corners from the additional side count fixes the total.

These 12 values will be produced during a solution process; counting alone determines neither their order nor the number of equations solved together. The 16 cells in the drawing must not replace the number of nodes.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 12 values to be produced and 13 distinct data values.
  • Show the corners responsible for the overlap and explain their exclusion.
  • Recover 25 by counting the complete array a second way.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and transfer

Refine or move the boundary further away?

Keep T and N. Plan A keeps Smax = 160 € and replaces M with 8. Plan B keeps M = 4 and replaces Smax with 320 €. Calculate the spatial step sizes and the total number of nodes for each plan. Which refines the grid? Which moves the boundary further away? Can we claim that either already gives a more accurate price?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Recalculate ΔS using the upper limit and number of intervals for each plan.

Hint 2

The number of time rows remains 5. For plan A, count the columns including both endpoints.

Hint 3

Moving nodes closer together and moving an artificial boundary further away address different limitations. No price has yet been calculated.

Compare with the solution

Plan A: ΔS = 160/8 = 20 €, which is the original step size divided by 2. There are 8 + 1 = 9 columns and 5 rows, giving 45 nodes. On each row other than the maturity row, 7 nodes are interior: there will be 28 values to produce. This is spatial refinement on a fixed domain; the boundary remains at 160 €.

Plan B: ΔS = 320/4 = 80 €. The array dimensions remain 5 by 5, giving 25 nodes. The boundary is further away, but the step size is multiplied by 2: the spatial resolution becomes coarser.

Both plans keep Δτ = 0.25 year. Neither alone proves that a price is more accurate: a scheme and its stability conditions will be needed, followed by comparisons that vary the step size and upper limit separately. Even plan A does not remove the approximation at the right boundary. These checks will be developed later in the chapter.

Check your reasoning

  • Find 20 € and 45 nodes for A, 80 € and 25 nodes for B.
  • Distinguish spatial refinement from extending the domain.
  • Describe the limitation of each plan without attributing an unmeasured accuracy gain to it.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why do M intervals give M + 1 nodes? How can you find ΔS?

Intervals connect successive endpoints; the starting point is needed as well. With both boundaries included between 0 and Smax, ΔS = Smax/M. In our grid: 4 intervals, 5 nodes and 40 € per interval.

Recall 2Which convention makes the starting row known, and what does Uᵢⁿ mean?

We use time remaining τ = T − t. Row n = 0 is maturity, with a known payoff. Uᵢⁿ is a value in euros at Sᵢ = iΔS and τₙ = nΔτ; the superscript n is an index. Row n = N corresponds to today.

Recall 3With maturity and side data supplied, how many values remain to be produced?

There are M − 1 interior nodes on each of the N rows after the maturity row: (M − 1)N, giving 12 here. The other nodes are supplied, including those set by an approximate boundary value. Corners are counted only once.

Recall 4How can you check whether the asset price 100 € is a node of our grid?

The quotient 100/40 equals 5/2, which is not an integer. The asset price is not a node. Rounding the index would change the price; interpolation must be planned or the grid adapted, without inventing a contract price.

Recall 5Why are refinement and increasing Smax two different operations?

Refinement reduces the step size on a fixed domain. Increasing Smax moves the artificial boundary further away; with M fixed, it also increases the step size. Neither action alone establishes the accuracy or stability of a future solution process.
