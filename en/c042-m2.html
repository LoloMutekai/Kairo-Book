Level 2 · C042·M2

Norm, cosine and normalization

Your goalCalculate the length of document vectors, compare their directions using cosine similarity, and normalize them without mistaking this score for evidence of relevance.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

In a bicycle repair workshop, you are looking for a sheet about braking. For this exercise, we keep only the counts of the words “brake” and “wheel” in each text, in that order. The query contains 3 occurrences of “brake” and 4 of “wheel”; the sheet contains 4 and 3. All these counts are fictional. No model was queried to produce them.

Each text becomes an arrow: its horizontal displacement counts “brake”, and its vertical displacement counts “wheel”. This is its vector representation. An arrow has a length and a direction. Repeating the entire sheet makes its arrow longer but leaves the ratio between its components unchanged. This does not necessarily add useful information about adjusting the brake.

Here, the norm measures the length of the arrow. Cosine similarity compares directions by removing the effect of lengths. Normalization means bringing each nonzero arrow to length 1 by dividing all its components by its own norm. We can then compare directions using a dot product, which we will write out.

Before calculating, predict what should stay the same if the text of the sheet is copied 2 times: its length, its direction, or both? This counting model helps us understand the calculation. It understands neither the sentences nor the quality of a repair.

The rule to remember

Calculate each norm; check that it is nonzero; divide the dot product by the product of the norms. Normalizing each vector lets you recover the same cosine similarity using a dot product.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Compare the query with the braking sheet

Using the fictional counts q = (3 ; 4) and d = (4 ; 3), let us calculate the norms, the dot product and the cosine similarity, then recover the result after normalization. The first component always counts “brake”, and the second counts “wheel”.

  1. Define the symbols and the assumptions of the geometry

    q is the query vector; d is the sheet vector. The subscripts f and r label the “brake” and “wheel” components, respectively. Each component is measured here in occurrences. We choose perpendicular axes with the same unit and the same weight. Such a basis is called orthonormal. Without this choice, a different formula for length might be needed.

    The vectors being compared must have the same components in the same order. In this scene, their components are nonnegative real numbers, and the vectors are nonzero. We use exact arithmetic. The symbol · will denote the dot product of vectors; × denotes multiplication of numbers. The double bars ‖ ‖ denote the Euclidean norm.

  2. Measure lengths without confusing them with totals

    The Pythagorean theorem gives the square of the length as the sum of the squares of the components. The positive square root gives a length again. The squares are in occurrences squared, and the norm returns to occurrences.

    ‖q‖=qf2+qr2

    For q: 3 × 3 = 9, then 4 × 4 = 16. The sum is 25; its positive square root is 5, because 5 × 5 = 25. Thus ‖q‖ = 5 occurrences.

    For d: 4 × 4 = 16, then 3 × 3 = 9. The sum is 25, so ‖d‖ = 5 occurrences. Adding the components would count the selected words; it would not give the Euclidean length.

  3. Pair corresponding components

    The dot product multiplies components that have the same role, then adds the products. We pair “brake” with “brake” and “wheel” with “wheel”. Here, its unit is occurrences squared.

    q·d=qfdf+qrdr

    The contribution from “brake” is 3 × 4 = 12. The contribution from “wheel” is 4 × 3 = 12. The dot product is therefore 12 + 12 = 24 occurrences squared. Its value still depends on the lengths.

  4. Remove the lengths to compare directions

    θ denotes the angle between the arrows in the chosen geometry; if we measured it here, we would express it in degrees. c(q,d) names the cosine similarity score, which equals the cosine of this angle. We do not need to calculate the angle itself. For nonzero vectors:

    c(q,d)=cos(θ)=q·d‖q‖×‖d‖

    The denominator is 5 × 5 = 25 occurrences squared. The units in the numerator and denominator cancel. We obtain 24/25 = 24/25, or 0.96, which is dimensionless. This score is closer to 1 than to 0: the directions are close.

    For arbitrary real vectors, the cosine lies between -1 and 1. The value 1 indicates the same direction, 0 perpendicular directions, and -1 opposite directions. With our nonnegative counts, only scores from 0 to 1 are possible. A 0 score would be a defined result for nonzero vectors; it must not be confused with an impossible calculation.

  5. Normalize each vector by its own norm

    Here, the hat means “normalized vector”. Dividing the vector means dividing each of its components by the same positive norm. This operation preserves direction and gives a unit norm. It does not force the sum of the components to equal 1.

    q^=q‖q‖;d^=d‖d‖

    q̂ = (3/5 ; 4/5) = (3/5 ; 4/5), or (0.60 ; 0.80). Similarly, d̂ = (4/5 ; 3/5), or (0.80 ; 0.60). Each component is now dimensionless. Let us keep the exact fractions for the following operations.

  6. Check the unit norm and recover the score

    Check q̂: the sum of the squares is 9/25 + 16/25 = 1. Its norm is therefore 1. For d̂: 16/25 + 9/25 = 1; its norm is also 1.

    The dot product of the normalized vectors is 3/5 × 4/5 + 4/5 × 3/5 = 12/25 + 12/25 = 24/25. This exactly recovers the previous cosine similarity. The product of norms in the denominator is now 1 × 1, giving the equality:

    q^·d^=c(q,d)

    Normalizing both vectors guarantees this equality. Normalizing only the query is not enough to equate the dot product with cosine similarity in general.

  7. State exactly what the result establishes

    The calculation establishes that the directions are close for our representation and geometry. It does not say that the sheet explains braking correctly. An incorrect sheet can use the same words in the same proportions. You must read its content to judge its relevance.

    The 0 vector, whose components all equal 0, has norm 0. It has no direction to compare: division by its norm and cosine similarity are undefined. Before any division, you must therefore check the norms and flag the 0 case. The mathematical calculation does not prescribe an alternative document result.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the steps and reconstruct the process from the counts. Why does the square root appear in the norm? Why do the norms disappear after normalization? Finally, name what the score does not prove about the sheet.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “To normalize, I divide by the sum of the components.”

    That would fix a sum, rather than the Euclidean length. Calculate the square root of the sum of the squares, then divide each component by this norm. Check that the sum of the squares of the resulting components equals 1.

    Review the steps “Measure lengths” and “Check the unit norm”

  • “The dot product increases when I copy the sheet, so it becomes closer.”

    Repeating the entire text multiplies both the raw dot product and the norm of the sheet. The quotient can therefore stay the same. When both vectors have unit norm, their dot product gives the cosine similarity; this guarantee does not hold for arbitrary lengths.

    Redo the exercise “Copy the entire sheet”

  • “If all the counts are 0, the cosine similarity is 0.”

    A 0 norm makes the denominator 0: the score is undefined. A valid 0 cosine requires perpendicular nonzero vectors. Keep a 0 result separate from the inability to calculate.

    Return to “State exactly what the result establishes”

Compare with the solution

p has norm 5 occurrences: the positive square root of 5 squared is 5. Dividing each component by this norm gives p̂ = (1 ; 0), which is dimensionless.

The raw dot product is 5 × 4 + 0 × 3 = 20 occurrences squared. The product of the norms is 5 × 5 = 25 occurrences squared. The cosine similarity is 20/25 = 4/5, or 0.80, which is dimensionless.

Another check: p̂ · d̂ = 1 × 4/5 + 0 × 3/5 = 4/5. The score remains below 1 because d has a nonzero “wheel” component, whereas p does not.

Return to q and d from the example. The text of the sheet is copied 2 times: its counts become d′ = (8 ; 6). The prime denotes this version. Predict the cosine similarity before calculating. Then calculate ‖d′‖, q · d′, the cosine similarity and d̂′. Does a larger raw dot product prove that the directions are closer?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A positive factor applied to the entire arrow multiplies its length by that same factor.

Hint 2

Compare the factor that appears in the numerator with the one that appears in the denominator.

Compare with the solution

The direction does not change, so we predict the same cosine similarity. The norm becomes 2 × 5 = 10 occurrences. To check it another way, the sum of the squares of the components of d′ equals the square of 10; its positive square root is therefore indeed 10.

The dot product is 3 × 8 + 4 × 6 = 24 + 24 = 48 occurrences squared. The denominator is 5 × 10 = 50 in the same unit. The cosine similarity is 48/50 = 24/25 = 0.96: it is unchanged.

d̂′ = (8/10 ; 6/10) = (4/5 ; 3/5) = d̂. Normalization has removed the common positive factor. The raw dot product has doubled, without bringing the directions closer and without proving that there is any additional useful content.

The reasoning holds for any factor λ strictly greater than 0: λ denotes a dimensionless number that multiplies all the components of d. The numerator and the norm of d are multiplied by λ, which cancels in the quotient. A 0 factor would make the comparison undefined; a negative factor would reverse the direction.

Check your reasoning

  • Predict the invariance, then justify the cancellation of the positive factor in the quotient.
  • Distinguish the increase in the raw dot product from the unchanged cosine similarity.
  • Check that the normalized components remain exactly those of d̂.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do you find the Euclidean norm of our count vector?

For the perpendicular axes with the same unit chosen here, add the squares of the components, then take the positive square root. The squares have the unit squared; the norm returns to the unit of the components.

Recall 2What does cosine similarity divide, and what conditions must you check?

Divide the dot product by the product of the norms. The vectors must be nonzero and expressed in the same coordinates and geometry. The result is dimensionless and compares directions.

Recall 3What check distinguishes Euclidean normalization from simply dividing by a total?

After dividing each vector by its own positive norm, the sum of the squares of its components equals 1. The norm is therefore 1, without requiring the sum of the components to equal 1. The dot product of the two normalized vectors recovers the cosine similarity.

Recall 4Why does copying the entire sheet leave its cosine similarity with the query unchanged?

The common positive factor multiplies the dot product and the norm of the sheet. It cancels in the quotient. The length changes; the direction and the cosine similarity stay the same.

Recall 5Distinguish a 0 cosine, an undefined cosine and evidence of relevance.

A 0 cosine concerns perpendicular nonzero vectors. A 0 norm makes the cosine undefined. A defined score, even a high one, does not prove that the sheet correctly meets the need: it assesses only the chosen representation.

Explain why normalizing both vectors reduces the cosine similarity to their dot product. Finish by stating what still needs to be checked in the content of the sheet and how the 0 vector is handled mathematically.
