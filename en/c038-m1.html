Level 2 · C038·M1

Signal and typed absence

Your goalRepresent a reading as a known value or a named absence, then show why 0, absence and an implausible value cannot replace one another in a calculation.

Before you start
c019-m5 : clocks and persistence · c015-m5 : diagnosis · c024-m5 : reading quality · c037-m5 : controlled cleaning · c009-m5 : reading notes

✿01 See the idea

A fictional cold room has four temperature probes: door, back, top and floor. At a given moment, a small program takes a reading from each to report the mean temperature of the room. Here is what comes back: the door sends 6000 millidegrees Celsius, or 6 °C; the back sends 0, or 0 °C; the top probe does not respond; the floor probe sends 400000 millidegrees, or 400 °C.

These four lines are different kinds of information. The 0 at the back is a real reading: 0 °C is a possible temperature and carries information. The silence from the top probe is not a reading: we know nothing. And 400 °C is a number that was received, but it falls outside the range from -40 to 150 °C that we consider plausible for this probe. A fault is one possible explanation, not a certainty.

Typed absence means never collapsing these differences into a single number. Each reading is either known, with its value, or absent, with a named reason: here, unread or outside range. A 0 used to replace an absence looks like a reading, is added without any error and ends up in the mean. That is precisely what makes it dangerous.

The raw signal arrives in millidegrees: a thousandth of a degree Celsius. To read it in degrees, divide by 1000. This conversion only makes sense if there is a number to convert; it therefore comes after the existence check and never replaces it.

The scene is fictional: none of the values comes from an actual observation, and the plausible range is a choice for the course, not a setting validated for an installation. Here we only address the distinction between value and absence. The age of a reading, alarms and signs of life are covered in nearby units in the chapter.

The rule to remember

A reading is a known value or a named absence: check existence, then range, before calculating; never replace an absence with 0, and never treat a 0 as an absence.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Classify four readings, then calculate without mixing their states

Return to the fictional cold room: door 6000 m°C, back 0 m°C, top with no signal, floor 400000 m°C. Fictional plausible range: from -40 to 150 °C. Calculate the mean for the probes whose values are known, and compare it with three incorrect replacements.

  1. Name the quantities and assumptions

    The number of probes is n = 4, with no unit. For probe i, the raw signal r is an integer in millidegrees Celsius, or it does not exist. When it exists, the value v in degrees Celsius is:

    vi=ri1000

    Let L = -40 °C and H = 150 °C be the fictional bounds of the plausible range. K is the set of known, plausible probes:

    K={i tel que ri existe et L≤vi≤H}

    Assumptions: a failed reading produces no number, and the program does not invent one; the range is a modelling judgement that signals doubt without proving a fault; the mean is a convenient summary of this scene, not the physical temperature of the room.

  2. Classify each reading before any calculation

    Door: the signal exists, and 6000 / 1000 = 6 °C. Since -40 ≤ 6 ≤ 150, the reading is known. Back: the signal exists and equals 0 / 1000 = 0 °C. A 0 exists: the reading is known and just as valid as the previous one.

    Top: no signal, so no number to convert. The reading is absent, with reason unread. Floor: 400000 / 1000 = 400 °C, or 250 °C above the upper bound. The reading is absent, with reason outside range. We keep the raw number for later diagnosis without using it in the mean.

  3. Count known and absent readings

    We have 1 unread absence and 1 absence outside range, giving a = 1 + 1 = 2 absences. The number of known probes is therefore:

    |K|=n−a=4−2=2

    Coverage is 2 / 4 = 0.5: half the probes are known. This figure must always accompany the mean, because it tells the reader what the mean is based on.

  4. Calculate the mean using typed readings

    Sum only the values in K and divide by their count. The mean is defined only if K contains at least one probe.

    v¯=∑i∈Kvi|K|
    v¯=6+02=62=3 °C

    Check: 3 × 2 = 6, which gives back the sum 6. Also, 3 remains between the smallest known value (0 °C) and the largest (6 °C), as any mean does.

  5. Compare with three incorrect replacements

    Absences replaced with 0: the sum stays 6, but we divide by 4. This gives 1.5 °C, or 1.5 °C less. This result makes the room seem truly colder, and nothing indicates that two probes provided no information.

    Implausible value kept: the sum becomes 6 + 400 = 406, and the mean 101.5 °C, or 98.5 °C more than the mean using typed readings. A single faulty probe then determines the result.

    0 treated as absent: remove the back probe, leaving 2 − 1 = 1 probe. The mean becomes 6 °C: the only cold reading has disappeared.

  6. Check the empty case

    If no probe were known, K would be empty: 2 would become 0, division would be impossible and the mean would not exist. The correct answer is then a named absence, such as no known probe, and never 0 °C. Since 0 °C is within the plausible range, no filter would catch this error.

    This calculation describes a rule for the scene. It does not say whether the mean is the right summary for a real cold room, or whether the chosen range suits a real sensor.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the diagram, explain why the mean using typed readings divides by 2 rather than 4, and why the 0 at the back stays in the sum while the top probe is left out.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If the probe does not respond, I put 0 so the calculation works.”

    0 is a possible reading: it contributes to both the sum and the count. A silent probe has no value; mark it absent, with its reason, and divide by the number of known probes. Repeat the comparison of replacements.

    Review the comparison of replacements

  • “A 0 means there is nothing to read.”

    Check whether the signal exists, not its value. A test such as “if the reading” also excludes 0 and makes the coldest reading disappear. An existing 0 is known, and it counts.

    Review the ranking of readings by state

  • “Any number received must be a reading.”

    A number can fall outside the plausible range. Do not include it in the mean, but do not silently discard it: mark it absent with reason outside range and keep it for diagnosis. It raises doubt; it does not prove a fault.

    Return to the idea of a plausible range

Mean of five probes

Another fictional trace from 5 probes, already in degrees Celsius: A 5, B 0, C 0, D with no signal, E -60. The plausible range remains from -40 to 150 °C. Classify each reading, give the number of known probes and the mean using typed readings. Also calculate the mean that would result if D and E were replaced with 0.

Hint 1

Classify first: does a reading exist, and then is it within the range?

Hint 2

Two values are 0. Are we asking whether they exist, or whether they are 0?

Compare with the solution

A, B and C exist and are within the range: they are known. D has no signal: absent, unread. E is -60 °C, or 20 °C below the lower bound: absent, outside range.

Absences: 1 + 1 = 2. Known probes: 5 − 2 = 3. Sum: 5 + 0 + 0 = 5, because B and C are 0 but count. Mean using typed readings: 5 / 3 = 1.67 °C, rounded to two decimal places.

With D and E replaced with 0, divide the same sum by 5: 5 / 5 = 1 °C. The difference comes only from the denominator. Check: 1.67 × 3 gives back 5.

Check your reasoning

  • D and E classified as absent, with two different reasons.
  • A, B and C all known, including the zeros.
  • Division by the number of known probes rather than the total.
  • Numerical comparison with replacement by 0.

A dashboard that displays 0

A fictional dashboard displays “mean temperature: 0 °C”. Yet all 3 probes in the trace were unread. State what is wrong, calculate the number of known probes and propose the correct display.

Hint 1

Calculate |K| before calculating a mean.

Hint 2

Can you divide by the number of known probes when that number is 0?

Compare with the solution

Known probes: 3 − 3 = 0. The mean requires division by this number, which is 0: it does not exist. Displaying 0 °C instead asserts a specific temperature.

The error is serious because 0 °C lies within the plausible range: no range check detects it, and a reader may believe the room is at freezing point. The correct display is a named absence, such as “unknown: no known probe”, with coverage 0 out of 3.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculation of |K| before any mean.
  • Division by 0 recognised as impossible.
  • Proposed display uses a named absence and includes coverage.
  • Explanation of why 0 passes the range check.

Freezing, no freezing or unknown

Fictional course rule: freezing occurs if the known value is less than or equal to 0 °C. Apply it to the four probes in the example. Give the verdict for each, count the verdicts of each kind and check that the total equals the number of probes. Then explain why a simple “freezing: yes or no” is not enough.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A verdict can have three values, not two.

Hint 2

Can an absence satisfy or violate the rule?

Hint 3

For the floor, do not be guided by the fact that 400 exceeds 0.

Compare with the solution

Door: 6 °C, above 0, so no freezing. Back: 0 °C, less than or equal to 0, so freezing. Top: absent, unread, so unknown. Floor: absent, outside range, so unknown, even though 400 exceeds 0.

Known verdicts: 1 + 1 = 2. Including the absences: 2 + 2 = 4, accounting for all 4 probes. A “yes or no” would force two probes with no information to take a side: either a false freezing verdict or a false “no freezing” that may hide a risk. The unknown verdict makes the decision honest.

Check your reasoning

  • Three distinct verdicts: freezing, no freezing, unknown.
  • The 0 at the back recognised as freezing.
  • The floor classified as unknown despite its large value.
  • Total verdicts equal to the number of probes.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is a typed absence?

A reading with no value, accompanied by a named reason such as unread or outside range, instead of a replacement number.

Recall 2Why is 0 not an absence?

0 is a possible, measurable value: it contributes to both the sum and the count. An absence has no value to count.

Recall 3Why check the range before averaging, and what should you do with a value outside range?

An implausible number would distort the mean. Mark it absent with reason outside range, without silently discarding it, because it is still useful for diagnosis.

Recall 4What do you divide by for the mean using typed readings?

By the number of known, plausible probes rather than the total. If there are none, the mean does not exist.

Recall 5What information must accompany a partial mean?

The number of known probes out of the total, so the reader can see what the figure is based on.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Classify each reading as a known value or a named absence before any calculation; convert and check the range only when a number exists; count the known readings; divide by that count; report coverage; never replace an absence with 0 or treat a 0 as an absence.
