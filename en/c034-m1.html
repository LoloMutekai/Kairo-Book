Level 1 · C034·M1

Riemann sums

Your goalEstimate a volume from a variable flow rate, justify the chosen rectangles, and check what their sum allows us to conclude.

Before you start
c033-m5 : affine approximation

✿01 See the idea

A tap fills a tank. Its stream grows steadily: knowing the initial flow rate does not tell us how much water will have entered by the end. To work out the amount of water, imagine short time intervals and replace the changing flow rate in each interval with a constant flow rate.

At a constant flow rate, the volume entering is the flow rate multiplied by the duration. On a graph of flow rate against time, this product is the area of a rectangle: its height is in litres per minute, its width is in minutes, and its area represents litres. Adding the rectangles gives an estimate of the accumulated volume.

We use just one scenario, which is entirely fictional: over 4 min, the flow rate increases along a straight line from 2 to 6 L/min. We assume that the flow rate is known at every instant, that water enters without any leaks or outflow, and that there is no measurement error. We want the volume that enters during this interval; if the tank already contained water, this would not be its final total volume.

Take 4 equal intervals. If we keep the flow rate at the start of each interval, the rectangles stay below the increasing flow rate. If we keep the rate at the end, they extend above it. The volume in our model lies between the two sums. Choosing a height is therefore part of the calculation: “adding the flow rates” is not enough.

The rule to remember

Divide the time into intervals; choose a flow rate in each interval; multiply each height by its duration; add the volumes; check the units and whether the approximation is an underestimate or an overestimate.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Bound the volume entering with 4 intervals

Over the interval from 0 to 4 min, the fictional flow rate increases by 1 L/min each minute. Let us construct the left and right endpoint sums without assuming a constant flow rate throughout the filling process.

  1. Describe the flow rate and its duration

    t is the elapsed time in minutes; q(t) is the flow rate at that instant in L/min. We set q(t) = q_d + ct, where q_d = 2 L/min is the initial flow rate and c = 1 litre per minute per minute. The product ct is therefore in L/min. The rule is affine and increasing throughout the interval, rather than only at the instants shown in the diagram.

    a = 0 min and b = 4 min are the time endpoints. V denotes the volume that enters between a and b, in litres. The number n = 4 is a positive integer with no unit. The common width h is calculated by dividing the duration to be covered by the number of intervals.

    h=b−an=4−04=1 min

    The endpoints are 0, 1, 2, 3 and 4 min. Check: 4 × 1 = 4 min; the whole interval is covered once, with no gaps or overlaps.

  2. Write what a Riemann sum adds together

    Number the intervals with i, an integer from 1 to n. Endpoint i is tᵢ = a + ih. In the interval from the preceding endpoint to tᵢ, choose an instant τᵢ, pronounced “tau subscript i”, in minutes. Its height is q(τᵢ). The symbol Σ means “add the term for all the indicated values of i”. S_n denotes this sum, in litres.

    Sn=∑i=1nq(τi)h

    In the formula, the general term is q(τᵢ)h: the volume that the chosen flow rate would deliver if it remained constant during this interval. τᵢ can be the start, the end or the midpoint; it must stay within the corresponding interval. The rectangles replace the actual flow rate, and we need to examine the resulting difference.

  3. Construct each left endpoint rectangle

    For the left endpoints, the chosen instants are 0, 1, 2 and 3 min. The rule gives 2, 3, 4 and 5 L/min, respectively. For example, at t = 2 min: 2 + 1 × 2 = 4 L/min.

    Each height must be multiplied by h = 1 min. The rectangle volumes are therefore 2 × 1 = 2 L; 3 × 1 = 3 L; 4 × 1 = 4 L; 5 × 1 = 5 L.

    G_n denotes the left endpoint sum. Group the volumes to check the addition: 2 + 3 = 5 L and 4 + 5 = 9 L. Then 5 + 9 = 14 L.

    G4=1×(2+3+4+5)=14 L

    Since the flow rate increases in each interval, its starting rate is a minimum. The left endpoint rectangles therefore underestimate V. This conclusion uses the increase throughout the interval.

  4. Shift the heights to the right endpoints

    D_n denotes the right endpoint sum. The chosen instants are now 1, 2, 3 and 4 min. The heights are 3, 4, 5 and 6 L/min. Multiply their sum by the same width.

    D4=1×(3+4+5+6)=18 L

    The flow rate at the end of an interval is now a maximum. Using it gives an upper bound for the volume entering during the interval, so the sum gives an upper bound for V. We have 14 L ≤ V ≤ 18 L. These are bounds under our model, rather than a statistical interval derived from readings.

  5. Check the result with another calculation

    The interior heights are common to both sums. When we take the difference, they cancel; only the final and initial flow rates remain. This identity holds for intervals of equal width, even when the flow rate is not affine.

    Dn−Gn=h[q(b)−q(a)]

    The difference in flow rates is 6 − 2 = 4 L/min. The difference in volumes is 1 × 4 = 4 L, matching 18 − 14 = 4 L. The identity checks the arithmetic; by itself, it does not prove that V lies between the sums if the flow rate stops being monotonic.

    Another check on the size of the result: the flow rate stays between 2 and 6 L/min for 4 min. The volume is therefore between 8 and 24 L. Our bounds based on intervals lie within this initial range. None of these checks validates the model of a real tap.

  6. Use narrower rectangles

    With n = 8, the width becomes 4 ÷ 8 = 1/2 min, or 0.5 min. Without repeating all the additions yet, the difference between the sums must become 0.5 × 4 = 2 L. The refinement exercise will ask you to reconstruct both sums.

    Why does this subdivision help? Over a shorter interval, our continuous flow rate changes less, so the rectangle represents it better. Continuous here means that a sufficiently close time gives a flow rate as close as desired, with no jump. For a continuous flow rate on a closed interval, including its endpoints, Riemann sums approach the same accumulated volume as the interval widths tend to 0, regardless of the instant chosen in each interval. We do not give the rectangles 0 width in a finite calculation.

    Here, h = (b − a)/n: increasing n decreases h. The formula for the difference already shows that the bounds get closer. We stop at constructing and checking the sums; the next unit will introduce the concept of an integral.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I add the flow rates and get litres directly.”

    The sum of the flow rates is still in L/min. Each chosen flow rate must be multiplied by the duration of its interval. Our initial width is 1 min, which numerically hides this omission; at 0.5 min, omitting the width changes the result.

    Check the units in the first exercise

  • “The final flow rate lasts throughout the filling process.”

    The final flow rate of 6 L/min describes only one instant. Keeping it for 4 min would give 24 L, a rough bound here. To construct the right endpoint sum, take the final flow rate of each interval and multiply it by the duration of that interval.

    Revisit the choice of right endpoint heights

  • “More rectangles always guarantee an exact volume.”

    A finite number of rectangles generally gives an approximation. For our increasing flow rate, you can measure the width between the bounds; refinement reduces it without making it 0. If the variation between readings is unknown, hidden peaks prevent us from applying this guarantee without an additional assumption.

Exercise 1 · Application

The first narrower rectangle

The same filling process is divided into 8 intervals. In the first, we keep the initial flow rate of 2 L/min for 0.5 min. What volume does this rectangle represent? Is it the exact volume that enters during this interval?

Hint 1

Write the product with its units before calculating.

Hint 2

The actual flow rate increases from the start of the interval.

Compare with the solution

The rectangle represents 2 × 0.5 = 1 L. The minutes cancel: (L/min) × min = L. Keeping only 2 would give a flow rate rather than a volume.

This rectangle underestimates the volume for the interval: the actual flow rate becomes greater than the initial flow rate. The multiplication is exact for the chosen rectangle; whether it matches the volume that enters is a separate question.

Check your reasoning

  • Multiply the flow rate by the width 0.5 min.
  • Give 1 L and justify the underestimate using the increasing flow rate.

Exercise 2 · Reconstructing the calculation

Move to finer intervals

For the 8 intervals of 0.5 min, the left endpoint flow rates are 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 4.5, 5 and 5.5 L/min. Calculate G_n, then D_n, and compare the width between the bounds with the original subdivision.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Group the flow rates already used at the left endpoints and the new ones between them.

Hint 2

For the right endpoint sum, replace only the first flow rate with the flow rate at the end of the filling process.

Compare with the solution

The original flow rates give 2 + 3 = 5 L/min and 4 + 5 = 9 L/min, for a sum of 14 L/min. The flow rates inserted between them give 2.5 + 3.5 = 6 L/min and 4.5 + 5.5 = 10 L/min, for a sum of 16 L/min.

In total, 14 + 16 = 30 L/min. The left endpoint sum is 0.5 × 30 = 15 L. We can group the flow rates before multiplying because all the widths are equal.

Replacing the first flow rate with the last adds 0.5 × (6 − 2) = 2 L. The right endpoint sum is therefore 15 + 2 = 17 L. Thus 15 L ≤ V ≤ 17 L. The width decreases from 4 to 2 L, consistent with dividing h by 2. The volume in the model has not changed; our bounds have become tighter.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover the sum of the flow rates, 30 L/min, before calculating the volume.
  • Obtain the bounds 15 L and 17 L.
  • Justify the width 2 L and retain the assumption of an increasing flow rate.

Exercise 3 · Justification and limitation

How well does the midpoint choice work?

Return to the 4 intervals of 1 min, but choose the flow rate at the midpoint of each. These flow rates are 2.5, 3.5, 4.5 and 5.5 L/min. Calculate their Riemann sum. A colleague claims that midpoints always give the exact volume: examine her claim, first for our affine flow rate and then for an arbitrary continuous flow rate.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare the midpoint flow rate with the flow rates at both endpoints of the same interval.

Hint 2

With a straight line, the midpoint rectangle has an excess triangle and a missing triangle of equal area. An arbitrary curve does not necessarily have this symmetry.

Compare with the solution

The sum of the heights is 6 + 10 = 16 L/min. The midpoint Riemann sum is therefore 1 × 16 = 16 L.

In our affine model, each midpoint rectangle has as much excess area at the start as missing area at the end: these are triangles with the same base and height. The two errors cancel on each interval. The result 16 L therefore matches the volume in the model in this particular case.

An equivalent geometric check: half the sum of the endpoint flow rates, multiplied by the duration, gives the area of the trapezium under a straight line. For our subdivision, (14 + 18) ÷ 2 = 32 ÷ 2 = 16 L, matching the midpoint sum. The previous bounds do contain this value.

The colleague generalizes too far: a continuous flow rate can form a bump between the chosen points, so the areas no longer cancel. Choosing midpoints still produces a Riemann sum; for a finite subdivision, its exactness here requires the affine form, rather than continuity alone.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 16 L by multiplying the heights by the width.
  • Explain the cancellation for a straight line.
  • Distinguish exactness in this model from a guarantee for every continuous flow rate.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does a term q(τᵢ)h represent in our sum?

The volume of the rectangle that keeps the flow rate at the chosen instant τᵢ throughout the interval of duration h. The unit is (L/min) × min = L.

Recall 2What must be specified in addition to the number of intervals?

Their endpoints and the instant chosen in each to set the height: the start, the end or another point. Here the widths are equal, with h = (b − a)/n.

Recall 3Why do the left and right endpoint sums bound our volume?

The flow rate is increasing on each interval: the left endpoint height is a minimum and the right endpoint height is a maximum. The ordering of their sums would not be enough without this assumption.

Recall 4Which calculation checks that our bounds get closer?

For equal intervals, D_n − G_n = h[q(b) − q(a)]. The endpoint flow rates stay fixed, so reducing h reduces the width between the bounds in our monotonic case.

Recall 5Does a sum calculated exactly necessarily give the exact volume?

No. The arithmetic can be exact while the rectangles only approximate the flow rate. Midpoints are exact here because of the affine form; continuity alone does not make a finite sum exact.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without rereading: from the variable flow rate to time intervals, from intervals to chosen heights, from flow rate × duration products to their sum, and then from assumptions to bounds. Recognize the scope of a calculation step

What you understand
A rate multiplied by a duration represents an accumulation with the rate held constant. The subdivision and the choice of rate determine the approximation. The accuracy of an addition does not demonstrate how faithfully this replacement represents the original.
