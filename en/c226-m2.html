Poisson process

Your goalIn a fictional message scenario, calculate the mean μ = λΔ for a time window, the relative weights of its counts, and the union of two disjoint windows, then state what a single trace does not prove.

✿01 See the idea

An inbox receives messages at unpredictable times. We are not looking for the exact time of the next message: we count how many arrive within a given time window. The whole page uses this entirely fictional scenario: the rate of 2 messages per hour is a teaching setting, not a measured value.

The count N(t) is the number of arrivals in the time interval (0, t], with N(0) = 0. We model it as a Poisson process under three assumptions: arrivals occur separately, never two at the same instant; the rate λ, in messages per hour, does not change; two windows that do not overlap have independent counts. Two events are independent when the probability of both occurring is the product of their probabilities.

Why does a specific distribution follow? Divide a window into n time slices so short that a slice receives at most one message. Each slice receives one with probability p = μ / n, independently of the others, where μ is the mean number of messages expected in the window. The count is then binomial: the number of slices with a message among n independent trials with the same probability p.

Compare two adjacent counts. The ratio of the probability of k + 1 arrivals to that of k arrivals is (n − k) / (k + 1) × p / (1 − p). As n increases, with μ fixed, it tends to μ / (k + 1). This ratio is enough to reconstruct the entire distribution: start with weight 1 for 0 arrivals, and each additional arrival multiplies the weight by μ / k.

The common factor that turns these weights into probabilities is the probability of 0 arrivals, exp(−μ). We accept it here: it is the probability that the wait for the first arrival exceeds the window, when that wait is memoryless, as in the previous unit on waiting times. Of its other prerequisites, this page uses only the ideas of probability and mean; everything else is defined as the text proceeds.

The rule to remember

In a window of duration Δ, the mean is μ = λΔ; the weight of count k is obtained by starting from 1 and multiplying by μ / j for each additional arrival j; across independent disjoint windows, multiply the weights and add the cases.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Inbox messages over three hours

Fictional scenario: an inbox receives an average of λ = 2 messages per hour. Window A lasts 1 hour; window B, which follows it immediately, lasts 2 hours. We calculate the mean for each window, the relative weights of the counts for A, and the case of a single arrival over the union A ∪ B, then review a fictional trace of 6 arrivals.

  1. Define the quantities and assumptions

    t is the time in hours since the inbox was opened. For a window F, N(F) is the number of messages that arrived during F and Δ(F) is its duration in hours. λ is the mean rate, in messages per hour, assumed known and constant. The mean for the window is μ(F) = λ × Δ(F); its unit is messages, since messages per hour × hours gives messages.

    μ(A)=λ×Δ(A)

    The model assumes separate arrivals, a constant rate, and independent counts over disjoint windows. If a burst, a quiet period, or a message that triggers another breaks one of these assumptions, the following formulas no longer apply as written.

  2. Calculate the means while keeping track of units

    The durations are already in hours, matching the time unit of λ: no conversion is needed. The product is a mean in messages, not a guaranteed count.

    μ(A)=2×1=2 messages ; μ(B)=2×2=4 messages

    Check: window B lasts 2 times as long as A, so its mean is 2 times as large: 4 compared with 2.

  3. Build the relative weights for window A

    Apply the recurrence from the intuition section: the weight for 0 arrivals is 1, then the weight of count k is the weight of count k − 1 multiplied by μ / k.

    w0=1,wk=wk−1×μk

    With μ = 2, the weight of count 1 is 2. The next factors are μ / 2 = 1, μ / 3 = 2/3, and μ / 4 = 1/2.

    w2=2×1=2 ; w3=2×2/3=4/3 ; w4=4/3×1/2=2/3

    Check using the direct formula given below: for k = 2, μ × μ / 2 = 2, as with the recurrence. Counts 1 and 2 have the same maximum weight: there is more than one most probable count, and the probability of obtaining the mean is less than one.

    wk=μkk!

    Finally, the probability of count k is written as follows, where exp(−μ) is the common factor accepted above.

    P(N(A)=k)=e−μwk

  4. Combine two disjoint windows

    Window A ∪ B lasts 3 hours. Its mean can be calculated directly or by adding those of A and B; both methods must give the same number.

    μ(A∪B)=λ×(Δ(A)+Δ(B))=2×3=6=2+4

    Find the weight for a single arrival in total. Two cases are mutually exclusive: no arrivals in A and one in B, with weight 1 × 4; or one in A and none in B, with weight 2 × 1. Multiply within each case because the windows are disjoint and independent, then add because the two cases cannot occur together.

    1×4+2×1=4+2=6=w1(6)

    Check: the resulting weight is that of count 1 for the mean of the union, 6. The exp(−μ) factors also multiply correctly, since exp(−2) × exp(−4) = exp(−6).

  5. Review a fictional trace without drawing conclusions

    Here are 6 fictional waiting times between successive arrivals, in minutes: 20, 45, 30, 25, 50, and 15. Their cumulative sums give the arrival times 20, 65, 95, 120, 170, and 185 minutes. Window A ends at 60 minutes and B at 180 minutes: A contains 1 arrival, B contains 4, and the last arrival falls after both windows.

    Counts 1 and 4 are compatible with means 2 and 4: count 1 has a maximum weight for A, and 4 is exactly the mean for B. The rate calculated from the trace is:

    6185×60=360185≈1.95 messages par heure

    This number is close to λ, but it summarizes only 6 arrivals from an invented trace. It does not test independence, a constant rate, or the choice of λ. A real trace would require its own checks, beyond this unit.

Explain it in your own words

Explain why the weights for A and B are multiplied for a given case, then why the two cases for a single arrival in total are added. What would happen to this calculation if the windows overlapped?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The rate λ directly gives the number of messages in a window.”

    λ is a rate in messages per hour; the mean count in a window is μ = λΔ, with Δ expressed in the same time unit as λ. Convert minutes to hours before multiplying.

    Redo the exercise on units

  • “If μ is 2, the window always receives 2 messages.”

    μ is a mean. With μ = 2, counts 1 and 2 have the same maximum weight, and counts 0, 3, or 4 remain possible. Only the distribution of weights describes the randomness.

    Review the relative weights

  • “A trace whose calculated rate is close to λ proves that the process is Poisson.”

    A trace compatible with a rate does not test the independence of windows or whether the rate is constant. It illustrates the model without validating it; reading code or course material does not validate it either.

    Review what the evidence does not establish

Exercise 1 · Explained application

A window specified in minutes

Still using λ = 2 messages per hour, window C lasts 30 minutes. Calculate its mean μ(C) and the weights of counts 0, 1, and 2. What would multiplying 2 by 30 give without conversion?

Δ(C)=3060 heure
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First express the duration in hours, the time unit of λ.

Hint 2

The weight of count 0 is 1; that of count 1 is μ; that of count 2 is the previous weight multiplied by μ / 2.

Compare with the solution

Duration: 30 / 60 = 1/2 hour. Mean: 2 × 1/2 = 1 message. Weights: 1 for count 0, 1 for count 1, then 1 × 1/2 = 1/2 for count 2.

Without conversion, 2 × 30 = 60: the result is 60 times too large because it mixes minutes and hours. It would predict 60 messages on average in half an hour, whereas the rate gives only 2 per hour.

Check your reasoning

  • The duration is converted to hours before multiplication.
  • The mean is given in messages.
  • The weight of count 2 uses the factor μ / 2.
  • The result without conversion is identified as a unit error.

Exercise 2 · Explained application

A mean that is not an integer

Still using λ = 2 messages per hour, window D lasts 45 minutes. Calculate μ(D), then the weights of counts 1, 2, and 3. Which count is most probable? Is it the integer nearest to the mean?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Convert 45 minutes to hours, then multiply by λ.

Hint 2

Use the factor μ / k twice: first for k = 2, then for k = 3.

Compare with the solution

Duration: 45 / 60 = 3/4 hour, so μ(D) = 2 × 3/4 = 3/2 messages.

Weights: count 1 has weight 3/2; count 2 has weight 3/2 × 3/4 = 9/8; count 3 has weight 9/8 × 1/2 = 9/16. The weight of count 0, which is 1, lies between 9/8 and 9/16.

The most probable count is therefore 1, with weight 3/2. The integer nearest to the mean is 2, but its weight 9/8 is smaller: the most probable count is not always the rounded mean.

Check your reasoning

  • Conversion to hours followed by an exact μ(D), without early rounding.
  • Weights calculated using the factor μ / k, in order.
  • Comparison of the ranking of counts using their weights.
  • Distinction between the most probable count and the rounded mean.

Exercise 3 · Guided proof

Two arrivals over the union of C and D

Windows C and D follow each other without overlapping. Calculate the weight of 2 arrivals in total over C ∪ D in two ways: by separating the cases according to the number of arrivals in C and D, then directly using the mean of the union. The two results must agree.

P(N(C∪D)=2)∝w2(μ(C∪D))
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

There are three cases: 0 and 2, 1 and 1, 2 and 0 arrivals in C and D.

Hint 2

Within each case, multiply the weights for C and D; then add the three cases.

Hint 3

For the direct method, first add μ(C) and μ(D), then apply the recurrence up to count 2.

Compare with the solution

Method by cases. 0 in C and 2 in D: 1 × 9/8 = 9/8. One in C and one in D: 1 × 3/2 = 3/2. Two in C and 0 in D: 1/2 × 1 = 1/2. The sum is 9/8 + 3/2 + 1/2 = 25/8.

Direct method. μ(C ∪ D) = μ(C) + μ(D) = 1 + 3/2 = 5/2. The weight of count 2 is μ × μ / 2 = 5/2 × 5/2 / 2 = 25/8, identical to the result by cases.

This agreement verifies the calculation: since the windows are disjoint and independent, products and sums of cases reconstruct the weight for the union. It holds only under these assumptions; for overlapping windows, multiplying the weights would not make sense.

Check your reasoning

  • All three cases are listed, with no omissions or duplicates.
  • Multiplication within each case, addition across cases.
  • The mean of the union is the sum of the means.
  • The two methods are compared and their agreement is justified by independence.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the mean μ for a window, and in what unit?

μ = λΔ, with λ in messages per hour and Δ in hours; μ is expressed in messages.

Recall 2How do you go from the weight of count k − 1 to that of count k?

Multiply by μ / k, starting with weight 1 for 0 arrivals. The probability is this weight multiplied by exp(−μ).

Recall 3Why can the weights for two windows be multiplied?

Because they are disjoint and the model assumes their counts are independent. Without this assumption, the product no longer represents both counts occurring together.

Recall 4What does a trace whose calculated rate is close to λ establish?

Only that the rate calculated from the trace is compatible with the chosen rate. It does not test independence or whether the rate is constant.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Name the count, the rate, and its unit; state the three model assumptions; convert the duration before calculating μ = λΔ; build the weights using the factor μ / k; multiply the weights over independent disjoint windows, then add the cases; review a trace without treating it as proof of the model.
