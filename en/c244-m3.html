Level 4 · C244·M3

Quasi-explicit calibration

Your goalSeparate the search for the centre and width of an SVI slice from its inner linear solve, then check the resulting coefficients and residuals.

Before you start
c243-m5 : SSVI · c051-m5 : error diagnostics · c220-m5 : numerical reproducibility · c198-m5 : independent validation · c244-m1 : initialisation · c244-m2 : constraints

✿01 See the idea

On a calibration worksheet, you have 3 observations for options with the same expiry. They show a left wing that is higher than the right wing. You want to fit an SVI slice: a curve of total variance against relative strike. All the data on this worksheet are fictional, chosen to allow exact calculation.

Imagine a curve template whose centre and width you fix first. Once you have chosen this geometry, you still need to adjust the level, slope and amplitude of a rounded component. These adjustments add linearly: you can solve for them together, rather than searching for all the parameters by trial and error.

The calibration is called quasi-explicit because this separation leaves an outer search for the centre and width. The inner solve depends on each pair tried; it must be repeated whenever that pair changes. The constraints still have to be satisfied in this solve.

We will solve exactly for a single trial geometry, then explain how it fits into the search. Interpolation between expiries and price range diagnostics belong to the following units.

The rule to remember

Fix the geometry, solve for the linear coefficients under the chosen constraints, and recalculate the error; repeat to compare geometries.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Solve for a slice on the calibration worksheet

For a single expiry, the observed pairs (k ; W) are (-3/20 ; 3/40), (0 ; 1/20), (3/20 ; 9/200). They are exact and fictional. For this outer trial, fix the centre m = 0 and the width s = 1/5. Each observation receives the same weight.

  1. Define the slice and what is fixed

    k = ln(K/F) is the natural logarithm of the ratio of strike price K to forward price F, both positive and expressed in the same currency. This ratio and k are dimensionless. W is the observed total variance: the square of annualised implied volatility multiplied by the time T in years; the units cancel. w(k) is the total variance predicted by the curve.

    w(k)=a+b[ρ(k−m)+(k−m)2+s2]

    a sets the level, b the amplitude, and ρ the asymmetry; m is a centre used in the parametrisation, which is not necessarily the minimum of w; s is a strictly positive width. All these parameters have no physical unit in these coordinates. s is the SVI width, not implied volatility. We keep the same expiry, observations and weights throughout the inner solve.

    The bounds and arbitrage tests from the previous unit remain relevant. We will specify a simple inner domain; it does not replace their full audit.

  2. Identify the linear coefficients

    For each observation i, set yᵢ = (kᵢ − m)/s and let zᵢ be the positive square root of the sum of 1 and the square of yᵢ. Here the index i denotes left, centre or right. Since s is positive, the square root of the sum of the squares of kᵢ − m and s is s zᵢ. Setting c = bs and d = ρbs gives:

    wi=a+dyi+czi

    yᵢ and zᵢ are now known, dimensionless numbers. a, d and c have units of total variance, so they also have no physical unit. For fixed m and s, the model is linear in these coefficients, which allows the inner solve.

    On the right, y = 3/20 ÷ 1/5 = 3/4. Its square is 9/16; 1 + 9/16 = 25/16. The positive square root is 5/4, verified by 5/4 × 5/4 = 25/16. On the left, y is -3/4 and z remains 5/4. At the centre, y is 0 and z is 1.

  3. Write the inner problem before solving it

    H is the matrix whose rows contain the coefficients of a, d and c; θ is the column (a ; d ; c). W is the column of observations in the order left, centre, right. The product Hθ gives the predictions. Here H has 3 rows and 3 columns:

    H=[1-3/45/410113/45/4]

    The residual rᵢ is the prediction minus the observation: rᵢ = a + d yᵢ + c zᵢ − Wᵢ. The cost J adds their squares; it has units of total variance squared. We seek the coefficients that minimise it within the allowed domain D.

    J=∑ri2=∑(a+dyi+czi−Wi)2

    The sums run over all observations. This is linear least squares: a quadratic cost in a, d and c, even though the transformed positions come from a square root. Our worksheet allows us to solve Hθ = W exactly. With more noisy observations, we minimise J without requiring every equation to be satisfied.

  4. Subtract the wings to isolate d

    The wing equations are W left = a − (3/4)d + (5/4)c and W right = a + (3/4)d + (5/4)c. Subtracting left from right eliminates a and c because their coefficients are identical.

    The difference between the observations is 9/200 − 3/40 = -3/100, or -0.03 units of total variance. The difference between the transformed positions is 3/4 − (-3/4) = 3/2. Thus d = -3/100 ÷ 3/2 = -1/50, or -0.02 units of total variance.

    Sign check: the right wing is lower, while its position is greater. The coefficient d must therefore be negative; it is.

  5. Average the wings to recover c, then a

    The average of the wings eliminates d: the opposite terms cancel. It is (3/40 + 9/200)/2 = 3/25/2 = 3/50. At the centre, the equation gives W centre = a + c = 1/20.

    Subtracting the central equation from the average eliminates a. The difference in level is 3/50 − 1/20 = 1/100. The coefficient of c is 5/4 − 1 = 1/4. Thus c = 1/100 ÷ 1/4 = 1/25, or 0.04 units of total variance.

    This leaves a = 1/20 − 1/25 = 1/100, or 0.01 units of total variance. Check: a + c = 1/100 + 1/25 = 1/20; the central observation is recovered.

  6. Return to the SVI parameters and check the chosen domain

    Since s > 0 and c > 0, we recover b = c/s = 1/25 ÷ 1/5 = 1/5, or 0.20, and ρ = d/c = -1/50 ÷ 1/25 = -1/2, or -0.50. Returning via b = c or ρ = d would overlook the change of variables. If c is 0, the constraint |d| ≤ c requires d = 0: b is 0 and ρ is no longer identifiable from the curve.

    For this worksheet, choose D defined by a ≥ 0, c ≥ 0, |d| ≤ c and c + |d| ≤ 2s. The first bound is deliberately conservative: some positive slices allow negative a. Since zᵢ ≥ |yᵢ|, c zᵢ + d yᵢ ≥ 0 in this domain; w therefore remains at least equal to a.

    The last bound requires the asymptotic slopes b(1 ± ρ) to be no greater than 2. It can also be written |d| ≤ 2s − c. Here 2s = 2/5, |d| = 1/50 ≤ 1/25 and |d| ≤ 9/25. a is positive; our candidate is feasible for D and its |ρ| is strictly less than 1.

    These positivity and slope checks do not certify the absence of butterfly arbitrage. They do not compare several expiries either. A calibration that satisfies D still requires these separate checks. If the unconstrained solution lies outside D, the inner problem must be solved under constraints; clipping its coefficients separately is not enough, as the final exercise will show.

  7. Recalculate the predictions using two forms

    On the right, d y = -3/200 and c z = 1/20; the prediction is 1/100 + (-3/200) + 1/20 = 9/200. On the left, the sign of the product d y reverses: 1/100 − (-3/200) + 1/20 = 3/40. At the centre, we recover 1/20. The 3 residuals are 0; their sum of squares is J = 0.

    The independent check returns to the original SVI form. On the wings, the square root of the sum of the square of k and the square of s is s z = (1/5) × (5/4); substitute a, b, ρ, m and s into the first formula and recover the same W values. This also checks the parameter conversion, rather than merely repeating Hθ.

    The determinant of H is 2 × (3/4) × (5/4 − 1) = 3/8, which is nonzero. The columns are therefore independent: for this geometry, the inner coefficients are unique. On a more general worksheet, you must check this independence and sensitivity to rounding; a nearly dependent system can give unstable parameters despite a small error.

  8. Put the solve back into the outer search

    Let Φ(m,s) be the smallest cost J obtained after solving the inner problem in D for the pair (m,s). Φ also has units of total variance squared. An outer procedure proposes a new pair with s > 0, rebuilds yᵢ, zᵢ and H, solves again for a, d, c, then compares Φ. The bounds, observations and weights must remain comparable across trials.

    This is what remains numerical in “quasi-explicit”. In the general case, this search may encounter several minima; a numerical stopping point does not certify the global optimum. Here, J = 0 already reaches the lowest possible error bound on our worksheet, since a sum of squares is nonnegative.

    This 0 error is nevertheless insufficient to identify the 5 SVI parameters uniquely, since the uniqueness proved applied only to the 3 coefficients for fixed m and s. It is neither a test on new observations nor a validation of the entire surface.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the steps. Explain why subtracting the wings isolates d, why their average allows you to recover c, and why changing s requires you to repeat the solve. Then name something that 0 J does not prove.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The calibration is linear, so I can solve for all the parameters at once.”

    The positions yᵢ and zᵢ depend on m and s. Only a, d and c are linear once that pair is fixed. The outer search must rebuild the inner problem at each trial.

    Reread the outer and inner blocks

  • “I apply bounds to the coefficients after solving: that gives the best allowed fit.”

    Changing a coefficient moves several predictions. The other coefficients must be able to readjust together. The counterexample in the final exercise gives a feasible solution that is better than simple clipping.

    Compare the costs in the exercise on the bound

  • “The error is 0: the slice is validated and the parameters are certain.”

    J concerns only the supplied points and the chosen weights. The independence of H gives uniqueness conditional on m and s, not uniqueness of all the parameters. Arbitrage, stability and independent validation checks remain.

    Review the check and what the error establishes

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and counterexample

A new bound on the same worksheet

A fictional specification adds a ≥ 0.02 to the domain D. Only a is replaced by 1/50, while d = -1/50, c = 1/25 are retained. Calculate the cost of this clipping. Compare it with the feasible candidate a = 1/50, d = -1/50, c = 3/100. Can we claim that clipping a solves the constrained problem?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

If only a increases, every prediction increases by the same amount.

Hint 2

For the second candidate, the centre returns to 1/20. On a wing, the decrease in c is multiplied by 5/4, whereas the increase in a is not.

Compare with the solution

Clipping increases a by 1/50 − 1/100 = 1/100. The 3 residuals therefore equal 1/100, each with square 1/10000. The cost becomes J clipped = 3 × 1/10000 = 3/10000, or 0.000300 units of total variance squared.

For the proposed candidate, a + c = 1/50 + 3/100 = 1/20: the central residual is 0. On the wings, the change is (1/50 − 1/100) + (3/100 − 1/25) × (5/4) = -1/400. The residuals are therefore (-1/400 ; 0 ; -1/400). Each nonzero square is 1/160000; J trial = 2 × 1/160000 = 1/80000 units of total variance squared.

The candidate satisfies the new bound on a, c ≥ |d| and c + |d| ≤ 2/5. Since 1/80000 < 3/10000, clipping is not optimal. We have exhibited a better feasible candidate; this is enough to refute the claim of optimality, without claiming that this second candidate is itself the constrained minimum. The coefficients must be optimised again together.

Check your reasoning

  • Recalculate the residuals on the same observations and with the same weights.
  • Check that the counterexample is feasible before comparing costs.
  • Distinguish a better candidate from a proof of optimality.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do we fix before solving for an SVI slice linearly?

The centre m and the strictly positive width s. The coordinates yᵢ and zᵢ become known; we then solve for a, d and c. Changing m or s requires us to rebuild and solve the problem again.

Recall 2How do we convert the linear coefficients into SVI parameters? Which limiting case requires care?

c = bs and d = ρbs, so b = c/s and ρ = d/c for positive c. If c is 0, d must also be 0 in our domain; b is 0 and ρ is not identifiable.

Recall 3Why are the difference and then the average of the wings sufficient on our worksheet?

The transformed positions are opposites and their z values are equal. The difference eliminates a and c to isolate d. The average eliminates d; its difference from the centre isolates c, then a follows from the centre.

Recall 4Why does applying bounds to the coefficients afterwards fail to guarantee the constrained minimum?

The coefficients act together on the same predictions. After clipping, the others can readjust and reduce the cost. The problem must be solved with its constraints and its residuals recalculated.

Recall 5What does 0 J demonstrate on this worksheet, and what still needs checking?

The supplied observations are reproduced exactly, and their quadratic cost reaches its lower bound. This does not demonstrate uniqueness of all the parameters, stability, complete absence of arbitrage or quality on new observations.
