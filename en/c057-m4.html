Level 1 · C057·M4

Jacobian

Your goalBuild a Jacobian by ordering its inputs and outputs, then use it to predict and check small changes.

Before you start
c033-m5 : affine approximation · c039-m5 : changes of basis · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c057-m1 : partial derivatives · c057-m2 : differential · c057-m3 : gradient

✿01 See the idea

You cut a rectangular sheet of length x = 6 cm and width y = 4 cm. You want to know how changing its sides will affect the available area and the length of its boundary. Call its area A, in cm², and its perimeter P, in cm. The scene and all its dimensions are fictional.

Increasing only x adds a strip of height y: y determines how sensitive the area is to x. Increasing only y adds a strip of length x. For the boundary, each side that changes has an opposite side of the same length: the perimeter therefore increases by 2 times each small increase in length.

Let a be the change in x and b the change in y, both in cm. When they are positive, the diagram shows the strips ya and xb, together with a corner ab. The strips are enough to predict the change to first order; the corner will explain the difference from the exact area.

The Jacobian arranges these sensitivities: one row per output, one column per input. With inputs ordered as (x, y) and outputs as (A, P), reading a column answers “if I change only this side, which outputs change?”. Reading a row answers “how does each side affect this output?”.

The rule to remember

Fix the order of the inputs and outputs. Multiply each row of sensitivities by the input changes to obtain the predicted change in the corresponding output.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Predict the area and boundary length after the same cut

The sheet changes from (6 cm, 4 cm) to (6.20 cm, 4.10 cm): a = 1/5 cm = 0.20 cm and b = 1/10 cm = 0.10 cm. Predict the changes from the initial sensitivities, then check them by recalculating the outputs.

  1. Define the function and its domain

    F denotes the function that returns the column of outputs A and P from the column of inputs x and y. The parentheses (x, y) indicate the same order of coordinates. The starting point p is (6, 4), expressed in cm. We assume a perfectly rectangular sheet, with no thickness or cutting loss, and strictly positive dimensions before and after the change.

    F(x,y)=[A(x,y)P(x,y)]=[xy2(x+y)]

    Initially: A(p) = 6 × 4 = 24 cm²; P(p) = 2 × (6 + 4) = 2 × 10 = 20 cm.

  2. Give each entry a meaning

    For a function with n inputs and m outputs, its Jacobian matrix J_F(p) has m rows and n columns. Its entry (i, j) is the partial derivative of output Fᵢ with respect to input xⱼ, evaluated at point p. Here i identifies an output and j an input; ∂ means that the other inputs stay fixed. Each coefficient has the unit of its output divided by the unit of its input.

    (JF(p))ij=∂Fi∂xj(p)

    Differentiating xy with respect to x leaves y as the coefficient; differentiating with respect to y leaves x. For the perimeter, each derivative is 2. The area row collects the same derivatives as its gradient; in the Jacobian, they are arranged in a row to produce this output.

    JF(x,y)=[∂A∂x∂A∂y∂P∂x∂P∂y]=[yx22]

    At p, the numerical matrix is [[4, 6], [2, 2]], with size 2 × 2. For inputs expressed in cm, the coefficients in row A are in cm²/cm, hence in cm; those in row P are dimensionless. A Jacobian matrix can also be rectangular when the numbers of inputs and outputs differ.

  3. Move from sensitivities to changes

    h denotes the column of changes (a, b), in cm. ΔA and ΔP denote the exact changes from the starting state to the final state; here Δ is the symbol for “change”. The symbols dA and dP denote the predicted linear changes: together they form J_F(p)h, the differential applied to h.

    h=[ab];[dAdP]=JF(p)h=[ya+xb2a+2b]

    The multiplication uses a row of the matrix and the column of changes: multiply corresponding components, then add. The result has size 2 × 1. Add the contributions to area together, then the contributions to perimeter together.

    The approximation is F(p + h) ≈ F(p) + J_F(p)h when h is small and F is differentiable at point p. Differentiable means that the error in this approximation becomes negligible relative to the size of the change as that change tends to 0. The existence of partial derivatives alone is not enough in general; their continuity in a neighborhood of the point is a sufficient condition. Here the polynomial formulas satisfy it.

  4. Calculate each product, then each sum

    For the area: the strip due to x contributes 4 cm × 0.20 cm = 0.80 cm²; the strip due to y contributes 6 cm × 0.10 cm = 0.60 cm². Their sum gives dA = 0.80 + 0.60 = 1.40 cm².

    For the perimeter: 2 × 0.20 = 0.40 cm and 2 × 0.10 = 0.20 cm. Thus dP = 0.40 + 0.20 = 0.60 cm. The sensitivities stay those of the starting point throughout this calculation.

    [dAdP]=[4622][0.200.10]=[1.40 cm²0.60 cm]

  5. Add the starting values

    The product J_F(p)h gives changes, not final outputs. To predict the final area, add dA to A(p): 24 + 1.40 = 25.40 cm². The predicted perimeter is 20 + 0.60 = 20.60 cm.

    These values are the affine prediction around p: a starting value followed by a linear response to the changes. The Jacobian alone does not contain the value F(p).

  6. Recalculate exactly and explain the difference

    The new sides are x + a = 6.20 cm and y + b = 4.10 cm. Direct recalculation: A(p + h) = 6.20 × 4.10 = 25.42 cm². The actual change in the model is ΔA = 25.42 − 24 = 1.42 cm², compared with dA = 1.40 cm².

    ΔA=(x+a)(y+b)−xy=ya+xb+ab=dA+ab

    Check using the diagram: only the corner ab = 0.20 × 0.10 = 0.02 cm² is missing. The exact value minus the approximate value is indeed 25.42 − 25.40 = 0.02 cm². The exact fractions are ΔA = 71/50 cm² and ab = 1/50 cm²; here the displayed decimals are exact.

    For the boundary, P(p + h) = 2 × (6.20 + 4.10) = 2 × 10.30 = 20.60 cm, exactly the prediction. Its change is ΔP = dP = 0.60 cm. The perimeter formula is linear: it creates no ab term. The word “small” does not provide a universal tolerance; here recalculation gives the difference to compare with the intended use.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the matrix and reconstruct it from the strips. Why does y multiply a? Why does the product give changes? Finally, explain why the same calculation is approximate for the area and exact for the perimeter.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The inputs go in rows, regardless of order.”

    With our columns of changes, outputs occupy rows and inputs occupy columns. Write the names A, P on the left and x, y above before filling in the entries. The entry in row A and column y is ∂A/∂y = x; swapping entries without swapping coordinates changes the calculation.

    Reread the headings of the sensitivity table

  • “J_F(p)h directly gives me the final area and perimeter.”

    h contains the input changes: J_F(p)h provides the predicted output changes. You still need to add A(p) = 24 cm² and P(p) = 20 cm to obtain the final prediction. Check each unit separately.

    Revisit adding the starting values

  • “The Jacobian always gives the exact change.”

    For our area, ΔA = dA + ab. The product of the changes does not appear in the linear prediction. The perimeter prediction is exact for a reason specific to its formula; for the area, reduce the changes and check what remains.

Compare with the solution

The y column contains 6 cm for the area and 2, dimensionless, for the perimeter. It multiplies b = 0.10 cm. Thus ΔA = 6 × 0.10 = 0.60 cm² and ΔP = 2 × 0.10 = 0.20 cm.

The corner ab is 0 cm² because a = 0 cm. The linear changes are therefore exact here, including for the area: when x stays fixed, xy depends linearly on y. This does not make the prediction exact for all simultaneous changes.

The matrix is still the one at the starting point. For the area: dA = 4 × 0.20 + 6 × (-0.10) = 0.80 + (-0.60) = 0.20 cm². For the boundary: dP = 0.40 + (-0.20) = 0.20 cm, exactly.

The exact area is 6.20 × 3.90 = 24.18 cm². Thus ΔA = 24.18 − 24 = 0.18 cm²: the area increases despite the decrease in y.

This time ab = -0.02 cm² is negative. The check gives ΔA = 0.20 + (-0.02) = 0.18 cm². The prediction therefore overestimates the increase in area by 0.02 cm². Here the term ab is a signed algebraic difference, rather than the positive area of an added corner.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same scene

Reduce the changes and test an objection

Return to the positive changes in the example, but divide each by 2: a becomes 0.10 cm and b becomes 0.05 cm, still starting from the initial sheet. Someone claims: “Everything is divided by 2, even the difference in area.” Predict dA and dP, find the difference, and answer this objection. Check using the exact area.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The Jacobian stays at the same point: its linear response is divided by 2.

Hint 2

The difference is ab: each factor is divided by 2. Multiply the two new factors before drawing a conclusion.

Compare with the solution

Linearity gives dA = 1.40 ÷ 2 = 0.700 cm² and dP = 0.60 ÷ 2 = 0.300 cm. These are the predicted changes, without adding the starting values.

The omitted term is now 0.10 × 0.05 = 0.005 cm². It has been divided by 4, because both factors have been divided by 2. The claim confuses the linear response with its error.

Direct check: the new sides are 6.10 cm and 4.05 cm. Their product gives 24.705 cm²; the exact change is 24.705 − 24 = 0.705 cm². We recover 0.700 + 0.005 = 0.705 cm². For this sheet, the errors therefore decrease faster than the changes when a and b are reduced simultaneously in the same proportion.

Check your reasoning

  • Divide the linear response by 2 while keeping the same starting point.
  • Justify the factor 4 for the error using the product of the changes.
  • Recover the exact change 0.705 cm² by another method.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do a row and a column of the Jacobian represent? What is its size?

For n inputs and m outputs, J_F has m rows and n columns. A row collects the sensitivities of one output to all inputs; a column describes the effect of one input on all outputs. The order must match that of the vectors.

Recall 2Reconstruct the Jacobian of the area and perimeter of the sheet. Why does the first entry contain y?

With inputs (x, y) and outputs (A, P), J_F(x, y) = [[y, x], [2, 2]]. With y fixed, increasing x adds a strip of height y. The coefficients in row A are in cm and those in row P are dimensionless when x and y are in cm.

Recall 3What does J_F(p)h give, and how do you obtain the predicted final outputs?

It gives the predicted linear changes: dA = ya + xb and dP = 2a + 2b, with h the column (a, b). Then add F(p) to predict F(p + h). The response has the units of the outputs, row by row.

Recall 4What assumption allows the local approximation? Is the existence of partial derivatives enough?

F must be differentiable at the starting point. Continuous partial derivatives in a neighborhood are sufficient; their mere existence is not enough in general. In our polynomial model, this condition is satisfied.

Recall 5Which term determines the error in the area prediction? What happens to it if the changes are divided by 2?

ΔA − dA = ab. Dividing a and b each by 2 divides this product by 4. For the perimeter, ΔP = dP exactly, because its formula is linear. Recalculating the final state lets you check the error.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Draw the strips of the sheet to explain the coefficients and the corner ab, then state the assumptions of the approximation.
