Level 2 · C138·M4

Boosting

Your goalBuild a prediction through successive corrections, recalculate residuals, and distinguish improvement on training examples from a choice validated on new orders.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A kitchen prepares meal orders. It wants to predict how long they will take from the number of portions. An initial rule predicts the same time for every order. It predicts too long a time for small orders and too short a time for large ones: these errors have a direction that the next tree can learn.

Boosting builds an ensemble in stages. Each small model learns to correct what is missing from the prediction already built. Here, we study regression boosting with squared error: subsequent trees learn the residuals, meaning observed time minus predicted time. A positive residual calls for an increase; a negative residual calls for a decrease.

We keep the previous trees. The new tree provides a correction that we multiply by a learning rate and then add to the total. This rate controls the step size; it measures neither a probability nor a percentage of successful orders. The next tree will see the errors after this step.

All orders and times on this page are fictional. To isolate the mechanism, every tree will have the same fixed threshold: at most 2 portions, or more. Only its leaf values will be learned. In typical training, the split is also selected using the training data; we do not cover that search or classification variants here.

The rule to remember

Predict with the current ensemble, subtract that prediction from the observed times, fit a tree to these residuals, add its scaled correction, then recalculate the residuals.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Orders, a baseline, and 2 corrections

Orders A, B, C, and D contain 1, 2, 3, and 4 portions, respectively. Their observed times are 4, 6, 10, and 12 min. These same 4 orders are used to fit the leaves. The rate is ν = 1/2 = 0.5, with no unit. Follow the calculations in this order: A, B, C, D.

  1. Define what we are fitting

    x is the number of portions, counted in portions; y is an observed time, in minutes. The index i identifies an order among n = 4 orders. F(x) is the predicted time, in minutes. Q(F) denotes the mean squared error on these orders, in min²: square each error, then take the average. The symbol Σ means to add the terms for all the indicated indices i.

    Q(F)=1n∑i=1n(yi−F(xi))2

    We give every order the same weight. The number of portions is available when making a prediction; the actual time is used for training. The trees in this example have constant leaves and the fixed threshold from the intuition section. Choosing Q defines what “better” means on these data; it guarantees no quality on future orders.

  2. Set up the initial prediction

    F0, the ensemble before any tree, is a constant. The mean of the times minimizes the sum of squares among constants: a shift above or below this mean adds a positive squared term to the total. Add the times before dividing by the number of orders.

    A and B give 4 + 6 = 10 min; C and D give 10 + 12 = 22 min. The total is 10 + 22 = 32 min. Thus F0(x) = 32 ÷ 4 = 8 min for every order.

    Check: the deviations from 8 min are −4, −2, +2, and +4 min. Their sum is 0 min, as expected for deviations from the mean.

  3. Give the first tree the right targets

    m denotes the position of the tree in the sequence. Fm-1 is the ensemble before this addition and hm is the new tree. The residual r has indices m and i: they identify the round and the order being considered. m and i are indices with no unit. Each residual and each output of h is in minutes.

    rm,i=yi−Fm−1(xi)

    In the first round: A gives 4 − 8 = −4 min; B gives 6 − 8 = −2 min; C gives 10 − 8 = 2 min; D gives 12 − 8 = 4 min.

    Each leaf learns the mean of the residuals that reach it. This is the constant that minimizes their sum of squared deviations: for any other choice, the sum of deviations from the mean cancels out, leaving only the squared shift multiplied by the number of examples in the leaf as an added term.

    On the left, A and B: (−4 − 2) ÷ 2 = −6 ÷ 2 = −3 min. On the right, C and D: (2 + 4) ÷ 2 = 6 ÷ 2 = 3 min. h1 therefore predicts a correction, with these signs, rather than the observed times.

  4. Scale down the step and add it to the total

    ν, the letter nu, is the learning rate. Here 0 < ν ≤ 1: it multiplies a correction in minutes by a coefficient with no unit. Fm is the sum after the addition. The previous trees keep their leaves; only hm has just been fitted.

    Fm(x)=Fm−1(x)+νhm(x)

    The absolute size of the shift is 1/2 × 3 = 3/2 min, or 1.5 min. For A and B, we get 8 − 1.5 = 6.5 min. For C and D, 8 + 1.5 = 9.5 min. This is F1.

    The new residuals, in order, are −2.5, −0.5, +0.5, +2.5 min. For example, for A: 4 − 6.5 = −2.5 min. The smaller step deliberately leaves a correction to learn in the next round.

  5. Fit the second tree to the new residuals

    The threshold remains fixed at 2 portions, but the targets have changed. The mean on the left is (−2.5 − 0.5) ÷ 2 = −3 ÷ 2 = −1.5 min. The mean on the right is +1.5 min. These are the leaves of h2.

    Its scaled shift is 1/2 × 3/2 = 3/4 min, or 0.75 min. F2 is therefore 6.5 − 0.75 = 5.75 min on the left and 9.5 + 0.75 = 10.25 min on the right.

    The residuals become −1.75, +0.25, −0.25, +1.75 min. The residual for B changes sign: 6 − 5.75 = +0.25 min. Improving a leaf on average does not mean that every error changes in the same direction in every round.

  6. Check the squares and signs

    Each absolute error magnitude appears 2 times by symmetry. We can therefore calculate the mean of the 4 squares by adding the squares of the 2 distinct magnitudes, then dividing by 2.

    Initially: 4 × 4 = 16 min² and 2 × 2 = 4 min²; their sum is 20 min². Q(F0) = 20 ÷ 2 = 10 min².

    After the first tree: 5/2 × 5/2 = 25/4 min² and 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4 min². Their sum is 13/2 min²; Q(F1) = 13/2 ÷ 2 = 13/4 min², or 3.25 min².

    After the second: 7/4 × 7/4 = 49/16 min² and 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16 min². Their sum is 25/8 min²; Q(F2) = 25/8 ÷ 2 = 25/16 min², or 1.5625 min².

    Another check: the residuals in each group remain the negatives of those in the other group. The mean of the predictions therefore remains 8 min. Opposite signs cancel out in a simple sum; they never cancel out in a sum of squares. The decrease in Q was calculated on the data used to fit the leaves.

  7. Predict without knowing the next observed time

    A new order of 3 portions follows the right leaves. Keep the baseline and all corrections: 8 + 1/2 × 3 + 1/2 × 3/2 = 10.25 min. No residual can be calculated for this new order until its time is observed.

    The retained model therefore consists of the baseline, the trees, and their rate; the last leaf alone is insufficient. The trees were fitted in a specific order. Once fitted and fixed, their contributions can be added in another order without changing the total.

  8. Choose how far to continue

    Reducing the rate limits each step, but may require more trees. The rate, tree size, and number of trees are chosen together by examining validation orders, reserved for selection rather than fitting the leaves. A separate final test set is then used to evaluate the chosen model.

    With our fixed threshold, A and B always receive the same prediction, even though their times differ. Repeating rounds cannot create a separation that none of the permitted trees can express. More flexible trees, on the other hand, can learn details that are useless for future orders: this is overfitting. The validation exercise asks you to check the boundary between these two datasets.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why the second tree learns different values with the same threshold. Then reconstruct the prediction for a large order, naming the baseline, each correction, and the rate. Where does an observed time enter during training, and then during prediction?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Each tree relearns the times, then I average them.”

    In this version of boosting, the added trees learn the residuals of the current total. We add their scaled corrections to the baseline. Making independent time predictions again and then averaging them would describe a different mechanism. Review the targets for the first tree and how they change in the second round.

    Review the targets and the addition of corrections

  • “A negative leaf predicts an impossible time.”

    The leaf expresses a correction in minutes. If the baseline is too high, a negative residual calls for a decrease. Apply the rate first, then add to the total: the final time for the small order remains positive in our example. However, the general mechanism alone does not guarantee that every prediction is positive.

    Reread the tree of signed corrections

  • “Training error is decreasing, so I keep adding trees.”

    The observed decrease concerns the orders used for training. Compare it with an error calculated on orders reserved for validation. If this second error increases, continuing may make the model less suited to the prediction task. The validation exercise lets you observe exactly this divergence.

Hint 1

The threshold also sends this order to the left: the predictions are 8, 6.5, and 5.75 min.

Hint 2

Compare the squared errors on this order with the training values of Q. Such a small validation set does not necessarily represent future orders.

Compare with the solution

F0 predicts exactly 8 min: squared error 0 min². F1 predicts 6.5 min: residual 8 − 6.5 = 1.5 min, giving a square of 9/4 min², or 2.25 min².

F2 predicts 5.75 min: residual 8 − 5.75 = 9/4 min. The square is 81/16 min², or 5.0625 min². With a single order, this square is also the validation mean squared error.

This validation would select F0 among the proposed candidates, while training Q decreases from 10 to 3.25 then 1.5625 min². The leaves fitted to the initial orders move their predictions closer to their group means; here, they move the prediction for the held-out order further away.

This is a numerical counterexample to the promise that “better learning means better prediction.” This single fictional order is insufficient to recommend a model for the kitchen. It was used for selection: it is no longer a final test independent of that choice. A separate evaluation, using relevant and authorized orders, would still be needed.

Check your reasoning

  • I keep the validation order out of leaf fitting.
  • I give the squares 0, 9/4, and 81/16 in min².
  • I distinguish the candidate selected in this exercise from established quality for the actual kitchen.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must the next tree learn in our regression boosting?

The residuals of the training orders: observation minus prediction from the current ensemble. These targets must be recalculated after every addition. This residual rule corresponds here to squared loss.

Recall 2How do we move from the current total to the next one, and with what units?

Multiply the output of the new tree, in minutes, by the rate with no unit, then add this shift to the current total, in minutes. Keep the baseline and previous trees for future predictions.

Recall 3What role does the rate play, and why must the number of trees be chosen along with it?

The rate scales down each correction. Smaller steps may require more trees; this combination is selected using separate validation. The rate is not a probability of success.

Recall 4Why does order matter during training, even though the final corrections are added together?

The targets of the new tree depend on the total already learned: changing this history changes the residuals. Once the trees are fixed, changing the order in which their contributions are added does not change the resulting mathematical function.

Recall 5What does a decrease in squared error on training orders fail to prove?

It does not prove better prediction on new orders. Validation guides selection; the final test then evaluates the chosen model. Reading or an answer obtained with help does not establish independent mastery of the mechanism.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the chain: training data → mean baseline → signed residuals → mean leaves → scaled correction → new total → new residuals. Then recall why validation can contradict a decrease in training error. An assisted answer remains an assisted answer; try again another day without support before judging your mastery.
