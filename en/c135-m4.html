✿01 See the idea

Imagine learning the short pattern A played by a doorbell. Someone replaces it with a higher-pitched version B. You might wonder whether you would recognize the pattern despite the change in register. The question concerns what remains recognizable beyond the original sound.

In our fictional scene, a synthesizer produces 3 successive pure tones. A uses 300, 375, and 450 Hz; B uses 600, 750, and 900 Hz. A pure tone has a single frequency, counted in oscillations per second: the unit is the hertz, abbreviated Hz. Here, increasing this frequency makes the tone higher in pitch. Pitch is the perceived quality of being low or high; it does not mean loudness.

Contour records only the direction of changes: rising, falling, or repeating. A and B rise. Relationships between frequencies specify how far apart the tones are; rhythm also describes when they begin. The first diagram therefore shows neither a musical score nor a frequency scale: it separates these cues so that they can be compared.

To analyze the recognition task, let us distinguish receiving the tones, briefly retaining their order, comparing them with the learned pattern, and then deciding "same pattern" or "different." This breakdown helps us ask questions about the task; it prescribes neither an algorithm for the brain nor a location in the brain. Hearing the tones, recognizing the pattern, and knowing how to play it are different performances.

The values and transformations are invented for the exercise. This page plays no audio: you will check the structure described and design a recognition check, without measuring your hearing or musical memory.

The rule to remember

Specify the preserved cue and the task being asked: an exact relationship between signals makes a hypothesis testable, without providing the reader's answer.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Does the higher-pitched doorbell preserve the structure of the pattern?

Pattern A is given by the frequencies 300, 375, 450 Hz, in that order, at times 0, 400, 1200 ms. For B, each frequency is multiplied by 2, without changing these times. Let us check pitch and time relationships separately, then state what a listening task would still need to establish.

  1. Name the quantities and assumptions

    The letters a, b, and c identify the successive positions of the tones. The letter f denotes a frequency of A in Hz, and g a frequency of B in the same unit. Each letter has the subscript a, b, or c to indicate the tone concerned. The letter t, with the same subscripts, denotes the onset time, when the tone begins, in milliseconds, abbreviated ms; 1000 ms equals 1 s. Frequency and onset time are therefore different quantities.

    We assume positive frequencies, strictly ordered onsets, brief tones without overlap, and the same sound characteristics apart from frequency for A and B. The values are exact in our idealized description. No note names or musical scale are prescribed. A real experiment would also need to describe the equipment, how the pattern is learned, and the listening conditions.

  2. Describe the pitch relationships in A

    For each transition, let us divide the next frequency by the preceding one. These ratios describe the pitch intervals in our model; their Hz/Hz unit cancels out. The letter r has the subscript ab for the first transition and bc for the next. A ratio greater than 1 indicates a rise; its reciprocal would correspond to the transition in the opposite direction.

    rab=fbfa=375300=5/4
    rbc=fcfb=450375=6/5

    The decimal values are 1.25 and 1.20, without units. Let us keep the fractions to make exact comparisons. The contour is "up, up," but these rises do not have the same ratio.

  3. Construct B and check the transposition

    Here, a transposition shifts the whole pattern by multiplying its frequencies by the same positive, dimensionless factor k. With k = 2, we obtain, in order: 2 × 300 = 600 Hz, 2 × 375 = 750 Hz, and 2 × 450 = 900 Hz.

    gbga=kfbkfa=fbfa

    The common factor cancels out in the ratio. Numerically, 750/600 = 5/4 and 900/750 = 6/5, exactly the ratios in A. Check another way: the factors for individual notes are 600/300 = 2, 750/375 = 2, and 900/450 = 2. They are equal. The absolute frequencies change; the successive ratios are preserved.

  4. Calculate the time intervals

    An onset time is a position on the clock. To find how much time separates two onsets, we subtract their times. Δt denotes this gap, with the subscript ab between a and b, and bc between b and c; here, the symbol Δ means "difference." We are not calculating the duration of a note.

    Δtab=tb−ta=400−0=400 ms
    Δtbc=tc−tb=1200−400=800 ms

    The time ratio ρ, the Greek letter called "rho," compares the second gap with the first: 800/400 = 2, without units. The second gap lasts 2 times as long as the first. A and B have the same onsets, so they have the same gaps.

    Check: 400 + 800 = 1200 ms, which recovers 1200 − 0. From the first onset to the last, 1200/1000 = 1.2 s elapses. Since the end of the last tone is not given, this number is not the total duration of the pattern.

  5. Separate preserved structure from the memory response

    Our description establishes that B preserves the frequency ratios and onsets of A. It provides a precise reason to propose B as a transposed version of the learned pattern. It contains no listener's answer. To find out what a listener recognizes, we must collect their decision without showing them the values or announcing the transformation.

    Familiarity means the feeling of having heard something before; it is not enough to verify an answer. A comparison with a different but plausible variant would help determine whether the listener distinguishes the patterns rather than always answering "same." The exercise on adding to frequencies prepares this variant.

    Success in this task would concern recognition of the pattern under the chosen conditions. It would demonstrate neither the ability to name each note, nor the ability to play the tune, nor a general improvement in memory or intelligence. The reasoning on this page remains a task analysis, rather than an experimental result.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If everything rises, it must be the same pattern.”

    Contour indicates only the direction of movement. Compare the successive ratios and the gaps between onsets: different rises can share this contour.

    Test the variant obtained by addition

  • “The last onset gives the total duration of the pattern.”

    It gives a time point. Subtract the first onset time to obtain the time between these markers; to know the total duration, you also need the end of the last tone.

    Revisit the calculation of time intervals

  • “The ratios are preserved, so the person recognizes the tune.”

    The calculations describe the signal presented. Collect the answer separately after learning, with different variants and without cues that reveal the answer. Then limit the conclusion to this task.

Middle frequency = 600 × (5/4) = 750 Hz. It does equal 2 × 375: the transposition factor remains the same.

Exercise 2 · Objection to resolve

Adding the same amount does not transpose the pattern

To make another version of the same doorbell, we add 150 Hz to each frequency of A and preserve its onsets. The new frequencies are denoted by h, with the subscripts a, b, c. Calculate them, then their successive ratios. Is the contour "up, up" enough to call this a transposition?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with the three additions; then compare next frequency / preceding frequency.

Hint 2

A transposition requires the same multiplicative factor, rather than the same addition in hertz.

Compare with the solution

We obtain, in order: 300 + 150 = 450 Hz, 375 + 150 = 525 Hz, 450 + 150 = 600 Hz.

The first ratio is 525/450 = 7/6, instead of 5/4. The second is 600/525 = 8/7, instead of 6/5. The frequencies still rise, but their ratios have changed: this is not a transposition of A under our definition.

This variant could help check reliance on contour alone in a listening task. The calculations do not establish whether a listener will find it easy or difficult to distinguish.

Check your reasoning

  • Give the frequencies 450, 525, 600 Hz and the exact ratios.
  • Reject the transposition claim while retaining the observation that the contour is identical.
  • Avoid treating a calculated difference as measured auditory discrimination.

Compare with the solution

The new onsets are 0, 200, 600 ms. The gaps are 200 − 0 = 200 ms and 600 − 200 = 400 ms. Their ratio is 400/200 = 2, matching 2 for A. The timing is compressed; the time proportions are preserved. The frequency ratios of B remain 5/4 and 6/5.

To isolate the change in register first, have the listener learn A, then present B and the variant obtained by addition at their original onset times. Hide the descriptions and version names, vary the presentation order, and ask "same pattern transposed or different pattern?" before giving feedback. Record the answer and confidence rating separately. Several balanced trials with variants are needed to assess performance; no data are provided here.

Then test time compression in a comparison where the frequencies remain fixed: changing register and speed together would prevent us from attributing a difficulty to either one. The protocol must also specify the delay after learning and keep the other sound conditions comparable.

A correct answer would help document recognition under these conditions. It would prove neither an improvement in general memory nor a specific brain mechanism. ✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In the doorbell, what do frequency, contour, and rhythm distinguish?

Frequency describes the oscillations per second of a pure tone. Contour records rising, falling, or repeating. The rhythm studied here describes ratios between intervals separating onsets; it does not say how long each tone lasts.

Recall 2Why does multiplication by a common factor preserve pitch relationships?

In next frequency / preceding frequency, the same positive factor appears in the numerator and denominator: it cancels out. The absolute frequencies change; their successive ratios remain identical.

Recall 3Why does the variant obtained by addition challenge reliance on contour alone?

It also rises, but its successive ratios differ from those of A. Contour is a coarser cue than the exact sequence of ratios. This alone does not predict a listener's answer.

Recall 4What evidence is missing after we have shown that a structure is preserved?

An answer collected after learning, without revealing the transformation, with different variants to check the decision. The calculation concerns the signal; recognition is a performance that must be observed, with conclusions limited to its conditions.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct this sequence: describe the tones and their onsets; distinguish contour from ratios; recover transposition through a common factor; compare time gaps; separate signal structure, memory of the pattern, and the observed answer. Finally, explain why success in this task does not demonstrate a general cognitive improvement.
