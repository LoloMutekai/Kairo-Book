Level 4 · C265·M2

Value and policy

Your goalDistinguish a choice rule from an expected return, calculate V and Q for a given policy, and explain why changing subsequent decisions changes the value of an action.

Before you start
c233-m5 : verification theorem · c103-m5 : regularization · c168-m5 : Radon-Nikodym · c264-m5 : monitoring and rollback · c265-m1 : MDP

✿01 See the idea

A robot is carrying a crate. At the start D, it can set the crate down where it is and finish with 3 points, or move forward into a corridor C, earning -1 point. In the corridor, delivering ends the episode with 10 additional points; giving up ends it with 2 additional points. The scoring scheme and all probabilities are fictional.

Its usual rule sometimes chooses to set the crate down and sometimes chooses the corridor; in the corridor, it still chooses between delivering and giving up. This rule is a policy. It describes how to choose an action in each state. A policy can choose at random or always take the same action.

The value of a state answers a different question: what will the entire continuation yield on average if the robot keeps following this rule? The value of an action first fixes the action to take, then lets the policy choose what follows. Entering the corridor has a different value depending on whether the robot always delivers there or often gives up.

We give less weight to a reward received after an additional action: its coefficient is 0.50. This choice of objective is called discounting. This coefficient is neither the probability of delivery nor a measure of the robot’s reliability. Does it predict a better value than setting the crate down immediately? Keep this question in mind before doing the calculations.

The rule to remember

The policy chooses; V evaluates the state before the choice; Q evaluates a first action that is fixed in advance, followed by continuation under the stated policy.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What is the start worth under this choice rule?

We evaluate a known policy π without training it. The robot’s rewards are in points (pt). The weight of a reward received one action later is γ = 1/2 = 0.50, which has no unit. All episodes end after at most 2 actions.

  1. Specify what the state and the end mean

    St is the state before the action at index t; At is that action. Rt+1 is the reward received immediately after it. t is an integer index with no physical unit. D denotes the start, C the corridor, and F the end of the episode. State and action labels have no unit. At F, there are no further decisions or rewards: the remaining return is 0 pt.

    From D, setting down leads to F with 3 pt; moving forward leads to C with -1 pt. From C, delivering leads to F with 10 pt; giving up leads to F with 2 pt. Points add up according to the scoring scheme, even when episodes have different lengths.

    We assume that states are fully observed and sufficient to describe what follows: the past adds no information once the state and action are known. This is the Markov property. The stated transitions and rewards are deterministic; randomness in this example comes entirely from the policy. The same state does not occur with different numbers of steps remaining: no additional time dependence is needed.

  2. Write the policy as a distribution over actions

    π(a | s) is the probability of choosing action a in state s. a and s are generic labels; the vertical bar means “given”. Here the policy is stationary: this distribution depends only on the current state. A deterministic policy is the case where one action has probability 1 and the others 0.

    0≤π(a|s)≤1,∑aπ(a|s)=1

    The sum is over the possible actions in a nonterminal state. At the start: π(set down | D) = 1/4 and π(move forward | D) = 3/4. In the corridor: π(deliver | C) = 1/2 and π(give up | C) = 1/2. The probabilities have no unit; they sum to 1 at the start and 1 in the corridor. Successive random choices use these distributions conditional on the state.

  3. Define the return, then the value functions

    The return Gt adds future rewards from t onward, reducing their weight. T is the index of the terminal state, k counts the actions elapsed since t, and γ is the constant discount factor per action. The ∑ sign means to add the terms for all integer k between the bounds. The first reward has weight 1, and the next has weight γ. Gt is in points.

    Gt=∑k=0T−t−1γkRt+k+1

    We take 0 ≤ γ ≤ 1; since the episode has a bounded length and rewards are finite, the expectation exists even without discounting. For an infinite task, these assumptions alone would not be enough. At the end, the empty sum is 0. The expectation E is the average over possible trajectories, weighted by their probabilities.

    Vπ(s)=Eπ[Gt|St=s]

    V with superscript π is the value of the state under π; the superscript names the policy and does not indicate a power. Q with superscript π at (s, a) is the expectation of the same return when starting from s, first fixing a, and then following π. This definition even covers a first action that π would never choose. V and Q are in points, like G; they are not probabilities.

    V depends on the rule from the first choice onward. Q fixes the first choice but still depends on the rule for all subsequent choices. We will calculate these expectations using the branches of our scene, without assuming an already learned table of values.

  4. Evaluate the corridor

    In the corridor, the next action ends the episode. Its reward is therefore the entire remaining return: Q under π at (C, deliver) is 10 pt and Q under π at (C, give up) is 2 pt. Each choice’s probability must weight its reward to obtain the value before that choice.

    Vπ(C)=12×10+12×2=5+1=6 pt

    Check: 6 pt lies between 2 and 10 pt, and the weights sum to 1. None of these results says which reward will actually be received in a particular episode.

  5. Evaluate each first action at the start

    Fixing set down gives 3 pt immediately, then the episode ends: Q under π at (D, set down) = 3 pt. Fixing move forward gives -1 pt now and leads to the corridor with certainty. From D, the entire return calculated in the corridor arrives one action later, so we multiply it by γ.

    Qπ(D,avancer)=-1+12×6=-1+3=2 pt

    The immediate reward for moving forward is -1 pt, and its action value is 2 pt: they differ because Q includes what follows. If γ were 0, the future would have no weight. With our settings, setting down has a higher action value than moving forward under continuation π. This does not determine the result for a different continuation.

  6. Return to the value before the first choice

    For a nonterminal state s, the possible first choices form a partition. Conditioning on this choice gives the following relation: we average the Q values using the probabilities of π, without maximizing. This identity concerns actions at the same state; it does not yet expand the general equation over subsequent states.

    Vπ(s)=∑aπ(a|s)Qπ(s,a)

    At the start, the contribution from setting down is 1/4 × 3 = 3/4 pt. The contribution from moving forward is 3/4 × 2 = 3/2 pt. Adding them gives V under π at D = 9/4 = 2.25 pt.

    Check: 2 pt < 2.25 pt < 3 pt. π assigns positive weight to each first action: its value lies strictly between their Q values. Replacing this average with the maximum 3 pt would evaluate a different first choice.

  7. Check by listing complete trajectories

    Setting down immediately has probability 1/4 and return 3 pt. Moving forward and then delivering has probability 3/4 × 1/2 = 3/8; its return is -1 + 0.50 × 10 = 4 pt. Moving forward and then giving up has probability 3/8; its return is -1 + 0.50 × 2 = 0 pt.

    We multiply successive probabilities because the second is conditional on reaching the corridor. Their total probability mass is 1/4 + 3/8 + 3/8 = 1. The contributions to the average return are 3/4 pt, 3/2 pt, and 0 pt. Their sum gives 9/4 = 2.25 pt, exactly as in the calculation by state.

    This check starts from the rewards of each trajectory and directly applies the definition of G. Yet no episode has return 2.25 pt: this is an expectation. The trajectories yield 3, 4, or 0 pt. An average value can therefore be correct without matching an individual observation.

Hide the calculations. Explain why the cost of moving forward is not discounted and why the continuation is, then reconstruct V at D using the actions and using the trajectories. State where the policy enters each line of reasoning.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Q is the probability that the action succeeds.”

    Check the unit: the probabilities of π have no unit, whereas Q is in points of expected return. In the corridor, delivering is worth 10 pt even though its probability of being chosen under π is only 1/2. An action value can be negative under a different scoring scheme.

    Review the definitions and units of π, V, and Q

  • “V is always the largest Q value in the state.”

    For a fixed policy, V averages the Q values using its probabilities. Here 2.25 pt differs from the maximum 3 pt. The maximum corresponds to the best first choice under the continuation being evaluated, rather than to the random choice made by π.

    Return to the average over the first choice

  • “If I always deliver afterward, I can keep the same Q value under π.”

    The symbol π also specifies future decisions. Always delivering in the corridor changes this continuation and therefore the value of moving forward. Rename the policy and recalculate before comparing.

    Work through the change of continuation

Exercise 2 · Calculation · modify the first choice

Change only the random choice at the start

A policy μ assigns probability 1/2 to set down and 1/2 to move forward at D; at C it follows π exactly. Do the Q values at the start change? Calculate V under μ at D, then compare it with V under π at D and with the action values.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

When evaluating Q, the first action is fixed: its own weight in the random choice at the start does not enter the calculation.

Hint 2

The continuation in the corridor is identical. Only the weights in the average defining V change.

Compare with the solution

Q under μ at (D, set down) remains 3 pt and Q under μ at (D, move forward) remains 2 pt: the continuation after each action is unchanged. Changing the probability of the first action does not change the return when that action is fixed.

V under μ at D = 1/2 × 3 + 1/2 × 2 = 3/2 + 1 = 5/2 = 2.50 pt. This is greater than 2.25 pt and still lies between 2 and 3 pt. μ gives more weight to the better Q value at the start. This evaluates the fictional scoring scheme; no real improvement by the robot has been measured.

Check your reasoning

  • I keep the Q values and justify this by the identical continuation.
  • I write the probabilities of μ and obtain 2.50 pt.
  • I check the bounds and distinguish a mathematical comparison from a real measurement.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · examine an objection

Choose differently in the corridor

Someone claims: “Moving forward is worth 4 pt under π, because afterward I will always deliver.” Repair this statement. Define the deterministic policy ρ that moves forward at D and delivers at C; calculate Q under ρ at (D, move forward) and V under ρ at D. Compare with setting down for certain and explain why the previous ranking of Q values is not enough for this new comparison.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

π does not always deliver in the corridor. The argument uses a different continuation policy.

Hint 2

Write the return of the only trajectory followed by ρ: immediate cost, then discounted delivery reward.

Compare with the solution

The correct statement is: “Moving forward is worth 4 pt with a continuation that delivers for certain.” Policy ρ chooses move forward at D and deliver at C with probability 1; the other actions there have probability 0. It therefore differs from π both at the start and in the corridor.

Its certain trajectory yields -1 + 0.50 × 10 = 4 pt. Q under ρ at (D, move forward) and V under ρ at D are therefore both 4 pt. Setting down for certain gives 3 pt. Under π, moving forward was worth only 2 pt, because giving up remained possible in the corridor.

The ranking reverses here: moving forward with the new continuation exceeds setting down, whereas moving forward under π was worth less. The initial actions have kept the same rewards and destinations; the difference comes from future decisions. This comparison is exact in our known model with bounded episodes; it is neither a learning algorithm nor validation outside the model.

Check your reasoning

  • I rename the policy and state its choices explicitly at D and C.
  • I obtain 4 pt with the correct discounting and keep 2 pt for Q under π.
  • I explain why changing the continuation requires reevaluating the actions, without promising a real result.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does π(a | s) describe, and what constraint must its values satisfy?

The probability of choosing a in state s, with no unit. In each nonterminal state, these probabilities are nonnegative and sum to 1. A deterministic policy concentrates this weight on one action.

Recall 2How do immediate reward and discounted return differ in the trajectory that moves forward and then delivers?

Moving forward immediately earns -1 pt. The return from D is -1 + 0.50 × 10 = 4 pt: the second reward receives weight γ because it arrives one action later.

Recall 3What is fixed in V under π and in Q under π?

V starts from a state and follows π from the first choice onward. Q starts from a state, fixes a first action, then follows π. Both are expectations of return, in points here; both depend on the continuation.

Recall 4How can you reconstruct V from Q and the policy at the same state?

Multiply each Q under π at (s, a) by π(a | s), then sum over the actions. This is a weighted average, not a maximum. Here V under π at D is 2.25 pt, between 2 and 3 pt.

Recall 5Why is the same action, move forward, worth 2 pt under π and 4 pt under ρ?

The continuation has changed: π can give up in the corridor, whereas ρ always delivers. Q fixes only the first action; its superscript specifies all subsequent decisions. A Q value calculated for one policy cannot be transferred to another without justification.

Recognize a choice rule without inventing a value function for it

What you understand
A policy associates choices with states; a value function evaluates their future return under a given continuation. A decision rule can exist without a reading of it providing a V or Q estimate. The robot is a fictional mathematical example, not a description of the system.
