Level 4 · C234·M1

State space

Your goalBuild a state space for a partially observed cart, then distinguish the transition, the observation, and the available information.

Before you start
c233-m5 : verification theorem · c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c227-m5 : Ito-Tanaka

✿01 See the idea

A sensor above a track reports where a cart passes. It does not report how fast the cart is moving. Imagine the cart at the same marker, sometimes moving slowly and sometimes quickly: the sensor can display the same position even though the subsequent motion will differ.

To describe what happens next, we keep both position and velocity. Their pair is the state; the state space contains all pairs allowed by the model. The sensor reveals only a projection of this pair, with its measurement error added. The word latent means that observations do not give the state directly.

The transition describes how the cart evolves between readings. The observation model describes how this state produces a reading. A disturbance to the motion changes the cart; a sensor error changes the display. Putting them in the same place would make the model describe a different situation.

The scene and all its values are fictional. We assume a track with no ends and signed coordinates, with no bound on velocity in this model. The numerical noise values in the example will be revealed to check the operations; an ordinary observer does not know them.

The rule to remember

Choose a state that captures what the next transition depends on, then write separately what the sensor observes of that state.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Describe a step before trying to estimate

The cart starts at p = 12 m and v = 3 m/s. The time step is Δ = 5/2 s = 2.5 s. For this step alone, we reveal a position disturbance η = -1 m, a velocity disturbance ξ = 1 m/s, and a sensor error ε = 2 m. Let us construct the next state and then the reading, keeping both distinct from an estimate.

  1. Choose the coordinates and their spaces

    The index k counts steps and has no unit; k + 1 denotes the next step. pk is position, in metres (m), and vk is signed velocity, in metres per second (m/s). xk is the column containing both. A column is a vector written vertically. E denotes the state space; ℝ is the set of real numbers.

    xk=[pkvk]∈E=ℝ2

    The coordinates of E are the numerical values in the specified units. They keep their distinct roles: directly adding position and velocity would have no physical meaning. yk, in metres, denotes the scalar reading; it belongs to the observation space ℝ, of dimension 1, whereas xk has 2 components.

  2. Specify what the state must capture

    The time step Δ is known, constant, and strictly positive. During a step, the nominal part keeps velocity constant and advances position by Δvk. At the end, the vector w for the next step adds the deviations that were not represented: its components are η, in m, and ξ, in m/s. We write it as (η, ξ)ᵀ; the symbol ᵀ turns the pair written as a row into a column. This discrete model does not impose a continuous acceleration law.

    The components of the initial state have finite expected absolute values. The vectors w are independent across steps and independent of the initial state, with a common distribution, 0 mean, and finite expected absolute values for their components. The measurement noise εk is in m, has 0 mean and the same distribution at each step, and is independent across steps, independent of the initial state, and independent of the entire sequence of w vectors. The components η and ξ of the same vector may be dependent. Their distributions and the distribution of the initial state must be specified to obtain a complete probabilistic model; the values for a single step do not determine them.

    The Markov property means here that, once xk is given, earlier history provides no further information about the distribution of the next state. Write hk for the history of states and observations up to the current step. L denotes a probability distribution, and the vertical bar means “given”. Under the stated independence assumptions:

    L(xk+1|hk)=L(xk+1|xk)

    The state is therefore sufficient for this transition. This does not mean that a reader who knows only yk knows xk. The conditional distribution of yk given xk depends on xk through the sensor model, rather than on earlier history. These properties do not require any particular form for the noise distributions.

  3. Separate transition and observation

    F is the transition matrix, an array of coefficients of size 2 × 2. H is the observation matrix, of size 1 × 2. Read the multiplication of a matrix by a column row by row: multiply corresponding entries, then add.

    xk+1=Fxk+wk+1,F=[1Δ01]
    yk=Hxk+εk,H=[10]

    The top row of F gives pk + Δvk; the bottom row keeps vk unchanged. The diagonal coefficients 1 have no unit, Δ has units of seconds, and the 0 coefficient at the bottom left has units of inverse seconds. In H, 1 selects position; the 0 coefficient of velocity can be read as 0 s. Thus Hxk is in metres. Having the correct matrix size does not remove the need to check these units.

    To read the transition without indices, let p and v denote the starting coordinates, and p′ and v′ the ending coordinates. The prime means “at the next step”. The equations become p′ = p + Δv + η and v′ = v + ξ, where η and ξ are the disturbances for the step. The sensor relation yk = pk + εk uses the state at the time of the reading. Sensor noise does not move the cart forward.

  4. Calculate the nominal transition

    Start with Fxk, before the disturbances. Multiplying velocity by a duration produces a displacement: Δvk = 5/2 × 3 = 15/2 m = 7.5 m. Add this displacement to the starting position.

    Fxk=[15/201][123]=[39/23]

    The nominal position is 12 + 7.5 = 19.5 m; the nominal velocity remains 3 m/s. The numerical column above uses m and m/s in that order. Since the process noise has 0 mean and is independent of the past, Fxk is also the mean of the next state given the exact current state. A conditional mean is not the realization of the step.

  5. Add the disturbance acting on the cart

    The revealed values η = -1 m and ξ = 1 m/s are particular realizations of the noises. A distribution with 0 mean can produce a nonzero deviation during a step. Add each deviation to the component with the same unit.

    xk+1=[39/23]+[-11]=[37/24]

    The ending position is 19.5 + (-1) = 18.5 m, and the ending velocity is 3 + 1 = 4 m/s. The new velocity does not retroactively replace the starting velocity in Δvk: our transition convention adds the velocity disturbance at the end.

  6. Produce the reading at the correct time

    The sensor now selects the position of the next state. It discards the velocity component, then adds its own error. This operation gives a number in metres, rather than a new state column.

    yk+1=Hxk+1+εk+1=37/2+2=41/2 m

    The reading is therefore 18.5 + 2 = 20.5 m. The actual position of the cart in this fictional scene remains 18.5 m, and its velocity is 4 m/s. The exact calculation gives y = 41/2 m; its decimal representation in French notation is 20.5 m.

  7. Check, then specify what remains hidden

    Reverse check of the motion: 18.5 − 19.5 = -1 m recovers η; 4 − 3 = 1 m/s recovers ξ. Sensor check: 20.5 − 18.5 = 2 m recovers ε. These checks verify the construction and are possible because the exercise reveals the deviations.

    Another check, with all noises set to 0 in the same model: we must recover the nominal state (19.5 m; 3 m/s) and a reading of 19.5 m. Measurement noise alone can change y without affecting x. These checks distinguish the roles of the noises, beyond just the numerical result.

    If only the reading 20.5 m were available, neither ε, nor velocity, nor the exact position would be revealed. H is not invertible: several state columns give the same observed position, even without noise. Estimating the state will involve combining successive observations with the model and the associated uncertainty. That is the topic of the next unit; no Kalman update has been performed here.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The last number displayed is the whole state.”

    The reading y is a scalar, in metres. The state x also contains velocity. Even if ε equals 0, knowing p does not determine v. Return to points A and B in the state plane: the same position, distinct velocities.

    Review the plane of possible states

  • “I can add sensor noise to the transition.”

    Assign each deviation to what it changes. η and ξ modify the state of the cart; ε modifies the reading. Recalculate the motion and the reading separately, then recover each noise value by subtraction.

    Revisit the separation and the checks

  • “Noise with 0 mean disappears at every step.”

    Having 0 mean means that its mean under its distribution is 0. Here η = -1 m and ξ = 1 m/s are particular nonzero values. The mean transition and the realized state must therefore remain distinct.

Exercise 1 · Identification and justification

What the sensor actually provided

At the end of the step, the sensor gives 20.5 m. Someone proposes x = 20.5 m as the complete state. Explain the problem with dimension, then state what the reading lets you know if ε is unknown, and if ε equals 0.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write the expected components of x, with their units, before looking at the reading.

Hint 2

The relation y = p + ε contains no velocity. Setting ε to 0 does not add that information.

Compare with the solution

The state is a column with 2 components: position in m and velocity in m/s. The reading is just a scalar in m. If ε is unknown, the reading imposes only p + ε = 20.5 m: it gives neither p exactly nor v.

In the hypothetical case of a sensor with no error, we would know p = 20.5 m, but v would remain undetermined. This hypothetical case differs from the revealed step in the example, where ε = 2 m. In that step, the subtraction 20.5 − 2 recovers 18.5 m only because ε was supplied.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish the state dimension 2 from the observation dimension 1.
  • Give the units of each component.
  • Separate known noise, unknown noise, and the case with no noise, without inventing a measured velocity.

Change the duration, starting from the state

Then quantify the error if you start from 20.5 m as though this reading were the actual position, keeping the correct velocity.

Hint 1

The top right entry of F is the duration of the step, rather than a coefficient to keep independently of the clock.

Hint 2

The duration becomes Δ = 5/2/2 = 5/4 s = 1.25 s. The other coefficients remain unchanged.

F=[15/401]

The displacement is 5/4 × 4 = 5 m. The next position is 18.5 + 5 = 23.5 m; velocity remains 4 m/s. Without sensor noise, y = 23.5 m. Reverse check: subtracting 5 m recovers 18.5 m.

Starting from the reading would give 20.5 + 5 = 25.5 m. The difference would be 25.5 − 23.5 = 2 m: the initial sensor error is carried forward as though it belonged to the cart. No statistical update has been authorized in this exercise.

  • Explain and calculate the difference of 2 m caused by the initial confusion.

Exercise 3 · Constructing a counterexample

Can velocity be removed from the state?

To test the representation, set the noises to 0 and use Δ = 2.5 s again. At the marker p = 12 m, compare state A with velocity 3 m/s and state B with velocity 1 m/s. Their current readings are identical. Calculate their next positions, then refute the claim: “p is always sufficient to determine the next transition, whatever the initial state.” What would need to be checked to reduce the state in another model?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A reduced state must give the same transition rule for all full states that it treats as identical, if this reduction is required for every initial distribution.

Hint 2

Calculate p + Δv in cases A and B. Do not conclude that every sequence of positions necessarily lacks any Markov property in every model.

Compare with the solution

For A, the displacement is 7.5 m and the next position is 19.5 m. For B, the displacement is 2.5 × 1 = 2.5 m and the next position is 14.5 m. Yet their current readings without noise both equal 12 m. The future difference reaches 19.5 − 14.5 = 5 m.

A rule based on the current position alone cannot reproduce these deterministic transitions for all allowed initial states. Keeping v distinguishes precisely the futures that p alone treats as identical. With noise, the criterion concerns the conditional distributions of the futures, rather than just their means.

A reduction could be justified under additional constraints, for example if velocity were already specified as a function of position. We would need to establish that states treated as identical lead to the same distribution for the next reduced state. Our counterexample refutes the general sufficiency of p in this model; it does not claim to rule out every reduced representation under other assumptions.

Check your reasoning

  • Give the same current reading and distinct future positions.
  • Connect keeping velocity in the state to the sufficiency of the state for the transition.
  • Limit the conclusion to the allowed states and assumptions; state the distributional criterion for a general reduction.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does a point in the state space of our cart represent?

A position–velocity pair allowed by the model. With the specified units, x = (p, v)ᵀ belongs to real space of dimension 2; position is in m and velocity in m/s.

Recall 2In what sense does the state capture the past?

Under the noise assumptions of the model, the distribution of the next state given the entire history is the same as the distribution given the current state. This property does not assert that the latest observation makes the state known.

Recall 3How can you reconstruct F and H without reciting their entries?

The next position receives p + Δv, and the nominal velocity remains v: this gives the rows of F. The sensor selects only p: this gives H = [1, 0]. Then add process noise to the state and measurement noise to the reading.

Recall 4Why can Fx be a correct mean without being the realized state?

Process noise with 0 mean disappears in the conditional mean given x. Its value during a step can be nonzero: the realized state is Fx + w. The revealed disturbances in the example illustrate this difference.

Recall 5Why does knowing an exact position fail to reveal the whole state?

The selection H discards velocity. States A and B have the same current position but different future positions. The model and several observations can help estimate what remains hidden; a reading alone is not that state.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the state and observation spaces with their units; justify keeping velocity in the state; write the transition and the sensor relation with their distinct noises. Then reconstruct the numerical step and its checks, and explain what the latest reading leaves unknown.
