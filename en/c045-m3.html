Level 1 · C045·M3

Trigonometric substitutions

Your goalUse an angle to transform a square root from a circle equation, change the differential and limits together, then check the resulting area.

Before you start
c034-m5 : average value · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c045-m1 : integration by parts · c045-m2 : partial fractions

✿01 See the idea

A workshop cuts a flat piece in the shape of a quarter disk, with radius 6 cm. What area of material is needed? The dimensions are fictional; the curved edge is assumed to be perfectly circular, and thickness does not affect the area.

Imagine very thin vertical strips. Near the centre, they are tall; towards the right edge, they become short. Adding their small areas gives the total area. The height contains a square root because a position on the circle imposes a relationship between the horizontal distance covered and the remaining height.

An angle provides another way to follow the same curved edge. As the radius turns from vertical to horizontal, its projections give a width and a height. Sine and cosine express these projections. The square root can then become a simple cosine, provided we know its sign.

The piece stays the same under substitution; only how we move across it changes. Equal steps in angle do not produce strips of equal width. We must therefore also transform the infinitesimal width, called the differential. Here we will work with the square root of a difference of squares; improper integrals belong to the next unit.

The rule to remember

Choose an angle and its interval; transform the square root, differential and limits; then integrate in a single variable.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The piece of radius 6 cm, strip by strip

Calculate the area of the filled quarter disk, from its vertical edge to x = 6 cm. Ignore any cutting waste. Keep an exact expression before giving a decimal value.

  1. Define the lengths and the area

    O is the centre; R = 6 cm is the constant radius. x is the horizontal distance from O; y is the height of the curved edge above the horizontal axis. All these lengths are in centimetres. In the upper right quarter, x and y are positive or 0. The Pythagorean theorem gives x squared + y squared = R squared, so the height is the positive square root.

    y=R2−x2,0≤x≤R

    A denotes the area in square centimetres. The symbol ∫ means the accumulation of the areas of the strips. dx indicates the small width measured along x, in centimetres. Height multiplied by dx is therefore an area. The integrand is continuous over the entire closed interval: no improper limit is needed here.

    A=∫0RR2−x2dx

  2. Review the precise trigonometric facts needed

    θ, read as “theta”, is the angle from the vertical in the triangle in the drawing. Sine is the ratio opposite side/hypotenuse, so x/R; cosine is the ratio adjacent side/hypotenuse, so y/R. These are numbers without units. A radian measures an angle through the ratio arc length/radius. π is the ratio of a circle’s circumference to its diameter; a right angle is π/2 rad, or 90 degrees.

    sinθ2+cosθ2=1

    This identity follows from the Pythagorean theorem after division by R squared. sin² θ means the square of sin θ. The other rules we will use are: the derivative of sine is cosine; the derivative of cosine is negative sine; the derivative of sin(2θ) is 2 cos(2θ). They hold for angles in radians. Finally, the double angle formula cos(2θ) = cos² θ − sin² θ gives the following when sin² θ is replaced by 1 − cos² θ:

    cosθ2=1+cos(2θ)2

  3. Choose the substitution and check the sign

    Let x = R sin θ with θ between 0 and π/2 rad. On this interval, sine takes every value from 0 to 1 in order; each horizontal position across the piece corresponds to a single angle. The radius is strictly positive and cos θ is positive or 0.

    R2−x2=R1−sinθ2=R|cosθ|=Rcosθ

    The absolute value |cos θ| is essential before checking the interval: the square root of a square is always positive or 0. Our choice of angles then allows us to remove the bars.

  4. Transform the width of the strips as well

    Differentiating x = R sin θ gives dx/dθ = R cos θ, so dx = R cos θ dθ. dθ represents a change in angle in radians, with no physical dimension; R cos θ dθ is a length. A change of variable in an integral replaces the entire product height × width:

    R2−x2dx=(Rcosθ)(Rcosθdθ)=R2cosθ2dθ

    Each factor R contributes a length. Their product is 6 × 6 = 36 cm². Forgetting the second factor would remove the width of the strips and leave only a length.

  5. Convert the limits to angles

    At x = 0 cm, sin θ = 0 and θ = 0 rad. At x = R, sin θ = 1 and θ = π/2 rad. Neither x nor dx remains in the integral. Sine is increasing on the chosen branch; its derivative is 0 at the final endpoint, but the substitution remains valid for this integral of a continuous function.

    A=36∫0π2cosθ2dθ cm²

    Keeping 6 as the upper limit would mean an angle of 6 radians, which no longer describes this passage across the piece.

  6. Integrate, then evaluate at the correct limits

    The double angle identity replaces cos² θ with a sum we can integrate. The constant factor becomes 36 ÷ 2 = 18 cm². An antiderivative G is a function whose derivative gives back the transformed integrand. Here is G, expressed in cm²:

    G(θ)=18θ+9sin(2θ)

    Why is the coefficient of sine 9? Differentiating sin(2θ) multiplies by 2, so we divide 18 by 2 to compensate. Adding a constant to G would not change the difference between its values at the limits.

    A=G(π2)−G(0)=18π2+9sinπ−0=9π cm²

    Sine is 0 at 0 and at π. The exact area is therefore 9π cm². There is no need to return to an antiderivative in x: the limits have been converted and evaluated in θ.

  7. Check the calculation and interpret the decimal value

    Check by differentiation: G′(θ) = 18 + 2 × 9 cos(2θ) = 18[1 + cos(2θ)] = 36 cos² θ. This recovers the transformed product, with the correct factor.

    Geometric check: the quarter disk contains the right triangle that joins the endpoints of its radii. This triangle has area R × R ÷ 2 = 18 cm². The piece itself lies inside a square of side R, with area 36 cm². Our result lies strictly between these two areas, as expected. The geometric formula for the disk, πR², also gives a quarter equal to 9π cm².

    For the decimal value only, we bound π between 3.14159 and 3.14160. Multiplying by 9, the area lies between 28.27431 cm² and 28.27440 cm². Both bounds round to 28.27 cm² to the nearest hundredth: this value is not the exact expression 9π cm².

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculation and describe how the same strip is represented: why does a cosine appear in its height and another in its width? Then justify the sign and the limits without merely reciting the result.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I replace the square root, then keep the same small step.”

    The small step depends on the variable. Differentiate x = R sin θ before replacing dx. The height and the differential each contribute R cos θ; their product contains R squared and cos² θ.

    Review “Transform the width of the strips as well”

  • “The square root of cos² θ always equals cos θ.”

    It equals |cos θ|. First check where the angle lies; our interval guarantees a positive or 0 cosine. Without this check, you could give the piece a negative height.

    Review “Choose the substitution and check the sign”

  • “The limits are the edges of the piece, so I keep their numbers.”

    The edges are the same points, but their coordinate changes: a length becomes an angle. Start again from x = R sin θ for each limit, then write only angles with dθ.

    Repeat the calculation with an angle measured from another direction

Recover the missing strip width

For the same piece, a student writes A = 6 ∫ from 0 to π/2 of cos θ dθ and obtains 6 cm. Which transformation was forgotten? Write the corrected product and recover the exact area.

Hint 1

Write the height and the width separately after setting x = R sin θ.

Hint 2

The width is dx = R cos θ dθ; multiply it by the height before integrating.

Compare with the solution

The student replaced the height with R cos θ, then treated dθ as though it were dx. The result in centimetres already reveals a length rather than an area.

ydx=(6cosθ)(6cosθdθ)=36cosθ2dθ

The double angle identity leads to G(θ) = 18θ + 9 sin(2θ). Between 0 and π/2, the sine term is 0 at the limits and the area is 9π cm². The square root alone did not represent the whole strip.

Exercise 2 · Independent application

The part before the halfway point across the width

In the same piece, what area lies between x = 0 and x = 3 cm? Find the final angle, the height at the edge of this part, then its exact area. For this calculation, sin(π/6) = 1/2 and sin(π/3) = √3/2; these values come from an equilateral triangle split into two right triangles.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate x/R, then find the angle on the branch already chosen.

Hint 2

The antiderivative G stays the same; only its final limit changes. At the limit, 2θ = π/3.

Compare with the solution

The ratio x/R is 3/6 = 1/2, so θ = π/6 rad. The height satisfies y squared = 36 − 9 = 27 cm². Since 27 = 9 × 3, we obtain y = 3√3 cm by keeping the positive square root.

A=18π6+932=3π+923 cm²

The coefficient of the square root is exactly 9/2, or 4.5 cm². The remaining area is 6π − (9/2)√3 cm²; adding the two parts cancels the square root terms and recovers 9π cm². Dividing the width into 2 equal parts does not divide the area into 2 equal parts, because the strips on the left are taller.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain π/6 rad from x/R, using the correct branch.
  • You distinguish the height 3√3 cm from the area in cm².
  • You evaluate G at the new limit and check the sum with the remaining area.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same scene

Measure the angle from the horizontal

Now parameterise the whole piece by x = R cos φ, where φ is the angle from the horizontal, between 0 and π/2 rad. Determine the height, dx and the order of the limits as x goes from 0 to R. Recover the area without copying the sine substitution. You may use sin² φ = [1 − cos(2φ)]/2.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

As x increases, cos φ increases: on this branch, the angle φ decreases.

Hint 2

dx contains a negative sign. Reversing the limits also changes the sign of the integral.

Compare with the solution

On the given branch, sin φ is positive or 0. The height is therefore R sin φ. The derivative of cosine gives dx = −R sin φ dφ. When x = 0, φ = π/2; when x = R, φ = 0. The direction of travel is towards decreasing angles.

A=36∫π20−sinφ2dφ=36∫0π2sinφ2dφ

An antiderivative of 36 sin² φ is 18φ − 9 sin(2φ). Its difference between 0 and π/2 again gives 9π cm². We can check its derivative: 18 − 2 × 9 cos(2φ) = 36 sin² φ. The choice of angle changes the expressions and the direction of travel, while the area stays the same.

Check your reasoning

  • You justify the positive square root R sin φ.
  • You keep the negative sign of dx together with the decreasing limits.
  • You show the change of sign when the limits are reversed and recover the positive area.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why choose x = R sin θ when facing the square root of R squared minus x squared?

The identity 1 − sin² θ = cos² θ transforms the square root into R|cos θ|, for positive R. A branch where cosine is positive or 0 then gives R cos θ.

Recall 2After changing the square root, what else must be transformed?

The differential and the limits. Here dx = R cos θ dθ; the entire product becomes R² cos² θ dθ, with angles as limits.

Recall 3Why specify radians and the interval of the angle?

The derivatives of sine and cosine used here hold in radians. The interval guarantees a traversal without repetition and determines the sign needed to remove the absolute value.

Recall 4How can you check the antiderivative G without repeating the entire integration?

Differentiate G(θ) = 18θ + 9 sin(2θ). This recovers 18 + 2 × 9 cos(2θ), hence 36 cos² θ. Then check the evaluation at the limits and the units separately.

Recall 5What does x = R cos φ change about how we move across the same piece?

The height becomes R sin φ; dx = −R sin φ dφ. As x increases from 0 to R, φ decreases from π/2 to 0. Reversing these limits compensates for the negative sign.
