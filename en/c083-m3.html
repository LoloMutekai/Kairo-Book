Retrieval

Your goalDistinguish recall, recognition and an assisted answer, then describe what a retrieval attempt actually allows us to observe.

Before you start
c082-m5 : perceptual errors · c056-m5 : effect size · c083-m1 : working memory · c083-m2 : encoding

✿01 See the idea

You have studied the packing list for your hiking bag. The sheet is now hidden. You are asked what it contained: some items come back to you, while another cannot be recalled. Then “something to light your way” brings back the word flashlight. The answer is available after this cue; it was not produced during the first attempt.

Retrieval means bringing back information learned earlier and making it usable, starting from a question, a context or a cue. Encoding, covered in the previous unit, concerned how the information was learned. Here, we observe what the person manages to retrieve in a given situation.

In recall, you produce what is missing: naming the items in the bag without seeing their list. Cued recall provides extra support, such as “something to light your way”. In recognition, you must identify a previously studied item among the items presented. Recognition also uses memory; its instructions and the information available differ from those in recall.

Failing to retrieve an item does not establish that its memory has disappeared: difficulty accessing it is possible, as is insufficient initial learning. Conversely, a word that comes to mind confidently may be absent from the studied list. You must compare the answer with a reference, without treating a feeling of familiarity as a check.

Our list and all the answers that follow are fictional. We count the words in a teaching example; we measure neither a real person’s memory nor the effect of a method. We stay at the level of observable behaviour, without drawing a supposed neural mechanism.

The rule to remember

Write the answer before receiving any help; then compare it with the reference; keep what came unaided separate from what came after a cue.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The hidden list, followed by the flashlight cue

The fictional reference contains water bottle, map, jacket, flashlight, whistle and pouch. After studying it and then hiding it, the person writes “water bottle, map, jacket, sandwich”. They add “flashlight” only after the cue “something to light your way”. Let us describe each stage precisely.

  1. Set the instructions and the comparison rule

    The shared question is “What was on the packing list for the bag?”. Here, unaided means without a visible list, suggested answers or an extra cue. The question itself remains a starting point for searching memory; unaided therefore does not mean without any context.

    Word order is not assessed. Each distinct item counts at most 1 time; repeating water bottle would add nothing. We compare the names as written in this scene, without ambiguous synonyms. The reference is fixed before marking and does not change to include sandwich.

  2. Name the quantities

    N is the number of expected items; C the number of reference items named; I the number of intrusions; P the total number of distinct items produced; O the number of omissions. These are counts of items, for the same attempt and the same list. Here N = 6 items.

    The answer produced divides into correct items and intrusions: P = C + I. The reference divides into retrieved items and omissions: N = C + O, so O = N − C. These equalities depend on our rule: no duplicates counted and an unambiguous classification. They do not describe a person’s total knowledge.

    P=C+I;O=N−C

  3. Compare the initial attempt, word by word

    Water bottle belongs to the reference; so do map and jacket. This gives C = 3 correct items. Sandwich does not belong to the reference: I = 1 intrusion. Being plausible for a hike is not enough, because the task asks for this particular list to be retrieved.

    To count all the items produced, add the correct items and the intrusions: P = 3 + 1 = 4 items. This total must not be treated as a count of accurate memories.

    To find what is missing from the reference, subtract only the correct items: O = 6 − 3 = 3 items. These are flashlight, whistle and pouch. Subtracting P instead of C would treat sandwich as replacing an expected item.

    O=6−3=3 objets omis

  4. Check using another way of counting

    Now reread the reference, ticking each correctly named item and circling the others: water bottle, map and jacket are ticked; flashlight, whistle and pouch are circled. The direct count finds 3 omissions, without using subtraction.

    The parts of the reference combine again: 3 + 3 = 6 items, matching N. The answer contains 4 items: 3 correct items and 1 intrusion. These checks concern different lists and complement each other.

    Check case: if no reference item is retrieved, C = 0 and the 6 items are omitted, even if items outside the list are named. The number of omissions therefore does depend on C.

  5. Add the cue while preserving the sequence of events

    The cue “something to light your way” is given after the first answer; flashlight comes next. The addition supplies 1 correct item. The cumulative correct total becomes 3 + 1 = 4 items, and the number of items produced becomes 4 + 1 = 5. Sandwich remains an intrusion.

    The remaining omissions are whistle and pouch: 6 − 4 = 2 items. Record “3 correct before help” and “flashlight after a cue” separately. If you report the total 4, state that help was given; this total does not represent entirely unaided recall.

    The appearance of flashlight shows that the word could be produced in this assisted condition. The cue changes the task; it proves neither that flashlight would have been retrieved without it nor that it will still be retrieved later.

  6. Draw a conclusion with the appropriate scope

    Conclusion: in the fictional initial attempt, 3 expected items were retrieved and 1 item was wrongly added. Another item came after the cue. That is what the record establishes. It provides no memory diagnosis, learning gain or evidence of lasting memory.

    To explore what remains accessible later, a new attempt would be needed before rereading, with the same instructions and any help again reported. The content studied and the help already received are part of its history: a later answer would not be an independent repetition of the first. Consolidation mechanisms belong to the next unit.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the steps and explain why we subtract C, rather than P, to count omissions. Then describe what changes after the cue and what must remain written in the initial record.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “If I cannot retrieve flashlight, its memory must have disappeared.”

    Failure can arise from difficulty accessing the information, but also from insufficient initial learning; this attempt alone does not distinguish between them. Compare the unaided condition with the cued condition, without turning this comparison into a diagnosis.

    Review what retrieval means

  • “The word feels familiar, so I could retrieve it unaided.”

    Recognizing a presented word and producing it without suggested answers are different tasks. Hide the list and write an answer first; check it only afterwards. Correct recognition is not evidence of unaided recall.

    Distinguish the instructions in the first exercise

  • “After the cue I have 4 correct answers, so my unaided recall score is 4.”

    Keep the 3 initially correct items separate from the assisted addition. The cumulative total describes the end of the exchange, but the cue does not change the conditions of the first attempt.

Hint 1

Hint 2

The instruction about lighting gives a function of the item; it does not directly present its name.

Compare with the solution

The open instruction asks for free recall: you must produce the names, with the general question as your starting point. The instruction about lighting asks for cued recall: extra information points towards flashlight.

The instruction offering flashlight and sandwich asks for recognition: the candidate names are visible. This task also uses memory, but making the correct choice does not show that you would have named flashlight in free recall.

Check your reasoning

  • I name free recall, cued recall and recognition.
  • I justify each answer using the information provided, without treating recognition as separate from memory.
  • I distinguish success in choosing from the ability to produce the name without that choice.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does retrieval mean in the bag scene?

Bringing back information learned earlier to answer under a given condition. The question and the available cues are part of that condition.

Recall 2What distinguishes free recall, cued recall and recognition?

Free recall asks you to produce the names using a general question. Cued recall adds a cue. Recognition presents candidates to identify; it also uses memory.

Recall 3Why is the total number of items named not enough to count omissions?

It may include intrusions, which are absent from the reference. Subtract from the expected items only those that were correctly named, then check the missing names.

Recall 4Why does flashlight, produced after the cue, not change the initial unaided recall?

The first answer was obtained before this cue. Flashlight adds an answer in an assisted condition, which must remain reported; it does not change the earlier conditions.

Recall 5What is proved by neither a single failure nor an immediate success?

Failure alone does not prove that the memory has disappeared. Immediate success does not prove lasting availability. Further observations are needed, with the conditions and any help documented.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the sequence for the hidden list: which question starts the search, what must you produce, and when do you check? Then explain how to identify an intrusion and an omission, why flashlight after a cue remains assisted, and what evidence is still missing for a claim of lasting retrieval.
