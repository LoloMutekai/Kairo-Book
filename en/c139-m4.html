Level 2 · C139·M4

Choosing the number of groups

Your goalCompare numbers of groups using inertia and silhouette scores, then defend a choice while stating what these scores do not prove.

Before you start
c137-m5 : choosing the rank · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c067-m5 : pipeline · c139-m1 : k-means · c139-m2 : Gaussian mixtures · c139-m3 : EM

✿01 See the idea

You need to sort parts on a table by length. The 4 lengths are 10, 12, 20 and 22 cm. You see a short pair and a long pair. Yet if you give each part its own compartment, every compartment becomes perfectly homogeneous. This perfection does not answer the question: how many families of lengths are worth distinguishing?

The parts and measurements are fictional. We use only their length, giving each part the same weight. No family labels are supplied. Here, a group is a set of parts assigned to the same compartment; a partition assigns all parts to nonempty groups with no overlap.

Inertia measures the spread around the center of each group. It favors homogeneous compartments but imposes no cost for increasing their number. The silhouette asks a different question of each part: is it closer to the other parts in its own group than to the parts in the neighboring group?

Look at the boxes in the diagram before reading the scores. Moving from 2 to 3 groups splits the short pair into singleton parts. The lengths and the differences between them stay the same; only the partition changes.

The rule to remember

Fix the data and the distance; compare the partitions using a stated criterion; examine the resulting groups and treat the choice as a hypothesis to test.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Should the short pair be split?

Call the parts A, B, C and D, with respective lengths 10, 12, 20 and 22 cm. We compare the partition {A, B} ; {C, D} with the partition {A} ; {B} ; {C, D}. We are not running an automatic search: these candidate partitions are fully specified.

  1. Set the comparison framework

    n is the number of parts, here 4; K is the number of groups, here 2 or 3. These are counts with no units. i and j denote parts; xᵢ and xⱼ are their lengths, in cm. The distance d(i, j) is the absolute value of their difference, so it is a nonnegative difference expressed in cm.

    d(i,j)=|xi−xj|

    We use the same parts, the same weights and the same distance for the comparisons. We treat the lengths as exact in this exercise. Imprecise measurements would require checking whether the choice withstands variations in those measurements.

  2. Calculate inertia and understand its pitfall

    C(i) denotes the group containing i; μ, the letter mu, denotes the mean center of the group indicated by the subscript. J is the total inertia, the sum of squared differences from the centers. The symbol ∑ means that we add a term for each part i. The centers are in cm; J is in cm².

    J=∑i(xi−μC(i))2

    For K = 2, the centers are (10 + 12)/2 = 11 cm and (20 + 22)/2 = 21 cm. Each part is 1 cm from its center: J = 4 × 1 × 1 = 4 cm².

    For K = 3, A and B each become their own center: their differences are 0 cm. C and D stay 1 cm from the center at 21 cm. So J = 0 + 0 + 2 × 1 = 2 cm². The decrease is 2 cm².

    If each part had its own group, K would be 4 and inertia would be 0 cm². Minimizing J alone would reward this fragmentation. More generally, the minimum achievable inertia cannot increase when more groups are allowed: splitting a group and recalculating its means cannot worsen its best fit. Results from an algorithm that has not converged properly may, however, fail to follow this trend.

  3. Build the silhouette definition before calculating it

    For a part i in a group containing at least 2 parts, a(i) is its mean distance to the other parts in its own group: i is excluded from the count. For each other group, calculate the mean distance from i to all its parts. b(i) is the smallest of these means; it is neither the distance to the single nearest neighbor nor the distance to the group center.

    a(i) and b(i) are in cm. The silhouette s(i) compares their difference with the larger of the two mean distances. max means “take the larger value.” S is the mean silhouette over all parts, each given the same weight.

    s(i)=b(i)−a(i)max(a(i),b(i));S=1n∑is(i)

    s(i) and S have no units. s(i) close to 1 indicates a part much closer to its own group than to the others; close to 0, weak separation; negative, a part closer on average to another group. The score lies between −1 and 1. It is not a probability of membership.

    A necessary convention: if a part is alone in its group, set s(i) = 0. There is no other member with which to define a(i). If a(i) and b(i) are both 0, also set s(i) = 0. We compare S only for 2 ≤ K ≤ n − 1: a single group provides no neighboring group, and one group per part provides no within-group comparison.

  4. Calculate the silhouette of part A

    In the partition with 2 groups, the only other part in the group containing A is B. So a(A) = |12 − 10| = 2 cm. The other group contains C and D; we need to use both distances and then average them.

    d(A, C) = 20 − 10 = 10 cm; d(A, D) = 22 − 10 = 12 cm. Thus b(A) = (10 + 12)/2 = 11 cm. Since 11 > 2, the denominator is b(A).

    s(A)=11−211=9/11≈0.818

    Check: a(A) is smaller than b(A), so the score must be positive. The cm units cancel in the quotient, leaving no unit. We keep the exact fraction for the final mean.

  5. Move from individual scores to the partition

    For B, a(B) = 2 cm. Its distances to C and D are 8 and 10 cm; b(B) = (8 + 10)/2 = 9 cm. So s(B) = (9 − 2)/9 = 7/9 ≈ 0.778.

    By symmetry of the lengths and groups, C has the same score as B, and D the same score as A. The list is therefore (9/11 ; 7/9 ; 7/9 ; 9/11). We average over the parts, rather than just the distinct values.

    S2=2×9/11+2×7/94=79/99≈0.798

    The subscript of S indicates the number of groups here. Check: a mean of equally weighted scores stays between the smallest and the largest; our result does lie between 0.778 and 0.818.

  6. Repeat the comparison after the split

    At K = 3, A and B are singletons: s(A) = s(B) = 0, by convention. For C, a(C) = 2 cm. The neighboring groups are now {A} and {B}: their mean distances are 10 and 8 cm, respectively. The minimum is 8 cm, so s(C) = (8 − 2)/8 = 3/4.

    For D, a(D) = 2 cm. Its distances to the groups {A} and {B} are 12 and 10 cm. So b(D) = 10 cm and s(D) = (10 − 2)/10 = 4/5.

    S3=0+0+3/4+4/54=31/80≈0.388

    Check: the singleton parts stay in the denominator n = 4. Removing them from the mean would change the definition of the score and make the comparison misleading.

  7. Choose and limit the conclusion

    According to the mean silhouette, the proposed partition with 2 groups is preferable to the one with 3: 0.798 > 0.388, with no units. It keeps the pairs coherent without turning the short pair into singleton parts. The lower inertia of the other partition, 2 cm² instead of 4 cm², is not enough to reverse this judgment.

    The accurate statement is: “Among these partitions and for the chosen distance between lengths, I select 2 groups.” It does not say that the parts really come from 2 manufacturing designs. A geometric score favors certain group shapes; a partition useful for storage may also depend on compartment constraints.

    With an algorithm, we would compare several initializations for each K, then the sensitivity of the groups to small changes in the observations. If portions of the same part were measured several times, we would not treat them as independent parts for this check. An unstable choice or similar scores would call for a more cautious conclusion.

    This unit evaluates hard partitions. For the Gaussian mixtures in the preceding units, likelihood and covariance assumptions raise a different model comparison problem; our score does not prove the number of components in a mixture.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results. Reconstruct the comparison from the lengths: why does inertia decrease, what does the silhouette compare, and why do singleton parts change the conclusion? Finish with a statement that precisely limits your choice.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I choose the number of groups that gives the lowest inertia.”

    One compartment per part gives 0 cm² of inertia without demonstrating useful families. Compare a measure of separation and examine the resulting partition. In our comparison, the decrease in J accompanies a decrease in S.

    Review the inertia pitfall and the final comparison

  • “b is the distance to the neighboring center or the nearest neighboring part.”

    For each other group, average all distances from the part being studied, then take the smallest of these means. For A with 2 groups, b(A) is 11 cm; its distance to part C, 10 cm, is not enough.

    Reread the definition of b and the calculation for A

  • “A part on its own is perfectly grouped, so its silhouette is one.”

    There is no other member for the within-group mean. The silhouette of a singleton is set to 0 by convention. Giving it 1 would artificially reward fragmentation: check the effect in the diagnostic exercise.

    Correct the calculation that rewards singleton parts

Exercise 1 · Application · individual calculation

Is B as well separated as A?

Return to the partition {A, B} ; {C, D}. Before calculating, predict whether B is better or less well separated than A. Calculate a(B), b(B) and s(B), then compare with s(A) = 9/11. All lengths remain those of the example.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A and B have the same within-group distance. Compare their distances to the long pair.

Hint 2

For b(B), average 8 cm and 10 cm; the denominator of s will be the larger of the two mean distances.

Compare with the solution

B is closer to the long pair than A, while remaining the same distance from its partner. It is therefore less well separated by this criterion.

a(B) = 12 − 10 = 2 cm. b(B) = (8 + 10)/2 = 9 cm. Since b(B) is the larger value, s(B) = (9 − 2)/9 = 7/9 ≈ 0.778, with no units. This score is lower than 0.818 for A, in line with the prediction.

Check your reasoning

  • State and explain the direction of the comparison before calculating.
  • Exclude B from its own within-group mean and use all parts in the other group.
  • Report distances in cm and the silhouette with no units.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis · reconstructing a mean

A table rewards singleton parts

For {A} ; {B} ; {C, D}, a table displays the scores (1 ; 1 ; 3/4 ; 4/5). Calculate their mean, find the error in the definition, then correct the mean and the decision between the proposed partitions.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

There is no other part with which to calculate a within-group mean for A or B.

Hint 2

Replace the scores of the singleton parts with the conventional value, but keep all 4 parts in the mean.

Compare with the solution

The sum of the reported scores is 1 + 1 + 3/4 + 4/5 = 71/20. The table would give S = 71/20/4 = 71/80 ≈ 0.888. This number would exceed 0.798 and favor fragmentation.

The error is assigning 1 to the singletons. Their a is not a defined mean: the group contains no other member. By convention, their s is 0. The correct mean is therefore (0 + 0 + 3/4 + 4/5)/4 = 31/80 ≈ 0.388.

The proposed partition with 2 groups remains preferable according to S. The check concerns the definition of the score as much as the additions: exact arithmetic applied to the wrong score would have produced the wrong decision.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover the erroneous mean 71/80 and explain why it leads to a different decision.
  • Justify the singleton convention without claiming to have calculated an empty within-group mean.
  • Keep the same number of parts in the denominator and recover 31/80.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · units and scope of the choice

The same parts measured in millimeters

Convert all lengths to mm: each distance is multiplied by 10. For A, with 2 groups, recalculate a(A), b(A) and s(A). Does the score change? Someone concludes: “This result proves the number of manufacturing designs.” Respond, then propose a useful check before generalizing to other parts.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The difference b − a and the maximum of a and b are multiplied by the same positive factor.

Hint 2

Distinguish invariance under a change of units from proof about the origin of the parts.

Compare with the solution

a(A) = 10 × 2 = 20 mm and b(A) = 10 × 11 = 110 mm. The difference is 90 mm; s(A) = 90/110 = 9/11 ≈ 0.818, with no units.

The same positive factor multiplies the numerator and denominator: it cancels. All silhouettes and their mean stay the same. Inertia, which involves squared distances, changes scale; this does not change the ordering of these partitions when everything is converted in the same way.

The conversion creates no information about manufacturing. We can only maintain the geometric choice between the partitions examined. To test its stability, we could vary the lengths within an actually documented margin of measurement error, then compare new parts from the same sampling source. A stable result would still not, on its own, prove the industrial origin of the groups. No result from this check is available here.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 20 mm and 110 mm, then recover exactly 9/11.
  • Explain how the factor cancels without confusing distance with squared distance.
  • State a limited conclusion and a check, without attributing an invented result to that check.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why is lower inertia not enough to choose the number of groups?

It measures squared differences from the centers without penalizing the number of groups. Giving each part its own group makes these differences vanish. We need to examine a comparison criterion and the usefulness of the groups.

Recall 2How can you reconstruct a, b and the silhouette of a part that has partners?

a averages its distances to the other members of its group. b is the minimum of the mean distances to the other groups. s = (b − a)/max(a, b), with no units; S averages s over all parts. Set s to 0 if a and b are both 0.

Recall 3What should you do with the silhouette of a singleton part and its place in the mean?

Its silhouette is 0 by convention because the mean over the other members of its group is empty. The part remains included in the overall mean; it is neither perfect nor removed from the calculation.

Recall 4What conclusion can you defend for our parts, and what evidence is still missing?

The proposed partition with 2 groups has a better mean silhouette than the one with 3, for these parts and this distance. This does not prove the number of manufacturing families. Stability on other observations and usefulness for storage remain to be checked.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the reasoning: state the parts, partitions and distance; explain why inertia rewards splits; define the two mean distances used in the silhouette; handle singleton parts; calculate the mean over all parts; conclude for the candidates examined and name the missing check.
