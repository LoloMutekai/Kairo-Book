Level 4 · C230·M4

Sobolev spaces

Your goalDecide whether a profile with a corner belongs to a Sobolev space by checking its weak derivative and the required integrals.

Before you start
c165-m5 : duality · c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c167-m5 : initial and boundary value problem · c230-m1 : test functions · c230-m2 : distributions · c230-m3 : weak derivatives

✿01 See the idea

A flexible strip rests on a support shaped like a roof: it rises in a straight line to the midpoint, then slopes back down. At the peak, there is no unique tangent. Must we reject this profile when we want to measure its height and variation?

On each side, the slope is fully known. At the join, the height is the same on both sides: the strip has no jump. The weak derivative describes these slopes without requiring a classical derivative at the peak. A Sobolev space adds a requirement: the profile and the required weak derivatives must satisfy a specified integrability condition.

The scene and its dimensions are fictional. The physical horizontal coordinate s is in metres and the height h is in centimetres. Set L = 1 m and H = 1 cm. We will work with x = s/L and u = h/H, both dimensionless. The drawings show these normalized coordinates. We are calculating neither a force nor an actual mechanical energy.

Look separately at the profile and its slopes. A corner in the profile may be allowed; a jump in height will require a different analysis. The decision will follow from an integral identity and finite integrals, rather than how smooth the drawing looks.

The rule to remember

State the domain and the order of the derivatives; identify the weak derivatives; check their integrability and that of the profile.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Accept the roof profile without inventing a tangent

On Ω = (0, 2), the normalized profile is u(x) = x up to the midpoint x = 1, then u(x) = 2 − x. Its height at the peak is 1. Let us check that it belongs to H^1(Ω) and calculate its squared norm.

  1. Define what membership means

    Ω, the letter omega, denotes the open domain. All integrals are Lebesgue integrals in x, dimensionless under our normalization. L^p(Ω) consists of measurable functions for which the integral of |u| to the power p is finite; functions that are equal almost everywhere represent the same element. “Almost everywhere” means except on a set of measure 0, such as an isolated point.

    For a real exponent p that is at least 1 and finite, W^(1, p)(Ω) consists of the elements of L^p(Ω) that have a weak derivative g also belonging to L^p(Ω). The first index specifies the required order, and the second the integrability exponent. The letter W names this space; g = D u denotes the weak derivative, defined by the following identity.

    ∫Ωuφ′dx=−∫Ωgφdx pour toute φ∈Cc∞(Ω)

    φ, phi, is a dimensionless test function, smooth and 0 outside a compact subset of Ω; φ′ is its classical derivative. Here C denotes smooth functions, the subscript c indicates compact support, and the superscript ∞ indicates that derivatives of every order exist. These test functions do not reach the boundary. The weak derivative, if it exists as a function, is unique almost everywhere.

    The name H^1(Ω) abbreviates W^(1, 2)(Ω). More generally, on an interval, W^(m, p) requires weak derivatives D^j u in L^p up to and including the integer order m, where j ranges from 0 to m and D^0u = u. We consider only order 1 and exponent 2. This definition includes no condition on boundary values.

  2. Identify the candidate slopes

    On the left side, differentiating x gives g = 1. On the right side, differentiating 2 − x gives g = -1. Set g(1) = 0 for convenience: this isolated value will not change any integral. We still need to check that this candidate is the weak derivative, since differentiating the pieces separately is not enough when there is a join.

    g(x)={1si 0<x<1-1si 1<x<2

  3. Check the join by integration by parts

    Take any test function φ. It vanishes near the endpoints. Integrate each side separately to make the contributions at the peak explicit.

    ∫01xφ′dx=φ(1)−∫01φdx
    ∫12(2−x)φ′dx=−φ(1)+∫12φdx

    The terms φ(1) and −φ(1) cancel because the heights of the pieces agree. The sum is −∫ over the left side of φ + ∫ over the right side of φ, which is exactly −∫Ω gφ. The identity holds for every test function: g is indeed D u. The change in slope produces no Dirac delta in this first derivative.

  4. Integrate the square of the height

    Let A denote the integral of |u| to the power 2. On the left side, u = x. An antiderivative of x to the power 2 is x to the power 3 divided by 3. Evaluating at the endpoints therefore gives 1/3. The change of variable t = 2 − x turns the right side into the same integral; t is an auxiliary dimensionless horizontal coordinate.

    A=∫01x2dx+∫12(2−x)2dx=13+13=23

    Thus A = 2/3, approximately 0.67, dimensionless. The integral is finite: u belongs to L^2(Ω). The square of the height must not be replaced by the area under the roof.

  5. Integrate the square of the weak derivative

    Let B denote the integral of |g| to the power 2. Each side has normalized width 1. On the left, the square of the slope is 1; on the right, it is also 1, because squaring removes the sign. The integral of each constant is its value multiplied by the width.

    B=∫Ω|g|2dx=12×1+(-1)2×1=2

    B equals 2 and is dimensionless and finite: g belongs to L^2(Ω). We have now checked the requirements on u and D u. The strip therefore belongs to H^1(Ω), even though u has no classical derivative at the midpoint.

  6. Assemble the norm, including the profile

    The Sobolev norm measures both the size of the profile and that of its weak derivative. For W^(1, p), we choose the following convention; the double bars denote a norm, and the power 1/p means the pth root.

    ‖u‖W1,p=(∫Ω|u|pdx+∫Ω|Du|pdx)1p
    ‖u‖H12=A+B=23+2=83

    The squared norm is therefore 8/3, approximately 2.67, dimensionless; the norm itself is the square root of 8/3, also dimensionless. B alone measures variation: its square root is a seminorm, because every constant height has derivative 0. It does not distinguish constant profiles from one another.

    Normalization comes before this addition. With height in centimetres and horizontal coordinate in metres, the two physical integrals would have different dimensions; adding them without scales would be unjustified. Our norm is that of the normalized profile, rather than a measured mechanical property.

  7. Check with another integration

    To check A without invoking symmetry, expand the square on the right: (2 − x) to the power 2 = x to the power 2 − 4x + 4. An antiderivative is x to the power 3/3 − 2x to the power 2 + 4x. Between 1 and 2, this gives 1/3, as with the change of variable. The square of g equals 1 almost everywhere: its integral equals the width 2, confirming B.

    The terms A and B are nonnegative and finite, and their sum exceeds each one: this check would fail if the negative sign of the slope had been retained in B. Giving u or g a different value at an isolated point would not change these integrals. Finally, we have imposed no differential equation and proved nothing about Sobolev embeddings.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “There is a corner, so the profile is excluded.”

    A classical derivative everywhere is not required. Check the identity with all test functions, then the integrability of u and D u. Here continuity of the height cancels the contributions at the join, and the slopes are square integrable.

    Revisit the join and the integrals

  • “Changing the height at the peak must change the norm.”

    A change at a single point changes neither the equivalence class under equality almost everywhere nor the integrals. It can make the drawn representative less regular without changing the Sobolev element. The exercise on the isolated value distinguishes these two descriptions.

    Test a change at a single point

  • “Differentiating each piece is enough, even if the height jumps.”

    You must retain the terms at the join in integration by parts. A jump in height can produce a Dirac delta: this distribution is not a square integrable function. Revisit the candidate with a vertical join in the last exercise.

    Look for the jump term

Exercise 1 · Distinguish the object from its representative

An isolated value on the same graph

The file describing the strip keeps u(x) everywhere except at the midpoint, where it records ũ(1) = 7 instead of 1. Is this the same element of L^2 and H^1? What happens to the weak derivative and the squared norm? Distinguish this file from a join actually spread over a positive width.

Compare with the solution

The modified set is the singleton containing 1, with measure 0. Thus ũ and u are equal almost everywhere. Their integrals against all test functions and their integrals of squares agree. They represent the same element of L^2 and H^1.

The weak derivative remains g almost everywhere; the squared norm remains 8/3, dimensionless. The new value 7 makes the pointwise representative discontinuous, but produces no jump term in an integral. Moving the strip over a positive width would change the function on a set of positive measure and require a new calculation.

Exercise 3 · Test a candidate

A vertical join in the description of the strip

Another file proposes, on the same domain, a profile v equal to 0 before x = 1 and 1 after it. It is assigned slope 0 almost everywhere, and v ∈ H^1 is claimed. Test this claim. Then replace the jump with a linear ramp of width ε, centred at the midpoint, and calculate the integral of its squared slope for ε = 1/4 and then ε = 1/8. Everything is normalized and dimensionless.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate ∫Ω vφ′ using the fact that φ vanishes near the right boundary.

Hint 2

A Dirac delta at the midpoint acts on φ by giving φ(1).

Hint 3

On the ramp, the slope is the height 1 divided by the width ε; it is 0 elsewhere.

Compare with the solution

v is bounded and square integrable on this domain of finite width. But ∫Ω vφ′ = ∫ from 1 to 2 of φ′ = −φ(1), whereas −∫Ω 0φ = 0. For a test function with nonzero φ(1), the identity fails with the proposed slope. The distributional derivative is δ at the point 1, the Dirac delta that assigns to φ its value at the midpoint.

This Dirac delta cannot be represented by a function g in L^2. To see this, take test functions with value 1 at the midpoint, bounded in absolute value by 1, and supported in an interval whose width tends to 0. Their L^2 norm tends to 0. If g belonged to L^2, the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality would force ∫gφ to tend to 0, whereas the Dirac delta always gives 1. This is a contradiction: this profile with a jump does not belong to H^1.

For a ramp with positive width ε less than the domain width, the profile is continuous and piecewise affine. The contributions at the joins cancel as for the roof. Its weak derivative equals 1/ε on the ramp and 0 elsewhere. The integral of its square is (1/ε) to the power 2 × ε = 1/ε. At ε = 1/4, it equals 4; at ε = 1/8, it equals 8, dimensionless.

Each ramp of positive width belongs to H^1; their slope cost diverges as the width tends to 0. This calculation illustrates the cost of the join and accompanies the proof using test functions; these ramp examples alone would not prove that every possible square integrable weak derivative is ruled out.

Check your reasoning

  • Check the weak identity at the join and obtain the correct sign of the Dirac delta.
  • Distinguish membership in L^2 from membership in H^1.
  • Justify the exclusion of the Dirac delta using test functions, then explain the factor inversely proportional to the width without confusing illustration with proof.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must you check for membership in W^(1, p) on our interval?

For p at least 1 and finite: u belongs to L^p and its weak derivative D u is represented by a function in L^p. You must check the identity against all test functions and the finiteness of the integrals, rather than just differentiate the pieces.

Recall 2Why does the roof peak create no Dirac delta in D u?

The heights on the left and right agree. Their boundary terms at the peak have opposite signs and cancel. The change in slope remains a piecewise bounded function, hence square integrable on our interval.

Recall 3What does the squared H^1 norm contain, and why is keeping only D u insufficient?

It adds ∫|u| to the power 2 and ∫|D u| to the power 2, in normalized coordinates. The square root of the second integral alone is a seminorm: it assigns 0 to every constant height.

Recall 4Do a pointwise value and a jump in height have the same effect?

No. Changing an isolated value changes neither the equivalence class under equality almost everywhere nor the integrals. A jump between pieces of positive width can produce a Dirac delta in the derivative; the join must be examined using test functions.

Recall 5What do the indices of W^(m, p) mean, and do they impose boundary values?

m is the highest order of weak derivatives required in L^p, and p is the integrability exponent; order 0 includes u. The definition used imposes no boundary value. H^1 denotes W^(1, 2).
