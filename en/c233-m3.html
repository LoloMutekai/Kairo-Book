Level 4 · C233·M3

Drift control

Your goalExtract a drift control from HJB, minimize its local cost subject to a bound, and distinguish this decision from a proof of global optimality.

Before you start
c227-m5 : Ito-Tanaka · c059-m6 : deterministic discrete Bellman · c167-m5 : initial and boundary value problem · c184-m5 : Longstaff-Schwartz · c233-m1 : costs, constraints and admissibility · c233-m2 : HJB

✿01 See the idea

A cart moves along a line marked on the floor. We look only at its lateral offset: it is 6 m on the positive side. A motor can push it towards the line, but small, unpredictable disturbances keep shifting it sideways. All the data in this scene are fictitious; no actual cart performance is measured.

Here, drift is the instantaneous mean velocity of the lateral movement, conditional on what has already been observed. The control chooses this drift. It does not choose the next jolt. Moving back towards the line on average therefore does not guarantee that every path gets closer to it.

Pushing harder costs more. To decide, we balance this immediate effort against the change in future cost as the cart moves. The slope of this future cost indicates which direction to act in; the price of effort determines how strongly to act.

We will carry out this minimization for a supplied slope, then respect the limited power of the motor. We are not solving the full value equation: a control calculated using an approximation of future cost remains a candidate control.

The rule to remember

In HJB, isolate what depends on the control; minimize effort plus drift multiplied by the value slope; then impose the bound in the convex quadratic case studied here.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Choosing the lateral push for the cart

At time t, the known offset is x = 6 m. The price of effort is ρ = 2 s, the noise coefficient is σ = 1 m/√s, and the control is bounded by U = 2 m/s. We will use an approximation v of the value to calculate a local choice, then hold this control for Δ = 0.5 s to check its consequences.

  1. Define the movement, the units and the available information

    s is the current time and t the decision time, in seconds; T is the finite deadline, later than t. gsjldc gqqglw gobubk npddbz tmdqlu gjxqsu onaq The control u_s, in m/s, is progressively measurable: it uses the observations available up to s, without knowing future jolts. It satisfies |u_s| ≤ U. The standard Brownian motion W_s has increments independent of the past, with mean 0 and variance equal to the elapsed time; we assign it the unit √s.

    dXs=usds+σdWs

    The coefficient σ is constant and independent of the control. The state is observed without error. There is no inertia, obstacle or early stopping here. Bounded controls ensure square integrability over the finite horizon. Changing these assumptions would change the problem.

  2. Connect the choice of drift to HJB

    We minimize the expectation of accumulated effort plus a squared terminal offset. ubwbce nzbmvx xukxhk mcjpdq sogjuf mwrjib qbir J(t,x;u) is the cost of an admissible strategy u; V(t,x) is the smallest possible cost, the infimum of J. The weight ρ is strictly positive. J and V are in m².

    J(t,x;u)=Et,x[∫tTρ2us2ds+XT22]

    In the classical formulation, if V can be differentiated 1 time with respect to time and 2 times with respect to space, HJB takes the form below. V_t is its time derivative, in m²/s; V_x is its spatial slope, in metres; V_xx is its curvature, which is dimensionless. The terminal condition sets V(T,x) to the square of x divided by 2. We reuse this equation from the previous unit without proving its validity again.

    0=Vt+σ22Vxx+min−U≤u≤U{ρ2u2+uVx}

    In this line, u is a candidate velocity at the point (t,x), rather than the whole strategy u_s. Each term is in m²/s: cost per unit time. When choosing u with the value slope fixed, V_t and the diffusion term are constant with respect to u.

  3. Build the local problem without assuming the value has been solved for

    For this teaching calculation, take v(t,x) to be the square of x divided by 2, an approximation that extends the terminal cost alone to all times. It is smooth and satisfies the terminal condition, but nothing says that it solves HJB before T. Its slope p = v_x equals x and its curvature q = v_xx equals 1. At the chosen point, p = 6 m. From now on, p and q denote these supplied derivatives, rather than those of an optimal value already established.

    hp(u)=ρ2u2+pu

    h_p is the part of the Hamiltonian that depends on the control; its unit is m²/s. The quadratic term penalizes effort. The term pu measures the marginal change in future cost carried by the drift. Here p is positive: a negative velocity moves down the slope of v.

  4. Find the unconstrained minimum and verify its nature

    Without a bound, the derivative of h_p with respect to u is ρu + p. Setting it to 0 gives the control u_libre; the second derivative is ρ, which is strictly positive. We therefore have a unique unconstrained minimum, rather than just a 0 slope.

    ulibre=−pρ=-62=-3 m/s

    Calculating h_p: u_libre squared is 9 m²/s². The effort is 2 × 9 ÷ 2 = 9 m²/s. The transport term is 6 × (-3) = -18 m²/s. The sum is -9 m²/s. This number can be negative: h_p includes a marginal decrease in future cost; it is not the total cost J, which remains nonnegative.

    Another way to check is to complete the square:

    hp(u)=ρ2(u+pρ)2−p22ρ

    The square vanishes exactly at u_libre and cannot become negative. This identity checks the sign of the control and the minimum value.

  5. Respect the motor bound

    The unconstrained control -3 m/s lies outside the interval [−2, +2] m/s. For this strictly convex parabola, minimizing over the interval means taking the permitted point nearest to the unconstrained minimum. This is projection onto the interval; this rule does not extend to an arbitrary cost without examination.

    uretenue=max(−U,min(U,−pρ))

    We choose u = -2 m/s. Its square is 4 m²/s²; the effort is 2 × 4 ÷ 2 = 4 m²/s. The transport term is 6 × (-2) = -12 m²/s. Thus h_p = 4 + (-12) = -8 m²/s.

    Independent check: the gap from the unconstrained minimum is -8 − (-9) = 1 m²/s. The completed square gives the same penalty: ρ times the square of (u − u_libre), divided by 2, is 1 m²/s. Throughout the admissible interval, h_p′(u) = ρu + p is positive: the minimum is indeed at the left endpoint.

  6. Restore the noise and state the limitation of the calculation

    The diffusion has not disappeared. Its contribution D is the square of σ multiplied by q and divided by 2. With our curvature q = 1, D is 0.5 m²/s. Adding D to the admissible minimum gives -8 + 0.5 = -7.5 m²/s. For our approximation v, which is independent of time, v_t = 0: this number is also the HJB residual, meaning the right-hand side after substituting v for V. It differs from 0. Our v is therefore not a solution of this equation at this point.

    The local minimum of h_p is exact for the supplied slope. However, this does not prove that the strategy constructed in this way minimizes J over the whole horizon. A true value function, an admissible control and verification conditions will be needed; we do not anticipate the theorem in a later unit.

    To check the physical meaning, hold the chosen control constant for Δ = 0.5 s. Integrating the movement over this interval gives exactly:

    Xt+Δ=x+uΔ+σ(Wt+Δ−Wt)

    The mean displacement is -2 × 0.5 = -1 m; the final mean is 6 + (-1) = 5 m. The conditional variance is the square of σ multiplied by Δ, giving 0.5 m². These moments do not predict the position of a particular path.

    The return towards the line concerns the mean under this held control. If u is then recalculated from the observed state, the result is a state feedback control: the push depends on the current position. The moments for an interval with constant control are then not automatically those of this entire strategy.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I take u = p/ρ: a positive slope calls for a positive push.”

    We are minimizing a cost. The term pu increases as u increases if p is positive. Solve ρu + p = 0 and check the sign with the completed square; with the given data, the unconstrained minimum is -3 m/s.

    Review the unconstrained minimum and its check using a square

  • “The best choice cancels the jolts, since σ does not appear in its formula.”

    The diffusion term is constant with respect to u in our minimization with a fixed slope. It remains in HJB and in the variance of the displacement. If σ depends on u, it also enters the minimization. Even when σ is independent of u, the true slope V_x may depend on the noise model.

    Compare the noise levels in the last exercise

  • “I have a minimum and the bound is respected: my strategy is optimal for the whole path.”

    You have minimized h_p for a supplied slope. The HJB residual here is -7.5 m²/s instead of 0. Admissibility and this local minimum do not turn a value approximation into a global solution.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and limitations

The noise increases, the supplied slope stays the same

Use ρ = 2 s and U = 2 m/s again at the point x = 6 m. Keep v, p and q from the example, but replace σ with 2 m/√s. Determine the local control, D, the HJB residual and the variance after Δ = 0.5 s with the control held constant. Can we conclude that noise never changes an optimal strategy?

Hint 1

With p fixed, identify the terms that depend on u.

Hint 2

The noise coefficient appears squared in D and in the variance. Separate the local calculation from solving for V.

Compare with the solution

h_p is unchanged. The unconstrained control remains -3 m/s and the admissible control -2 m/s. The square of the new σ is 4 m²/s; D = 4 × 1 ÷ 2 = 2 m²/s.

Since v_t = 0, the residual becomes -8 + 2 = -6 m²/s. The final mean remains 5 m; the variance is now 4 × 0.5 = 2 m².

This result concerns a supplied slope kept fixed. For a truly optimal strategy, we must solve for the value of the problem with its noise: V_x may then change. If the coefficient σ itself depends on u, its term enters the choice directly. No general invariance to noise has been proved.

Check your reasoning

  • The variance uses the square of σ.
  • The unchanged control is linked to the fixed slope, without making the noise disappear.
  • The nonzero residual and the absence of a global proof are stated explicitly.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In dX_s = u_s ds + σ dW_s, what do we choose and what can we not choose?

We choose the conditional mean velocity u_s using the information already available. We do not choose the future Brownian increment. An admissible control cannot anticipate the next jolts.

Recall 2Why do we minimize only quadratic effort plus pu in our example?

With the value derivatives fixed, the time term and the diffusion contribution D do not depend on u, because σ is constant. If the diffusion depends on the control, this simplification must be reconsidered.

Recall 3Recover the unconstrained minimum without reciting the result: what sign and what proof?

The derivative is ρu + p; setting it to 0 gives u = −p/ρ. The second derivative ρ is positive. The completed square also gives the minimum: the negative of the square of p divided by (2ρ), confirming the sign.

Recall 4How do we impose the bound, and why is this method valid here?

We take max(−U, min(U, −p/ρ)). The cost is a strictly convex parabola: its minimum over the interval is the admissible point nearest to the unconstrained minimum. A nonconvex cost would require further examination.

Recall 5Why does our exact local control not prove that a strategy is globally optimal?

It uses the slope of an approximation v. Its HJB residual is nonzero in the example. The value, admissibility and verification conditions over the horizon must be established separately; local minimization alone does not prove them.

What you understand
