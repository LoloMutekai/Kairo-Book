Level 2 · C053·M2

Entropy of a finite distribution

Your goalCalculate average surprise in bits from a finite probability distribution, then explain what this average allows us to conclude.

Before you start
c029-m5 : Bayes · c030-m5 : Poisson · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c025-m5 : O, Omega and Theta · c053-m1 : surprise and information

✿01 See the idea

At the counter, an opaque urn contains tickets of the same size. There are 4 marked A, 2 marked B, 1 marked C and 1 marked D. You mix them and draw a ticket; each has the same chance of being chosen. Which letter will you discover? This scene and all its values are fictional.

A is a good prediction, but it remains uncertain. Discovering D is more surprising than discovering A. Surprise concerns a particular outcome; entropy answers another question: what average surprise should we expect from the distribution of letters before we look?

To calculate this average, we need to count the surprise of A often and that of D rarely. Giving each letter the same weight would no longer describe our draw. In an urn where all letters are equally likely, we would need to change both the weights and the surprises.

The previous unit gives the surprise: the negative of the logarithm of the probability. Here we use base 2, so the unit of information is the bit. We will move from this surprise to its average, without constructing a compression code.

The rule to remember

For each possible letter, multiply its surprise by its probability, then add the results. A rare outcome can be very surprising while having little weight in the average.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Before reading the ticket: how much average surprise?

Keep exactly the urn shown in the drawing. Its composition is known and the draw is uniform over the tickets. We want the entropy of the letter X, rather than that of the identity of each ticket. If we repeat the scene, we replace the ticket and mix again.

  1. Define the distribution and the average we want

    X is the random letter read after the draw. For a letter i, pᵢ = P(X = i) is its probability, with no unit; P means “probability”. S denotes the set of letters with strictly positive probability and K is their number. Here S contains A, B, C and D, so K = 4. The index i runs through these letters; it is not a draw number.

    Iᵢ is the surprise associated with i, in bits. H(X) is the entropy of X, in bits for this draw. The symbol ∑ asks us to add a term for each letter in S. A weighted average multiplies each value by its weight; here the weights are the probabilities and their sum must equal 1.

    A distribution for this draw is all we need. Independence of successive draws is not required to define H(X). We are not calculating the entropy of a sequence here.

  2. Convert counts into probabilities

    The total N is 4 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 8 tickets. If nᵢ is the count of i, in tickets, then pᵢ = nᵢ/N: the units cancel, since each ticket is equally likely.

  3. Recover each surprise using a power and a reciprocal

    The logarithm asks which exponent produces the number. The reciprocal of p_A is 2, so I_A = 1 bit. The reciprocal of p_B is 4 = 2 to the power 2, so I_B = 2 bits. ghyhje pmcbsf cpdbmc hbsfss lsbkex dgcptb qxno

    These checks recover the logarithms exactly: no rounding is needed for these probabilities, which are reciprocals of powers of the base. With other probabilities, the surprises would not necessarily be integers.

  4. Apply the weights before adding

    The contribution of A is p_A × I_A = (1/2) × 1 = 1/2 bit per draw. That of B is (1/4) × 2 = 1/2 bit per draw. B is less frequent than A, but more surprising: their contributions are equal here.

    For C, (1/8) × 3 = 3/8 bit per draw; for D, (1/8) × 3 = 3/8 bit per draw. Each of these letters contributes less than A, despite its greater surprise. We therefore need to know both the surprise and its weight.

  5. Add and state the result with its unit

    The contributions of A and B total 1 bit per draw; those of C and D total 3/4 bit per draw. We add these contributions to the average without dividing again by the number of letters: the probabilities have already provided normalized weights.

    H(X)=148=1.75 bit par tirage

    The result 7/4 is exact; its decimal representation 1.75 is also exact here. This number describes an average predicted by the distribution, before the ticket is revealed. It does not say that each ticket will provide exactly this amount of surprise.

  6. Check by going through the tickets rather than the letters

    Mentally go through each ticket in the urn. The A tickets provide 4 × 1 = 4 bits; the B tickets, 2 × 2 = 4 bits; C and D, respectively 3 and 3 bits. The total for this list is 14 bits.

    There are 8 equally likely tickets: their average is 14/8 = 7/4 bit per draw. We recover H through another way of counting. Tickets bearing the same letter do receive the same surprise, that of the letter.

    This list of possibilities is not an observed experiment. A sequence of 8 draws with replacement could have different counts; we do not automatically assign it the total 14.

  7. Check the sign and the bound of the average

    A positive probability cannot exceed 1, so each surprise is positive or 0 and H cannot be negative. For K letters with positive probability, the upper bound is the base 2 logarithm of K; it is reached when these letters are equally likely.

    0≤H(X)≤log2(K)

    Why this bound? The logarithm is concave: the logarithm of a weighted average of positive numbers is at least their weighted average of logarithms. Take the reciprocals of the pᵢ as the numbers and the pᵢ as the weights. Their average is K, because each product pᵢ × (1/pᵢ) equals 1. The average of their logarithms is precisely H. Equality here requires all the reciprocals to be equal, and therefore the probabilities to be equal too.

    In our urn, K = 4 and its logarithm is 2 bits: 0 ≤ 1.75 ≤ 2. The calculation respects this bound; the exercise on the uniform urn will reach the upper limit.

  8. Handle impossible outcomes without inventing a logarithm

    If a letter has probability 0, it does not belong to S. We can also keep its entry in the list by agreeing that its contribution is 0. This convention is written 0 × log(0) = 0, but it does not give any finite value to the logarithm of 0: we omit the term. It corresponds to the limit of p × log(p) as p approaches 0 through positive values.

    If A becomes certain, its probability is 1 and its surprise is 0; the other letters no longer contribute and H is 0 bit per draw. An empty urn is different: no draw from it defines probabilities through the rule nᵢ/N, because N = 0.

    We have measured the uncertainty of the letter under a chosen distribution. This calculation ignores any order in a sequence and measures neither the meaning of the letters nor the quality of a decision. Coding and compression will be covered in the next unit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I average the surprises by dividing by the number of letters.”

    This average gives each letter the same weight, although A appears on more tickets. Multiply each surprise by the probability of its letter; do not divide again once these contributions have been added. The first exercise asks you to correct this calculation.

    Correct the unweighted average

  • “If I draw D, the entropy becomes 3 bits.”

    It is the surprise of outcome D that equals 3 bits. For the unchanged distribution, entropy before revelation remains 1.75 bit per draw. D contributes only 3/8 bit per draw to this average, because its weight is 1/8.

    Separate D’s surprise from its contribution

  • “A 0 probability makes the entire entropy impossible to calculate.”

    Ignore the contribution of the impossible outcome; do not ask a calculator for the logarithm of 0. Then check that the remaining probabilities total 1. If no draw is defined, as with an empty urn, this condition is no longer satisfied.

    Review the case of impossible outcomes

Compare with the solution

The sum of the surprises is 1 + 2 + 3 + 3 = 9 bits; divided by 4, it gives 9/4 = 2.25 bits. This is an average with equal weights of the surprises from the initial distribution; it is not its entropy.

The corrected calculation is (1/2) × 1 + (1/4) × 2 + (1/8) × 3 + (1/8) × 3 = 1/2 + 1/2 + 3/8 + 3/8 = 7/4 bit per draw. The probabilities account for the number of tickets bearing each letter.

Exercise 2 · Application · a new distribution

The same letters, equal chances

The staff at the counter replace the contents with 2 A tickets, 2 B tickets, 2 C tickets and 2 D tickets. The total remains 8. Predict the direction of the change, then calculate the probabilities, surprises and entropy. By how much does H change compared with the initial urn?

Hint 1

Each letter now has probability 1/4. Recalculate its surprise with this new probability.

Hint 2

The contributions are all identical: multiply one of them by 4, then compare the exact entropies.

Compare with the solution

Before calculating, we predict greater uncertainty: no letter now has the high probability that A had. Each probability is 2/8 = 1/4, and its surprise is 2 bits since 2 to the power 2 = 4.

Each contribution is (1/4) × 2 = 1/2 bit per draw. H is therefore 4 × (1/2) = 2 bits per draw. The difference is 2 − (7/4) = 1/4 = 0.25 bit per draw.

We reach the bound for this number of letters. The identities of the letters are preserved, but the distribution and the surprises have changed. This result says nothing about the meaning of the tickets or an actual file size achieved.

  • I recalculate the surprises with 1/4, without reusing those of the initial urn.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · limiting cases and justification

Certain is different from empty

The staff at the counter now place 8 A tickets and no other tickets in the urn. Calculate the entropy of the letter, explaining how you handle the absent letters. They then remove all the tickets. Can we assign the same entropy of a draw to this empty urn using the rule from the example? Justify your answer by checking what constitutes a probability distribution.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A certain letter has a probability equal to 1 and 0 surprise. Impossible letters do not contribute to the sum.

Hint 2

For an empty urn, try to define nᵢ/N before calculating logarithms. Can we obtain a sum of probabilities equal to 1?

Compare with the solution

With the A tickets, p_A = 8/8 = 1, and I_A = −log in base 2 of 1 = 0 bit. The absent letters have 0 contribution, without evaluating their logarithms. H = 1 × 0 = 0 bit per draw: we already know which letter will be drawn.

In the empty urn, N = 0 and no letter can be drawn. The quotient nᵢ/N is undefined. Assigning 0 to each probability would give a sum of 0, instead of 1: this would not be a distribution for the draw.

We therefore cannot calculate the same entropy from this rule. Software can choose a 0 result for an empty input; that return value then belongs to its contract, rather than proving that an empty draw has 0 entropy. To discuss a “no ticket” event, we would need to define another model, with its own outcomes and probabilities.

Check your reasoning

  • I distinguish a distribution concentrated on A from a draw that is undefined.
  • I find 0 bit per draw in the certain case without evaluating log(0).
  • I check normalization and refuse to turn the empty urn into a probability distribution through an implicit convention.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you reconstruct entropy from surprises, rather than simply reciting a result?

Define the random letter and its distribution; for each outcome with positive probability, calculate its surprise and then multiply it by its probability; add these contributions. Base 2 gives bits.

Recall 2Why is D more surprising than A while contributing less to H?

D provides 3 bits when it appears, but its weight is only 1/8: its contribution is 3/8 bit per draw. A provides only 1 bit of surprise, but its weight 1/2 gives a contribution of 1/2, which is greater.

Recall 3Which checks come before the calculation, and which bound checks the result?

The probabilities are positive or 0 and their sum is 1. For K outcomes with positive probability, H lies between 0 and the base 2 logarithm of K; the upper bound corresponds to equal probabilities. Here 1.75 is between 0 and 2 bits per draw.

Recall 4How do you handle an impossible letter, and why does an empty urn pose a different problem?

Omit the contribution of the impossible letter, without calculating log(0). The probabilities of the remaining letters must still total 1. In an empty urn, the draw and its distribution are not defined by nᵢ/N; this is not a certain case.

Recall 5Does H describe the surprise of each ticket or the order of a sequence?

H describes the average surprise for the distribution of the letter before revelation. A particular outcome can be more or less surprising. Calculating this distribution alone does not include the order of a sequence; it proves neither achieved compression nor the quality of a decision.
