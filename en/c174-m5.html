Level 3 · C174·M5

Conditions for validity

Your goalJustify a Feynman-Kac representation by checking the diffusion, regularity and integrability, then recognize what a smooth formula does not guarantee.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A microscopic marker moves randomly in a straight channel. We track its signed position relative to an origin. To isolate the mathematical question, we idealize the channel as the whole real line: no wall reflects or absorbs the marker. At the final time, its position receives a score equal to the square of its distance from the origin.

You want to predict the mean of this score. Feynman-Kac relates this mean over possible movements to a partial differential equation, abbreviated PDE. But writing the equation is not enough. We need a motion defined up to the final time, a function regular enough to apply the Ito formula, and a random term whose mean is actually 0.

The values are fictional: a start at 1 mm, a fluctuation amplitude σ = 2 mm per square root of a second and a horizon T = 3 s, starting from t = 0 s. The drawing shows possible paths, without simulation or readings. The score remains in square millimetres; it is neither a concentration nor a temperature.

This unit verifies a version of Feynman-Kac with no source term or exponential weighting. It distinguishes a sufficient condition, which permits a specific argument, from a necessary condition, without which no argument could work. Failing our sufficient criterion requires another justification; it does not automatically rule out every representation.

The rule to remember

Before taking expectations in the Ito formula: fix the domain and the terminal term, check regularity, then check the integrability of the stochastic term.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The square of the final position: a justified representation

In the idealized channel, the displacement is a Brownian motion with constant amplitude σ. The terminal score g(y) is the square of y. Let us construct a candidate, then check each step that allows us to identify its value with an expectation.

  1. Fix the motion, domain and units

    t is the starting time, s a current time and T the final time, in seconds, with t ≤ s ≤ T. x is the initial position, in mm. W is a Brownian motion: its increments are independent, centred and Gaussian, and their variance is the elapsed time. W therefore has units of the square root of a second. X is the random position, in mm; σ is constant, in mm per square root of a second.

    Xs=x+σ(Ws−Wt),t≤s≤T

    This explicit solution is continuous and remains finite on every finite time interval, almost surely. It solves dX = σ dW with 0 drift. For a more general stochastic differential equation, abbreviated SDE, dX = b(s,X) ds + σ(s,X) dW, b denotes the drift in mm per second. Coefficients that are continuous, globally Lipschitz in position, uniformly in time over the finite horizon, and of at most linear growth form a standard set of sufficient conditions for existence and uniqueness without explosion. Here the constant coefficients satisfy these conditions directly.

    Globally Lipschitz means that the difference between the coefficient values is bounded by a constant times the distance between the positions. At most linear growth bounds its size by a constant plus a constant times the distance from the origin, in the chosen units. These assumptions say nothing yet about the regularity of the function we seek.

  2. State exactly the version being verified

    The domain is the real line ℝ, with no boundary condition. E with subscript t,x denotes expectation under the motion starting at x at time t; it is not naive conditioning on an event of probability 0. g is the terminal score, in square millimetres; y denotes a possible position, in mm. u(t,x), with the same units as g, is the candidate function.

    ∂tu+σ22∂xxu=0,u(T,x)=g(x)

    ∂t u is the time derivative, in square millimetres per second; ∂x u is the position derivative, in mm; ∂xx u is the second position derivative, with no units. The generator L acts here as Lφ = (σ squared / 2) ∂xx φ, for a regular test function φ with the same units as u. Applied to u, it has units of square millimetres per second, like ∂t u.

    The verification version used here assumes that u has 1 time derivative and 2 position derivatives, continuous on the closed interval and the whole line, including the endpoints. This regularity is written as:

    u∈C1,2([t,T]×ℝ)

    It also assumes that g(X at time T) is integrable and that the time integral of E[|σ ∂x u(s,X at time s)| squared] is finite. The integrand is adapted: its value depends only on the past and present of the motion. These conditions suffice to apply Ito and take expectations up to T.

    This is the direction “verified classical solution ⇒ representation”. A finite expectation does not by itself prove the existence of all these derivatives. If they exist only before T, the passage to the terminal limit must also be justified, for example by an integrable dominating variable. Such a variable bounds the absolute values of the quantities whose convergence is needed. We do not need this additional argument for our polynomial, which is regular up to T.

  3. Construct the candidate without confusing the means

    Let τ = T − t be the remaining duration in seconds. The displacement σ(W at time T − W at time t) is centred and has variance σ squared × τ, in square millimetres. Expanding the square of the sum of x and the displacement makes the cross term vanish in expectation; the square of x and the variance of the displacement remain.

    g(y)=y2,u(t,x)=x2+σ2(T−t)

    Numerically: τ = 3 − 0 = 3 s; σ squared = 2 × 2 = 4 square millimetres per second; terminal variance = 4 × 3 = 12 square millimetres. The square of x is 1 × 1 = 1 square millimetre. Thus u(t,x) = 1 + 12 = 13 square millimetres.

    Probabilistic check: the square of the mean position is only 1 square millimetre. The difference from 13 is exactly the variance 12. The score has a finite expectation even though it is unbounded on the line.

  4. Check the PDE and the terminal condition

    The candidate is a polynomial, so all the required derivatives exist and are continuous. As t increases, the remaining duration decreases: ∂t u = −σ squared = -4 square millimetres per second. The curvature is ∂xx u = 2, with no units. The diffusion coefficient in the PDE is 4 / 2 = 2 square millimetres per second.

    ∂tu+Lu=-4+2×2=-4+4=0

    This residual has units of square millimetres per second and vanishes at every position, rather than only at the start. At t = T, the remaining duration is 0 s and u(T,x) is indeed the square of x. The data are terminal: they are imposed at T, even though the diffusion moves forward from t to T.

    Nondegeneracy means here that σ does not vanish. It is not required for the Ito verification argument alone once a regular candidate and the integrability checks are already available. It plays a different role in some theorems that establish this regularity.

  5. Verify the decisive step of taking expectations

    Ito along the motion gives d[u(s,X at time s)] = (∂s u + Lu) ds + σ ∂x u dW. The PDE cancels the ds term. Here ∂x u(s,y) = 2 y, in mm, so the random coefficient is 2 σ X at time s. At the start, ∂x u is 2 mm. Let M denote the integral of this coefficient against dW, initialized at 0; M has units of square millimetres.

    A=∫tTEt,x[(2σXs)2]ds=2×2σ2[x2τ+σ2τ22]

    A is the integrated expectation of the squared stochastic coefficient. To calculate it, E[X at time s squared] = square of x + σ squared × (s − t). Integrating the constant part gives 1 × 3 = 3 square millimetres times seconds. Integrating the part linear in time gives σ squared × τ squared / 2: τ squared = 9 seconds squared; the quotient is 9/2 seconds squared; the contribution is 18 square millimetres times seconds.

    The sum is 3 + 18 = 21 square millimetres times seconds. The factor 2 σ is 4 mm per square root of a second; its square is 16 square millimetres per second. Finally A = 16 × 21 = 336 millimetres to the power 4, a finite quantity.

    This is a concrete sufficient condition: the stochastic integral is a square integrable martingale with mean 0. A martingale preserves its value in conditional expectation given the past. The Ito isometry identifies E[terminal M squared] with A; it therefore also controls the size of the term whose mean we set to 0. A mere local martingale, obtained only after stopping to bound the process, does not allow us to conclude that its mean is 0 without further control.

  6. Identify the result and limit the conclusion

    g(XT)=u(t,x)+MT⇒u(t,x)=Et,x[g(XT)]

    The justified result is 13 square millimetres. The probabilistic approach using mean and variance and the analytical approach using the PDE agree; the check on A completes the link between them. This reasoning identifies any candidate with the properties of this version; it does not assert uniqueness in a class of functions without growth or integrability restrictions.

    In a bounded channel with an absorbing wall, we would have to stop the motion at its first exit and include the boundary data in the score. A reflecting wall would require yet another model. Changing the domain without changing the representation is therefore unjustified. Finally, a mean calculated over a finite number of paths remains an approximation: neither its numerical existence nor a short discretization step proves the assumptions above.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the formulas. Explain why the polynomial is regular enough, why its lack of a bound does not prevent the mean from existing, and exactly which check allows the random term to be removed when taking expectations.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The score is smooth, so Feynman-Kac applies.”

    Regularity allows derivatives; it does not control the tails of the distribution. Check the expectation of the score and the stochastic term separately. The exercise with the exponential score keeps the same motion but makes integrability fail.

    Test regularity without integrability

  • “There is a dW, so the term must have mean 0.”

    The coefficient depends on the path. Its adaptedness and a check such as finite A justify a true square integrable martingale here. A purely local property is not enough to take expectations up to the final time.

    Repeat the stochastic integral check

  • “If it works on the line, I keep the formula in a channel with walls.”

    The domain is part of the problem. Absorption at an exit introduces a stopping time and a boundary score; reflection changes the motion. Start again with the definition of the domain and the terminal data.

    Reread the domain and scope of the result

Observe later, at the same place

The start is now at t = 1 s and x = 1 mm, under the same law, with T = 3 s and the same σ. Calculate the predicted mean score and the quantity A. Start from the remaining duration; justify that the check gives a finite value.

Hint 1

The time elapsed before the start does not enter the variance of the new Brownian increment.

Hint 2

For A, integrate the mean square of X: square of x × τ + σ squared × τ squared / 2, then multiply by the square of 2 σ.

Compare with the solution

τ = 3 − 1 = 2 s. The variance of the increment is 4 × 2 = 8 square millimetres. The predicted score is therefore 1 + 8 = 9 square millimetres.

For the check: τ squared = 4 seconds squared, then τ squared / 2 = 2 seconds squared. The integral of the mean square is 1 × 2 + 4 × 2 = 2 + 8 = 10 square millimetres times seconds. Thus A = 16 × 10 = 160 millimetres to the power 4, which is finite.

Sense check: less diffusion time here gives a variance and mean score below 12 and 13. The regularity and terminal condition remain those of the same polynomial; the new value of A makes the integrability check explicit for this start.

  • Calculate the remaining duration before the variance.
  • Recover 9 with its units.
  • Work through the integration giving A, rather than assuming it is finite because the time is finite.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · refute a justification

A smooth score whose mean diverges

Return to the initial start and the same marker. Only the score changes: h(y) = exp(a × square of y), with a = 1 per square millimetre; h has no units. The final position Y is Gaussian with mean x = 1 mm and variance v = 12 square millimetres. Its density is proportional to exp(−(y − x) squared / (2v)). Examine the coefficient of the square of y in the product score × density. Does the regularity of h suffice to give a finite expectation?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the exponents of the score and density, then expand the square of y − x.

Hint 2

The leading coefficient is a − 1/(2v). If it is positive, the exponential grows in the tails instead of decaying.

Compare with the solution

The total exponent is (a − 1/(2v)) × square of y + (x/v)y − square of x/(2v). Each term has no units. Here 2v = 24 square millimetres, so the threshold is 1/24 per square millimetre, or approximately 0.0417 per square millimetre.

The leading coefficient is 1 − 1/24 = 23/24 per square millimetre, strictly positive. The positive quadratic term dominates the linear term as the absolute value of y grows. The product score × density is not integrable: E[h(Y)] is infinite. Yet h has derivatives of every order.

For this Gaussian distribution, the product is integrable when a is strictly below the threshold, because the quadratic exponent becomes negative. At equality, only an exponential with a linear exponent remains, or a constant if x is 0: the integral over the whole line still diverges. With our a, we therefore cannot claim a finite representation. This does not invalidate the quadratic score in the example, whose checks were carried out separately.

Check your reasoning

  • Find the coefficient 23/24 and its sign.
  • Relate the positive sign to divergence on the line.
  • Use this same motion to explain why regularity and integrability are separate requirements.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What data must be fixed before writing the representation?

The motion, its initial law, the domain, the horizon and the terminal score. On a domain with a boundary, the boundary behaviour and the corresponding data must also be specified; the formula for the line cannot be transferred without justification.

Recall 2What does the regularity of the candidate allow, and what does it not guarantee?

The continuous time and space derivatives allow us to apply Ito to u(s,X at time s) here. They guarantee neither a finite mean score nor mean 0 for a stochastic integral without further control.

Recall 3Exactly which check allows the random term to be set to 0 in expectation?

An adapted coefficient and a finite integrated expectation of its square make the stochastic integral a square integrable martingale with mean 0 here. Saying only “local martingale” leaves this step to be justified.

Recall 4Does an unbounded score necessarily make the representation invalid?

No. The square of the Brownian position has a finite expectation over our finite horizon. Its growth must be checked under the law in question; the exponential score in the last exercise grows too fast under this same law.

Recall 5Which direction of Feynman-Kac have we actually justified?

A classical candidate satisfying the PDE, the terminal data and the stated checks equals the expectation of the score. We have neither automatically deduced a classical solution from every finite expectation nor validated an actual numerical calculation.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without reference material: start from the marker and the score, name the domain and the final time, then relate each assumption to its role in the Ito proof. Recover the mean square using mean and variance, check the PDE and explain the calculation that makes the stochastic term vanish in expectation. Finish with a change to the score that breaks integrability.
