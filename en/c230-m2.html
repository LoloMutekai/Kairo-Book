Level 4 · C230·M2

Distributions

Your goalDefine a distribution as a continuous linear functional on test functions, work out the readings for a locally integrable function and a Dirac mass, then check linearity, continuity and equality using a single source example.

Before you start
c165-m5 : duality · c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c167-m5 : initial and boundary value problem · c230-m1 : test functions

✿01 See the idea

A thin rod is heated. In this fictional scene, two tiny heating elements each supply power concentrated at a point: 3 W at x = 0 m and 5 W at x = 2 m. Uniform background heating adds 2 W per metre along the entire rod. The scene recalls the sources in the diffusion problems of C167·M5; we do not solve an equation, we only describe the source.

For power concentrated at a point, the “density” in watts per metre no longer has a finite value: no ordinary function describes it properly. Yet a real instrument never reads a point. It reads a weighted average with a smooth sensitivity that is 0 outside a small interval. This is exactly a test function from C230·M1: infinitely differentiable and 0 outside a bounded interval. We call it a probe and denote it by φ; it is dimensionless.

A distribution is the rule that turns a probe into a reading, here in watts. We no longer ask “what is the source at x?” but “what does probe φ read?”. Two requirements make the rule usable. It is linear: doubling the probe doubles the reading, and adding two probes adds their readings. It is continuous: a small change in the probe, including its derivatives, produces a small change in the reading. It is a continuous linear functional on a function space, using the language of duality from C165·M5.

All values on this page are fictional. The rod, its powers and its probes serve a mathematical exercise; they are neither measurements nor a validated device.

The rule to remember

A distribution reads a smooth probe and returns a number: the reading is linear in the probe and bounded on each compact set; we know the distribution through all its readings, never through a value at a point.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A rod, two point sources and three probes

Source S consists of 3 W at a = 0 m, 5 W at b = 2 m and a background of 2 W/m. Profile φ is even, nonnegative, 0 outside the interval [−1 ; 1] m, has value 1 at its centre and has integral 1 m (fictional values). Probe A is centred at 0 m, probe B at 2 m, and probe C at 1 m. Let us calculate what each probe reads, then check linearity, continuity and equality.

  1. Define the objects and assumptions

    Points a and b carry powers α and β, in watts. The symbol δa denotes the Dirac mass at a: its reading on a probe φ is φ(a). The background is the constant function c, in W/m. A locally integrable function f, meaning one that is integrable on every bounded interval, becomes the distribution [f]; the source is the sum S:

    S=αδa+βδb+[c]

    The readings are defined using the probe, with a finite integral because φ is bounded and 0 outside a bounded interval:

    ⟨[f],φ⟩=∫f(x)φ(x)dx et ⟨δa,φ⟩=φ(a)

    By addition of distributions, the reading of S is the sum of the readings:

    ⟨S,φ⟩=αφ(a)+βφ(b)+c∫φ(x)dx

    Assumptions: α, β and c are known positive constants; φ is real-valued; the probe centred at s is the translate x ↦ φ(x − s), which keeps the same peak and the same integral, in metres. None of this is a measurement.

  2. Read S with probe A

    Probe A is centred at a. Its value at a is 1. At b, which is 2 m from its centre, beyond the radius 1 m, it is 0: source b is invisible to A. The background term is 2 × 1 = 2 W, because W/m multiplied by m gives watts.

    ⟨S,φA⟩=3×1+5×0+2×1=5 W

  3. Move the probe

    Probe B is centred at b: it detects source b but no longer detects source a. Probe C is centred at the midpoint, at 1 m; each source is 1 m from it, exactly the radius, where the profile is 0. Probe C therefore detects only the background.

    ⟨S,φB⟩=3×0+5×1+2×1=7 W
    ⟨S,φC⟩=3×0+5×0+2×1=2 W

    The same distribution S gives three different readings: 5 W, 7 W and 2 W. None is “the value of S”. Narrow probes localise sources.

  4. Check linearity

    Take the probe φ = 2 × (probe A) + (probe B). Its value at a is 2 because probe B is 0 there. At b its value is 1 because probe A is 0 there. Its integral is 2 × 1 + 1 = 3 m. Direct reading:

    ⟨S,2×φA+φB⟩=3×2+5×1+2×3=6+5+6=17 W

    Reading by linearity, without evaluating the combined probe again:

    2×5+7=10+7=17 W

    The two methods give 17 W and 17 W: the check is consistent.

  5. Check continuity of order 0

    Continuity means that for every closed bounded interval K, there are a constant C and an order m such that, for every probe that is 0 outside K, the reading satisfies the bound below. Here m equals 0: no derivative of the probe is involved, and the reading has order 0.

    |⟨T,φ⟩|≤C∑k=0m‖φ(k)‖∞

    Take K = [−3 ; 3] m, which contains a, b and the supports of the three probes. Each Dirac contribution is bounded by its power times the largest |φ|. The background integral is bounded by c times the length 6 m of K, times the largest |φ|. Hence, with ‖φ‖ denoting the largest |φ|:

    |⟨S,φ⟩|≤(α+β+2cK)‖φ‖∞
    3+5+2×2×3=8+12=20 W

    Probe B is 0 outside [1 ; 3] m, hence supported in K, and its maximum is 1: the bound is 20 W. The reading 7 W satisfies it.

  6. A single probe does not determine S

    Compare S with the background alone, [c], whose reading for each of these probes is 2 × 1 = 2 W. On probe C, S reads 2 W: the same reading. On probe A, S reads 5 W compared with 2 W. The two distributions are therefore different.

    Two distributions are equal when all their readings agree, for every probe. Agreement on one probe, or on three, does not establish equality.

  7. Review the units and limitations

    The profile is dimensionless. α φ(a) is in watts; c ∫ φ dx is in W/m × m, hence in watts. Each reading is therefore in watts, as is the constant C.

    We have neither differentiated S nor tried to give a meaning to its value at a point: differentiation belongs to C230·M3. Nor have we proved the equivalence between the bound and sequential continuity; here we take the bound as the definition.

Explain it in your own words

Why does probe C read the same value for S and for the background alone, even though S is not equal to the background? What else must be examined to distinguish two distributions?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A distribution is read like a function: we can always ask for its value at a point.”

    A distribution acts on probes, not on points. Only the Dirac returns the value of the probe at a point, which is not a value of the source. Probe C, at the midpoint between the two sources, reads only the background.

    Review the two probe diagrams

  • “The Dirac is a function that equals infinity at a point and has integral one.”

    No ordinary function reproduces all the readings of a Dirac. For a bounded density, a narrow probe gives a reading that tends to 0 with its width, whereas the Dirac continues to read the peak. The last exercise calculates this.

    Redo the exercise on the narrow probe

  • “If two distributions give the same reading on one probe, they are equal.”

    They must give the same reading on every probe. In the example, S and the background alone read 2 W on probe C, but 5 W compared with 2 W on probe A.

    Review the comparison of the probes

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Read a shifted probe

Use the same source S and the same profile φ. Probe D is centred at 1.5 m. Assume, as a fictional value, that the profile equals 0.5 at 0.5 m from its centre. What power does it read?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate the distance from each source to the centre of the probe, then compare it with the radius.

Hint 2

A point-source term is 0 when the distance reaches the radius; remember the background.

Compare with the solution

Source a is 1.5 m from the centre, at least the radius 1 m: its term is 0. Source b is 0.5 m away, where the profile equals 0.5: 5 × 0.5 = 2.5 W. The background adds 2 W. Reading: 4.5 W.

⟨S,φD⟩=3×0+5×1/2+2×1=9/2 W

Check: this reading lies between that of probe C, which detects only the background, and that of probe B, which detects the full contribution from source b.

Check your reasoning

  • Distances compared with the radius.
  • Term for the Dirac at a is 0, term for the Dirac at b is weighted by the profile value.
  • Background added in watts.

Exercise 2 · Reasoning

Linear or not?

Two reading rules are proposed. T(φ) = α φ(a), with α = 3 W, and Q(φ) = φ(a)², dimensionless. Test them on probe A and then on twice that probe. Which one can be a distribution?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A linear functional satisfies reading(2φ) = 2 × reading(φ).

Hint 2

A single counterexample is enough to reject linearity; a successful test does not prove it.

Compare with the solution

For T: probe A equals 1 at a, so T(A) = 3 W; twice the probe equals 2, so T(2A) = 6 W, and 2 × T(A) = 6 W: the test succeeds.

For Q: Q(A) = 1 × 1 = 1; Q(2A) = 2 × 2 = 4; however, 2 × Q(A) = 2. The values differ: Q is not linear, so it is not a distribution.

T is indeed the weighted Dirac from the scene, α δa; its general linearity follows from the linearity of φ ↦ φ(a), which this test alone does not prove.

Check your reasoning

  • Explicit comparison of reading(2φ) with 2 × reading(φ).
  • Conclusion about Q based on a counterexample.
  • Observation that success on one probe does not prove the linearity of T.

Exercise 3 · Guided proof

Why the Dirac is not a density

We model a source of one watt at a by δₐ. Suppose that a function f, with |f| ≤ 5 W/m on [−0.1 ; 0.1] m, reproduces this reading for every probe that is 0 outside this interval. Take the narrow probe ψ(x) = φ(x/ε) with ε = 0.1 m and peak 1 at its centre. Calculate the integral of ψ, the Dirac reading, and the bound on the reading from f, then draw a conclusion.

⟨δa,ψ⟩=ψ(a)=1 W
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The change of variable y = x/ε gives ∫φ(x/ε)dx = ε × ∫φ(y)dy.

Hint 2

Bound |∫ f ψ dx| by the maximum of |f| times ∫ψ, since the probe is nonnegative.

Hint 3

Compare the bound with the Dirac reading.

Compare with the solution

Integral of the narrow probe: 0.1 × 1 = 0.1 m. Dirac reading: ψ(a) = 1 W, the peak value. Reading from f: at most 5 × 0.1 = 0.5 W.

Since 0.5 is strictly less than 1, f cannot reproduce the Dirac reading: the assumption leads to a contradiction.

This reasoning works for any finite bound: by making ε small enough, the upper bound falls below the Dirac reading. For a function that is only locally integrable, the same idea relies on the fact that the integral of |f| over a shrinking interval tends to 0, an integration result we accept here without proof.

Check your reasoning

  • Correct change of variable for the integral.
  • Dirac reading equal to the peak value.
  • Comparison of the bound and the reading, followed by the conclusion that this bounded density cannot represent the Dirac.
  • Limitation stated: the general locally integrable case is accepted without proof.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is a distribution?

A rule that assigns a number ⟨T, φ⟩ to each test function φ, linear in φ and bounded, on each closed bounded interval, by a combination of the probe and its derivatives.

Recall 2What does the Dirac mass at a read on probe φ?

The value φ(a) of the probe at point a. No ordinary function represents it through integration.

Recall 3How does a function f become a distribution?

If f is locally integrable, the reading is ∫ f φ dx. It is finite because the probe is bounded and 0 outside a bounded interval.

Recall 4When are two distributions equal?

When they give the same reading on every test function. Agreement on a few probes is not enough.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define the probe (test function) and the unit of the reading; write the reading for each part of the source (integral for a function, point value for a Dirac); check linearity by combining probes; bound the reading on a compact set using the probe and its derivatives; compare two distributions on every probe; never read a general distribution at a point.
