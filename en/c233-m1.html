Level 4 · C233·M1

Costs, constraints and admissibility

Your goalWrite down what is being minimized, then determine which decisions respect the available information, the state dynamics and the constraints of the model.

Before you start
c227-m5 : Ito-Tanaka · c059-m6 : Deterministic discrete-time Bellman · c167-m5 : Initial and boundary value problem · c184-m5 : Longstaff-Schwartz

✿01 See the idea

A small tank holds 2 L and can hold at most 4 L. You must choose the volume to pump before knowing the next withdrawal. This will be 1 L or 3 L, each with the same probability 1/2. The tank must neither overflow during filling nor run short of water during withdrawal. All data, probabilities and expenses in this scene are fictional.

Pumping more costs more and leaves more water to store after demand has been met. Pumping less may be forbidden if high demand would leave the tank short of water. So answer these questions in order: which decisions are feasible with what I know now, and then which costs the least on average?

A hard constraint rules out a decision. A cost assigns a preference among the remaining decisions. A terminal penalty can make a final reserve undesirable; it cannot make a negative volume or an overflow possible.

The first diagram separates the decision from future information. The second tracks the volumes for an allowed decision. The calculation will cover only this tank; the final steps will give the continuous-time formulation, without solving a Bellman or HJB equation.

The rule to remember

Specify the information and the dynamics; filter for admissible decisions; compare their expected costs. An acceptable average does not replace a constraint on paths.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A tank, a decision, and demand that is still unknown

The pump allows a volume u in { 0, 1, 2, 3 } L. Pumping is completed before withdrawal, with no leakage, evaporation or second purchase. Its price c is 2 €/L. The terminal cost is h times the square of the final reserve, with h = 1 €/L². No cost is discounted.

  1. State what information the decision can use

    x = 2 L is the known initial stock, C = 4 L the capacity, D the random demand in litres and u the volume pumped in litres. Before choosing, we know the distribution of D, but have no indication of its realization. There is no auxiliary random draw to choose u: the control here is therefore a fixed number.

    Ω, the set of outcomes, contains the low and high cases. ℙ is their probability distribution. ℱ denotes a family of events whose occurrence we can determine from the available information. Initially, this family does not distinguish the two outcomes; after withdrawal, it reveals D. A non-anticipative control uses only the information available at the time of the choice.

    Call the reserve after pumping, before demand, Z, and the final reserve Y. The same u appears on both branches:

    Z=x+u;Y=x+u−D

    These dynamics are fully defined for every control. A negative result indicates unmet demand; the model does not silently replace this result with 0 L.

  2. Build the admissible set before ranking

    The control must belong to the set allowed by the pump and satisfy 0 ≤ Z ≤ C as well as 0 ≤ Y ≤ C for every outcome of positive probability. No spillage is allowed. Filling imposes u ≤ C − x = 4 − 2 = 2 L.

    High demand imposes u ≥ 3 − 2 = 1 L. Low demand gives a weaker lower bound; it rules out no further control. Since the volume only increases during filling and then decreases during withdrawal, checking the extreme values covers every instant in this scene.

    𝒜={0,1,2,3}∩[1,2]={1,2} L

    𝒜 denotes the admissible controls; ∩ keeps the values common to both sets. With u = 0 L, Y is 1 L or -1 L. Its average is (1 + (-1))/2 = 0 L: this non-negative average nevertheless hides a shortage. The constraint applies to outcomes, rather than to this average.

  3. Write down the cost and check its units

    J(u) is the average expense in euros associated with u. The immediate cost cu is paid in every outcome. The terminal cost g(Y) = h times Y squared penalizes the remaining reserve. This fictional choice represents an increasing burden of storage; it does not penalize a shortage, since a shortage is already forbidden.

    J(u)=cu+𝔼[hY2]

    𝔼 is the expectation under ℙ: the sum of the costs in each outcome multiplied by their probabilities. Here both probabilities are p = 1/2 = 0.50, with no unit, and p + p = 1. The symbol g is the terminal cost function, c a price per litre, and h a price per litre squared. Thus cu is in euros; multiplying the square of Y by h also gives euros.

    J(u)=cu+h(x+u−1)2+(x+u−3)22

    The volumes in this last expression are in litres. To obtain a cost in euros, retain the units of c and h. The average is taken after squaring; the square of the average reserve would be a different criterion.

  4. Calculate the expense for the first control

    For u = 1 L, Z = 2 + 1 = 3 L. After low demand, Y = 3 − 1 = 2 L; after high demand, Y = 3 − 3 = 0 L. Each reserve satisfies the bounds.

    Square each final reserve, because this is the chosen function g: 2 × 2 = 4 L² and 0 × 0 = 0 L². The sum is 4 L², so the average is 4/2 = 2 L².

    The average terminal cost is 1 × 2 = 2 €. Pumping costs 2 × 1 = 2 €. We now add amounts in euros:

    J(1)=2+2=4 €

  5. Compare the second control and draw a conclusion within this model

    For u = 2 L, Z = 2 + 2 = 4 L. The final reserves are 3 L and 1 L. Their squares are 9 L² and 1 L²; the sum is 10 L² and the average 5 L².

    Pumping costs 2 × 2 = 4 €. The average terminal cost is 1 × 5 = 5 €. Therefore J(2) = 4 + 5 = 9 €. There is no other control in 𝒜: the minimum is 4 €, attained at u = 1 L.

    V denotes the value of the problem: the lowest cost that can be approached among admissible controls. In this finite case, that cost is attained. We have enumerated the allowed controls without using a dynamic programming recurrence.

    V=inf{J(u):u∈𝒜}=4 €

  6. Check using a different decomposition

    We can also add the expenses within each outcome before averaging them. For u = 1 L: [2 + 1 × 4 + 2 + 1 × 0]/2 = 4 €. For u = 2 L: [4 + 1 × 9 + 4 + 1 × 1]/2 = 9 €. This gives the same ranking without confusing a certain expense with a random reserve.

    The conclusion is conditional on the model: allowed discrete volumes, the stated distribution, late observation, no leakage and the chosen criterion. No real cost or measured benefit of a system has been established. A control conditional on demand would need to be analyzed with different initial information, as in the final exercise.

  7. Formulate the same requirements in continuous time

    For a continuous version of the same tank, t measures time in hours between 0 and a finite horizon T. The control αt would be a flow rate in L/h and Xtᵅ the volume in litres produced by a specified stochastic evolution equation. The superscript α indicates which control is used. The volume u in the preceding calculation was a total volume, rather than this flow rate.

    Fix a probability space (Ω, ℱ, ℙ) and a filtration (ℱt), meaning increasing families of observable events. Ω contains the possible outcomes, ℱ the events to which we assign a probability, and ℙ that probability. Under these requirements, α is progressively measurable: for every time s, its restriction to [0, s] × Ω is measurable with respect to ℬ([0, s]) ⊗ ℱs. ℬ denotes the Borel sets of time, and ⊗ the event family on the time–outcome product. This condition gives joint measurability compatible with the available information; simply saying “adapted”, meaning known at each time, is not generally enough to guarantee it.

    Let f(t, Xtᵅ, αt) be the running cost in €/h and g(Xᵅ_T) the terminal cost in euros. The integral adds the running costs over time, then the expectation averages them across outcomes:

    J(α)=𝔼[∫0Tf(t,Xtα,αt)dt+g(XTα)]

    The symbol d in dt indicates integration with respect to duration. The precise choice of f, g and the evolution equation is part of the data; this formula does not deduce them from the word “control” alone. In the discrete calculation, cu plays the role of the accumulated running cost and g(y) = h times y squared that of the terminal cost.

  8. Specify the admissible class and its limits

    In the strong formulation adopted here, the probability space and the noises are fixed in advance. We call a control admissible if it is progressively measurable, takes values in the set U of allowed flow rates almost everywhere with respect to the product of time measure and probability, and its state equation has a unique strong solution over the entire horizon. “Strong” means constructed using the noises already fixed; “unique” means that two solutions with the same noises and the same control coincide almost surely. These properties must be established for the chosen dynamics; we do not assume that the definition of the cost proves them.

    The solution must also remain in K = [0, C], the set of allowed volumes. We require a single property of the entire path: ℙ(Xtᵅ ∈ K for all t ∈ [0, T]) = 1. “Almost surely” allows an exceptional set of outcomes of probability 0; in our finite model, every outcome has positive probability, so all must be checked. A constraint in expectation or a tolerance for shortages would impose different requirements.

    Here we require integrable costs: 𝔼[∫ |f(t, Xtᵅ, αt)| dt + |g(Xᵅ_T)|] is finite, with the integral over [0, T]. The bars | | indicate absolute value. This assumption guarantees a well-defined, finite cost for every control in our class. In the finite example, all cost expressions are finite; only the constraints and the information rule out controls.

    The set 𝒜 consists exactly of the controls satisfying these conditions, and V = inf { J(α) : α ∈ 𝒜 }. The infimum is the greatest lower bound: writing it does not promise that a control attains it. Individually finite costs do not guarantee a common finite lower bound for all controls either. If 𝒜 is empty, the problem is infeasible and we set V = +∞. A finite penalty added to the cost replaces none of these requirements. The next unit will prepare for HJB starting from a problem specified in this way.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The average reserve remains positive or 0, so the tank never runs short of water.”

    For the control that pumps nothing, the average is 0 L but the high-demand outcome gives -1 L. Fix the check by examining every outcome of positive probability, as well as the reserve before withdrawal.

    Revisit the construction of the admissible set

  • “I choose a small amount if demand will be low and more if it will be high.”

    This statement uses information that is not yet available at the time of pumping. Write down when D is revealed. As long as this is after the choice, u must be identical on both branches; the final exercise explicitly changes this timing.

    Reread the split after the decision

  • “A large penalty necessarily makes the cheapest solution admissible.”

    A finite penalty changes the ranking of a set of controls. It guarantees neither compliance with a capacity limit nor non-anticipation. First build 𝒜; then minimize J over 𝒜. In continuous time, also add the appropriate definition of the solution and integrability.

Exercise 1 · Identifying constraints

Final states are not enough

In the original model, someone proposes u = 3 L. Calculate the reserve before withdrawal and the final reserves. Does the pump allow this control? Does the tank allow it? Do not evaluate its cost before deciding whether it is admissible.

Compare with the solution

The control 3 L belongs to the set allowed by the pump, whose maximum is 3 L. But Z = 2 + 3 = 5 L exceeds C = 4 L before any demand.

The final reserves would be 5 − 1 = 4 L and 5 − 3 = 2 L, both within the bounds. They do not undo the earlier overflow. The control is excluded from 𝒜; a favorable cost could not bring it back in.

Exercise 3 · Transfer to a different information setting

Demand is revealed before pumping

Return to the original price c = 2 €/L. An announcement that is now exact reveals D before pumping. The proposed rule is u = 0 L if D = 1 L, and u = 1 L if D = 3 L. Check the constraints, calculate the expected cost and show that this rule is optimal under this new timing. Why could it not be used in the original problem?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In each outcome, find the smallest pumping volume that meets demand, then examine how the cost changes if more is pumped.

Hint 2

The final reserve is 1 L in the low-demand outcome and 0 L in the high-demand outcome. The timing of information is part of the data.

Compare with the solution

In the low-demand outcome, u = 0 L gives a reserve before demand of 2 L and leaves 1 L. Pumping costs nothing and the terminal cost is 1 × 1 × 1 = 1 €. In the high-demand outcome, u = 1 L gives 3 L before demand and 0 L after it; the total expense is 2 €.

Each branch respects the pump limit and capacity. The expected cost is (1 + 2)/2 = 3/2 € = 3/2 € = 1.50 €. In the low-demand outcome, any larger control increases both the pumping expense and a final reserve that is already non-negative. In the high-demand outcome, 1 L is the smallest control that meets demand; beyond it, pumping and final reserve both increase. Since c and h are positive, the proposed rule minimizes the costs separately in both outcomes and therefore their expectation.

The rule is admissible with the exact initial announcement. In the original problem, the two outcomes were indistinguishable at the time of the choice: this rule anticipated D. Its cost below 4 € does not invalidate the first minimum, because the set of admissible rules has changed. We cannot freely interchange “choosing before knowing” and “choosing in each outcome”.

Check your reasoning

  • You check the reserves before and after withdrawal in each branch.
  • You obtain 1.50 € with the original price.
  • You justify optimality branch by branch, going beyond calculating the cost alone.
  • You explain that the same control formula is anticipative or non-anticipative depending on the available information.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the difference between a terminal penalty and a hard constraint?

The penalty contributes to the ranking of controls by cost. The hard constraint excludes controls that violate it; a finite penalty does not guarantee compliance.

Recall 2Why must the control in the original model be the same on both branches?

Demand is known only after pumping and no other signal distinguishes the outcomes at the time of the choice. A control cannot use this future that has not yet been observed.

Recall 3Which checks define an admissible control in continuous time here?

Information and progressive measurability; allowed control values; a unique strong solution over the horizon; almost-sure path constraints; absolute integrability of the cost. These requirements must be specified for each model.

Recall 4How can you reconstruct the cost of the selected control without rereading the formula?

Pumping 1 L costs 2 €. The remaining volumes 2 L and 0 L give an average terminal cost of 2 €. The total is 4 €. Average the squares of the remaining volumes, then add the certain expense.

Recall 5Does writing V as an infimum promise an optimal control?

No. The infimum is the greatest lower bound; it may not be attained. In our non-empty finite set, comparing all controls shows that it is attained. An empty admissible set indicates an infeasible problem.

Without support, reconstruct the timing for the tank, the constraints before and after demand, the admissible set and the two components of the cost.
