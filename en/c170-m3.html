Level 3 · C170·M3

Quadratic variation

Your goalCalculate a sum of squared increments, then justify its Brownian limit while distinguishing a finite record from a probabilistic result.

Before you start
c169-m5 : optional stopping under assumptions · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c170-m1 : construction and properties · c170-m2 : increments

✿01 See the idea

A camera locates a small particle on an axis marked in millimetres. It sees the particle start at the origin, move to the right, return, move to the left and return again. At the end, its total displacement is 0. Yet the observations have retained a record of its movements.

To retain this record without letting the signs cancel, we can square each displacement between observations, then add the squares. This sum depends on the times at which we look. Quadratic variation will be a limit of such sums as the intervals become small; it is not simply the name of a sum calculated once.

The record shown is entirely fictitious: at times 0; 0.25; 0.50; 0.75; 1 s, the positions are 0; 0.50; 0; -0.50; 0 mm. These numbers chosen on paper are neither physical readings nor the output of an actual random draw.

We will then use the Brownian model to understand why a sum of many small squares can retain a positive limit. We must distinguish what this record shows from what this model guarantees.

The rule to remember

Take the differences between successive positions, square each difference, and add the squares; to speak of Brownian quadratic variation, then specify the model, the refinement and the sense of the limit.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the particle record to the Brownian limit

Keep this record over T = 1 s. For the probabilistic calculation, assume separately a model Xₜ = σBₜ, with σ = 1 mm/√s. We will calculate the observed sum, then its mean and dispersion under this model.

  1. State the model and its limitations

    t is time, in seconds, and Xt is position, in millimetres. B is a standard Brownian motion starting at 0: its paths are continuous and its increments over disjoint intervals are independent, Gaussian and centred. Under this physical convention, Bt is in √s and the variance of Bt − Bs is t − s, in seconds, for s ≤ t. Here the symbol s denotes an earlier time; after a numerical value, s abbreviates seconds.

    σ, pronounced sigma, is a constant nonnegative coefficient, in mm/√s. It sets the amplitude of the movement; its square is in mm²/s. For an interval of duration h, the increment of X has mean 0 and a variance obtained by multiplying this square by h. Here the model includes neither drift nor camera error. The observation times are set in advance and do not depend on the path. These assumptions are necessary for the proof that follows.

  2. Define what we are adding

    π, pronounced pi, denotes a deterministic partition of the interval [0, T]: an increasing list t0, …, tn, with t0 = 0 and tn = T. n is the number of intervals, with no unit; i is the interval index. Set hᵢ = tᵢ − tᵢ-1, in seconds, and ΔᵢX = X at the end of this interval minus X at its start, in millimetres. Δ means a difference.

    Sπ=∑i=1nΔXi=XT−X0
    Qπ=∑i=1n(ΔXi)2

    The symbol ∑ asks us to add over the indicated intervals. Sπ is a signed sum, in mm, that retains only the endpoints. Qπ is a sum of squares, in mm², that also depends on the intermediate observations. Its definition involves no division by n.

  3. Calculate the increments before their squares

    Our fine partition has n = 4 intervals of h = T/n = 1/4 = 1/4 s, or 0.25 s. The positions are 0; 0.50; 0; -0.50; 0 mm.

    Each operation subtracts the previous position: 0.50 − 0 = 0.50 mm; 0 − 0.50 = -0.50 mm; -0.50 − 0 = -0.50 mm; 0 − (-0.50) = 0.50 mm.

    The signed sum is 0.50 + (-0.50) + (-0.50) + 0.50 = 0 mm. Independent check: final position minus initial position = 0 − 0 = 0 mm. The returns explain the cancellation.

  4. Add the squares without a hidden average

    The square of each increment is 1/4 mm², or 0.25 mm²: a negative sign disappears because it is multiplied by itself. Over the first half, the sum is 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.50 mm²; over the second, 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.50 mm².

    Qfin=0.50+0.50=1 mm²

    Check using the equal magnitudes: 4 × 0.25 = 1 mm². In contrast, the square of Sπ is 0 mm². Dividing Qπ by n would give 0.25 mm²: that would be a mean of squared increments, a different quantity.

  5. Change the observations while keeping the endpoints

    Keep only the times 0; 0.50; 1 s. The corresponding positions are all 0 mm. The grouped increments are 0 mm and 0 mm; Qgrossier = 0 + 0 = 0 mm². The fine record gave 1 mm². A finite sum therefore depends on the partition.

    To understand the difference, call the two consecutive displacements being grouped a and b, each in mm. The larger displacement is a + b. The following identity shows the cross term, in mm²:

    (a+b)2=a2+b2+2ab

    Here the displacements being grouped have opposite signs: the cross term cancels the squares. For displacements with the same sign, it would be positive and refinement would instead decrease the sum. Sums of squares are therefore not monotone under refinement.

  6. Move from fictitious data to the moments of the model

    E denotes expectation, meaning the mean over the paths of the model, and Var denotes its variance. For an increment Y = ΔᵢX over a duration hᵢ, the 0 mean gives its moment of order 2. The Gaussian distribution also gives its moment of order 4:

    E[Y2]=σ2hi;E[Y4]=3(σ2hi)2

    Where does the coefficient 3 come from? Take a standard normal variable Z, with mean 0 and variance 1, with no unit. Its density φ satisfies φ′(z) = −zφ(z), where z ranges over the real values of Z and the prime indicates a derivative. Integration by parts gives the following identity; the integrals are over the whole real line and the boundary term is 0 because of Gaussian decay:

    ∫z4φ(z)dz=3∫z2φ(z)dz=3

    The last equality comes from the moment of order 2 of Z, which equals 1. For positive σ, writing Y = σ√hᵢ Z transfers this identity; for 0 σ, all increments are 0. The variance of the square of Y is obtained by subtracting the square of its second moment from its moment of order 4.

    In our model, the second moment is 1 × 1/4 = 1/4 mm²; the moment of order 4 is 3 × 1/16 = 3/16 mm⁴. The variance of the square is 3/16 − 1/16 = 1/8 mm⁴. These are the moments of a random increment, not averages calculated from our small record.

  7. Calculate the mean and variance of the sum

    Adding the expectations amounts to multiplying the total duration by the square of σ:

    E[Qπ]=σ2∑i=1nhi=σ2T

    The squared increments over disjoint intervals remain independent, so their variances add. The Gaussian moment gives the factor 2 in the following formula. For a uniform partition, all hᵢ equal T/n, giving:

    Var(Qπ)=2σ4∑i=1nhi2=2σ4T2n

    Numerically, the mean is 1 × 1 = 1 mm². The sum of the squared durations is 4 × 1/16 = 1/4 s². The variance of Qπ is therefore 2 × 1/4 = 1/2 mm⁴, or 0.50 mm⁴.

    Check using the increments: 4 × 1/8 = 1/2 mm⁴. Our Qfin = 1 mm² equals the expectation exactly because of how the fictitious data were chosen. The strictly positive variance reminds us that this equality is not imposed on every finite record.

  8. Specify the limit and what it proves

    The mesh |π| is the largest duration hᵢ, in seconds. Since each duration is at most the mesh, the sum of the squared durations is at most the mesh multiplied by T. The error between Qπ and its mean has mean 0, so its mean square is the variance just calculated. For a sequence of deterministic partitions of [0, T] whose mesh tends to 0:

    E[(Qπ−σ2T)2]≤2σ4T|π|→0

    This is convergence in mean square: the expectation of the squared error tends to 0. It implies convergence in probability: for any fixed positive tolerance, the probability of a deviation exceeding it tends to 0. The result does not depend on a uniform step size, but this proof still assumes deterministic partitions.

    [X]T=σ2T=1 mm² pour notre modèle

    [X]t denotes the quadratic variation of the process X up to time t: in this Brownian setting, it equals t times the square of σ. For standard Brownian motion, [B]t = t, in seconds under our unit convention. This proof in mean square does not establish simultaneous convergence on every path and for all partitions chosen after observation. Nor does it validate the particle model from the record alone.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the steps. Why does the final displacement cancel while the sum of squares stays positive? Then reconstruct the expectation and variance of this sum, and explain which step justifies the word limit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I square the total displacement and get the same result.”

    Calculate the differences first. In the record, the square of their sum is 0 mm² whereas the sum of their squares is 1 mm². Grouping before squaring introduces cross terms that can cancel the observed movement.

    Revisit the increments and the two sums

  • “For quadratic variation, I divide by the number of frames.”

    The definition adds the squared increments without dividing. Here, dividing by the number of intervals would give 0.25 mm² instead of 1 mm². Also check that you are using increments, not positions centred by their mean.

    Reread the definition of Q and its unit

  • “A mean equal to T times the square of σ is enough to prove the limit.”

    A constant mean alone does not constrain the fluctuations. We must also control the variance: it tends to 0 as the mesh of the deterministic partitions tends to 0. The fictitious record and a single equality do not replace this argument.

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Stop at the first return

For the same particle, keep only the observations at times 0; 0.25; 0.50 s. Calculate the increments, their signed sum and their sum of squares. Explain why their units differ.

Read the positions 0; 0.50; 0 mm. Subtract the previous position each time.

Hint 2

Square each increment separately before adding. The sign of the return disappears.

Compare with the solution

The increments are 0.50 − 0 = 0.50 mm and 0 − 0.50 = -0.50 mm. Their sum is 0 mm, as confirmed by the difference between the final and initial positions.

The sum of the squares is 0.25 + 0.25 = 0.50 mm². A sum of displacements remains in mm; each square multiplies two lengths, so it is in mm². Returning does not reset this sum to 0.

Check your reasoning

  • The increments have the correct signs and equal 0.50 mm and -0.50 mm.
  • The signed sum is 0 mm and the sum of the squares is 0.50 mm².
  • You explain the squared unit without confusing it with a displacement.

Exercise 2 · Comparison and objection

The camera misses the excursions

Over the full duration T = 1 s, compare the partitions at times 0; 0.50; 1 s and at times 0; 0.25; 0.50; 0.75; 1 s. Someone claims: “Adding observations always increases Q.” Give the sums for our record, then assess the general claim using the grouping identity.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In the coarse record, only the positions at the origin are retained.

Hint 2

Whether the square of the larger increment a + b exceeds the sum of the separate squares depends on the sign of ab.

Compare with the solution

The coarse partition gives increments 0 and 0 mm, so Qgrossier = 0 mm². The fine partition gives Qfin = 1 mm². Including the excursions increases the sum here.

This does not prove a law of increase. For nonzero increments a and b with the same sign, 2ab is positive: the square of a + b exceeds the sum of their squares. Adding the intermediate point would then decrease Q. With opposite signs, as in our record, the reverse holds. Brownian convergence is justified by the moments and the mesh; it does not require these sums to be monotone.

Check your reasoning

  • You associate 0 mm² and 1 mm² with the correct observations.
  • You use the sign of the term 2ab to refute the word always.
  • You distinguish a finite comparison from a proof of convergence.

Exercise 3 · Transfer to the model

Predict a finer record without inventing its positions

The same camera is programmed for 8 equal intervals over T = 1 s, with σ = 1 mm/√s. No new position is provided. Calculate the step size, E[Q] and Var(Q) under the Brownian model. Can we state the exact value of the next Q? Justify the role of variance in the limit.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The step size is T/n. Reuse the mean and variance formulas for a uniform partition, with the new number of intervals.

Hint 2

Comparing a mean and a dispersion does not calculate a realization. Also give the unit of Var(Q) the correct power.

Compare with the solution

The step size is 1/8 = 1/8 s, or 0.125 s. The mean remains 1 × 1 = 1 mm². The sum of the squared step sizes is 1/8 s², giving Var(Q) = 2 × 1/8 = 1/4 mm⁴, or 0.25 mm⁴.

The variance was 0.50 mm⁴ for 4 intervals; it is divided by 2 when the number of intervals is doubled. Direct check: 2 × 1 × 1 × 1 / 8 = 1/4 mm⁴.

We do not know the next Q. Its mean is 1 mm², but its variance remains positive at this finite step size. Over a fixed duration, a deterministic mesh tending to 0 forces the mean squared error towards 0; this justification concerns the random paths of the model, not a sequence of positions we might have invented.

Check your reasoning

  • You find 0.125 s, 1 mm² and 0.25 mm⁴, with all three units.
  • You check the division of the variance by 2 without changing T or σ.
  • You refuse to state an exact realization from its expectation alone.
  • You connect the limit to the decrease in mean squared error.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can we reconstruct Q on a partition without confusing it with the square of the final displacement?

Subtract successive positions, square each increment, then add without dividing by the number of intervals. The sum is in mm²; squaring the sum adds cross terms.

Recall 2Why is the expectation of Q equal to σ²T for Xₜ = σBₜ?

The expectation of the square of each centred increment is the product of the square of σ and its duration h. The durations sum to T, so E[Q] is the product of the square of σ and T. This does not make every finite sum equal to its mean.

Recall 3What information is missing if we only know the mean of Q?

Control of the fluctuations. For deterministic partitions, the variance is 2 times σ to the power 4 times the sum of the squared durations. This sum is at most T times the mesh. As the mesh tends to 0, the mean squared error tends to 0: Q converges to T times the square of σ in mean square, and therefore in probability.

Recall 4Does refining the partition always increase the sum of squares?

No. Grouping two increments a and b adds the term 2ab to the separate squares. Its sign depends on the displacements. The proof of the Brownian limit uses the mesh and the moments without assuming monotonicity.

Recall 5Does the fictitious record giving Q = 1 mm² establish Brownian quadratic variation?

No. It establishes only a sum over the chosen observations. Quadratic variation, equal to t times the square of σ, depends on the Brownian model and an explicitly stated passage to the limit. Connecting the points with segments does not reveal the movement between observations.

Under the stated Brownian model, reconstruct the mean, the product of the square of σ and T, then the variance and its bound using the mesh. Finish by saying what a finite sum, a mean and a limit each allow us to assert. Reading guide · distinguish dispersion from quadratic accumulation

What you understand
An empirical variance describes the dispersion of values around their mean; Q adds the squares of successive increments. Their visual resemblance does not make them interchangeable.
