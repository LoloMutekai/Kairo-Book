Level 2 · C058·M3

Calculations with Fubini's theorem

Your goalCalculate the mass of the same plate in both orders of integration, reconstructing the bounds and justifying the change of order.

Before you start
c045-m5 : introductory quadrature · c057-m5 : Hessian · c058-m1 : double integrals · c058-m2 : triple integrals

✿01 See the idea

A thin plate has the shape of a right triangle. Its material becomes denser as you move upwards. To find its mass, imagine narrow strips: calculate what one strip contributes, then add the strips until you have covered the whole plate.

You can sweep from left to right using vertical strips or from bottom to top using horizontal strips. The plate and its density stay the same. However, the endpoints of each strip change: the first diagram starts at the bottom edge, the second at the diagonal.

Fubini's theorem allows this calculation by successive integrations under the assumptions specified in the example. Changing the order means grouping the contributions of the same small pieces of surface differently. Leaving out a piece or adding another one changes the problem.

The geometry and all the values in this scene are fictional. We calculate exactly the mass predicted by this model; no real plate has been weighed. The exercises cut up this same plate without changing its density law.

The rule to remember

Before changing the order of the integrals, keep the same domain, redraw a cross section in the new order, then write its bounds. The inner integral treats the other variable as a fixed parameter.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

One mass, two ways to divide the plate

The plate occupies D: 0 ≤ y ≤ x ≤ L, with L = 2 m. The coordinates x and y are lengths in metres. Its surface density is ρ(x, y) = c × y, with c = 3 kg/m³. Let us calculate its total mass M.

  1. Relate density to mass and check the assumptions

    ρ, the Greek letter rho, denotes mass per unit area, in kg/m². The coefficient c is a density gradient, in kg/m³: multiplying c by y does give kg/m². On a small rectangle with sides dx and dy, in metres, the mass contribution is represented by ρ × dx × dy, in kilograms. The double integral adds these contributions in the limit.

    M=∬Dρ(x,y)dxdy

    Fubini's theorem allows the change of order if the integral of |ρ| is finite; |ρ| denotes the absolute value of the density. In our elementary setting, a continuous function on this closed and bounded triangle satisfies this condition. Here ρ is continuous, nonnegative and bounded by cL = 6 kg/m². The area of D is L squared / 2 = 2 m²: the integral of |ρ| is therefore finite, at most 12 kg. We stay on this bounded domain; no rule for changing the order of improper integrals is assumed.

  2. Write the bounds before calculating

    For a vertical cross section, x is fixed: y starts at 0 and stops at x. Then x ranges from 0 to L. For a horizontal cross section, y is fixed: x starts at y and stops at L. Then y ranges from 0 to L. These descriptions are equivalent to 0 ≤ y ≤ x ≤ L.

    M=∫0L(∫0xcydy)dx=∫0L(∫yLcydx)dy

    The differential closest to the integrand indicates the variable of the inner integral: dy in the first calculation, dx in the second. The parentheses enclose the result to be integrated next. The density c × y stays the same; only the way we move through D changes.

  3. At fixed x, integrate first with respect to y

    Call the result of the inner integral g(x). The constant c and the parameter x remain fixed while y varies. An antiderivative of c × y with respect to y is c × y squared / 2, since differentiating it gives c × y again.

    g(x)=∫0xcydy=[cy22]0x=cx22

    The square brackets mean: evaluate the antiderivative at the upper bound, then subtract its value at the lower bound. The term at y = 0 vanishes. The coefficient c/2 is 3/2 kg/m³, or 1.50 kg/m³. g(x) is expressed in kg/m: we still need to multiply by a width dx and add the strips. This is not yet the mass of the whole plate.

  4. Add the vertical strips

    Let us integrate g(x) from 0 to L. An antiderivative of x squared is x cubed / 3. The denominator becomes 2 × 3 = 6; c remains constant.

    M=∫0Lcx22dx=cL36

    L squared is 4 m² and L cubed is 8 m³. The coefficient c/6 is 1/2 kg/m³. Thus M = (3/6) × 8 = 4 kg. Multiplying the kg/m by the metres of dx does give a mass in kilograms.

  5. Return to the domain to integrate first with respect to x

    Call the result of the horizontal cross section h(y). This time y is fixed: c × y is constant with respect to x. Its antiderivative with respect to x is c × y × x. Between x = y and x = L, this gives c × y × (L − y).

    h(y)=∫yLcydx=cy(L−y)

    The horizontal length L − y depends on y. At height y = 1 m, it is 2 − 1 = 1 m, and the density is 3 kg/m². Therefore h(1 m) = 3 kg/m. Again, this is a mass per unit length along the sweep, to be integrated next with respect to y.

  6. Add the horizontal strips

    Expanding h(y) gives cLy − c × y squared. Since c and L are constant, an antiderivative is cL × y squared / 2 − c × y cubed / 3. We evaluate it at L and at 0.

    M=∫0Lcy(L−y)dy=cL32−cL33

    The first term is (3/2) × 8 = 12 kg. For the second, c/3 = 1 kg/m³, giving (3/3) × 8 = 8 kg. The difference gives 12 − 8 = 4 kg, as in the other order.

  7. Check the result and its meaning

    The two calculations differ by 0 kg. Algebraically, 1/2 − 1/3 = 1/6, which explains why the expressions are equal. The result 4 kg is positive and stays below the bound 12 kg obtained by using the maximum density everywhere on D.

    This check combines the same geometric bounds, the same final unit and agreement between the calculations. It does not replace the assumptions of Fubini's theorem. A mass that happened to be correct would not justify an incorrectly described domain; a modelled mass is not a real measurement.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the formulas. Why does a horizontal cross section start at x = y? Why is c × y constant in an integral with respect to x, but not in an integral with respect to y? Reconstruct both calculations, naming the unit of the inner result.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “To reverse the order, I keep the bounds and just swap dx and dy.”

    The inner bounds depend on the variable held fixed. In D, a horizontal cross section runs from x = y to x = L. Keeping an upper bound x while integrating with respect to x does not describe this cross section. First rewrite 0 ≤ y ≤ x ≤ L and look at the horizontal diagram again.

    Find the start and end of the horizontal cross section again

  • “Since c × y contains y, its antiderivative is always c × y squared / 2.”

    Read the variable of integration. With respect to y, this antiderivative is suitable. With respect to x, y is fixed: the antiderivative is c × y × x. After the inner integral, no free inner variable should remain.

    Compare the calculations of g(x) and h(y)

  • “Fubini's theorem allows any integrals to be exchanged as long as we obtain a number.”

    Check a sufficient assumption before changing the order. Here continuity, the bounded triangle and the bound on the density ensure that the absolute integral is finite. A calculation on another domain, with a singularity or an improper integral, requires a new examination.

    Review the assumption and the mass bound

Exercise 1 · Read an inner integral

One cross section is not the whole plate

On the original plate, fix x = a, with a = L/2 = 1 m. Calculate g(a), the result of integration with respect to y. Give its unit and state what is still needed to obtain a mass.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At x = a, the bounds for y are 0 and a; a is fixed during this integration.

Hint 2

Integrate c × y, then substitute each bound for y. The differential dy contributes a unit of length.

Compare with the solution

g(a) = c × a squared / 2. Here a squared is 1 m²: g(a) = (3/2) × 1 = 3/2 kg/m, or 1.50 kg/m. The lower bound gives a term equal to 0.

The integral with respect to y has turned the density in kg/m² into a quantity in kg/m. For a narrow strip of width dx, the mass contribution is represented by g(x) × dx. For a region of finite width, we must integrate g(x) with respect to x over that region; multiplying by the whole width would wrongly assume that g is constant.

Check your reasoning

  • Write the bounds 0 and a, then evaluate an antiderivative.
  • Obtain 1.50 kg/m and explain why this is not yet a mass.

Exercise 2 · Choose a suitable order

Keep only the bottom of the plate

The same plate is cut at height a = 1 m. We keep only its points such that y ≤ a. Write an integral with dx on the inside, then calculate the mass kept. The density remains ρ(x, y) = c × y.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At each y between 0 and a, the strip still starts at x = y and ends at x = L.

Hint 2

Use h(y) = c × y × (L − y), then evaluate its antiderivative between 0 and a.

Compare with the solution

The domain kept is 0 ≤ y ≤ a and y ≤ x ≤ L. The horizontal order requires no further subdivision.

∫0a(∫yLcydx)dy=cLa22−ca33

The coefficient cL/2 is 3 kg/m²; a squared is 1 m². The positive term is therefore 3 kg. Since a cubed is 1 m³, the term to subtract is 1 kg. We keep 3 − 1 = 2 kg.

Check: this domain is contained in D and the density is nonnegative. Its mass 2 kg is therefore between 0 and 4 kg. Replacing only L by a would also have removed the right part and would have kept a different triangle.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep x from y to L and let y range from 0 to a.
  • Justify both terms of the antiderivative and obtain 2 kg.
  • Check that the domain is contained in the original domain and check the final unit.

Exercise 3 · Apply the method by subdividing the domain

A cut may require two integrals

This time, start again from the whole plate and keep its right part x ≥ a, with a = 1 m. Describe this part in both orders, calculate its mass in the simpler order, then check with the other. Justify the point where the bounds change.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The vertical strips run from y = 0 to y = x, with x from a to L.

Hint 2

For a horizontal strip, compare the constraints x ≥ a and x ≥ y. The stronger constraint changes as y passes through a.

Hint 3

From 0 to a, the left edge is x = a; from a to L, it is x = y. Add the masses of these two regions.

Compare with the solution

With vertical strips, a ≤ x ≤ L and 0 ≤ y ≤ x. We integrate g(x) = c × x squared / 2 between a and L. The mass is c × (L cubed − a cubed) / 6. The difference of the cubes is 8 − 1 = 7 m³: the mass is (3/6) × 7 = 7/2 kg, or 3.50 kg.

In the other order, all the constraints must still hold. For 0 ≤ y ≤ a, x runs from a to L. For a ≤ y ≤ L, x runs from y to L. The line y = a separates the regions; counting it as a shared boundary does not change their mass.

∫0a(∫aLcydx)dy+∫aL(∫yLcydx)dy

The first region is a rectangle of width L − a = 1 m. Its mass is (L − a) × c × a squared / 2 = 1 × 3/2 = 3/2 kg. The second is the part above y = a: total mass minus bottom mass, giving 4 − 2 = 2 kg. The total 3/2 + 2 = 7/2 kg confirms the first calculation.

Another check: the small triangle removed has mass c × a cubed / 6 = 1/2 kg. Adding it to the 7/2 kg kept gives the 4 kg of the whole plate again.

Check your reasoning

  • Write all the geometric constraints before choosing the bounds.
  • Split the horizontal order into y ≤ a and y ≥ a, with the correct left edge.
  • Obtain 3.50 kg in both orders and check the mass removed.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What sufficient assumption justifies Fubini's theorem on our plate?

The integral of |ρ| must be finite. Here ρ is continuous and bounded on a closed and bounded triangle; its absolute value therefore has a finite integral. This condition must be reexamined when an improper integral is involved or near a singularity.

Recall 2How do we go from 0 ≤ y ≤ x ≤ L to the horizontal bounds?

Fix y. The left edge satisfies x = y and the right edge x = L; y ranges from 0 to L. We therefore integrate with respect to x from y to L, then with respect to y from 0 to L.

Recall 3Why does the antiderivative of c × y change with the variable of integration?

With respect to y, the antiderivative is c × y squared / 2. With respect to x, c and y are fixed: the antiderivative is c × y × x. The differential indicates which variable varies.

Recall 4What does the result of the inner integral mean, and what check does the second integration provide?

Integrating a density in kg/m² with respect to a length gives a quantity in kg/m. The second integration with respect to a length gives the mass in kg. Agreement between the two orders complements the check of the bounds and assumptions.

Recall 5Why does the part x ≥ a require subdivision in the horizontal order?

Its left edge must satisfy both x ≥ a and x ≥ y. It is x = a for y ≤ a, then x = y for y ≥ a. We therefore split the outer integral at y = a, keeping the same points of the plate.
