Level 2 · C145·M3

Fixed effects

Your goalRemove stable differences in levels between workshops, calculate a slope from their within-workshop changes, and explain what is missing for a causal interpretation.

Before you start
c074-m5 : identification assumptions · c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c101-m5 : rolling-origin forecasting · c145-m1 : endogeneity · c145-m2 : instrumental variables

✿01 See the idea

2 workshops, A and B, manufacture the same parts. Each week, we record the time spent setting up the machine and the number of parts produced that meet specifications. B has equipment that may give it a stable advantage. Comparing A directly with B risks confusing this advantage with the relationship between setup and production.

Instead, look at how each workshop changes relative to itself. In A, setup increases from 4 to 6 h/week and production from 12 to 16 parts/week. In B, they increase from 1 to 3 h/week and from 22 to 26 parts/week. The levels differ, but the movements go in the same direction.

Observing the same workshops at several dates gives us a panel. A workshop fixed effect here represents a contribution to the production level that is specific to that workshop and constant throughout the observation period. To remove it, we subtract the workshop mean from each observation. We center both setup and production in this way.

All the data are fictional, chosen for a pencil-and-paper calculation with 2 workshops and 2 weeks. We study a model with a fixed effect for each workshop and a common slope. We do not assume that setup time was assigned at random.

The rule to remember

Comparing each workshop's deviations from its own mean removes a stable additive shift. This preserves within-workshop changes and leaves open the question of factors that change along with them.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A common slope, levels specific to each workshop

Use exactly the observations from the first diagram again. Each row covers a full week; parts and hours are counted in the same way in both workshops. We seek the slope of the within-workshop changes, then the fitted levels for A and B.

  1. Set out the model and state its assumptions

    i denotes the workshop, A or B; t denotes the week, start or end. These indices have no units. xᵢt is setup time in h/week; yᵢt is production in parts/week. The model is additive:

    yit=αi+βxit+uit

    αᵢ is the level specific to the workshop, in parts/week, constant throughout the observation period. It may be related to that workshop's usual setup times. β is the common slope, in parts per hour of setup. uᵢt collects the unmodeled factors that vary between observations, in parts/week. An equipment advantage enters αᵢ only if its contribution is indeed stable and additive; centering does not remove a difference in slopes between machines.

    The least-squares calculation does not require us to assume that uᵢt follows a normal distribution. To justify the slope being unbiased under this model, a sufficient assumption is strict exogeneity: the theoretical mean of uᵢt, conditional on αᵢ and the workshop's entire sequence of x values, is 0. In other words, knowing past, present, and future hours must provide no information about these residual factors. This assumption also concerns later responses to a shock.

    A causal interpretation requires justification of the model and this assumption, particularly in the face of orders that could increase both setup and production at the same time. We will not infer them from our fictional fit. No common week effect is added in this unit.

  2. Calculate a mean for each workshop

    T is the number of weeks observed per workshop, here 2, with no unit. A bar above a letter denotes its mean over these weeks. The symbol Σ means “add up,” here over t from 1 to T. Thus:

    x¯i=1TΣtxit

    For A: x̄ᴀ = (4 + 6)/2 = 10/2 = 5 h/week; ȳᴀ = (12 + 16)/2 = 28/2 = 14 parts/week.

    For B: x̄ʙ = (1 + 3)/2 = 4/2 = 2 h/week; ȳʙ = (22 + 26)/2 = 48/2 = 24 parts/week. The mean pooling all workshops would not remove their individual levels.

  3. See exactly what centering eliminates

    The mean of the model within the workshop is ȳᵢ = αᵢ + βx̄ᵢ + ūᵢ, where ūᵢ is the mean of the uᵢt values. The same αᵢ appears at each date, so its mean is itself. Subtract this equality from the model for each observation.

    yit−y¯i=β(xit−x¯i)+(uit−u¯i)

    The term αᵢ − αᵢ equals 0 parts/week, even if αᵢ is related to x. A tilde now denotes a deviation from the workshop mean: x̃ᵢt = xᵢt − x̄ᵢ and ỹᵢt = yᵢt − ȳᵢ. These deviations retain the units of the original variables. The varying factor uᵢt − ūᵢ remains: centering does not automatically make it independent of x̃ᵢt.

  4. Center all observations and check the signs

    A, start: x̃ = 4 − 5 = -1 h/week; ỹ = 12 − 14 = -2 parts/week. A, end: x̃ = 6 − 5 = 1 h/week; ỹ = 16 − 14 = 2 parts/week.

    B, start: x̃ = 1 − 2 = -1 h/week; ỹ = 22 − 24 = -2 parts/week. B, end: x̃ = 3 − 2 = 1 h/week; ỹ = 26 − 24 = 2 parts/week. In these sentences, the indices of x̃ and ỹ are omitted for readability.

    Check: within each workshop, the setup deviations add up to 0 h/week and the production deviations to 0 parts/week. For example, -1 + 1 = 0 and -2 + 2 = 0. A negative deviation means “below this workshop's mean,” never a negative setup time.

  5. Choose a slope using only within-workshop variation

    We fit a line through the origin to the centered observations. For a candidate slope b, in parts/hour, we minimize the sum of squares of ỹᵢt − b x̃ᵢt. Centering y alone would leave x representing levels between workshops; both x and y must be transformed.

    Let Sxy be the sum of products x̃ᵢtỹᵢt and Sxx the sum of squared x̃ᵢt values, over all observations. Sxy is in h·parts/(week·week) and Sxx in h·h/(week·week). Expanding the squares, the part that depends on b is Sxx × b × b − 2 × b × Sxy: a parabola whose minimum occurs at Sxy/Sxx if Sxx is strictly positive.

    β^=Σi,tx~ity~itΣi,tx~it2=SxySxx

    Here Σ with i,t adds up the observations for the 2 workshops over the 2 weeks; the hat indicates an estimate. The letters xy and xx in Sxy and Sxx label the sums. We obtain the same slope by fitting the original model with a distinct level for each workshop: for a given slope, the best level is ȳᵢ − b x̄ᵢ. We do not add another constant to all these levels, which would be redundant.

  6. Calculate the numerator, denominator, and slope

    Each observation contributes a product to the numerator. For A: (-1) × (-2) = 2 and 1 × 2 = 2, giving 4 h·parts/(week·week). For B: (-1) × (-2) = 2 and 1 × 2 = 2, giving 4 h·parts/(week·week). Hence Sxy = 4 + 4 = 8 h·parts/(week·week).

    For the denominator, we sum the squared setup deviations. A contributes (-1) × (-1) + (1) × (1) = 1 + 1 = 2 h·h/(week·week). B contributes (-1) × (-1) + (1) × (1) = 1 + 1 = 2 h·h/(week·week). Thus Sxx = 2 + 2 = 4 h·h/(week·week), strictly positive.

    β^=84=2 pièces/heure

    The fitted slope is exactly 2 parts/hour, or 2.00 parts/hour in decimal notation. In this fit, setup 1 h/week above the workshop mean accompanies production 2 parts/week above the mean. The word “accompanies” describes the fitted relationship; the calculation does not establish a causal intervention.

  7. Recover the levels and check by another method

    The estimated level for A is ȳᴀ − β̂x̄ᴀ = 14 − 2 × 5 = 14 − 10 = 4 parts/week. For B it is 24 − 2 × 2 = 24 − 4 = 20 parts/week. These levels are the model's intercepts, not observed production without setup: that case is not measured here.

    Reconstruction: A gives 4 + 2 × 4 = 12, then 4 + 2 × 6 = 16 parts/week. B gives 20 + 2 × 1 = 22, then 20 + 2 × 3 = 26 parts/week. The fitted residuals, differences between observed and fitted production, are 0 parts/week for these chosen data.

    A check independent of centering: subtract the start week directly from the end week. In A, the slope is (16 − 12)/(6 − 4) = 2 parts/hour; in B, (26 − 22)/(3 − 1) = 2 parts/hour. With 2 dates per workshop, these differences give the same result here. With different changes, the common slope is calculated as the sum of products divided by the sum of squares; it is generally not the simple mean of the workshop slopes.

    The perfect fit checks our arithmetic; it does not prove that the factors uᵢt are absent or that exogeneity holds. No uncertainty interval or significance test is inferred from this small example.

  8. Recognize what fixed effects cannot identify

    If setup time never changed within each workshop, all x̃ᵢt values would be 0 h/week. Then Sxx would be 0 h·h/(week·week): the slope quotient would be undefined. Different levels between A and B do not replace within-workshop variation.

    A stable characteristic, such as machine type, is absorbed into the workshop level: its coefficient cannot be estimated separately in this model. By contrast, an unusually large order or a temporary breakdown varies over time and remains in the residual term if it is not modeled.

    “Fixed” therefore describes the level αᵢ, not the absence of change in all the data. Week-specific effects would be additional terms requiring justification; they are not automatically included in workshop fixed effects. This unit stops at removing a constant additive component for each workshop.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the results. Explain why a separate mean for each workshop must be subtracted from both x and y. Then recover the slope and name a varying factor that this calculation does not remove.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Subtracting the mean of the whole table is enough.”

    An overall mean preserves the shift between A and B. Return to their separate means: (5 h/week; 14 parts/week) for A, (2 h/week; 24 parts/week) for B. From each row, subtract its own workshop's mean for both x and y.

    Review the centered paths in the second diagram

  • “With fixed effects, stable differences between workshops are enough to calculate the slope.”

    These differences in levels are precisely what the workshop effects absorb. If x does not change within each workshop, its centered deviations and Sxx are 0: no slope is identifiable by this method. A constant machine type also receives no coefficient distinct from the workshop level.

    Return to the final step on within-workshop variation

  • “The machines are accounted for, so the slope must measure a causal effect.”

    What is removed is their stable additive contribution under the chosen model. Larger orders can still make setup and production vary together. The exogeneity of within-workshop variation must be examined, even when all fitted residuals are 0.

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Center B's final week

For B, the original observations remain (1 h/week; 22 parts/week) and (3 h/week; 26 parts/week). Recover B's means, center its final observation, then give its contribution to Sxy and Sxx, including units.

Hint 1

The reference is the mean of B's weeks; no observation from A enters this mean.

Hint 2

Multiply the centered deviations for Sxy; square the setup deviation for Sxx.

Compare with the solution

The means are (1 + 3)/2 = 2 h/week and (22 + 26)/2 = 24 parts/week. The final observation becomes (3 − 2; 26 − 24), giving (1 h/week; 2 parts/week).

Its contribution to Sxy is 1 × 2 = 2 h·parts/(week·week). Its contribution to Sxx is (1) × (1) = 1 h·h/(week·week). The deviations are positive because this week is above B's means; its level relative to A plays no role in these subtractions.

Check your reasoning

  • Use only B's weeks for its means.
  • Center x and y, then distinguish the product from the square.
  • Obtain 2 h·parts/(week·week) and 1 h·h/(week·week) with an explanation of the signs.

Exercise 2 · Invariance to prove

Shift B's level

In a purely arithmetic variant of the same table, add 10 parts/week to each of B's production values, without changing any hours or A. Predict the effect on the slope before calculating anything. Then recover the new production values, their mean, and B's estimated level. Explain what happens to the centered data.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

When the same constant is added to each production value in a workshop, its mean receives the same constant.

Hint 2

In the deviation from the mean, this constant cancels itself out. For the fitted level, start again from the new mean minus β̂ times the mean of x.

Compare with the solution

The slope must remain 2 parts/hour: only differences in levels change. B's production values become 22 + 10 = 32 and 26 + 10 = 36 parts/week. Their mean is (32 + 36)/2 = 34 parts/week.

At the start, the deviation is 32 − 34 = -2 parts/week; at the end, 36 − 34 = 2 parts/week. The x deviations remain -1 and 1 h/week. Sxy therefore remains 8 h·parts/(week·week), Sxx remains 4 h·h/(week·week), and their quotient remains 2 parts/hour.

B's estimated level becomes 34 − 2 × 2 = 34 − 4 = 30 parts/week. This is the old level 20 increased by 10 parts/week. This invariance is a property of the calculation; we have not simulated any real improvement in a workshop.

Check your reasoning

  • State that the slope is unchanged, then demonstrate this using centered deviations.
  • Find 34 parts/week for B's new mean and 30 parts/week for its fitted level.
  • Explain why a stable shift is absorbed into the workshop effect.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and objection

Hours are decided after orders arrive

Return to the original table and the slope 2 parts/hour. We learn that the workshops choose their setup time after receiving orders: a busy week may increase both setup and production. A colleague says: “Fixed effects have removed the equipment differences, so adding an hour will produce the calculated increase.” In your answer, distinguish the established calculation, the assumption requiring justification, and the observations needed to examine it.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Is order size constant throughout the observation period? Does a workshop mean make it disappear?

Hint 2

A relationship between x̃ and unmodeled varying factors may remain, even with a perfect fit.

Compare with the solution

The slope 2 parts/hour does describe the fit to the centered observations. Under our model, the stable additive contribution of equipment is removed. Varying order volume is not removed in this way; it may remain in uᵢt and be related to the chosen hours.

Strict exogeneity is therefore not established: knowing the hours may provide information about unmodeled orders. Fitted residuals of 0 do not test this assumption. We cannot state the causal effect of an added hour from this table alone.

To examine this objection, we would need to document orders, their timing, and the rule for assigning hours, then justify a strategy that separates these influences. These observations would have to be obtained in an authorized study; they are not present in our example. Observing them would not, by itself, guarantee causal identification.

Check your reasoning

  • Retain the calculation result without turning it into a promise about an intervention.
  • Name orders as a varying factor that may remain related to x after centering.
  • Connect the objection to exogeneity and specify which observations are missing, without claiming that they would automatically be sufficient.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which comparison underlies the workshop fixed-effects slope?

Each workshop's deviations from its own mean, over the same observed dates. The slope uses within-workshop variation, while stable additive contributions are absorbed into workshop levels. These levels may be related to x.

Recall 2How do we remove αᵢ, and what remains of the term uᵢₜ?

Subtract the workshop's mean model from the model for each observation: αᵢ cancels itself out. Both x and y must be centered; the term uᵢₜ − ūᵢ remains. An overall mean does not perform this removal separately for each workshop.

Recall 3Reconstruct the slope from the centered data and recover its unit.

Add the products x̃ỹ for Sxy, then the squared x̃ values for Sxx; divide Sxy by Sxx if Sxx is positive. Here, 8 h·parts/(week·week) divided by 4 h·h/(week·week) gives 2 parts/hour. The time periods cancel, leaving parts per hour.

Recall 4Why does x being constant within each workshop prevent us from estimating its slope this way?

Each deviation x̃ is 0 h/week; Sxx is 0 and the slope quotient is undefined. Differences in x between workshops do not replace within-workshop variation. An invariant factor is absorbed into the workshop level.

Recall 5Why do centering and fitted residuals of 0 not prove a causal effect?

Centering removes the stable additive component, not varying factors that may be related to the hours. Strict exogeneity concerns the model's residual factors and the entire sequence of hours; it cannot be inferred from the fit. Orders provide our concrete objection.

What you understand
Workshop fixed effects remove a stable additive component by centering each workshop separately. A slope on a series centered as a whole shares the algebra of the quotient, but does not perform this separation by workshop. This comparison does not create a canonical milestone for this chapter.
