Level 0 · C004·M1

Expressions

Your goalWrite the price of a basket of notebooks using a letter, then calculate a chosen case without changing the rule.

Before you start
c003-m5 : scientific notation

✿01 See the idea

You are putting together a basket of identical notebooks. Each notebook costs 3 €. The basket has shared packaging costing 2 €, paid for only once. Adding a notebook adds its price; it does not require another package.

Instead of copying out a list of prices for every basket, let us leave a place for the number of notebooks. We will call this number n. This letter represents a number, rather than the word “notebook”: when you choose the contents of the basket, you can replace n with the count.

An expression is a way of writing a combination of numbers, possibly letters, and operations. Here it preserves the instruction “repeat the price for each notebook, then add the packaging”. Evaluating the expression means calculating its value after assigning values to the letters.

All the prices and baskets on this page are fictional. The notebooks have the same price, with no discount or other charge. To let us test an empty basket too, our fictional rule charges for packaging even if no notebooks are bought; you should not assume this is a rule used in other shops.

The rule to remember

Ask yourself what repeats and what is paid for only once. Write this rule before choosing the number of notebooks.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the basket to an expression, then to the receipt

Write the rule for a number of notebooks that has not yet been chosen, then evaluate it for 4 notebooks at 3 € each and shared packaging costing 2 €. Justify each operation before carrying it out.

  1. Name the quantities and their units

    n denotes the number of notebooks, a whole-number count greater than or equal to 0; p is the unit price, in euros per notebook; e is the price of the shared packaging, in euros; T is the total price, in euros. The sign × calls for multiplication, + for addition, and = states that the expressions on either side have the same value.

    Here p = 3 € per notebook and e = 2 €. These prices stay fixed when n changes. Only whole notebooks are allowed; shared packaging is charged in every case, including n = 0. The amounts in this scenario are exact: no prices need to be rounded.

  2. Express the repeated part, then the fixed part

    Each notebook contributes the price p. For n notebooks, we therefore multiply p by n. The product is an amount in euros: euros per notebook multiplied by a count of notebooks. We then add e, another amount in euros, only once.

    T=p×n+e

  3. Read the abbreviated notation

    With the chosen prices, the total price in euros is calculated using the expression below. Between a number and a letter, we can omit ×: 3n means 3 × n. The number in front of the letter is its coefficient: here it gives the price repeated for each notebook.

    3n+2

    We carry out multiplication before addition: 3n + 2 is read as (3 × n) + 2. Parentheses tell us to work out the group they enclose first. Moving them can therefore change the rule, as you will check in the last exercise.

    The expression can be written without knowing n. It does not choose the basket for you. The equation T = p × n + e describes how to calculate the total; we have not set any target total that would require us to find n.

  4. Replace the letter with the chosen count

    Let us choose n = 4. Replacing n, also called substituting its value, means putting 4 in its place while keeping the operations and prices. The arrow below shows this replacement; it is not a calculation to carry out on n.

    3n+2→3×4+2

  5. Calculate the notebook cost before adding the packaging

    The notebooks cost 3 € per notebook × 4 notebooks = 12 €. We can then add the amounts: 12 € + 2 € = 14 €.

    T=3×4+2=12+2=14 €

    Starting by adding 4 and 2 would already lose the meaning of the quantities: a number of notebooks and an amount of money cannot be added. The calculation follows the groups in the scenario, rather than just the order in which the signs appear.

  6. Check with an itemized receipt

    Another way to count is to list each notebook. The receipt has 4 lines at 3 €, then a line for packaging. The notebooks alone give 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12 €. With 2 € for packaging, we get 14 € again.

    Next, subtract the price of the notebooks from the total: 14 − 12 = 2 €. This remainder is indeed the cost of the single package specified. The receipt checks the repetition; the subtraction checks the fixed part. These checks concern our fictional rule and do not verify an actual purchase.

  7. Test the empty basket without inventing a new rule

    For n = 0, the notebooks cost 3 × 0 = 0 €. The total is then 0 + 2 = 2 €. The flat charge remains, in line with the assumption stated at the start.

    If packaging were free when the basket is empty, we would need a different rule for that case. The expression does not decide the terms of sale: it expresses the ones we have set.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Point to the notebooks and then the packaging, and reconstruct the expression and its evaluation. Explain why replacing n changes neither the unit price nor the number of packages.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “3n means 3 + n; the letter is just a name.”

    n holds the chosen count. The price 3 repeats for each of the n notebooks: 3n means 3 × n. For n = 4, count the 4 labels in the diagram again: they give 12 € for the notebooks.

    Count the prices under the bracket again

  • “In 2 + 3 × n, I start with the addition on the left.”

    Writing the flat charge first does not change its place in the calculation: multiplication still takes priority. For n = 4, first calculate 3 × 4 = 12 €, then 2 + 12 = 14 €. Adding the prices first would mean inserting parentheses and repeating the packaging too: that would be a different rule.

    Review the order of operations and the units

  • “If the expression contains n, I must find n.”

    Here you choose the number of notebooks and calculate the corresponding total. No target amount forces you to make a particular choice. Evaluating an expression does not mean solving an equation; the next unit will introduce an equality that must be satisfied.

Compare with the solution

After substitution, the expression becomes 3 × 5 + 2. Multiplication gives 15 € for the notebooks; addition gives 17 € in total.

Check: 17 − 14 = 3 €. The basket contains exactly 1 extra notebook: the difference should be its price, with no extra packaging. This is indeed the case.

The unit price changes

Exercise 3 · Transfer · explain and refute

Is a shared result enough to prove a rule?

Return to the original prices. Compare 3n + 2 and (3 + 2)n: what would each expression charge for? Evaluate both for n = 4, then for n = 1. Someone claims that they represent the same basket because the result is identical for n = 1. Respond using the meaning of the prices and a calculation that settles the question.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In the second expression, the entire group in parentheses is multiplied by n. Where is the packaging price now?

Hint 2

Count the packages charged for 4 notebooks. Compare with the single package for our basket.

Compare with the solution

3n + 2 charges for n notebooks and shared packaging. For n = 4, the total is 12 + 2 = 14 €. In (3 + 2)n, each notebook has its own packaging: the group is worth 5 € per notebook packaged this way, then 5 × 4 = 20 €.

The second expression counts 8 € of packaging instead of 2 €. It charges 6 € too much for packaging in our scenario. Check: the totals differ by 20 − 14 = 6 €, exactly the same difference.

For n = 1, the first gives 3 × 1 + 2 = 5 € and the second (3 + 2) × 1 = 5 €. The result is identical because repeating the packaging only once or adding it only once amounts to the same thing in this case.

This shared case is not enough: the basket of 4 notebooks gives different totals and shows that the rules are not interchangeable. You did not look for an unknown number; you tested chosen numbers to understand what the expressions charge for.

Check your reasoning

  • You associate the second expression with packaging charged for each notebook.
  • You obtain 14 € and 20 € for 4 notebooks, then 5 € under each rule for 1 notebook.
  • You explain the difference of 6 € in terms of the extra packaging.
  • You refute the claim using the case where the results differ.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In the basket, what does n represent? What needs to be specified before calculating?

n represents the number of notebooks, a whole-number count greater than or equal to 0. To evaluate an expression containing n, you need to choose this number; the letter is neither the name of an object nor an extra notebook.

Recall 2How do you read 3n, and why does this operation correspond to the basket?

3n is read as 3 × n. Each notebook contributes the same price of 3 €: the price repeats with the number of notebooks. It is not 3 + n.

Recall 3What steps let you evaluate 3n + 2 for a chosen number of notebooks?

Replace n with the chosen number, without changing the operations. First multiply to obtain the price of the notebooks, then add 2 € for packaging only once. State the total in euros and check with an itemized receipt.

Recall 4Why does putting the packaging inside (3 + 2)n change our rule?

The parentheses make us calculate the group before multiplying it by n. The packaging price is then repeated with each notebook. Our scenario specifies shared packaging, added only once after the price of the notebooks.

Recall 5Does the expression for the total tell you how many notebooks to buy?

It gives a calculation for a chosen number; it does not impose that number. Here no target total is set. Evaluating means substituting, then calculating; finding a value that satisfies a condition will be a different task.
