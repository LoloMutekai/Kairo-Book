Level 2 · C090·M5

Stationarity

Your goalDecide whether the mean, variance and autocovariance of a dated series with a regular time step are compatible with independence from the date, by comparing two windows without drawing conclusions beyond the data, then explain what differencing does.

Before you start
c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c037-m5 : controlled cleaning · c090-m1 : timestamping · c090-m2 : autocovariance · c090-m3 : trend · c090-m4 : seasonality

✿01 See the idea

The water level in an imaginary collection tank is read at the same time each day. After six readings, you want to describe what an “ordinary day” looks like for this tank. A mean or a spread is meaningful only if the days are similar. If the tank is filling up, the early days are different from the later ones.

Stationarity is the assumption that the statistical rules of a phenomenon do not depend on the date: the same center, the same spread, and the same similarity between two readings separated by the same number of days. It allows variation. A stationary series keeps changing, but around a center that does not move.

This assumption determines what you can reuse. A mean estimated from the start of a series tells you about the end only if the center does not change. Trend (C090·M3) and seasonality (C090·M4) are two common ways for the center to depend on the date; regular timestamping (C090·M1) ensures that “the same time lag” has a meaning.

The values in this scene are fictional and constructed to keep the calculations readable: they are neither real readings, nor settings, nor results from a system. This page teaches a cautious way to compare windows rather than a complete statistical test.

The rule to remember

A series is weakly stationary when its mean, variance and autocovariance at a given time lag do not depend on the date. With data, we compare windows of the same size and retain the assumption as long as it is not contradicted, without ever proving it through this comparison alone.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Six tank readings, two windows

Fictional values: the water level in an imaginary tank is read at a fixed time on six consecutive days, in centimeters: 10, 14, 12, 18, 22 and 20. Window A contains days one to three; window B, days four to six. Are the means and variances of the two windows compatible with a stationary series?

  1. Set out the model, notation and assumptions

    Yt denotes the water level on day t, viewed as a random variable in the model; lowercase yt is the value read that day, in cm. Stationarity is a property of a model rather than a list of numbers: our six values are a single realization and cannot demonstrate it.

    Assumptions for interpreting the data: the readings are spaced exactly one day apart, with no missing day (C090·M1); no value has been corrected or replaced (C037·M5); the two windows have the same size n = 3 readings; the variance of a window uses division by n rather than n − 1, with the same convention in both windows. A different convention would change the figures rather than the approach.

  2. State what stationarity requires

    A model is weakly stationary (or second order stationary) when three conditions hold for every day t and every lag h, counted in days. First, the mean:

    E[Yt]=μ

    μ is the theoretical mean, in cm; it has no date index. Next, the variance:

    Var(Yt)=γ(0)

    γ(0) is the variance, in cm², finite and independent of t. Finally, the autocovariance at lag h (C090·M2):

    Cov(Yt,Yt+h)=γ(h)

    γ(h), in cm², depends only on h rather than t; when h is 0, it gives the variance. These conditions do not describe the full shape of the distribution: they concern only these three quantities. With irregular dates, “h days” would mix different durations, hence the assumption of a regular time step.

  3. Compare the means of the two windows

    With data, the theoretical mean is unknown. We replace it with the mean of the readings in a window W, denoted by m with subscript W, which is A or B; n is the number of readings in W:

    mW=1n∑t∈Wyt

    Window A, days one to three:

    mA=10+14+123=363=12 cm

    Window B, days four to six:

    mB=18+22+203=603=20 cm

    The difference between the means is 20 − 12 = 8 cm. Check: a mean stays between the smallest and largest readings in its window. 12 is between 10 and 14; 20 is between 18 and 22.

  4. Compare the variances of the two windows

    The variance of a window measures the spread of the readings around the mean of that window. We calculate the deviations, square them so that the signs do not cancel out, add them, then divide by n:

    vW=1n∑t∈W(yt−mW)2

    Window A: deviations -2, 2 and 0 cm; squares 4, 4 and 0 cm²; sum 8 cm². Window B: deviations -2, 2 and 0 cm; squares 4, 4 and 0 cm²; sum 8 cm².

    vA=83≈2.67 cm²;vB=83≈2.67 cm²

    Both variances equal 8/3 cm², or approximately 2.67 cm²; the exact value remains the fraction. Check: the deviations within a window cancel out. For A, -2 + 2 + 0 = 0; for B, -2 + 2 + 0 = 0. Equal variances say nothing about the center: here, they simply do not contradict the second condition.

  5. Relate the difference between the means to its uncertainty

    Is a difference of 8 cm large? We need to compare it with what sampling randomness would produce. If the readings in a window were independent and had the same variance, the variance of their mean would equal that variance divided by n (C066·M5, standard error). The variance of a difference between two independent means is the sum of their variances. This gives the ratio:

    R=(mB−mA)2vA+vBn

    The denominator estimates the variance of the difference between the means: (8/3 + 8/3) / 3 = 16/3 / 3 = 16/9 cm², or approximately 1.78 cm². The numerator is 8 × 8 = 64 cm². The ratio has no unit:

    R=6416/9=36

    Interpretation: if the two windows had the same theoretical mean, the squared difference would, on average, be of the same order as its estimated variance, so R would be of order one. Here R = 36, well above that. Check: R × denominator gives the numerator again, 36 × 16/9 = 64.

    Key limitation: this ratio is not a calibrated test. It assumes independent readings, although autocovariance may link them, and it uses 3 points per window. Depending on the sign of this dependence, the denominator may be too small or too large.

  6. Conclude within the limits of the data, then choose the next step

    Mean: the assumption of a common mean is poorly supported by these six fictional values, since R = 36. Variance: both windows give the same value, 8/3 cm², so there is no disagreement. Autocovariance: not assessed in this calculation; an exercise estimates part of it. Cautious conclusion: under the stated assumptions, the raw series is not compatible with a constant mean.

    The visible cause is a slope, hence the trend in C090·M3. If the model were Yt = a + b t + εt, where a is a starting level in cm, b a slope in cm per day and ε a stationary fluctuation with 0 mean, the difference between two successive days removes the level and retains the slope:

    Yt+1−Yt=b+εt+1−εt

    The mean of this difference is the constant b, which no longer depends on t, and the difference εt+1 − εt of a stationary series remains stationary. But differencing also changes the autocovariance and deals with neither seasonality (C090·M4) nor a changing variance. After any transformation, we need to repeat the comparison. The first exercise applies this transformation to the six readings.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Stationary means constant, with no variation.”

    A stationary series keeps fluctuating. The constraint concerns the rules: the mean, variance and autocovariance do not depend on the date. In the example, the deviations -2 and 2 cm around the mean of a window are normal; the problem is the shift in the center from 12 to 20 cm.

    Review the idea and the diagram of the center

  • “Two windows with the same mean and variance prove stationarity.”

    These are two conditions applied to two short samples. The autocovariance by lag, other windows and an uncertainty assessment are missing. Conversely, a high ratio R is not a verdict: it assumes independent readings. Work through these limitations in the exercise on lag one.

    Go to the exercises

  • “Differencing is enough to make a series stationary.”

    Differencing removes a linear slope from the mean rather than seasonality, a break or a changing variance, and it changes the autocovariance. After each transformation, repeat the window comparison, as at the end of the example.

    Return to the conclusion of the example

γ^(h)=1n∑t=1,…,n−hetet+h

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A window of 3 days contains two pairs of neighboring days.

Hint 2

A 0 deviation multiplied by any deviation gives a 0 product.

Hint 3

The divisor remains n, as for the variance of the window.

Compare with the solution

Products, in cm²: -2 × 2 = -4 and 2 × 0 = 0. Sum: -4 cm². Division by n: -4 / 3 = -4/3 cm², or approximately -1.33 cm².

Comparison: the variance of A is 8/3 cm², or approximately 2.67 cm². The autocovariance at lag one is half this variance in absolute value, with a negative sign. The only nonzero product pairs two deviations with opposite signs.

Limitation: only two products enter the sum, window B is not compared, and no uncertainty is assessed. This number therefore cannot tell us either that γ(h) does not depend on the date or that it does.

Check your reasoning

  • Two products with their signs, then division by n.
  • Comparison with the variance of the same window.
  • No conclusion about the third condition from a single window.

Hint 1

Separate what the calculation shows, a difference that is large relative to its estimated uncertainty, from what it assumes, independent readings.

Hint 2

Compare with the solution

Defensible position: with 3 readings per window, R = 36 is much greater than the order of one expected for equal theoretical means; the assumption of a constant mean is therefore poorly supported by these data, if the readings are independent. This qualification matters: the estimated autocovariance at lag one in A is negative, suggesting possible dependence and therefore potentially miscalibrated uncertainty.

Both are partly right. A difference between the means of two windows is an indication rather than the definition of a nonstationary model. And the small window size limits the scope of R without making it irrelevant: the difference is large relative to the observed spread.

Useful information: more readings and other windows; confirmation that the dates are regular and that no value has been corrected (C090·M1, C037·M5); the existence of a cycle that would also vary the mean (C090·M4); the form of the trend (C090·M3). None of these checks is performed here.

  • Distinction between what is calculated and what is assumed.
  • Qualification about independence and window size.
  • Three specific pieces of information to obtain before any transformation.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which three quantities must not depend on the date under weak stationarity?

The mean, the finite variance and the autocovariance, the last depending only on the time lag h rather than the date t.

Recall 2Is stationarity a property of a list of observed numbers?

No, it is a property of a model. An observed series can only be compatible or incompatible with it, for example through comparing windows of the same size, without definitive proof.

Recall 3What does the ratio R in the example compare, and what does it assume?

It divides the squared difference between the means of two windows by the estimated variance of this difference. It assumes independent readings and remains a rough measure with few points.

Recall 4What does the difference between two successive days do to a linear trend, and what does it fail to address?

It removes the starting level and turns the slope into a constant mean. It addresses neither seasonality nor a changing variance, and it changes the autocovariance.

Recall 5Why does a regular time step matter for autocovariance at lag h?

Because h counts identical steps. With irregular dates, the same h would mix different durations and the condition would lose its meaning.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the reasoning: set up a model of dated readings with a regular time step and no values corrected without your knowledge; state the three conditions, mean, variance and autocovariance by lag; estimate over two windows of the same size using the same convention; relate the difference between the means to an uncertainty while stating the independence assumption; interpret the result as compatible or incompatible, never as proof; choose a transformation only if a cause is identified, then repeat the comparison; recall that the values in the scene are fictional.
