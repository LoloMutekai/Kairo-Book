Level 2 · C058·M2

Triple integrals

Your goalBuild a triple integral as a sum of local quantities, then calculate the mass of solute in a tank and check its units.

Before you start
c045-m5 : introductory quadrature · c057-m5 : Hessian · c058-m1 : double integrals

✿01 See the idea

A tank contains a liquid with a solute dissolved in it. The concentration is higher near the surface than at the bottom. To find the mass of solute in the whole tank, a sample from the bottom is not enough: we must account for what each region of the liquid contains.

Imagine dividing the liquid into small rectangular blocks. In each block, multiply a local concentration by the volume of the block. This gives a small mass. Then add these masses, covering the whole tank without counting the same interior twice. Refining the partition allows us to represent variations in concentration more accurately.

The double integral in the previous unit accumulated a quantity over a surface. Here, the domain has a length, a width and a height: the geometric weight of each term is a volume. The word triple refers to these 3 spatial directions; it does not tell us to multiply the result by 3.

The entire scenario and its values are fictional. The tank is 3 m long, 2 m wide and 2 m high. At the instant studied, the concentration increases steadily from 2 kg/m³ at the bottom to 4 kg/m³ at the top. We are calculating the mass of the solute, rather than that of the entire liquid.

The rule to remember

A concentration multiplied by a small volume gives a small mass; the triple integral adds these contributions over the whole volume.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What mass of solute does the tank contain?

At this fictional instant, the liquid fills a rectangular block with dimensions L = 3 m, l = 2 m and H = 2 m. Its concentration varies only with height z, measured from the bottom. It is ρ₀ = 2 kg/m³ at the bottom and increases by 1 kg/m³ for each metre of height. Let us find the total mass M.

  1. Describe the domain and the concentration

    x and y are the horizontal distances from a corner of the base; z is the height above that base. These coordinates are in metres, in perpendicular directions. D is the set of points occupied by the liquid; the symbol ∈ means “belongs to”. L, l and H are the positive dimensions of the tank.

    D={(x,y,z):0≤x≤L,0≤y≤l,0≤z≤H}

    ρ, the Greek letter rho, denotes the mass concentration of the solute, in kg/m³. The symbol ρ0 denotes its value at the bottom; its subscript is a label. k is the vertical slope: 1 kg/m⁴, or an additional 1 kg/m³ per metre. Thus, k z has the same unit as ρ0.

    ρ(x,y,z)=ρ0+kz

    We assume that this profile is continuous, nonnegative and identical at every point in the same horizontal plane. The calculation describes a single instant, without modelling movement or mixing of the liquid. This profile is a teaching assumption; it is not inferred from a reading.

  2. Give meaning to the triple integral

    Divide D into n small rectangular blocks whose interiors do not overlap and which fill the whole tank. i is their index, from 1 to n; n and i are dimensionless numbers. In block i, Pᵢ is a chosen point and ΔVᵢ = Δxᵢ × Δyᵢ × Δzᵢ is the volume, in m³. The three Δ symbols denote its positive lengths. The product ρ(Pᵢ) ΔVᵢ is an approximate mass, in kg.

    Sδ=∑i=1nρ(Pi)ΔVi

    ∑ means “add the terms”. Sδ is this approximate sum, in kg. δ, lowercase delta, is the greatest edge length among the blocks, in metres. Letting δ tend to 0 requires refinement in every direction. The continuity of ρ on the closed, bounded rectangular block D guarantees a limit independent of the chosen points: this is the triple integral.

    M=∭Dρ(x,y,z) dV=limδ→0Sδ

    ∭ indicates accumulation over the volume domain D. dV is the volume element; in these perpendicular coordinates, it is written dx dy dz. The letters d are not numerical factors to remove. Integrating the concentration gives a mass; integrating the dimensionless constant function 1 gives the volume of D.

  3. Group the blocks by height

    The base has area A = L × l = 3 × 2 = 6 m². A horizontal layer of thickness Δz therefore occupies A Δz m³. Since its concentration depends on neither x nor y, the horizontal contributions combine into this area. For a very thin layer at height z, the mass is approximated by A ρ(z) Δz.

    As we refine the layers, we obtain M = A ∫ from 0 to H of ρ(z) dz. Here, ρ(z) abbreviates ρ(x,y,z), since x and y do not change its value. This grouping uses the constant cross section and horizontally uniform profile of this tank. We are not yet studying changes in the order of integration.

    M=A∫0H(ρ0+kz) dz

    This single integral calculates the mass per unit area of the base, in kg/m². Multiplying by A then gives a mass in kg. For comparison, the total volume is V = A H = 6 × 2 = 12 m³. V denotes this volume.

  4. Find an antiderivative and check its derivative

    Let F(z) be an antiderivative of ρ(z), meaning a function whose derivative is ρ(z). We use F(z) = ρ0 z + k z²/2. Its derivative is ρ0 + k z: differentiating the square produces the factor 2, which cancels the denominator 2. Both terms of F are in kg/m².

    F(z)=ρ0z+kz22;F′(z)=ρ0+kz

    The integral over the height is J = F(H) − F(0). J is the mass per unit area of the base, in kg/m²; choosing the letter J does not introduce a new physical quantity into the tank. Subtracting the value at the bottom sets the lower boundary of the integrated region exactly.

  5. Evaluate the column, then the whole tank

    At the bottom, F(0) is 0 kg/m². At the top, the constant term gives ρ0 H = 2 × 2 = 4 kg/m². The variable term gives k H²/2: H² = 2 × 2 = 4 m², then H²/2 = 2 m², and finally k H²/2 = 2 kg/m².

    J=4+2=6 kg/m²

    We now multiply this quantity by the area of the base, because all columns of equal area have the same profile. The m² cancels with the denominator in kg/m².

    M=AJ=6×6=36 kg

    The tank therefore contains 36 kg of solute in this model. This result is neither 36 m³ of liquid nor 36 kg/m³: the domain and the quantity being integrated have determined the final unit.

  6. Establish bounds before trusting the result

    The lowest concentration is 2 kg/m³; the highest is ρ0 + k H = 2 + 2 = 4 kg/m³. Each small mass is therefore bounded by these concentrations multiplied by its volume. Addition preserves these inequalities.

    24 kg≤M≤48 kg

    The bounds are 2 × 12 = 24 kg and 4 × 12 = 48 kg. Our mass of 36 kg is indeed between them. This check detects some errors; it does not by itself guarantee that the calculation is correct.

  7. Recover the average and use another check

    The average concentration throughout the volume is the mass divided by the volume: 36/12 = 3 kg/m³. Here, it coincides with the average of the extreme concentrations: (2 + 4)/2 = 6/2 = 3 kg/m³.

    Why does this other method work? The profile is affine in z and each height corresponds to the same area. The excess above the bottom concentration forms a triangle on the concentration–height graph: its area is half of height × increase. Its average over the height is therefore half the final excess. This recovers J and justifies the average of the extremes without repeating the antiderivative calculation.

    Final check: 3 kg/m³ × 12 m³ = 36 kg. For a curved profile or a tank with a varying cross section, the simple average of the extremes would no longer be justified. We do not treat it as a universal rule.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the steps and explain why each contribution contains a volume. Then reconstruct the move from the tank to the layers, the antiderivative calculation and the checks. State which assumption allows the whole column to be multiplied by the same area.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I add the concentrations of my blocks; the total will be the mass.”

    A concentration is in kg/m³. Multiply each concentration by the volume of its block, then add the kg. A sum of concentrations without these weights depends arbitrarily on the number of points in the partition.

    Revisit the move from a small volume to a small mass

  • “The concentration at the top multiplied by the volume gives the exact mass.”

    This product gives 48 kg, an upper bound for our increasing profile. It assigns the maximum concentration to the whole tank. For the exact mass, account for the concentration in each layer; the resulting average here is 3 kg/m³.

    Reread the bounds and the average check

  • “A triple integral always gives a volume.”

    The volume comes from the domain and dV; the quantity accumulated comes from the integrand. With the constant function 1, the integral gives 12 m³. With our concentration in kg/m³, it gives 36 kg. State the unit before calculating.

Exercise 1 · Interpretation and units

Same domain, different quantity

In the same tank, what is the triple integral of the dimensionless constant function 1? Then replace the profile with a uniform concentration equal to that at the bottom, 2 kg/m³. What mass do you obtain? Give the units and explain why this mass is smaller than in the example.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The constant function 1 leaves each small volume unchanged in the sum.

Hint 2

For a uniform concentration, all the small volumes are multiplied by the same factor in kg/m³.

Compare with the solution

The integral of 1 is the volume V = L l H = 3 × 2 × 2 = 12 m³. The integrand is dimensionless, so the unit of dV remains.

With the uniform concentration 2 kg/m³, the mass is 2 × 12 = 24 kg. In the original example, the concentration exceeds this value at every height above the bottom, so we find a greater mass, 36 kg. We have changed the profile, rather than the dimensions of the tank.

Check your reasoning

  • You find 12 m³ for the integral of 1.
  • You find 24 kg for the uniform concentration.
  • You justify the difference in units and the comparison with the original profile.

Exercise 2 · Exact calculation on paper

Does the lower half contain half the solute?

Return to the original profile ρ(z) = ρ₀ + k z. Divide the tank halfway up its height, at z = 1 m. Calculate the mass in the lower region, then deduce the mass in the upper region. Compare their volumes and masses. Keep fractions exact until the result in kg.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The lower region keeps the same base area. Only the upper limit of the integral becomes 1 m.

Hint 2

Use F(1) − F(0), then multiply by the area; the upper region contains the rest of the mass.

Compare with the solution

Each region has thickness H/2 = 1 m and volume A × 1 = 6 m³. The volumes are equal, but the concentrations are not.

For the lower region: F(1) − F(0) = 2 × 1 + 1 × 1/2 = 2 + 1/2 = 5/2 kg/m². The square of the limit is 1 m²; the variable contribution is indeed 1/2 kg/m².

Lower mass: 6 × (5/2) = 15 kg. Upper mass: 36 − 15 = 21 kg. Addition check: 15 + 21 = 36 kg.

The upper region contains more solute because its concentrations are higher. Dividing the volume into halves therefore does not allow us to divide the mass into halves.

Check your reasoning

  • You change the height limit while keeping the base area.
  • You obtain 15 kg and 21 kg, with an explicit antiderivative calculation.
  • You explain how equal volumes contain different masses.

Exercise 3 · Transfer to an approximate sum

Can a coarse partition bound the answer?

Keep the 2 regions from the previous exercise, each of thickness 1 m. For each region, approximate its mass by concentration at the lower boundary × volume. Repeat with the upper boundaries. Calculate both sums, place the exact mass between them and explain why a finite sum is not automatically the integral.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The concentrations at heights 0 m, 1 m and 2 m are respectively 2, 3 and 4 kg/m³.

Hint 2

Each region has a volume of 6 m³. The profile increases: the lower boundary underestimates each concentration and the upper boundary overestimates it.

Compare with the solution

Using the lower boundaries: 2 × 6 + 3 × 6 = 12 + 18 = 30 kg. Using the upper boundaries: 3 × 6 + 4 × 6 = 18 + 24 = 42 kg.

Monotonicity gives 30 kg ≤ M ≤ 42 kg. Our value of 36 kg lies within these bounds, which are tighter than those in the example. The actual masses of the regions are 15 kg and 21 kg; the boundary products are only approximations to them.

Making the layers thinner brings these bounds closer together for the continuous profile studied. The horizontal grouping remains exact because the concentration is uniform in each horizontal plane. For a concentration that also varies with x and y, the partition would need to be refined in those directions. A list of values without associated volumes would still not be enough.

Check your reasoning

  • You multiply each concentration by 6 m³.
  • You obtain 30 kg and 42 kg, in the correct order around 36 kg.
  • You justify the inequalities using the increasing profile, then distinguish a finite approximation from a limit.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What local contribution must be added to obtain the mass of the solute?

A concentration ρ(Pᵢ), in kg/m³, multiplied by the volume ΔVᵢ of the small block, in m³. The product is in kg; adding only the concentrations does not give a mass.

Recall 2What is missing from a sum of a few contributions for it to define the triple integral?

The limit as the size of all the blocks tends to 0. For our continuous concentration on a closed, bounded rectangular block, this limit exists and does not depend on the points chosen within the blocks. A finite partition generally provides an approximation.

Recall 3Why does a triple integral not always give a volume?

The unit of the result is that of the integrand multiplied by m³. The dimensionless constant function 1 gives a volume; a concentration in kg/m³ gives a mass in kg.

Recall 4Why does our calculation reduce to the base area multiplied by an integral over the height?

The tank has a constant horizontal cross section and the concentration depends only on z. Each layer of thickness dz has volume A dz. If the concentration varied horizontally, this simple grouping would no longer be enough.

Recall 5How can you check an integrated mass without calculating its antiderivative again?

Multiply the minimum and maximum concentrations by the volume to bound the mass. A partition and the increasing profile give tighter bounds here. A result within the bounds is only consistent with this check; that alone does not prove it is exact.
