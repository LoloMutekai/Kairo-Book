Level 3 · C220·M3

Processes

Your goalEstimate the benefit of dividing a computation among several processes, separating the serial part, communication cost and independence of random seeds.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A program launched on the machine becomes a process: an executable that is running, with its own memory, its own thread of instructions and a number assigned by the system. Two processes cannot see each other’s variables. They cooperate only by sending messages, which are copies of bytes.

Our scenario is entirely fictional. An estimate based on random draws, as in C176·M5, takes 120 s on a single process. The profile, as in C220·M1, attributes 12 s to reading the data and the final merge: this part cannot be divided among workers. The remaining 108 s consist of independent draws, already vectorized as in C220·M2. These values are not measurements and do not describe any real machine.

The idea is to divide the draws into 4 batches and give each batch to a worker process. Each worker runs on its own core, a unit capable of executing a thread of instructions at a given time. Our fictional machine has 16 free cores. The 4 batches progress at the same time, but the parent process must start the workers, send them their batches and receive their results. This communication takes time.

Two questions guide this page. How long does the computation take with 4 workers, under a model whose assumptions we state? And are the 4 streams of draws truly independent? A faster computation that produces an invalid sample has no scientific value.

The rule to remember

Time with n workers = serial part + parallelizable part divided by n + cost per worker multiplied by n. Each worker receives its own distinct, recorded seed.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A computation of random draws divided among 4 workers

Fictional values: D = 120 s with a single process, including S = 12 s that cannot be divided among workers. We launch n = 4 workers, each costing c = 1 s in startup and communication. The 1000 thousand draws have a fictional standard deviation of 3 u per draw. Let us calculate the time, the speedup, then the effect of a shared seed.

  1. Define the quantities, units and assumptions

    D is the time with a single process, in seconds. S is the serial part, in seconds. P = D − S is the parallelizable part, in seconds. n is the number of workers, with no unit, where n ≤ 16. c is the cost per worker, in seconds: startup, sending the batch and returning the result. Dn is the time with n workers, in seconds.

    Model assumptions: part P is divided into equal batches; each worker has its own core and does not slow down the others; the parent incurs the costs c one after another; S stays the same. A real machine may behave differently. Profiling from C220·M1 is used to compare the model with a measurement.

    Dn=S+Pn+n×c

  2. Find the parallelizable part

    Only part P is divided among workers. We obtain it from the total time: 120 − 12 = 108 s. Check: S + P = 12 + 108 = 120 s, the original time.

    P=D−S=120−12=108 s

  3. Calculate the three terms of the time

    Each worker receives P / n = 108 / 4 = 27 s of draws. The 4 costs add up: n × c = 4 × 1 = 4 s. The time is therefore S + P / n + n × c.

    Dn=12+27+4=43 s

    Check against the diagram: 12 serial seconds, then 4 s of overhead, then 27 s in parallel. The four lines in the diagram do indeed end at 43 s.

  4. Interpret speedup and efficiency

    Speedup A = D / Dn compares the time on a single process with the time when work is divided among processes; it has no unit. Efficiency E = A / n states what fraction of the ideal is retained.

    A=12043≈2.79;E=An≈0.70

    Four workers therefore do not give a speedup of 4: the exact value of A is 120/43, or approximately 2.79. Efficiency is approximately 0.70, because the serial part and communication cost must still be incurred.

  5. Check against the bounds

    Without any communication cost, the time would be S + P / n = 39 s and A would be approximately 3.08. Even with a huge number of workers and 0 cost, P / n would become negligible: S would remain. Speedup therefore cannot exceed D / S.

    A≤DS=12012=10

    The resulting order is: A ≈ 2.79, then A without cost ≈ 3.08, then the bound 10. This is consistent: removing a cost can only bring A closer to the bound.

  6. Keep streams of draws independent

    The 1000 thousand draws are divided into 1000 / 4 = 250 thousand per worker. For N independent draws, the standard error of their mean is σ / √N, in u. With distinct seeds, N = 1000 × 1000 and √N = 1000: the standard error is 3 / 1000 = 0.003 u.

    If the four workers copy the same seed, they produce the same sequence. Only 250 × 1000 independent draws remain, with square root 500.

    σN=3500=0.006 u

    The standard error is 2 times as large: the computation has been accelerated without gaining precision. A reproducible result can be wrong. A different seed must be derived for each worker, and these seeds must be recorded in the protocol, as in C017·M5.

Explain it in your own words

Why does speedup remain below 4, even when each worker computes its batch without waiting for the others? And why does a shared seed increase the standard error despite the 1000 thousand draws claimed?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “With 4 processes, the program runs 4 times as fast.”

    The time has three terms: S does not decrease, P / n decreases and n × c increases. In the example, 4 workers give a speedup of 2.79, rather than 4.

    Review the calculation of the three terms of the time

  • “Adding workers always helps.”

    The term n × c grows with n. In the model, 16 workers finish in 34.75 s, later than 8 workers in 33.50 s. Calculate the three terms before choosing n.

    Repeat the comparison in the exercises

  • “A shared seed makes the computation reproducible, so it is reliable.”

    Reproducible does not mean independent: identical streams count as a single stream. The standard error goes from 0.003 u to 0.006 u. Give each worker a distinct seed and record them.

    Review the step on independent streams

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Only 2 workers

Use D = 120 s, S = 12 s and c = 1 s again, but launch n = 2 workers. Calculate Dₙ, speedup A and efficiency E. Compare with the example. The values are fictional.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate P, then P / n and n × c separately.

Compare with the solution

P = 108 s, so each worker receives 108 / 2 = 54 s. The cost is 2 × 1 = 2 s. Then Dn = 12 + 54 + 2 = 68 s.

A = 120 / 68 ≈ 1.76 and E = A / 2 ≈ 0.88. With fewer workers, efficiency is better than in the example (approximately 0.70), but the time is longer (68 s versus 43 s). Efficiency and speed are two different criteria.

Check your reasoning

  • P / n calculated using P, rather than D.
  • Cost multiplied by the number of workers.
  • Speedup has no unit and time is in seconds.
  • Comparison of two distinct criteria.

Exercise 2 · Comparison

Eight or sixteen workers

The fictional machine has 16 free cores. With the same D, S and c, compare n = 8 and n = 16. Which finishes first? Explain using the three terms of Dₙ.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate P / n and n × c for each of the two cases.

Hint 2

Compare the decrease in P / n with the increase in n × c.

Compare with the solution

For 8 workers: P / n = 13.5 s and n × c = 8 s, so Dn = 12 + 13.5 + 8 = 33.5 s. For 16: P / n = 6.75 s and n × c = 16 s, so Dn = 34.75 s.

Eight workers finish first: the difference is 1.25 s. When going from 8 to 16, the parallelizable part decreases by 6.75 s, but the cost increases by 8 s. The speedups are approximately 3.58 and 3.45. This result depends on the model: a real machine can also slow down because of memory contention, which we have not modeled.

Check your reasoning

  • Both times calculated with the same formula.
  • Terms compared in the same unit.
  • Conclusion limited to the fictional model.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

Sixteen workers and a shared seed

The 1000 thousand draws are divided among 16 workers, with σ = 3 u. Compare the standard error with distinct seeds and with a single copied seed. What is the ratio, and why does it depend on the number of workers?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate each worker’s batch in thousands of draws.

Hint 2

The square root of 62.5 × 1000 is 250.

Compare with the solution

Each worker computes 1000 / 16 = 62.5 thousand draws. With distinct seeds, the standard error remains 3 / 1000 = 0.003 u. With a copied seed, only one batch is independent: 3 / 250 = 0.012 u.

The ratio is 4. Check: 4 × 4 = 16, the number of workers. Indeed, the effective number of draws is divided by n, so the standard error is multiplied by √n. Assumptions: independent draws within a stream and the same σ for all.

Check your reasoning

  • Batch per worker calculated before taking the square root.
  • Two standard errors compared in the same unit.
  • Ratio explained by √n, rather than by a simple division.
  • Assumptions of independence and a common σ stated.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do two worker processes launched by the same program share?

None of their variables without an explicit mechanism: each has its own memory and receives copies of its data. These copies and startup take time.

Recall 2How is the time with n workers written in our model?

D(n) = S + P / n + n × c: S cannot be divided among workers, P is divided into equal batches, and c is the cost of each worker.

Recall 3What do speedup and efficiency measure, and what bound limits speedup?

A = D / D(n), with no unit; E = A / n. Whatever n is, A cannot exceed D / S.

Recall 4Why must workers avoid copying the same seed?

They would produce the same draws: the number of independent draws does not grow, so the standard error remains that of a single batch.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Name the parent process, its workers and their separate memories; separate S, P and the cost c; write D(n) = S + P / n + n × c; calculate A = D / D(n) and E = A / n; check the bound D / S; give each worker a distinct, recorded seed; draw conclusions only within the model’s assumptions.
