Level 1 · C057·M1

Partial derivatives

Your goalCalculate how sensitive the volume of a tank is to one dimension, stating which dimension stays fixed and distinguishing a derivative from an average rate of change.

Before you start
c033-m5 : affine approximation · c039-m5 : changes of basis · c027-m5 : complex numbers

✿01 See the idea

On a plan for a tank with a square base, you can change the side length of the base or the internal height. The volume changes in both cases, but you are asking different questions. To isolate the effect of the side length, leave the height shown on the plan unchanged.

Let x be the side length of the square and y the height, in metres. The volume V is the base area multiplied by the height: x × x × y, in cubic metres. The initial tank has x = 2 m and y = 3 m; its geometric capacity is 12 m³. The dimensions are fictional. We are comparing tank plans, rather than the motion of water whose volume would be conserved.

Fixing y = 3 m leaves only one free input: x. You then have a function of one variable again, whose local slope you know how to find. This slope is called the partial derivative with respect to x. Fixing x in turn gives you another slope, the one with respect to y.

“Fixed” means unchanged during the chosen comparison. The height can take other values in other comparisons. The side length x always controls both dimensions of the square base: keeping y fixed still allows the entire base to widen.

The rule to remember

Choose the variable that changes, hold the others fixed, then differentiate the resulting function of one variable. State the point and the unit of the result.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What is the slope at the initial plan?

For the same tank, let us find the partial derivatives of the volume at the point (x, y) = (2, 3), then check their meaning using a length change of 0.50 m. All dimensions on the plan are assumed to be exact in this model.

  1. Set up the model and its domain

    V(x,y)=x2y

    V(x, y) denotes the internal volume associated with the lengths x and y. We assume a square base, vertical walls and negligible wall thickness. The lengths are strictly positive. The point under study is therefore inside the domain: small positive or negative changes are still possible. The polynomial x squared times y has the derivatives we are about to calculate here.

    When we write V(2, 3) = 12 m³, the arguments give the values of the lengths in metres. For example, the initial area is 2 × 2 = 4 m²; then 4 × 3 = 12 m³.

  2. Define “with respect to x” precisely

    The symbol ∂ is read as “partial”. ∂V/∂x denotes a derivative; it is not the quotient of two numbers named ∂V and ∂x. The symbol lim means that we seek the value the quotient approaches as the step h approaches 0. The step h is a signed change in x, in metres; it stays nonzero during the division. The height y has exactly the same value in both volumes.

    ∂V∂x(x,y)=limh→0V(x+h,y)−V(x,y)h

    The quotient compares a change in volume, in m³, with a change in length, in m. The derivative is therefore expressed in m³ per m, or m². Its role is to measure the sensitivity of the volume to the side length; the unit m² alone does not identify it as the base area.

  3. Expand before taking the limit

    Replace the volumes with their expressions. Expanding the square separates the initial volume from the terms introduced by h. The initial volume cancels, and each remaining term contains h: we can cancel this factor because h is nonzero.

    (x+h)2y−x2yh=2xyh+yh2h=2xy+yh

    With x and y fixed, yh approaches 0 as h approaches 0. The derivative is therefore 2xy. This gives the usual rule: y is a constant factor, while the derivative of x squared is 2x. We do not differentiate y with respect to itself in this calculation.

    ∂V∂x(2,3)=2×2×3=12 m²

    The local slope is positive: increasing the side length slightly at a fixed height increases the volume. The intermediate product xy is 6 m², and the factor 2 comes from squaring the side length.

  4. Change the free variable

    For ∂V/∂y, now keep x fixed. The area x squared is a constant coefficient multiplying y. The derivative of a constant coefficient times y is that coefficient:

    ∂V∂y(x,y)=x2

    At the initial plan: 2 × 2 = 4 m². We can also check this using the definition: V(x, y + h) − V(x, y) equals x squared times h; dividing by h gives x squared, whatever small step is allowed. Only in this case does the sensitivity coincide with the base area.

  5. Check with a finite step

    Take h = 1/2 m = 0.50 m and keep y = 3 m. The new side length is 2 + 0.50 = 2.50 m. Its square is 25/4 m² = 6.25 m²; the volume becomes 75/4 m³ = 18.75 m³. To measure the change, subtract the initial 12 m³: we obtain 27/4 m³ = 6.75 m³.

    V(2.50,3)−V(2,3)0.50=6.750.50=13.50 m²

    This average rate of change is 13.50 m², while the derivative at the start is 12 m². The algebraic check gives exactly 12 + 3 × 0.50 = 12 + 1.50 = 13.50 m². The extra term has not disappeared: we have not yet taken the limit as the step approaches 0.

    The derivative predicts an approximate increase of 12 × 0.50 = 6 m³, giving an approximate volume of 18 m³. The exact volume is 18.75 m³. Their difference of 0.75 m³ is precisely y times h squared. We have therefore also checked why the local prediction is not an equality for this finite change.

  6. Check the role of the chosen direction

    Start again from the initial plan and increase only y by 0.50 m. The height becomes 3.50 m, the base area stays at 4 m² and the volume is 14 m³. The change is 2 m³; the average rate of change 2 ÷ 0.50 = 4 m² equals the derivative with respect to y in this case.

    This equality is exact because, at fixed x, the volume depends linearly on y. It does not turn all average rates of change into derivatives. We have isolated each variable; describing simultaneous changes in all the variables will be the subject of the next unit.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the equations. Explain which length you hold fixed for each derivative, reconstruct the expressions, then justify the units. Why does the average rate of change with respect to x exceed the initial derivative, while the rate with respect to y equals it?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “To differentiate with respect to x, I also differentiate y.”

    First write “y fixed”. The remaining function is y times x squared: y stays as its coefficient, so the derivative is 2xy. Differentiating y as well would mean changing the question. The fact that a constant on its own has a 0 derivative does not remove a constant factor.

    Revisit the expansion at a fixed height

  • “The derivative 12 m² means that the volume always increases by 12 m³.”

    The derivative is a change in volume per metre of side length, at the stated point. Multiply it by the small change in side length for an approximation in m³. For h = 0.50 m, this approximation is 6 m³ and the exact increase is 6.75 m³. A finite step requires checking the difference.

    Reread the check with a finite step

  • “I can change both the side length and the height to measure the partial derivative with respect to x.”

    The comparison with respect to x requires the same height before and after. A simultaneous change mixes the effects. Return to the boxes in the diagram and name the free variable and the one that stays fixed before calculating.

Exercise 1 · Direct application

The side length on the plan has changed

The same tank now has x = 2.50 m and y = 3 m. Calculate the partial derivatives with respect to x and y at this new point. For each one, state the dimension held fixed and the unit; avoid confusing these results with the volume.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First differentiate symbolically: the rules we found are still 2xy and x squared.

Hint 2

Substitute the new dimensions only after choosing the free variable.

Compare with the solution

With respect to x, y stays fixed at 3 m. The product xy is 7.50 m², then 2 × 7.50 = 15 m². This is the new value of ∂V/∂x.

With respect to y, x stays fixed at 2.50 m. The derivative ∂V/∂y is 2.50 × 2.50 = 25/4 m² = 6.25 m². The volume of this plan is 18.75 m³: it is a different quantity.

Check your reasoning

  • The dimension held fixed in each case is named separately.
  • The results are 15 m² and 6.25 m², after substitution into the derivative expressions.
  • A sensitivity in m³ per m is distinguished from a volume in m³.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and interpretation

Reduce the step without changing the height

Start again from (2, 3). Only the side length increases, this time by h = 1/4 m = 0.25 m. Calculate the new volume, its change and the average rate of change. Compare this rate with the one in the example and with the initial derivative. Keep fractions until the result.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The new side length is x + h; the volume is still its square multiplied by the unchanged height.

Hint 2

The average rate of change is the change in volume divided by the step, rather than the new volume divided by the step.

Compare with the solution

New side length: 9/4 m = 2.25 m. Base area: 81/16 m². New volume: 243/16 m³ = 15.1875 m³.

Change: 243/16 − 12 = 51/16 m³ = 3.1875 m³. Dividing by 1/4 m gives 51/4 m² = 12.75 m².

Checking with the expansion gives 12 + 3 × 0.25 = 12.75 m². This rate is closer to 12 m² than the 13.50 m² from the previous step. It is still an average rate of change: the limit is established by the expression 2xy + yh, rather than just by a few numbers being close.

Check your reasoning

  • The height keeps the value 3 m at every evaluation.
  • The initial volume is subtracted before dividing by h.
  • The comparison distinguishes a numerical check from the limit argument.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and repair

A worksheet changes both settings

A worksheet moves from the plan (2 ; 3) to the plan (2.50 ; 3.50), then divides the change in volume by 0.50 m. The coordinates are the side length and the height, in metres. It calls this result the “partial derivative with respect to x at the initial plan”. Calculate its quotient, identify the reasons this conclusion is invalid and propose a correct comparison.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The base area of the new plan is 6.25 m²; which height should you use to obtain its volume?

Hint 2

Check the height of each plan, then ask yourself whether the limit as the step approaches 0 has been taken.

Compare with the solution

The new volume is 25/4 × 7/2 = 175/8 m³ = 21.875 m³. The change is 79/8 m³ = 9.875 m³; its quotient by 0.50 m is 79/4 m² = 19.75 m².

The height changed along with the side length: this quotient measures their combined change over this finite displacement. This is not the comparison that defines ∂V/∂x. Also, the step has not been taken to a limit; even a rate at a fixed height would not generally be enough to give the exact derivative.

Repair: compare V(x + h, y) with V(x, y) using the same y = 3 m, divide their difference by nonzero h, then let h approach 0. The example obtains 12 m² in this way. Simply repeating this calculation at h = 0.50 m would give 13.50 m², still an average rate of change.

Check your reasoning

  • The quotient 19.75 m² comes from the new volume minus the initial volume.
  • The two flaws are distinguished: a changed height and a finite step.
  • The repair fixes y and explains taking the limit; it does more than state another number.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do you keep fixed to calculate ∂V/∂x for our tank?

The height y. The same x controls both sides of the base; I differentiate the function x ↦ x squared times y, treating y as a constant coefficient.

Recall 2Reconstruct the two partial derivatives of V(x, y) = x squared times y and their values at the initial plan.

With respect to x, y is fixed: ∂V/∂x = 2xy, giving 12 m² at (2, 3). With respect to y, x is fixed: ∂V/∂y = x squared, giving 4 m².

Recall 3Why is the derivative of volume with respect to a length expressed in m²?

It relates a change in volume in m³ to a change in length in m. The m³ per m become m²; the result remains a sensitivity, rather than a volume.

Recall 4Which step is missing if you only calculate the average rate of change at a fixed height?

You must let the nonzero step h approach 0 and establish the limit. Here the rate is 2xy + yh; the term yh vanishes in the limit. Setting h = 0 directly in the initial quotient would give an impossible division.

Recall 5Why does a quotient obtained by changing x and y fail to define ∂V/∂x?

The two evaluations no longer have the same height. The definition with respect to x requires identical y before and after, followed by a limit as the step tends to 0. A displacement in which both dimensions vary asks a different question.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the example, reconstruct this sequence: define V and its units; choose the length that varies and name the one that stays fixed; form a difference of volumes divided by a nonzero step; expand, then take the limit; evaluate at the requested point; check the units and distinguish the local slope from a finite change. Reproduce both tank calculations, explaining every choice.
