Level 3 · C164·M5

Fubini-Tonelli

Your goalJustify a change in the order of integration by distinguishing non-negativity from absolute integrability, then calculate the same mass using both sweeps.

Before you start
c143-m5 : uniform convergence · c058-m5 : change of variables · c012-m5 : counterexamples · c164-m1 : σ-algebras · c164-m2 : measures · c164-m3 : Lebesgue · c164-m4 : monotone and dominated convergence

✿01 See the idea

A rectangular plate receives a deposit of powder only below a sloping boundary. To calculate the mass of this deposit, we can add up vertical strips, moving from left to right. We can also add up horizontal strips, moving upwards. Do these necessarily give the same mass?

For powder with a non-negative density, no strip removes what another contributes. Tonelli makes this intuition rigorous, even if the model accumulates an infinite mass. For a signed balance, where removal counts negatively, Fubini also requires us to check the total of the absolute values before changing the order.

Our plate is 2 m wide and 1 m high. The deposit occupies the triangle below the rising diagonal. Its density increases with height. The diagrams show the same domain; only the direction of counting changes. The thick lines are cross-sections, not strips with an already measured width.

All values for the plate and its variants are fictitious. We calculate the integral of a given model without claiming to measure a real deposit. The σ-algebras, measures, Lebesgue integrals and convergence results come from the prerequisites; here we specify the new notions of product and section.

The rule to remember

On a product of σ-finite measures, check measurability; use Tonelli for a non-negative function, or establish a finite absolute integral before invoking Fubini for a signed function.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Calculate the mass of the deposit without counting another region

On X = [0, 2] m and Y = [0, 1] m, the domain D satisfies 0 ≤ y ≤ x/2. The density is f(x,y) = κy on D and 0 elsewhere, with κ = 6 g/m³. Let us calculate its mass M in grams in both orders and justify their equality.

  1. Define the product, the sections and their units

    x and y are the coordinates, in metres. Here μ and ν are the length measures on X and Y: writing dμ(x) = dx and dν(y) = dy means integrating with respect to these lengths. Their product measure μ⊗ν measures area: a rectangle E×F has measure μ(E)ν(F). The product σ-algebra, written 𝒜⊗ℬ, is the smallest σ-algebra containing these rectangles, for E in the σ-algebra 𝒜 on X and F in the σ-algebra ℬ on Y.

    The notation 𝟙_D equals 1 on D and 0 elsewhere. Our function is f(x,y) = κy𝟙_D(x,y), in g/m²: κ is in g/m³ and y is in m. It is measurable because D is a closed triangle and y is a continuous function. The integral of f with respect to area is M, in g.

    A section is obtained by fixing a coordinate: y ↦ f(x,y) for fixed x, or x ↦ f(x,y) for fixed y. Call the integral of the first section A(x) and that of the second B(y). A and B are in g/m; integrating next over the remaining coordinate will give grams.

  2. State precisely what allows a change of order

    The following statements hold for σ-finite measure spaces (X,𝒜,μ) and (Y,ℬ,ν): each space is a countable union of sets of finite measure. A finite measure is therefore σ-finite; our bounded intervals satisfy this assumption. μ, ν and their units depend on the problem; the area measures of our plate provide a concrete instance.

    Tonelli: if u is measurable with respect to 𝒜⊗ℬ and takes values in [0, +∞], the integrals of the sections are measurable and the following equalities hold in [0, +∞]. We do not need to know a finite value in advance. Here u names a general function, not an additional density on the plate.

    ∫X×Yu d(μ⊗ν)=∫X(∫Yu(x,y) dν(y)) dμ(x)
    =∫Y(∫Xu(x,y) dμ(x)) dν(y)

    Fubini: if a real-valued function h is measurable with respect to the product σ-algebra and the integral of |h| with respect to μ⊗ν is finite, then the same equalities hold for h with a finite real value. |h| is its absolute value. The sections are absolutely integrable for μ-almost every x and ν-almost every y: exceptions are possible on a set of measure 0. The integrals of exceptional sections can be assigned an arbitrary value, for example 0, to define the outer functions everywhere.

    Saying that h is absolutely integrable means precisely that the integral of |h| is finite. By Tonelli applied to |h|, it is enough to prove finiteness in one order. A finite signed iterated integral, obtained through cancellation, does not replace this check. The assumptions are necessary: this course does not claim these statements outside the stated setting.

  3. Choose Tonelli before calculating

    Our f is measurable and non-negative. Tonelli therefore allows both sweeps even before we know whether M is finite. We handle the triangular domain by taking a function that is 0 outside D in the product rectangle X×Y; the theorem concerns this product.

    Here is the mechanism, without a full proof of the construction of the product measure. For a non-negative constant a multiplied by the indicator function of a rectangle E×F, both integrations give aμ(E)ν(F). The construction of the product measure extends this identity to measurable indicator functions; we accept this step here. Non-negative simple functions follow by addition. Every non-negative measurable function is an increasing limit of such functions; monotone convergence from the prerequisites then passes the identity to the integrals, including infinite ones.

    To understand the signed case, write h = h⁺ − h⁻, with h⁺ = max(h, 0) and h⁻ = max(−h, 0). These are the positive and negative parts, and |h| = h⁺ + h⁻. Absolute integrability makes their integrals finite: we can subtract the Tonelli identities without creating the undefined difference ∞ − ∞.

  4. First integrate along a vertical cross-section

    Fix x. The domain requires 0 ≤ y ≤ x/2. An antiderivative of κy is κ multiplied by the square of y, divided by 2. The exponents indicate powers; the entire limit x/2 must be squared.

    A(x)=∫0x/2κy dy=κ2(x2)2=κx28

    Simplifying the square of the limit gives the denominator 2 × 4 = 8. The coefficient κ/8 is 3/4 g/m³, or 0.75 g/m³. After multiplication by the square of x, A(x) is indeed in g/m. A single cross-section does not yet give the mass of the whole plate.

  5. Add up the vertical cross-sections

    We still need to cover the full width, from 0 to L = 2 m. An antiderivative of the square of x is the cube of x divided by 3; here L denotes the total width, not a new integration variable.

    M=∫0Lκ8x2 dx=κL324

    The square of L is 4 m² and its cube is 8 m³. The numerator is therefore 6 × 8 = 48 g, then M = 48/24 = 2 g. Division by 24 combines the 8 from the cross-section and the 3 from the antiderivative; it changes no unit.

  6. Change the limits while keeping the same triangle

    Now fix y. Solving y ≤ x/2 gives x ≥ 2y. The right edge remains x = L. The heights present range from 0 to H = 1 m, with H = L/2. At fixed y, κy is constant during integration in x.

    B(y)=∫2yLκy dx=κy(L−2y)

    The factor L − 2y is the length of the horizontal cross-section, in m; B(y) is therefore also in g/m. Keeping x between 0 and L while omitting the indicator function would incorrectly count a region with no deposit. The second diagram helps locate the correct starting point.

  7. Finish the horizontal calculation using exact fractions

    Expand B(y) to integrate separately the term proportional to y and the term proportional to its square over [0, H]. Together the two terms represent the same cross-section; their difference does not make our density f negative.

    ∫0HB(y) dy=κ(LH22−2H33)

    The square of H is 1 m² and its cube is 1 m³. The first term in parentheses is 2/2 = 1 m³. The second is 2/3 = 2/3 m³. Their exact difference is 1/3 m³.

    The mass is therefore 6 × (1 − 2/3) = 2 g, exactly as in the first order. Rounding 2/3 before subtraction would lose this exact check.

  8. Check the result and the scope of the theorem

    The rectangle has area 2 m²; the triangle occupies the fraction 1/2, giving 1 m². The density ranges from 0 to κH = 6 g/m². The mass must therefore lie between 0 and 6 g. Our result 2 g satisfies this bound, with an average density M/area = 2 g/m².

    Since f is non-negative, |f| = f and its absolute integral is now 2 g: it is finite. Fubini therefore also applies to f, but this conclusion comes after the calculation; Tonelli was already available at the start. A signed density will require its own absolute-integrability check, as in the exercise on removal.

    The words “almost everywhere” do not mean “for every cross-section”. A function can be absolutely integrable on the plate and have a non-integrable section at an exceptional coordinate belonging to a set of measure 0. In our bounded polynomial model, all cross-sections are integrable; the general theorem promises this property only almost everywhere.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Draw the triangle, recover the limits in each direction and explain why Tonelli applied before the mass was known. Then state what additional proof a signed balance would require.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Changing the order just means swapping dx and dy.”

    Fix the new coordinate, then solve the inequality defining the domain. Here y ≤ x/2 becomes x ≥ 2y: a horizontal cross-section starts on the diagonal, not at the left edge. Redraw the cross-section before rewriting the limits.

    Compare the directions of the cross-sections

  • “My signed iterated integral is finite, so Fubini applies.”

    Cancellation between positive and negative values does not control their sizes. Find an integrable upper bound for |h|, or calculate an iterated integral of |h| using Tonelli. If the absolute integral is infinite, Fubini gives no general permission to change the order; this alone does not prove that the two orders differ.

    Reread the assumptions and the positive and negative parts

  • “Tonelli guarantees a finite mass and finite section integrals everywhere.”

    Tonelli also allows an infinite value. Even under the stronger assumption of Fubini, finiteness of the section integrals is guaranteed almost everywhere, with possible exceptions on a set of measure 0. The singular model in the last exercise separates equality of the orders from finiteness.

    Test the singular density on the same plate

The left limit is 2 × 0.50 = 1 m. The cross-section runs from 1 to 2 m, a length of 1 m. The density at this height is κy = 3 g/m².

B(0.50 m) = 3 g/m² × 1 m = 3 g/m. Integration with respect to y is still needed to obtain grams. A geometric line has 0 area; B describes the quantity per unit height that neighbouring strips will accumulate.

Exercise 2 · Application · check Fubini

From the deposit to a signed balance

On the same triangle, a removal model subtracts a constant density c = 4 g/m². The balance becomes h(x,y) = (κy − c)𝟙_D(x,y). Justify Fubini before calculating the total balance. You may use the integral of f and the area already calculated; an upper bound on the absolute integral is enough.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

h is measurable and changes sign. The inequality |a − b| ≤ |a| + |b| gives |h| ≤ f + c𝟙_D here.

Hint 2

Integrate this non-negative upper bound. Once its finiteness is established, subtract the mass removed from that of the deposit.

Compare with the solution

The function h is measurable. For y near the lower edge, κy − c is negative; near the top vertex, it is positive. Tonelli therefore does not apply directly to h as a non-negative function. It applies to |h| and to the upper bound f + c𝟙_D.

The integral of |h| is at most M + c × area(D) = 2 + 4 × 1 = 6 g. This finite bound proves that h is absolutely integrable and allows Fubini. 6 g is an upper bound, not a claimed value of the absolute integral.

The total removal is 4 g. By linearity of the integral for integrable functions, the balance is 2 − 4 = -2 g in both orders. A negative balance means that removal exceeds the deposit in this mathematical variant; it does not describe a negative mass of powder.

Check your reasoning

  • Present a check of |h| before changing the order.
  • Distinguish the upper bound 6 g from the balance -2 g.
  • Relate the sign of the balance to subtracting the removal.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · prove a limitation of the model

A non-negative model can accumulate an infinite mass

Still on D, replace the density by r(x,y) = γ/y for strictly positive y, with γ = 1 g/m; set r = 0 on the edge y = 0 and outside D. This singular model is fictitious. Do Tonelli and Fubini apply? To decide finiteness without an antiderivative, set ℓ = 1 m. Study the disjoint strips defined below; n is their integer index, with no unit, starting at 1. Explain what happens when an arbitrary number of these strips are added together.

ℓ≤x≤L;ℓ2n+1<y≤ℓ2n;n≥1
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

All these strips lie in D, because y ≤ ℓ/2 and x ≥ ℓ. On a strip, y ≤ ℓ/2^n gives r ≥ γ2^n/ℓ.

Hint 2

The height of the strip is ℓ/2^(n + 1). Multiply this height, its width L − ℓ and the lower bound on density: the powers cancel.

Hint 3

If each strip contributes at least the same strictly positive mass, the total over N strips has no finite bound as the integer N increases.

Compare with the solution

r is a non-negative measurable function: the formula is continuous for positive y, and its other values are defined on measurable sets. Tonelli applies immediately. The density has unit (g/m)/m = g/m². Assigning a value on the lower edge alone does not remove the singularity in its neighbourhoods.

In each strip, the width is L − ℓ = 1 m and the height is ℓ/2^(n + 1). Multiplying the area by the lower bound γ2^n/ℓ gives γ(L − ℓ)/2 = 0.50 g. This is a lower bound independent of n, not the exact calculation of each mass.

∫Dr dx dy≥N×0.50 g, pour N≥1

N denotes the integer number of strips included, with no unit, starting at n = 1. The strips are disjoint; additivity on their finite union justifies the bound. Since N can be arbitrarily large, the integral is infinite. Tonelli states that both iterated integrals also equal +∞; their equality does not require finiteness.

Since |r| = r, the absolute integral is infinite: the assumption of Fubini is not satisfied. We must neither claim a finite mass nor treat this model as a physical measurement of powder. The proof relies on neighbourhoods of the edge, which have positive area, rather than on the edge itself.

Check your reasoning

  • Check measurability, non-negativity and inclusion of the strips in D.
  • Obtain the lower bound 0.50 g per strip with consistent units.
  • Justify divergence by adding an arbitrary number of disjoint strips.
  • Conclude: Tonelli applies, the integral is infinite, the absolute-integrability assumption of Fubini is not satisfied.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be checked for Tonelli, and what value can it allow?

A non-negative measurable function on the product of two σ-finite measure spaces. The product integral and both iterated integrals are equal, possibly with value +∞; Tonelli does not assert that they are finite.

Recall 2What must be proved to apply Fubini to a signed function h?

In the σ-finite measurable setting, prove that the integral of |h| is finite. We can apply Tonelli to |h| and check one order of integration. A finite signed iterated integral is not enough to establish this assumption.

Recall 3On our plate, how can we recover the horizontal cross-section from y ≤ x/2?

Fix y, then solve for x: 2y ≤ x ≤ L, with 0 ≤ y ≤ H = L/2. Keep the same triangle and rewrite its limits before calculating.

Recall 4Does Fubini guarantee an absolutely integrable section at every coordinate?

It guarantees this almost everywhere with respect to the measure used for each outer integration: exceptional sections may exist on a set of measure 0. We then define the inner integrals arbitrarily at just these exceptions to write the outer integral.

Recall 5Why does the absolute value protect the reasoning for signed functions?

Writing h = h⁺ − h⁻ and |h| = h⁺ + h⁻ shows that absolute integrability makes both masses finite. We can subtract the Tonelli identities without forming the undefined difference ∞ − ∞.
