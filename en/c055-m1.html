Level 1 · C055·M1

Sampling distribution

Your goalConstruct the distribution of a mean from the possible samples, preserving their probabilities and the sampling rule.

Before you start
c035-m5 : covariance and conditioning in finite settings · c024-m5 : measurement quality

✿01 See the idea

A bag contains 2 labelled objects: A has a mass of 4 g and B has a mass of 8 g. You draw an object, record its mass in grams, put it back in the bag and mix before drawing again. You then calculate the mean of the 2 recorded values. If you repeat the whole procedure, this mean can change even though the objects in the bag remain the same.

The objects and draws are fictional. The model assumes that each object has the same chance of being drawn, that the draws with replacement are independent and that the masses are known exactly. Replacing the object makes a repeat possible; independence is an additional assumption about the draws. No scale error is studied here.

The population is the set of objects A and B. Here, a sample is the ordered sequence of the 2 recorded values. A statistic is a quantity calculated from this sample; we choose its mean. The sampling distribution is the probability distribution of this statistic when the sampling procedure is repeated: what values can it take, and with what probabilities?

A then B and B then A are distinct outcomes of the draws, even though they produce the same mean. The first diagram keeps the outcomes separate; the next groups their means. The probabilities describe the model before the draws. They do not require any particular result in a short series of trials.

The rule to remember

Specify the population, procedure and statistic; list the samples and their probabilities; group those that give the same statistic by adding their probabilities.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Construct the distribution before drawing

Keep A with mass a = 4 g, B with mass b = 8 g and sample size n = 2. Each draw selects A or B with probability 1/2. Let us calculate all the possible means and their distribution, then check the result.

  1. Name what is fixed and what varies

    a and b are the fixed masses, in grams. X is the mass on the first draw and Y the mass on the next: before drawing, they are random variables because their values depend on the draws. They are measured in grams. n counts the observations and has no physical unit. M denotes their random mean; m is a value it takes after a particular set of draws. M and m are also measured in grams.

    M=X+Yn,n=2

    The population mean, denoted by μ, is fixed: μ = (a + b)/2 = 12/2 = 6 g. It concerns the objects in the bag; M concerns the drawn values, which can repeat the same object.

  2. List the pairs and justify their probabilities

    The possible pairs are (A, A), (A, B), (B, A), (B, B), giving 2 × 2 = 4 cases. Replacement allows (A, A) and (B, B). We keep the order so that we do not lose the case B then A.

    P denotes the probability of an event and has no unit. By independence, the probability of a pair is the product of the probabilities of its draws: 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Here the pairs are equally likely; without this property, counting cases would not be enough.

    P(X=a,Y=b)=P(X=a)×P(Y=b)=1/4

  3. Calculate the statistic for each pair

    Add the masses to get the sum of the recorded values, then divide by n to get their mean. This sum can include the same mass again: it does not describe the physical contents of the bag at a single moment.

    (A, A): (4 + 4)/2 = 8/2 = 4 g. (A, B): (4 + 8)/2 = 12/2 = 6 g.

    (B, A): (8 + 4)/2 = 12/2 = 6 g. (B, B): (8 + 8)/2 = 16/2 = 8 g.

    Check the calculation: each mean lies between the values in its pair. The constant pair A, A keeps 4 g; reversing A, B does not change its sum.

  4. Group the probabilities without discarding repeated results

    For M = 4 g, only (A, A) fits: probability 1/4. For M = 6 g, the 2 pairs (A, B) and (B, A) fit: probability 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2. For M = 8 g, only (B, B) fits: probability 1/4.

    We add at the centre because the events “A then B” and “B then A” are mutually exclusive within a single trial. The distribution of M is therefore the list of values 4 g, 6 g, 8 g with their respective probabilities 1/4, 1/2, 1/4.

    M can equal 6 g even though there is no object with this mass in the bag. The distribution of individual masses and the distribution of their mean concern different quantities.

  5. Check that all the probability is preserved

    The possible values do not overlap and account for all pairs. Their probabilities must therefore sum to 1: 1/4 + 1/2 + 1/4 = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1. Each probability lies between 0 and 1.

    This check would detect the loss of B then A. It does not, by itself, justify equal probabilities: these follow from our sampling assumptions. A table that sums to 1 can still represent the wrong procedure.

  6. Find the centre by another route

    The expected value, denoted by E(M), is the mean of the possible values weighted by their probabilities. The symbol E means expected value under this procedure. Its unit is the gram: multiplying a mass by a probability with no unit preserves the gram.

    E(M)=4×1/4+6×1/2+8×1/4=6 g

    The contributions are 1 g, 3 g and 2 g. The outer contributions give 3 g; adding the middle contribution gives 6 g, equal to μ.

    Another route: directly take the mean of the results from the 4 equally likely pairs. The outer results total 12 g and the middle results 12 g, giving 24 g. Their mean 24/4 = 6 g confirms the grouped calculation. This equality with μ does not force the mean of every sample to equal μ.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the diagrams. Reconstruct the pairs, then explain why grouping them changes the displayed probabilities. State which quantity stays fixed and which quantity varies before each new trial.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The possible means must all have the same probability.”

    Here, it is the pairs of draws that are equally likely. The value 6 g receives 2 pairs, each with probability 1/4: their probabilities must be added. Write the pairs under each mean again before counting.

    Review the grouping and the sum of probabilities

  • “Since μ is 6 g, a different result from the draws reveals an incorrect calculation.”

    μ describes the fixed population. In our model, E(M) equals it, rather than every observed value of M. Check the pair A, A: its mean is 4 g, and this case has positive probability.

    Examine an observed mean that differs from μ

  • “Putting the object back or keeping it out does not change the distribution of the mean.”

    Without replacement, A then A and B then B are impossible with this bag. The list of samples and their probabilities change. Start from the procedure again before reusing the table.

    Reconstruct the distribution without replacement

Exercise 1 · Application · count the matching pairs

A maximum mean or drawing B?

With replacement and the same assumptions, calculate the probability that M equals 8 g, then the probability of drawing B at least 1 time. Are these events identical? Justify your answer using the pairs, without starting from a memorized formula.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

To obtain a mean of 8 g, can an A value be present?

Hint 2

For B at least 1 time, look for the only pair that does not fit.

Compare with the solution

Only (B, B) gives M = 8 g. Its probability is 1/4, or 0.25 with no unit.

B at least 1 time corresponds to (A, B), (B, A) or (B, B). We can take the complement of (A, A): 1 − 1/4 = 3/4, or 0.75. The mixed pairs fit this event, but their mean is 6 g. The events are therefore not identical.

Check your reasoning

  • You link M = 8 g to the pair (B, B) alone.
  • You include the mixed pairs in the event B at least 1 time.
  • You obtain 1/4 and 3/4 as probabilities with no unit.

Exercise 2 · Interpretation · distribution and observed result

A mean away from the centre

You observe the pair (A, A). Calculate its mean and its signed deviation m − μ. Under the procedure with replacement, what is the probability of obtaining a mean different from μ? Explain why E(M) = μ remains compatible with this observation.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The required deviation is the observed value minus the population mean, in grams.

Hint 2

For M different from μ, keep the outer values of the distribution or take the complement of the middle value.

Compare with the solution

The pair (A, A) gives m = 4 g. The signed deviation is 4 − 6 = -2 g. The negative sign indicates an observed mean below μ, rather than an impossible outcome of the draws.

The probability of a mean different from μ is 1/4 + 1/4 = 1/2. Using the complement, 1 − 1/2 also gives 1/2.

E(M) = 6 g concerns all the possible means, weighted by their probabilities. It does not predict that every m equals 6 g. Our observation is a case allowed by this distribution; it contradicts neither the table nor its expected value.

Check your reasoning

  • You state m = 4 g and the deviation -2 g with the correct sign.
  • You justify 1/2 by naming the events included.
  • You distinguish an observed value from the expected value of the statistic.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · change an assumption

The same masses, without replacement

Use the same bag and 2 draws, but now keep the first object out of the bag until the end of the trial. The first draw is still uniform; the next selects the remaining object. List the pairs, calculate their probabilities and construct the new distribution of M. Restore the bag between trials. Can you reuse the distribution with replacement?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

After the first draw, 2 − 1 = 1 object remains. Is a constant pair still possible?

Hint 2

Do the possible pairs have different means? Add the probabilities that lead to the same value.

Compare with the solution

The only pairs are (A, B) and (B, A): 2 × 1 = 2 cases. The first object has probability 1/2; the next is determined, with conditional probability 1. Each pair therefore has probability 1/2.

Each pair gives (4 + 8)/2 = 6 g. This value includes all cases: its probability is 2/2 = 1. Every other value has probability 0. This is the new sampling distribution.

The population and statistic are the same, but the draws are no longer independent and the constant pairs are excluded. The distribution with replacement no longer applies. In this particular case, drawing without replacement until this small bag is empty makes the mean constant. This does not generalize to all sampling without replacement.

Check your reasoning

  • You exclude (A, A) and (B, B), and justify the probabilities of the remaining pairs.
  • You group all the probability at 6 g.
  • You explain the change in distribution through the procedure, pointing out the dependence between the draws.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does a sampling distribution describe?

A statistic calculated from samples produced by a specified procedure. It gives the possible values of this statistic and their probabilities; it does not directly describe just the individual values in the population.

Recall 2Why is the mean 6 g more likely than 4 g in our draws with replacement?

(A, B) and (B, A) each give 6 g. Their probabilities 1/4 add up to 1/2. Only (A, A) gives 4 g, with probability 1/4. The pairs are equally likely; the means are not.

Recall 3What does E(M) = μ mean, and what does it allow to vary?

Here, the expected value of the random mean M equals the fixed population mean μ, which is 6 g. A realization m can still differ from μ, such as 4 g for (A, A). An expected value does not guarantee the result of each trial.

Recall 4What must be specified before using a sampling distribution, and what does replacement change here?

The population, sample size, sampling rule and statistic. With replacement and independence, constant pairs are possible. Without replacement in this bag, only mixed pairs remain possible and the mean is always 6 g.
