Level 4 · C237·M1

Stein’s method

Your goalExpress a difference from a normal distribution as the expectation of an operator, solve a simple Stein equation, and distinguish a difference for a test from a bound on distributions.

Before you start
c236-m5 : Hörmander · c228-m5 : incompleteness · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions

✿01 See the idea

A workshop inspects 4 welds per batch. Each inspection returns “defect” or “no defect”. The counter N records how many defects are found. In our fictional model, the inspections are independent and each reports a defect with probability p = 1/2. This deliberately simple probability is used for the calculation; it does not describe any real production line.

To compare the shapes of distributions, we subtract the counter’s mean and divide by its standard deviation. The resulting score W has mean 0 and unit variance. Can we then replace its distribution with a normal bell curve? The diagram still shows separate probability masses: adjusting the center and scale does not determine the whole shape.

Stein’s method changes the question. We choose a function h that expresses what we want to compare, then construct another function f. A combination of the derivative of f and its value has mean 0 under the normal distribution. Evaluated using W, this same combination gives exactly the difference between the means of h.

The counter, its model, and all the values in this scene are fictional. Here we know its entire distribution, so we can check the calculation using Stein’s method against a direct calculation. In a larger problem, the aim would be to bound this residual without knowing the entire distribution.

The rule to remember

Choose the target distribution and the test h; solve the Stein equation; evaluate the residual under the distribution being studied; state exactly which difference has been calculated or bounded.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The counter has the right variance: what is missing?

Keep the 4 independent inspections with probability p = 1/2. We will compare the score W with a standard normal variable Z, then calculate the difference for the test h(x) = x to the power 4. The entire calculation concerns the theoretical distributions of the fictional model.

  1. Set up the model, units, and exact distribution

    For i from 1 to n = 4, Bᵢ equals 1 if weld i is reported as defective and 0 otherwise. It is a Bernoulli variable with parameter p; q = 1 − p = 1/2 is the complementary probability. The Bᵢ are independent and identically distributed. N is their sum. Its unit is the number of welds reported as defective per batch; p and q have no units.

    N=∑i=1nBi;μ=np=2

    The variance of a Bernoulli variable is pq = 1/4. Independence allows us to add these variances: v = npq = 1 in welds squared. The standard deviation σ is positive and satisfies σ × σ = v; here σ = 1 weld. μ is measured in welds and W has no units.

    W=N−μσ=N−2;σ=v

    Every binary sequence of inspection results has the same probability, since p = q. There are 2 to the power 4, or 16, sequences. When N is 0, 1, 2, 3 or 4, the corresponding counts are 1, 4, 6, 4 or 1. At the center, choosing the 2 defective positions gives 4 × 3 ÷ 2 = 6 possibilities. Dividing these counts by 16 gives the distribution below.

    Valeur de W-2-1012Probabilité1/161/43/81/41/16

    Check: the counts add up to 16; the symmetric masses give E[W] = 0. E denotes expectation, the weighted average using the probabilities, rather than an average of observed measurements. The contributions to E[W × W] add up to 2 × (1/4 + 1/4) = 1: the standardization is consistent.

  2. Recognize the normal distribution through an identity

    Z denotes a standard normal variable: mean 0, variance 1, and no units. Its density φ(x) is proportional to exp(−x × x / 2), normalized so that its integral is 1. Here x is a real coordinate with no units; φ′(x) = −xφ(x). The prime denotes a derivative with respect to x. For a real function f, the Gaussian Stein operator is A, defined by:

    Af(x)=f′(x)−xf(x)

    Suppose f is continuously differentiable, the terms f′φ and xfφ are absolutely integrable, and f(x)φ(x) tends to 0 at both infinities. Integration by parts gives the following identity: the boundary term vanishes and the derivative of φ supplies the factor −x.

    E[f′(Z)]=∫−∞∞f′(x)φ(x)dx=E[Zf(Z)]

    Thus E[Af(Z)] = 0. All the polynomials used here satisfy the conditions because of Gaussian decay. Taking f(x) = x gives E[Z × Z] = 1. Then taking f(x) = x to the power 3 gives E[Z to the power 4] = 3 × E[Z × Z] = 3. We have recovered the reference value for the test without assuming it for W.

  3. Turn a question about h into an equation for f

    h is the test function: h(W) represents the quantity whose mean we want to compare. Set gh(x) = h(x) − E[h(Z)], a test centered under the target distribution. We seek a function fh satisfying the Stein equation for every real x:

    fh′(x)−xfh(x)=h(x)−E[h(Z)]

    The subscript h reminds us that the solution depends on the test. Multiplying the equation by φ turns its left side into the derivative of the product fhφ. A solution chosen to have a 0 boundary term on the left is therefore:

    fh(x)=1φ(x)∫−∞xgh(t)φ(t)dt

    t is the real variable of integration. Centering ensures that the total integral of ghφ is 0, so the product fhφ also has a 0 boundary term on the right. For bounded continuous h, this solution is continuously differentiable and bounded; if E[|W|] is finite, the expectations below exist. For polynomial h, we instead check the necessary moments: W is bounded in our example and Z has moments of every order.

    E[h(W)]−E[h(Z)]=E[Afh(W)]

    This equality is the core of the method. If the residual is 0 for every solution associated with bounded continuous tests, all the corresponding expectations agree: the distributions are equal. A single test gives only a partial comparison. For a quantitative approximation, we will need to state a family of tests and bound their residuals uniformly.

  4. Solve for the test that emphasizes large deviations

    Choose h(x) = x to the power 4. A large deviation from the center then receives more weight than with the square. Its normal mean is 3. We seek an odd polynomial fh: the term −xfh must produce the power 4. The following solution can be checked directly:

    fh(x)=−x3−3x;fh′(x)=−3x2−3
    Afh(x)=(−3x2−3)−x(−x3−3x)

    The final product gives −x to the power 4 − 3x × x before subtraction. The terms in x × x cancel, leaving x to the power 4 − 3, exactly h(x) − E[h(Z)]. All terms have no units. The solution is polynomial and unbounded; its integrability and boundary conditions were checked in the previous step. We will now write f for this particular solution.

  5. First calculate the difference from the counter’s distribution

    Set m = E[W to the power 4], the mean of the 4th power of the score. The central value W = 0 contributes 0. Each endpoint has power 16 and mass 1/16, so it contributes 1. Each neighbor of the center contributes 1/4. Symmetry lets us double the sum on one side:

    m=2×(1/16×16+1/4×1)=5/2

    The mean of the test under W is therefore 5/2, or 2.50, with no units. Under Z, it is 3. The signed difference, always in the order studied distribution minus target distribution, is:

    E[h(W)]−E[h(Z)]=5/2−3=-1/2

    The negative sign tells us that the standardized counter has a smaller mean of this power than the normal distribution. The calculation using Stein’s method must recover the same sign and the same fraction.

  6. Form the local residual while keeping track of signs

    Write r(x) = Af(x) = f′(x) − xf(x). At the positive endpoint x = 2, the solution gives f(2) = −2 × (4 + 3) = -14. Its derivative is f′(2) = −3 × 4 − 3 = -15. The product xf(x) is therefore -28; this is different from f′(x).

    r(2)=-15−(-28)=13

    At the positive neighbor x = 1, f(1) = -4 and f′(1) = -6, so r(1) = -2. At the center, f(0) = 0, but f′(0) = -3: the central residual is -3, rather than 0. f is odd, f′ and xf are even, so r is even. Negative values of W have the same residual as their opposites.

    w-2-1012r(w)13-2-3-213

    Independent local check: substituting each w into w to the power 4 − 3 gives exactly the same residuals. The polynomial does solve the equation at every value W can take.

  7. Average using the correct probabilities

    We still need to multiply each residual by the probability of its value, then add. A residual evaluated at a single point does not yet represent an expectation. Each endpoint contributes 1/16 × 13 = 13/16; each neighbor contributes 1/4 × -2 = -1/2; the center contributes 3/8 × -3 = -9/8.

    E[r(W)]=2×(13/16+(-1/2))+(-9/8)

    The sum on one side is 5/16; both sides together give 5/8. Adding the central contribution -9/8 gives -1/2, or -0.50, with no units. This is indeed the difference -1/2 obtained directly. The center cannot be omitted merely because W equals 0 there: the derivative of f contributes at that point.

  8. State what the result establishes

    We have an exact equality between a difference of expectations and a Stein residual. Its absolute value is 1/2, with no units. This number concerns the test h(x) = x to the power 4; it is neither a defect rate, nor an error probability, nor an established bound on a distance between distributions.

    To specify a distance, first choose a family H of tests and take the greatest absolute difference over this family. The symbol sup denotes this supremum. Under the assumptions that provide a solution for each test, the method becomes:

    dH(W,Z)=suph∈H|E[Afh(W)]|

    A sufficiently rich family distinguishes distributions; a family that is too limited does not. The tests also need normalization: allowing all their multiples can make the supremum infinite. Our h is unbounded and its slope has no global bound; its difference alone therefore does not give a bound for tests that are bounded or have a bounded slope.

    The counter’s mean and variance agree with those of Z, but this test distinguishes them. Here we have proved neither a convergence rate as the number of inspections increases, nor a fourth moment theorem on chaoses. Stein’s method is the use of the operator; the additional structures used to bound its residuals will be studied in the following units.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the equations. Why must you choose h before f? Recover the central term of the residual, then explain how both calculations give the same difference without replacing W with Z.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “With mean 0 and unit variance, the counter must be normal.”

    These conditions control only the center and the square. The power 4 test gives 5/2 under W and 3 under Z. The distributions are therefore different, despite having the same mean and variance.

    Repeat the test that distinguishes the distributions

  • “Stein states that E[Af(W)] always equals 0.”

    The identity giving 0 applies to the target distribution Z and admissible functions. Under W, the mean is precisely the difference we are seeking. Also check the order f′ − xf: reversing the sign reverses the sign of the difference.

    Revisit the identity and then the Stein equation

  • “The difference 1/2 is a universal bound on probabilities.”

    It concerns a single unbounded function h. A bound on distributions requires a stated family of tests, normalization, and uniform control of the residual. No such control follows from our power alone.

    Examine the scope of the result in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Getting started

A test that does not detect the difference

In the same workshop, replace h with h(x) = x × x. The normal reference value is 1. Try f(x) = −x, check its Stein equation, then calculate E[Af(W)]. Can you conclude that the distribution is normal?

Hint 1

Differentiate −x, then subtract x multiplied by −x.

Hint 2

The score has mean 0 and variance 1, so its mean square is 1.

Af(x)=−1−x(−x)=x2−1

This is indeed h(x) − E[h(Z)]. The expectation of the residual is E[W × W] − 1 = 1 − 1 = 0. The square test does not distinguish the distributions in this example.

We cannot conclude that W is normal: the power 4 test has already given the nonzero difference -1/2. The characterizing identity must hold over a whole class of functions, rather than just f(x) = −x.

Check your reasoning

  • The derivative and the product xf are written separately.
  • The 0 residual is justified using E[W × W], rather than assumed because the target is normal.
  • The conclusion distinguishes agreement on a test from equality of distributions.

Restore an omitted central mass

Return to h(x) = x to the power 4 and f(x) = −x to the power 3 − 3x. Someone keeps only the nonzero values of W and reports E[Af(W)] = 5/8. Recover this sum, identify the omission, and restore the result without starting again from the moment of W.

Hint 1

Calculate f′(0) and 0 × f(0): these are two different terms.

Hint 2

The residual is even. Group the endpoints, the neighbors of the center, and then the center itself.

Compare with the solution

At x = 1, f is -4 and f′ is -6, so r is -2. At x = 2, f is -14, f′ is -15 and xf is -28, so r is 13.

Away from the center, the weighted sum is 2 × (1/16 × 13 + 1/4 × (-2)) = 5/8. This recovers the reported value, but it does not cover the whole distribution.

At the center, f′(0) = -3; the omitted contribution is 3/8 × (-3) = -9/8. The full expectation is 5/8 + (-9/8) = -1/2. The reason for the correction is the nonzero derivative at the center, rather than a change in probability.

Check your reasoning

  • The residuals come from f′ − xf rather than the moment already calculated.
  • Each value is weighted by its exact probability.
  • The central contribution is restored with its sign and the result is -1/2.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and scope of proof

A family of tests and an overstrong conclusion

Still using the same W, let a be a real parameter with no units and hₐ(x) = x to the power 4 + ax × x. Find E[hₐ(Z)], then a polynomial solution fₐ of the Stein equation. Calculate the difference under W as a function of a. Someone concludes that every difference in probabilities is less than or equal to 1/2: examine this conclusion.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Use linearity of expectation and add the solutions for the power 4 test and the square test.

Hint 2

The added term in the difference is a(E[W × W] − E[Z × Z]).

Hint 3

Which tests describe the probability of an event? Are the functions hₐ those tests?

Compare with the solution

The reference value is E[ha(Z)] = 3 + a, since E[Z × Z] = 1. Adding the solutions already checked gives fa(x) = −x to the power 3 − (3 + a)x.

Afa(x)=x4+ax2−(3+a)

The test gives E[ha(W)] = 5/2 + a. The difference is therefore (5/2 + a) − (3 + a) = -1/2, for every real a. The moments are finite in this model, so this combination is admissible.

Even this whole family does not provide the claimed bound on probabilities. A probability is represented by the mean of an indicator function, a function equal to 1 on the event and 0 elsewhere. The ha do not form this family; their part of degree 4 is not even bounded. We would need to choose the required class, solve its equations under the appropriate conditions, and control the residuals uniformly. Our calculation establishes exactly the differences for the stated family, without these additional steps.

Check your reasoning

  • The reference value 3 + a is correctly subtracted to center the test in the equation.
  • The solution fₐ is checked by differentiation and multiplication.
  • The cancellation of the term in a is linked to the shared variance.
  • The absence of a proved uniform bound is explained in terms of the class of tests.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which operator has mean 0 under the standard normal distribution, and why?

Af(x) = f′(x) − xf(x). Integration by parts with φ′ = −xφ gives E[Af(Z)] = 0, if both terms are integrable and fφ tends to 0 at both infinities.

Recall 2How does the chosen function h determine f and the difference to calculate?

Solve Afₕ = h − E[h(Z)]. Then E[h(W)] − E[h(Z)] = E[Afₕ(W)] whenever these expectations exist. The target determines the operator; W stays in the residual.

Recall 3How can you recover the solution for the power 4 without memorizing the polynomial?

The normal reference value is 3. Look for an odd polynomial whose −xf produces x to the power 4. f(x) = −x to the power 3 − 3x works: the terms in x × x cancel in f′ − xf.

Recall 4Why does the central value of the score contribute to the residual?

At the center, Wf(W) is 0, but f′(0) is -3. With probability 3/8, its contribution is -9/8. The full residual is -1/2, identical to the direct difference 5/2 − 3.

Recall 5What must be added to a calculation for a single test to claim an approximation of distributions?

State a sufficiently rich and normalized class of tests, specify the distance it defines, check that the solutions are admissible, then bound all the residuals in this class. Neither exact agreement for the square nor an isolated difference for a power is sufficient.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without support: start from the counter and its standardization; recover the normal identity by integration by parts; center the test h and solve for f; calculate the residual using all the masses; check against the direct difference; finish by naming the class of tests actually covered.

Supplementary reference · calculating with a normal variable and justifying its use

What you understand
Calculating a normal probability and proving that a variable approaches a normal distribution are two distinct questions. Stein’s method addresses the second by reducing it to residuals of test functions.
