Preregistration and horizon

Your goalWrite a verifiable forecast before the outcome: specify the available information, the horizon, the event and how it will be observed.

Before you start
c035-m5 : covariance and conditioning in finite settings · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c090-m5 : stationarity · c053-m5 : proxy, construct and validity · c062-m5 : auditing priors and sensitivity · c092-m1 : physical clock and content counter

✿01 See the idea

You run the reception desk for a workshop. Before the visitors arrive, you write: “At minute 25 after opening, there will be at least 8 people in the queue.” You assign a probability to this statement. If you later move the time to choose a moment when the queue is long, you are no longer checking the statement you announced.

Preregistration means keeping a dated version of the forecast and its rules before knowing the outcome. The document must allow someone else to ask the same question and establish the verdict. A statement such as “it will be busy soon” leaves too many choices open after the fact.

The horizon is the interval between the time you issue the forecast and the target time. You must distinguish this duration, the target time and the time when the observation reaches you. A message received late does not move the time it refers to.

The entire scene and all its values are fictional. We will use a small model of equally likely possibilities, specified for this exercise. It helps us state a coherent probability; it is neither an actual record of visitor numbers nor a method already validated for forecasting a real queue.

The rule to remember

Before seeing the outcome, specify what information may inform the forecast, what it targets, when it targets it and how the verdict will be established. Keep the original if you issue a new version.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Write the reception forecast record, then resolve it

The clock shows minutes elapsed since opening. At t = 10 min, we issue a forecast for H = 3 steps, each lasting Δ = 5 min. The question concerns the queue size at a specific instant, with a threshold of 8 people. Let us calculate the target and the probability, then keep the record before revealing the observation.

  1. Define the question precisely

    t denotes the issue time; T the target time; c the information cutoff, beyond which nothing is used for this forecast. These times are in minutes from the same origin. Here c = t = 10 min. H counts steps, with no physical unit, and Δ is the duration of a step, in minutes. h will denote the physical horizon in minutes.

    X(T) is the number of people waiting at reception exactly at T, excluding people who have already entered the workshop. We assume that a complete, dated count at that instant is possible. E is the event X(T) ≥ 8 people. The sign ≥ includes equality: 8 people are enough. “At T” means neither the total number of arrivals nor “at least once before T”.

    Y encodes the verdict with no unit: 1 if E occurs, 0 otherwise. If the count is missing or does not refer to T, Y remains unknown; this absence is not a verdict of 0. Before the observation, we specify this rule, the identity of the person counting and how their dated record will be kept.

  2. Convert steps to the target time

    Multiplying H by Δ converts a number of steps into a duration. Adding this duration to t then places the target on the shared clock.

    h=H×Δ=3×5=15 min;T=t+h=10+15=25 min

  3. Close off the information before forecasting

    I denotes the set of information admitted at cutoff c. For an observation record, a is its observation time and r its receipt time, in the same unit. We require a ≤ c and r ≤ c: having observed a fact in the past does not mean having received it in time.

    For example, a record observed at a = 9 min but received at r = 12 min is excluded from I, because r exceeds c = 10 min by 2 min. Reintroducing it in a retrospective calculation would change what was available when the forecast was made. The possibilities in the diagram are a fictional model declared at this cutoff; they do not come from this late record.

  4. Assign a probability under a declared model

    p denotes the probability of E conditional on I and the chosen model. P denotes the rule that assigns probabilities in this model; the bar ∣ is read as “given”. p has no unit and lies between 0 and 1. The teaching model specifies 4 possible queue sizes at T: 6, 8, 9 and 11 people. These outcomes are mutually exclusive and cover all the possibilities in this model; each receives mass 1/4, or 0.25.

    The outcomes 8, 9 and 11 satisfy E. There are 1 + 1 = 2, then 2 + 1 = 3. Since the masses are equal, counting the favourable outcomes and dividing by the total gives p. With different masses, we would need to add the favourable masses, rather than just count the boxes.

    p=P(E∣I)=34=0.75

  5. Keep the record before the outcome

    The record contains: issue time 10 min; cutoff 10 min; horizon 3 steps of 5 min, giving 15 min; target 25 min; event “at least 8 people waiting exactly at the target time”; probability 3/4; model of equally likely outcomes and admitted information; source of the count and verdict rule; handling of a missing observation. Each forecast receives an identity that allows it to be retrieved unambiguously.

    We keep this dated version without replacing it before consulting the observation. An amendment is still possible: it has its own date, states what it changes and leaves the original visible. An editable copy whose only date is entered by its author does not, on its own, prove that it existed before the outcome; verification in practice requires a reliable record of its preservation.

  6. Observe without moving the target

    Let us now reveal the fictional continuation: the person counting recorded X(T) = 6 people exactly at 25 min. Their record reaches us at 26 min. The receipt delay is 26 − 25 = 1 min, but the count does refer to the specified target.

    Since 6 < 8, Y = 0. The original still retains p = 3/4: a probability did not promise certainty. The complementary event had probability 1 − 3/4 = 1/4, or 0.25, under the model. This single outcome demonstrates neither good nor poor calibration, meaning lasting agreement between announced probabilities and observed frequencies in comparable cases.

  7. Check the units and that the question is the same

    Independent time check: T − t = 25 − 10 = 15 min; this recovers h = 15 min. The 3 steps are not minutes, and T is not the duration. Probability check: 4 × 1/4 = 1, and 3/4 + 1/4 = 1. Finally, the count used refers to the same place, population and instant as the forecast record.

    The probability describes the assumed model; the verdict describes the fictional observation. Keeping them side by side makes later evaluation possible. Choosing or calculating a score, comparing methods and judging their quality over a series of forecasts require the following units. This calculation measures no actual gain.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “H is 3, so the horizon lasts 3 minutes.”

    H counts steps. Each step lasts 5 min: h = 3 × 5 = 15 min. The target time is then 10 + 15 = 25 min. Write the unit next to each quantity.

    Review the step conversion and the time check

  • “This observation record predates the forecast, so I can use it.”

    Check its receipt time too. The record observed at 9 min was only received at 12 min, after the cutoff of 10 min. An honest reconstruction respects what was accessible at this cutoff.

    Return to closing off the information

  • “The queue eventually exceeded the threshold, so my forecast was verified.”

    The record targeted a queue size at a precise instant. Exceeding the threshold later does not answer that question. Keep the original target and verdict; announce another forecast if you want to address another instant or a threshold crossing before a deadline.

    Audit the target shift in the last exercise

Equality is included: 8 people satisfy “at least 8”. The favourable queue sizes are 8, 9 and 11, giving 3 outcomes. Their total mass is 3 × 1/4 = 3/4, or 0.75.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · defend a chronology

An arriving observation and a shifting target

The initial record targets 25 min. The observation of 6 people at this target arrives at 26 min. At 30 min, the queue contains 9 people. Someone wants to replace the target with 30 min. Someone else had, as early as 15 min and before the observation, prepared a new forecast still targeting 25 min. Explain what must be kept, then calculate the horizon of this last forecast in minutes and steps.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Link each count to its observation time, then compare it with the original target.

Hint 2

With the target unchanged, a new issue time shortens the remaining duration.

Compare with the solution

The observation received at 26 min establishes the verdict at 25 min: 6 < 8, so Y = 0. The 9 people at 30 min exceed the threshold, but concern another target. Moving the original target after the fact would alter the question being evaluated.

The forecast issued at 15 min can target T = 25 min: its physical horizon is 25 − 15 = 10 min, giving 10 ÷ 5 = 2 steps. It retains its own identity, cutoff and probability announced before the outcome. This probability has not been provided here; you must not invent it.

We therefore keep the original and its verdict, as well as the new version actually preserved before the outcome. A missing outcome would have left the verdict unknown; an outcome received late but correctly dated can resolve it. None of these cases authorises choosing the target that gives the preferred result.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the original target of 25 min and verdict of 0.
  • Distinguish receipt delay from moving the target.
  • Find 10 min and 2 steps for the new issue time, without inventing its probability.
  • Explain why a new version announced before the observation is acceptable if it leaves the original visible.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must a record kept before the outcome allow you to reconstruct?

Its issue time, information cutoff, target with its units, exact event, probability and assumptions, then the observation source and verdict rule, including the missing case. The original remains identifiable if a new version is issued.

Recall 2How do you distinguish H, Δ, h and T at our reception desk?

H counts the steps and Δ gives their minutes per step. h = H × Δ is a duration in minutes. T = t + h is the target time since opening, with t being the issue time. For the original: h = 15 min and T = 25 min.

Recall 3Why can an old observation record be excluded from the available information?

It may only have been received after the cutoff. Here, observing at 9 min and receiving at 12 min does not allow the record to be used at the cutoff of 10 min. Observation and receipt must both be compatible with this cutoff.

Recall 4Does the verdict 0 require retroactively correcting p = 3/4?

No. The record describes a probability announced before the observation; the verdict describes what happened. The complementary event had mass 1/4 in the model. We keep both to evaluate a series of forecasts later. A single outcome does not demonstrate their calibration.

Recall 5What separates a new forecast from an opportunistic modification of the original?

The new forecast is identified and preserved at its own issue time, before its outcome, with its rules. It leaves the original visible. Changing the original target or evaluated event after the observation changes the question instead of checking it.

Finish with what an isolated outcome does not allow you to conclude about the quality of the probabilities.
