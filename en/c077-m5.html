Level 2 · C077·M5

Evaluate error, latency and unavailability

Your goalProduce a transcription report that separates word errors, waiting time and requests that received no text, stating each denominator.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

At a library, a kiosk must transcribe an instruction before moving a book in a simulation. We submit 4 separate recordings of the same sentence: “put the book on the table”. A text may arrive quickly with a changed word; another may be accurate but late; a request may receive no text. The movement of the book is not evaluated here.

All traces, durations and limits on this page are fictional and chosen for the calculations. We record no one and contact no service. Every file does contain the expected sentence: silence is not part of this trial.

Evaluation therefore requires several questions. Does the returned text match the written reference? How long did it take to receive it? How many requests provided no text before the limit? The quality of the responses received alone does not answer the last question.

The first diagram sets the start and end events for the stopwatch. The second also keeps the request with no result: removing it from the drawing would already hide part of the problem.

The rule to remember

Keep all requests in the report; compare words and delays over a stated set; count missing results separately against a fixed deadline.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The library kiosk report

The normalized reference is “put the book on the table”, containing 6 words. The 4 files are submitted separately under the same fictional conditions. A returns “put the glass on the table”; B, “put the book on table”; C, “put the small book on the table”. D provides no result before 3000 ms. Let us calculate the report without hiding D.

  1. Set the protocol before counting

    A request is counted as soon as it is handed to the adapter. Here, an eligible result is a nonempty final text received no later than Δ = 3000 ms after that handoff. Eligible means measurable, not correct. An explicit error, an empty text or no response by the deadline would classify the request as having no result for this trial.

    We keep the same audio format, engine, settings and timing boundaries. To compare words, we remove punctuation, convert to lowercase and split at spaces. Accents remain significant. The reference was written before the responses. Changing these conventions would change what the rate measures.

    A identifies the request in the log, but the symbol M will denote the total number of requests. R will count eligible results and F requests without an eligible result. M, R and F are counts with no physical unit; M = 4, F = 1, so R = M − F = 3.

  2. Align the words before adding the errors

    Alignment means matching the words in the reference against those in the returned text. We seek the smallest number of operations: replacing a word, deleting an expected word or adding a word absent from the reference. Each operation costs 1; keeping a word costs 0.

    For A, “book” becomes “glass”: 1 substitution. For B, the expected segment “on the table” becomes “on table”: 1 deletion of “the”. For C, “the book” becomes “the small book”: 1 insertion of “small”. All other words remain in order. Each text differs from the reference and an operation is enough: fewer operations are impossible.

    S will count substitutions, D deletions and I insertions over eligible results. These are numbers of operations, not durations. Here S = 1, D = 1, I = 1; the total E = S + D + I = 2 + 1 = 3 operations. The symbol D for this count does not identify request D in the log.

  3. Choose the correct number of reference words

    N is the number of expected words in the files whose text is eligible. Each reference contains 6 words: for A and B, 6 + 6 = 12 words; including C, 12 + 6 = 18 words. We divide neither by requests nor by words produced, since deletion and insertion change the number of words produced.

    T is the word error rate and has no unit. Here its definition covers only eligible results, so it must be reported with R and M.

    T=S+D+IN=318=16

    As a percentage: T × 100 = 16.67 %. This is a ratio of word operations, not the percentage of entirely correct sentences. Many insertions could even make it exceed 100 %. If N were 0, this division would be undefined.

  4. Measure latency between two observed events

    For a given request, te is the instant when the complete file is handed over and tᵣ the instant when its final text becomes available. They are recorded in milliseconds using the same monotonic clock: a clock that measures durations without moving backwards when civil time is corrected. L is their difference, in ms. The measurement includes any internal waiting and processing within the chosen boundaries.

    L=tr−te=700−100=600 ms

    The clock origin has no meaning for the reader; subtraction removes it. B gives 900 ms and C 1500 ms. These durations are not the lengths of the audio recordings. For D, tᵣ was not observed: its response latency remains unknown; the trial records only a wait without a result up to 3000 ms.

  5. Calculate and check the mean delay of received texts

    The mean divides the sum of observed latencies among the R corresponding texts. First add A and B: 600 + 900 = 1500 ms. Add C: 1500 + 1500 = 3000 ms. Divide by R = 3: the mean is 1000 ms, or 1.0 s, since 1000 ms = 1 s.

    Independent check: (600 − 1000) + (900 − 1000) + (1500 − 1000) = 0 ms. The mean does lie between the smallest and largest latencies.

    Writing 3000 ÷ 4 = 750 ms would amount to assigning 0 latency to D. This number is not the mean of observed responses. Assigning the deadline to D would give another measurement, a trial closure time, which would need to be named as such.

  6. Report unavailability over all requests

    U is the fraction of requests with no eligible result before Δ. It has no unit: the numerator F counts missing results and the denominator M counts all attempts, including those with missing results.

    U=FM=14;U×100=25 %

    Check: R + F = 3 + 1 = 4 requests. The log accounts for every row. This U describes the requests in this trial and its deadline; it is neither a fraction of machine operating time nor a guaranteed probability for the next request.

    The cause still needs diagnosis: an unavailable engine, rejected input, an error and a missed deadline are different things. The count may group them for a usage report, but the trace must retain the reason for each. A low mean latency over received responses alone can coexist with many missing results.

  7. Report a result that can actually be interpreted

    Fictional report at Δ = 3000 ms: 3 eligible texts from 4 requests; 3 operations for 18 expected words, giving 16.67 % error over received responses; mean latency of received responses 1000 ms; 1 missing result out of 4, or 25 %. Keep the individual latencies: the mean hides their spread.

    Each rate describes exactly the stated set. D is excluded from the error rate conditional on receiving a response, but remains in U. If we want to penalize D as an empty transcription, we must state this change and add its expected words to both the deletions and the denominator; the application exercise calculates this.

    This small trial provides neither a reliability guarantee nor a real improvement. It also does not measure the seriousness of the changed word: confusing “book” and “glass” counts as a substitution, even though this difference may change the expected action.

  8. Keep the same questions if the kiosk then speaks

    If the same kiosk then reads the instruction aloud, we must still separate the quality of the sound produced, the wait until sound begins and missing playback. Receiving an audio file or starting asynchronous playback does not prove that a sound was heard. For latency to the first sound, the end boundary must be that event actually observed.

    Our word error rate evaluates transcription in this trial; it measures neither the naturalness nor the intelligibility of speech synthesis. Those qualities would require another protocol. We stop here at the error–latency–unavailability report, without training a model or testing a real voice pipeline.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Keeping only the received texts is enough: failures have no score.”

    Received texts can define the scope of the error rate, but all requests must remain in the log. Count F out of M and report R with the error rate; otherwise an engine that rarely responds can appear excellent.

    Review the report and its denominators

  • “D has no duration, so I assign it 0 ms.”

    A missing duration is not a 0 wait. No text from D was observed before 3000 ms; keep its latency unknown and its status missing. Divide the sum of observed delays by 3, then report the missing result separately.

    Find the stopwatch boundaries and the missing-result row again

  • “A technical response without an error proves a good transcription.”

    Check in turn for text within the deadline, differences from the reference and the scope of the technical status. A response may be empty or contain “glass” instead of “book”. The milestone shows why reading only an indicator can mislead.

Exercise 2 · Application · explicitly change the convention

Penalize the missing result as empty text

Using the same log at 3000 ms, we now decide to treat D as an empty transcription to calculate an error rate over all requests. How many additional deletions must be counted? Recalculate the numerator, denominator and percentage. Does U change?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

An empty output deletes all the words in its reference; D contains the same expected sentence as the other files.

Hint 2

Add the words from D to the deletions and to the reference words. The deadline and the list of results actually received remain identical.

Compare with the solution

D adds 6 deletions. The numerator becomes 3 + 6 = 9 operations. The denominator becomes 18 + 6 = 24 expected words.

The new ratio is 9/24 = 3/8, or 37.5 %. This result is consistent with its convention; it does not silently replace the 16.67 % rate calculated over received responses alone.

U remains 1/4 = 25 %: we have received no additional text. A conventional penalty for D creates neither a response nor an observed latency.

Check your reasoning

  • Add 6 to the numerator and denominator for distinct reasons.
  • Obtain 37.5 % while naming the new convention.
  • Keep 25 % missing results and an unknown latency for D.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · discuss an apparent improvement

A shorter deadline does not make the engine faster

Read the same traces again with the deadline reduced to 1200 ms and the initial convention: error and latency over eligible texts only. Without running a new trial, determine the eligible requests, U, the error rate and mean latency. Respond to the claim: “The mean has fallen, so the engine has improved.”

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare each previously observed delay with the new deadline. The text from C arrives after this limit.

Hint 2

Recalculate the report over A and B for the words and mean, but keep all requests for U.

Compare with the solution

A and B remain eligible, giving 2 responses. C arrives in 1500 ms, after 1200 ms; D remains missing. There are therefore 2 missing results under this new criterion: U = 2/4 = 50 %.

A and B contribute 2 operations for 12 expected words: 1/6, or 16.67 %. Their latencies sum to 1500 ms; the mean becomes 1500/2 = 750 ms.

No individual delay has changed. The mean has fallen because the slowest text has left the measured set; missing results have risen from 25 % to 50 %. Here the error rate over received responses is unchanged. This selection by deadline demonstrates no engine improvement.

Check your reasoning

  • Classify C as too late for the new deadline, while acknowledging its observed response in the original trace.
  • Obtain 50 % missing results, 16.67 % error over received responses and 750 ms mean latency conditional on receiving a response.
  • Explain the change in the measured population instead of attributing an improvement to the engine.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you reconstruct the word error rate and its scope?

Define normalization, align each text with its reference by minimizing substitutions, deletions and insertions, add these operations, then divide by the expected words in the stated set. Say whether missing results are excluded from the rate or penalized as empty texts.

Recall 2Why does the length of an audio file not give its transcription latency?

Audio duration measures the length of the sound. Our latency is a difference between instants on the same monotonic clock, from handing over the complete file to receiving the final text. Without an observed response event, this latency is unknown.

Recall 3Which denominator keeps requests with no result visible?

The number of all submitted requests. U divides requests without eligible text before the deadline by this total. This report depends on the criterion and deadline; it does not measure availability as a fraction of operating time.

Recall 4Why can shortening the deadline lower the mean without speeding up the engine?

Slow responses leave the eligible set. The mean over the remaining responses falls, while the fraction without a result on time rises. Compare the measured sets and missing results before claiming an improvement.

Recall 5What must be checked after a technical response reported as having no error?

The presence of the expected result within the deadline, its content compared with a reference and the final event actually observed. A response status, a produced file and a heard sound do not prove the same thing.
