Data collection plans

Your goalWrite a feasible data collection plan, allocate a sample across workshop sessions and distinguish planned measurements from those obtained.

Before you start
c003-m5 : scientific notation · c006-m5 : composition · c009-m5 : reading notes · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c024-m1 : population and sample

✿01 See the idea

You want to describe waiting times at the entrance to a workshop run by the local council on Wednesday. The registration list has closed and contains 40 people in the morning and 20 in the afternoon. You can track 12 arrivals. Observing only in the morning would make collection convenient, but would never show what happens in the afternoon.

A data collection plan is a rule written before observations begin: who can be selected, how to select them, when to observe and what to record. Here, the population consists of everyone registered for that Wednesday; the unit observed is a person on arrival. The planned sample is the list of people drawn; the achieved sample contains those whose waiting time was actually measured.

The sampling frame is the list used for the draw. Here we assume that it contains everyone registered, without duplicates, with their session recorded, and that the session lists do not overlap. Drawing at random from an incomplete list does not bring back the people left out. A selection rule can systematically skew observations: this is collection bias.

A census would mean tracking everyone registered. To stay within our capacity, we instead choose a separate random draw in each session, selecting the same fraction. This arrangement is called a proportionate stratified sampling plan: a stratum is a group defined before the draw, here the session. We will build this plan without calculating probabilities.

The setting, counts and final accounting are entirely fictional. We do not yet have any waiting times measured in the field. The diagrams explain an arrangement; they show neither an improvement in reception nor a finding about local residents.

The rule to remember

Define the population, list and selection rule; apply the same measurement definition; keep the distinction between planned and observed.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Preparing for Wednesday arrivals

The question is: what waiting times do people registered for this Wednesday experience on arrival? Let us write the protocol before observing. The capacity is 12 people and the lists contain 40 people registered in the morning and 20 in the afternoon. We assume that sessions do not overlap and that it is practically possible to track everyone selected.

  1. Define who is included in the count

    The target population is limited to people registered for this Wednesday, even if some do not attend. The register is closed before the draw; we check it for duplicates and check the assigned sessions. We will not be able to draw conclusions about all Wednesdays or unregistered visitors.

    N is the total count, Nm the morning count and Na the afternoon count; their counting unit is a person. Since the lists are disjoint and cover everyone registered, we can add their counts.

    N=Nm+Na=40+20=60 personnes

  2. Allocate capacity across both sessions

    n denotes the planned size of the draw, in people; f is the fraction selected, with no unit. Dividing n by N gives the share of registered people that our capacity allows us to select. The percentage expresses this same share out of 100.

    f=nN=1260=1/5=0.20

    This represents 20 % of registered people. We apply the same fraction to each session: nm = f × Nm and na = f × Na. nm and na are the numbers of people to draw from the corresponding lists.

    Morning: (1/5) × 40 = 8 people. Afternoon: (1/5) × 20 = 4 people. These results are whole numbers: no rounding is needed here. In another situation, a rounding rule that preserves the total would have to be set before the draw.

    Capacity check: 8 + 4 = 12 people. Allocation check: 8/40 = 1/5 and 4/20 = 1/5. The total fits the capacity and each session contributes the same fraction of its list.

  3. Write a selection procedure someone else can repeat

    For the morning, prepare 1 slip per registered person, identical apart from the identifier it carries. Mix the slips out of sight and draw 8 without putting them back. For the afternoon, repeat with the other list and draw 4 slips. Without replacement means that the same person cannot be drawn again within their session.

    We assume fair mixing and no selection of slips based on their contents. Keep the version of the lists, the rule and the identifiers drawn: repeating the protocol means being able to apply and check the same procedure, without necessarily obtaining the same people in a new draw.

    Choosing the first arrivals would be convenience sampling: it would depend on arrival time. Taking volunteers would let people select themselves. Setting counts by session without a random draw would merely set quotas; our plan also specifies the selection mechanism within each session.

  4. Define the observed waiting time precisely

    The variable W is the duration between crossing a marker at the entrance to the queue and the start of assistance at the reception desk, in seconds. Record 1 visit per selected person, their session and the times A and B, read from the same clock. A denotes arrival at the marker; B denotes the start of assistance. The times are expressed in seconds from the same origin, kept throughout the visit.

    The observer also tracks a person who finds no queue: the times may coincide and W then equals 0 s. The observer does not start the stopwatch only when the queue looks long. The same marker and the same measurement endpoint apply in each session.

    W=B−A=215−125=90 s

    For a fictional visit with a complete measurement, arrival is at 125 s and assistance begins at 215 s. Subtracting the times removes their common origin and gives 90 s. Since 60 s make 1 min, this gives 90/60 = 1.50 min. Reverse check: 125 + 90 = 215 s. A negative duration would indicate an inconsistency to investigate.

  5. Plan for absences before knowing the waiting times

    The protocol keeps everyone drawn in the accounting: complete measurement, absence, refusal or incomplete tracking. If a person does not attend the workshop, there is no visit: W is not applicable. For a visit that did occur, if the start or end time is missing, W remains unknown. None of these cases is replaced by 0 s; we do not spontaneously choose someone nearby to fill the gap.

    This plan provides for no replacements. We publish selected counts and complete measurements separately. Any alternative replacement protocol would have to be written in advance; it could not remove the reasons for missing data.

  6. Account for the results and limit the conclusion

    The fictional final accounting includes 2 people without a complete measurement in the morning and 1 in the afternoon. This leaves 8 − 2 = 6 measurements in the morning and 4 − 1 = 3 in the afternoon. In total: 6 + 3 = 9 complete measurements.

    Check using the complement: 2 + 1 = 3 people without a measurement; 9 + 3 = 12 selected people. Nothing has been removed from the accounting.

    Here r denotes the number of complete measurements, counted in people. The fraction of registered people actually measured is r/N = 9/60 = 3/20, or 15 %. The share of planned measurements actually obtained is r/n = 9/12 = 3/4, or 75 %. The denominators distinguish the target population from the planned draw.

    These percentages describe only this fictional accounting. They do not measure the quality of the plan. Even though the session proportions are preserved here, people might refuse tracking because they are in a hurry, and experience different waiting times. The initial draw does not solve this problem. We can describe the observed waiting times while reporting the selection and absences; whether they represent registered people who attended the workshop remains open to discussion. People who did not attend remain counted separately: we do not assign them a waiting time.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, dictate the plan to another observer: who can be drawn, how to choose, where to start and end the measurement, and what to retain if it is missing. Then explain why 12 selected people give only 9 known waiting times here.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Taking the first arrivals is the same as drawing at random.”

    Convenience does not define randomness. The first arrivals might experience a different queue. Use the complete lists and the separate draws described in the example, then observe the selected people on arrival.

    Review the selection rule

  • “Taking the same number of people in each session necessarily preserves the proportions.”

    The lists have different sizes. Compare the fractions selected, as well as the numbers drawn. The proportional allocation in the example gives 8 people in the morning and 4 in the afternoon; the final exercise examines equal counts.

    Compare the fractions in the final exercise

  • “An empty box means 0 seconds of waiting.”

    A waiting time of 0 requires a complete observation with coinciding times. Interrupted tracking leaves an unknown duration; a person who did not attend has no visit to measure. Keep the reason and retain the person in the accounting of the draw.

    Review how absences are handled

Exercise 3 · Comparison and argument

Same total, different plan

For the original register, a colleague proposes a random draw of 6 people per session. She claims: “The total remains 12, so it is the same proportional plan.” Calculate the fractions selected and respond. Then return to the original accounting: do 9 complete measurements prove that the known waiting times describe everyone registered?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Divide the number drawn by the number registered for the relevant session.

Hint 2

Compare the fractions, then distinguish the initial draw from the possible reasons for a missing measurement.

Compare with the solution

The total fits: 6 + 6 = 12 people. But the morning fraction is 6/40 = 3/20 = 0.15, while the afternoon fraction is 6/20 = 3/10 = 0.30.

The afternoon fraction is 2 times the morning fraction. The allocation is therefore not proportional to the counts in the register. A draw by session with equal counts could serve another objective, for example examining each session with the same number of people; this choice would have to be stated. It must not be presented as our proportional plan.

In the original accounting, 9 measurements out of 12 planned leave 3 people without a complete measurement. We must distinguish people who did not attend from visits that were not measured. Even an achieved allocation that preserves session proportions does not guarantee that measured visits resemble unmeasured visits in their waiting times. We must examine the reasons and limit the conclusion; neither the total nor the fraction obtained proves an absence of bias.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain 3/20 and 3/10, using the count for their respective session as the denominator.
  • You refute the claim that the plans are identical without declaring every plan with equal counts invalid.
  • You distinguish people who did not attend from visits that were not measured, without inventing a duration.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must you define before observing arrivals at the workshop?

The population and its list, the unit observed, the selection rule, the sessions observed, the definition of waiting time and how missing visits are handled. Another observer must be able to apply these rules.

Recall 2How should you allocate the 12 places between 40 people registered in the morning and 20 in the afternoon?

The register contains 60 people in total. The common fraction is 12/60 = 1/5; multiply it by each count, giving 8 in the morning and 4 in the afternoon. Check the total and the fractions by session, then draw at random from each list.

Recall 3Why is the word “random” insufficient to guarantee suitable data collection?

You must specify how the draw is made and from which list. People missing from the list cannot be chosen; those without a measurement after the draw may still differ from those observed. A draw does not guarantee the exact proportions of all characteristics.

Recall 4What is the difference between a waiting time of 0 and an unknown waiting time?

A waiting time of 0 is observed: arrival at the marker and the start of assistance coincide, so W = 0 s. If a time is missing, W is unknown; keep the person and the reason in the accounting without inventing a duration.

Recall 5What do 9/60 and 9/12 compare in the fictional accounting?

The first ratio compares people measured with everyone registered: 15 %. The second compares them with the people selected: 75 %. Neither proves that the known waiting times are representative.
