Level 4 · C228·M4

Ito’s formula with jumps

Your goalWrite Ito’s formula for a function of a level that drifts, fluctuates and jumps, derive its generator, then calculate and check this mean rate of change in a fictional example.

Before you start
c227-m5 : Ito-Tanaka · c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c173-m5 : completeness · c228-m1 : characteristic function · c228-m2 : Levy-Khintchine · c228-m3 : jump decomposition

✿01 See the idea

Consider a tank whose level X is measured in centimetres. It rises slowly and steadily, fluctuates continuously under small disturbances and, from time to time, a bucket empties into the tank all at once: the level jumps. All values in this scenario are fictional; they are used for a calculation, rather than taken from readings.

We are interested in a function f of the level. Our function will be the square, f(x) = x², in cm²: it can be interpreted as a fictional “energy” of the level. The question is: how fast does its mean change? Without jumps, Ito’s formula from C227·M5 gives two effects: the drift acts along the slope f′, and the continuous noise adds a curvature term proportional to f″.

With a jump, the slope rule is no longer enough. The level does not slide from x to x + a: it reaches it all at once. The exact effect on f is therefore the difference f(x + a) − f(x), rather than the slope multiplied by the jump. For a curved function, these two numbers differ, and the second diagram shows the gap.

One point remains. Jumps arrive at random, at a mean rate λ per hour. Their mean effect on f is therefore λ × [f(x + a) − f(x)] per hour. Accounting for this mean rate is called compensation: we subtract the predictable part of the jumps, leaving a random component with mean 0.

The scope is deliberately limited: a Brownian motion, a constant drift and a single type of jump, with fixed size and constant intensity. Random jump sizes and more general intensities are outside the scope here. This unit covers neither the spectral truncation of C166·M5 nor the completeness of C173·M5; incompleteness will be covered in the next unit of the chapter.

The rule to remember

Ito’s formula with jumps: between jumps, drift and curvature act as usual; each jump contributes f(Xbefore + a) − f(Xbefore), averaged at rate λ.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A fictional tank: the mean rate of change of the squared level

The initial level is x₀ = 5 cm. The drift is μ = 1 cm/h, the continuous fluctuation coefficient is σ = 4 cm/√h, and jumps arrive at intensity λ = 2 h⁻¹, each with size a = 3 cm. For f(x) = x², let us calculate the mean rate of change of f(X) at t = 0, then check it by another method. These values are fictional.

  1. State the model, symbols and assumptions

    The level follows the model below, where t is time in hours. x0 is the known initial level. μ is the drift, in cm/h. σ controls the continuous disturbances, in cm/√h, so σ² = 16 cm²/h. Wt is a standard Brownian motion: its increments are independent, have mean 0 and have variance equal to the elapsed time in hours. Nt counts the jumps up to t: it is a Poisson process with intensity λ, in h⁻¹.

    Xt=x0+μt+σWt+aNt

    Over a short time h, the probability of at least one jump is 1 − exp(−λh), close to λh. Each jump adds exactly a, in cm.

    Assumptions: W and N are independent; λ and a are constant; there is only one type of jump. We write Xs- for the level just before time s, the left limit: a jump at s takes it from Xs- to Xs- + a. We use Xs- because this level is known before the jump arrives.

  2. Measure a single jump in f(x) = x²

    Suppose a jump occurs when the level is x0. Before: f(5) = 5 × 5 = 25 cm². After: the level is 5 + 3 = 8 cm, so f = 8 × 8 = 64 cm². The exact change is 64 − 25 = 39 cm².

    The slope rule gives a different result. Here f′(x) = 2x, so f′(x0) = 10 cm, and 10 × 3 = 30 cm². The gap from the exact change is 39 − 30 = 9 cm², namely a². This is exactly the effect of the curvature of f, which is 2 for the square function.

    (x+a)2−x2=2ax+a2

  3. Write Ito’s formula with jumps and take the mean jump contribution

    Between jumps, the continuous Ito formula applies. At each jump time, the change f(Xs- + a) − f(Xs-) is added. This gives the following formula, where the final sum is finite because there are only finitely many jumps on [0, t].

    f(Xt)=f(x0)+∫0t[μf′(Xs−)+12σ2f″(Xs−)]ds+∫0tσf′(Xs−)dWs+∑s≤t, saut en s[f(Xs−+a)−f(Xs−)]

    Set g(x) = f(x + a) − f(x). Jumps arrive at intensity λ, so the sum of the g(Xs-) splits into a predictable part, λ g(Xs-) ds, and an integral with respect to Ns − λs. The latter has mean 0 because g is evaluated before the jump (Xs-) and remains integrable here.

    g(x)=f(x+a)−f(x)
    ∑s≤tg(Xs−)=∫0tλg(Xs−)ds+∫0tg(Xs−)d(Ns−λs)

    The integral with respect to W also has mean 0. For f(x) = x², X has finite moments, which justifies these claims about the means. Taking the mean leaves the following formula, where L is the generator.

    Ef(Xt)=f(x0)+E∫0tLf(Xs)ds
    Lf(x)=μf′(x)+12σ2f″(x)+λ[f(x+a)−f(x)]

    At time 0, X equals x0 with certainty: the rate of change of E f(X) at t = 0 is therefore L f(x0), in cm²/h.

  4. Calculate the three contributions with their units

    For f(x) = x², f′(x0) = 10 cm and f″ = 2. The drift gives μ f′(x0) = 1 × 10 = 10 cm²/h. The curvature gives ½σ² f″ = 8 × 2 = 16 cm²/h. The jumps give λ × [f(x0 + a) − f(x0)] = 2 × 39 = 78 cm²/h.

    Lf(x0)=μf′(x0)+12σ2f″(x0)+λ[f(x0+a)−f(x0)]
    1×10+8×2+2×39=10+16+78=104 cm²/h

    The three contributions have the same unit, cm²/h, so they can be added. The continuous contribution is 26 cm²/h, and the jump contribution is 78 cm²/h. The total rate is 104 cm²/h.

  5. See what the slope rule misses

    If we had replaced the jump by the slope times the jump size, the jump contribution would have been λ × 30 = 2 × 30 = 60 cm²/h instead of 78 cm²/h. The gap is 18 cm²/h, namely λa²: the number of jumps per hour multiplies the gap for a single jump. This omitted contribution is therefore proportional to the intensity.

  6. Check using the mean and variance

    Another method uses no Ito formula. Under this model, Nt has mean λt and the same variance. Thus E[Xt] = x0 + (μ + λa)t: the mean rises at 1 + 6 = 7 cm/h. The variance of Xt is (σ² + λa²)t: it grows at 16 + 18 = 34 cm²/h.

    EXt2=EXt2+VarXt

    At t = 0, the mean is x0 and its slope is 7. The rate of change of E[X²] is 2 × x0 × 7 + 34 = 70 + 34 = 104 cm²/h, the same number as 104: this checks the calculation.

    Limiting cases: if λ were 0, only the continuous contribution of 26 cm²/h would remain, giving Ito’s formula without jumps. If a were 0, the term f(x + a) − f(x) would likewise vanish. This result describes the fictional model; it says nothing about a real system.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A jump is handled like everything else: multiply the slope f′ by the jump size.”

    The slope describes an infinitesimal change. A jump is finite: we need f(x + a) − f(x). For the square function, the gap from the slope rule is a² per jump, giving λa² per hour on average.

    Repeat the single-jump calculation and the gap calculation

  • “Jumps are random, so their mean effect is 0 and λ does not enter the drift.”

    A fixed-size jump is not centred: Nₜ has mean λt. Only Nₜ − λt has mean 0. The mean of X rises at μ + λa per hour, as the check of the calculation shows.

    See the martingale level exercise

  • “The rate calculated at t = 0 gives E f(X) at any time: E f(X) = f(x₀) + rate × t.”

    The rate is a slope at the initial time. After that, X moves, so the rate changes: for the square function, E[X²] also contains a term (μ + λa)² t², equal to 49 times t² in this scenario. Do not extend the slope without this correction.

    Reread the intuition for the rate

Hint 1

Which contributions depend on λ, and which do not?

Compare with the solution

The drift and curvature do not depend on λ: their sum remains 26 cm²/h. Only the jump contribution changes: 4 × 39 = 156 cm²/h. The rate is therefore 26 + 156 = 182 cm²/h.

Check: λ was multiplied by 2, so the jump contribution was too: 2 × 78 = 156. The rate depends linearly on λ; multiplying λ does not multiply the whole rate by the same factor.

Exercise 3 · Reasoning

Choose the drift so that the level is a martingale

The example’s values of σ, λ, a and x₀ stay unchanged, but the drift μ can be chosen freely. First, with f(x) = x, find μ so that the rate of change of the mean of X is 0. Then, with this μ, recalculate the rate of change of E[X²] and compare it with σ² + λa².

Lf(x)=μ+λa pour f(x) = x

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For f(x) = x, what are f′ and f″?

Hint 2

With this μ, the drift contribution for the square becomes negative: treat it as a loss.

Compare with the solution

For f(x) = x, f′ = 1 and f″ = 0: this leaves L f = μ + λa. This rate is 0 if μ = −λa = −6 cm/h: the negative drift exactly compensates for the mean gain from jumps. The mean of X no longer changes; under the integrability assumptions stated above, X is then a martingale.

For the square with this μ: the drift is −6 × 10 = −60 cm²/h, the curvature contributes 16 cm²/h and the jumps contribute 78 cm²/h. The rate is −60 + 16 + 78 = 34 cm²/h.

Comparison: 34 is exactly σ² + λa². The level no longer drifts on average, but E[X²] still grows because of the noise and the jumps.

Check your reasoning

  • Find μ = −λa with its unit.
  • Treat the drift contribution for the square as a negative value in the sum.
  • Compare with the result σ² + λa² in the same unit, cm²/h.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1For a jump of size a from x, what is the effect on f(X)?

f(x + a) − f(x), rather than f′(x) × a: the two differ when f is curved.

Recall 2What is the mean rate of change of E f(X) at x for drift, Brownian noise and fixed-size jumps?

L f(x) = μ f′(x) + ½σ² f″(x) + λ [f(x + a) − f(x)], in units of f per hour.

Recall 3Why does λa enter the drift of the mean of X?

The jumps do not have mean 0: Nₜ has mean λt. Only Nₜ − λt has mean 0; the mean of X therefore rises at μ + λa per hour.

Recall 4Which assumptions does this calculation use?

W and N are independent, λ and a are constant, there is only one type of jump, and there is enough integrability for the stochastic integrals to have mean 0.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without looking: write the model X = x₀ + μt + σW + aN and its assumptions; state Ito’s formula with the jump term f(Xₛ₋ + a) − f(Xₛ₋); compensate N by λt to isolate a component with mean 0; derive L f; calculate each contribution with its unit; check using the mean and variance; interpret the rate as a slope at the initial time, rather than as a value at any time.
