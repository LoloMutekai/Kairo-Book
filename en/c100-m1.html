Level 2 · C100·M1

Predictive regression

Your goalFit a line to training readings, predict a held-out reading, and assess the error against a baseline with no slope, distinguishing fitting from prediction.

Before you start
c067-m5 : pipeline · c059-m6 : discrete deterministic Bellman · c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics

✿01 See the idea

A small water bath is warming slowly. A thermometer has provided 4 readings, one per minute, from time 0 to 3 min. We want to say what temperature it will show at time 4 min before taking that reading. The values in this scene are fictional: they are for calculations by hand and do not describe any real device.

Prediction does not mean guessing the future. It means choosing a simple rule from past readings, a line whose slope is expressed in °C per minute, then applying it at a time it has never seen. We choose the line that makes the sum of squared vertical deviations from the training readings as small as possible: this is the method of least squares.

Two actions must be distinguished. Fitting means choosing the slope and intercept using the training readings. Prediction means applying this line at a time whose reading was held out, then comparing. Only this comparison measures prediction: the line agrees with its own points because those points were used to choose it.

We also need a baseline with no slope: always predict the mean of the training readings, 22 °C. If the line does no better than this baseline on held-out readings, its slope adds no predictive value.

This unit does not establish the general quality of a model: a single held-out time illustrates the mechanism without measuring that quality. Coefficient penalties and the bias–variance tradeoff are covered in the following units of the chapter; we do not use them here.

The rule to remember

Fit using only the training readings: slope = sum of cross-products of deviations divided by the sum of squared time deviations, with the line passing through the mean point. Predict at a held-out time, then assess the error against the training mean baseline.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Predicting the reading at minute 4

Fictional water bath readings, in °C, at times 0, 1, 2, and 3 min: 20, 21, 23, and 24 °C. The reading at 4 min is held out: it is 26 °C, but it must not be used to fit the line. Let us predict it, then assess the error.

  1. Name the quantities and state the assumptions

    t is the reading time, in minutes; y is the temperature read, in °C; n = 4 is the number of training readings. The line we seek is written ŷ = b + a t: ŷ ("y hat") is the predicted temperature, a is the slope in °C per minute, and b is the y-intercept in °C, meaning the value of the line at the time origin.

    y^=b+a·t

    Assumptions: over the range of training times, from 0 to 3 min, the temperature follows an approximately straight line; the held-out reading comes from the same bath and thermometer; at least two training times differ, otherwise the slope is undefined. We add no probability distribution: choosing the line by least squares is purely algebraic.

  2. Center the readings on their means

    Mean time: t̄ = (0 + 1 + 2 + 3) ÷ 4 = 6 ÷ 4 = 1.5 min. Mean reading: ȳ = (20 + 21 + 23 + 24) ÷ 4 = 88 ÷ 4 = 22 °C.

    ui=ti−t¯, vi=yi−y¯

    The time deviations u are -1.5, -0.5, 0.5, and 1.5 min; the temperature deviations v are -2, -1, 1, and 2 °C. Check: in each series, the deviations cancel out and their sum is 0. Reason for centering: the mean point becomes the origin for the deviations, and the slope no longer depends on the intercept.

  3. Calculate the slope

    Choosing a means minimizing the sum of squared vertical deviations between the readings and the line. Once the line is constrained to pass through the mean point, this sum is written Σ (v − a u)²; setting its derivative with respect to a to 0 gives a Σ u² = Σ u v. The sums are taken over the 4 training readings.

    a=∑iuivi∑iui2

    Products u v, in min·°C: 3.0, 0.5, 0.5, and 3.0; their sum, the sum of cross-products, is 7 min·°C. Squares u², in min²: 2.25, 0.25, 0.25, and 2.25; their sum is 5 min². A positive product indicates that the time deviation and reading deviation have the same sign: it pushes the slope upward.

    a=75=1.4 °C/min

  4. Make the line pass through the mean point

    Setting the derivative with respect to b to 0 requires the vertical training deviations to cancel out: the line therefore passes through the mean point (t̄, ȳ). This gives b = ȳ − a t̄.

    b=22−1.4×1.5=22−2.1=19.9 °C

    The fixed line is therefore ŷ = 19.9 + 1.4 t, with t in minutes and ŷ in °C. From here on, it stays unchanged.

  5. Predict at the held-out time

    y^=19.9+1.4×4=19.9+5.6=25.5 °C

    The time 4 min was included in neither the means nor the sums: the prediction is made without it. The units agree, because a slope in °C per minute multiplied by minutes gives °C.

  6. Compare with the reading and the baseline

    The error is e = y − ŷ, where y is the held-out reading: e = 26 − 25.5 = 0.5 °C. It is positive: the line predicted slightly below the reading. The baseline with no slope predicts the training mean, 22 °C, and has an error of 26 − 22 = 4 °C.

    At this time, the line has an error of 0.5 °C compared with 4 °C for the baseline. This finding concerns a single reading: it demonstrates the method without measuring the quality of the model.

  7. Check the result

    Mean point: 19.9 + 1.4 × 1.5 = 22 °C, the mean of the readings, as required. Sign and order of magnitude: the readings rise from 20 to 24 °C between 0 and 3 min, so we expect a positive slope of the same order as this difference divided by this duration; 1.4 °C/min is reasonable. Units: minutes for t, °C for ŷ, °C/min for a.

    This unit’s calculs.py file also cross-checks the slope using the normal equations, verifies that the vertical training deviations cancel out, and checks that nearby lines leave a larger sum of squares. These checks do not replace holding out new readings.

Explain it in your own words

Why must the reading at 4 min be excluded from calculating the slope, even if it would bring the line closer to that point? Also explain why the error is assessed against the training mean.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The least-squares line passes through all the training points.”

    It passes through the mean point and balances the vertical deviations without making each one 0. Return to the step that positions the line: only the mean point is guaranteed.

    Review the intercept calculation

  • “Good agreement with the training readings proves good prediction.”

    These points were used to choose the slope and intercept: some agreement is built in. Only a held-out reading, compared with the baseline, measures prediction.

    Go to the exercise on holding out readings

  • “The line remains valid at any time.”

    It has only seen times from 0 to 3 min. Far from this range, as at 10 min, the calculation gives a number with nothing to support it.

    Review the extrapolation exercise

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

Predicting far beyond the training range

Using the line from the example (slope 1.4 °C/min, intercept 19.9 °C), calculate the predicted temperature at time 10 min. Compare this time with the training range, then state what you can claim.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Apply ŷ = b + a t: first the product, then the sum.

Hint 2

Compare 10 with the training times, from 0 to 3 min.

Compare with the solution

Product: 1.4 × 10 = 14 °C. Sum: 19.9 + 14 = 33.9 °C.

The time 10 min is far beyond the last training time, 3 min: no reading supports this result. We can state what the line calculates, but cannot claim that the bath will reach this temperature.

Check your reasoning

  • Product followed by sum, with the units °C/min, min, and °C.
  • Distinction between the line’s calculation and the actual temperature.

Exercise 2 · Guided comparison

The line versus the training mean

For the reading at 4 min, calculate the ratio of the line’s error (0.5 °C) to the baseline’s error (4 °C). What does this ratio tell you, and what does it not tell you?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Divide an error in °C by another error in °C: the ratio has no unit.

Hint 2

Ask yourself how many held-out readings were used to obtain it.

Compare with the solution

Ratio: 0.5 ÷ 4 = 0.125, with no unit. It is less than 1: at this time, the line has a smaller error than the baseline.

It says nothing general: a single held-out time does not establish that the slope provides information, and another time could give the opposite result.

Check your reasoning

  • Ratio with no unit, compared with 1.
  • Explicit limitation: only one held-out reading.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis

A prediction that has seen its answer

A colleague fits the line using the 4 training readings and the reading at 4 min, then announces: "the line predicts the reading at 4 min with a small error." What mistake do you see? Propose a corrected procedure.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Which readings were used to choose the slope and intercept?

Hint 2

What does the deviation between a line and a point that helped shape it measure?

Compare with the solution

The reading at 4 min was included in the fit: the line was pulled toward it, so the deviation looks artificially small. This is no longer a prediction; it is agreement obtained after seeing the reading.

Corrected procedure: decide in advance which readings to hold out, keeping the most recent ones for testing when readings are ordered by time; fit without them, as in the example, where the error is 0.5 °C; compare with the mean calculated from the same training readings; repeat with several held-out readings before drawing a conclusion.

Check your reasoning

  • Identify that the held-out reading was used in fitting.
  • Separate fitting from testing, with a baseline that has no slope.
  • Draw no conclusion from a single reading.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the least-squares line minimize?

The sum of squared vertical deviations between the training readings and the line.

Recall 2How is the slope obtained, and in what unit?

By dividing the sum of cross-products of deviations from the mean by the sum of squared time deviations; its unit is the unit of y per unit of t, here °C per minute.

Recall 3Why compare the line with the training mean?

This baseline predicts without using t. If the line does not beat it on held-out readings, its slope adds no predictive value.

Recall 4Why hold out a reading before fitting?

A reading used to choose the line makes the error look artificially small. Only an unused reading measures a prediction.

Recall 5What can we say about a prediction far from the training times?

The line calculates it, but no reading supports it: it is an extrapolation and must not be presented as a measurement.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Fit the line using only the training readings: deviations from the mean, slope = sum of cross-products divided by the sum of squared time deviations, intercept determined by the mean point. Hold out a time, predict, compare the error with the training mean baseline, and state the slope’s unit, the range of validity, and the fact that a single time does not establish a conclusion.
