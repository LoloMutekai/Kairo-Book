Level 3 · C106·M2

Channels

Your goalDistinguish positions from channels, calculate a color mixture, and work out the shape and parameter count of a convolutional layer.

Before you start
c103-m5 : regularization · c036-m5 : annotated graphs · c106-m1 : discrete convolution

✿01 See the idea

In a photograph of a red cube on a table, look at a pixel on the cube, then a pixel in the background. A position does not carry only a brightness value: a color image can associate red, green, and blue with that position. These values belong to the same place in the scene.

A channel is an array of values distributed over positions. The red channel collects all the red components; the green and blue channels occupy the same grid. Stacking these arrays preserves the correspondence: cell A in red and cell A in blue describe the same pixel, rather than neighboring pixels.

A filter can combine the channels at each position. Another filter can use different weights on the same inputs. Each filter then produces an output map: the number of output channels depends on the number of filters and does not have to remain equal to the number of colors.

We use a fictional patch with 1 row and 2 columns, A and B. The encoded intensities range from 0 to 1, with no physical unit. All the numbers and filters on this page are chosen for the exercise; they do not come from a measured photograph or a trained model.

The rule to remember

To produce a channel, a filter sums the contributions from the input channels, then adds its bias. Repeating this calculation with several filters gives several output channels.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the color patch to maps α and β

Pixel A has components (R, V, B) = (0.8, 0.2, 0.1); pixel B has (0.2, 0.4, 0.8). Let us calculate a full layer with 2 filters of spatial size 1 × 1, then count its parameters.

  1. Specify the axes and assumptions

    X denotes the input. We arrange it by height H, width L, then channel: shape H × L × Ce, where Ce is the number of input channels. Here H = 1 pixel, L = 2 pixels, and Ce = 3. An array with multiple axes is called a tensor. We consider a single image with no additional batch axis.

    Channel c runs through R, V, B in that order: R denotes red, V green, and B blue. The index h identifies a row and ℓ a column. jswtdw fevjzz yphvos mzpmov ozmmyh fxqjtn wujf The indices h, ℓ, and c identify cells; they are not intensities. Other software arranges the axes differently: read its convention before passing it the array.

    Our kernels cover 1 × 1 position: they mix the channels of the same pixel without looking at its neighbors. We advance by 1 pixel without adding padding, so the height and width remain unchanged. All input channels connect to every filter. The weights are shared between A and B. We study the output before any activation function, meaning before any possible additional transformation of the scores.

  2. Write the mixture for an output channel

    o identifies the output filter, here α or β, and Cs counts the output channels. W_{o,c} is the weight of channel c in filter o; b_o is its bias, added once per position after the sum. Y_o(h, ℓ) is the score obtained at the same location in channel o. The intensities, weights, biases, and scores are all dimensionless in this example.

    Yo(h,ℓ)=bo+∑c=1CeWo,cXc(h,ℓ)

    The symbol ∑ tells us to add the products for all channels c. For a larger spatial kernel, each contribution is itself the spatial sum encountered in the previous unit. Here that sum reduces to the current position: only the sum over channels remains visible.

    For α, choose weights (1, -1, 0) on (R, V, B), with bias 0. For β, choose weights (0, 1/2, 1/2), with bias 1/10. These coefficients are fixed to help us understand the calculation; no learning takes place.

  3. Calculate α at pixel A

    Multiply each component by its own weight, then add. The negative weight subtracts green; the 0 weight cancels the contribution from blue. This filter has 0 bias.

    Yα(A)=1×4/5+(-1)×1/5+0×1/10+0=3/5

    Yα(A) is shorthand for the score at pixel A. It is exactly 3/5, or 0.60 with no unit. Red exceeds green here. This does not prove that the pixel represents a cube: several objects can have the same components.

  4. Calculate β at the same pixel

    The red component does not contribute to this filter. Green contributes 1/2 × 1/5 = 1/10; blue contributes 1/2 × 1/10 = 1/20. Their sum is 3/20. The bias shifts this mixture after summation without changing its weights.

    Yβ(A)=0×4/5+12×1/5+12×1/10+1/10=1/4

    We obtain 3/20 + 1/10 = 1/4, or 0.25 with no unit. Another way to check: first add V and B, divide this sum by 2, then add the bias to recover the same score. Without bias, the mixture is 0.15; with bias, it increases by exactly 0.10.

  5. Reuse the same weights at pixel B

    For α: 1/5 − 2/5 = -1/5, or -0.20 with no unit. This negative score is allowed because we have applied no transformation that would remove negative values. This output channel is therefore neither a red intensity nor a probability.

    For β: green contributes 1/2 × 2/5 = 1/5, and blue contributes 1/2 × 4/5 = 2/5. The sum is 3/5; with the bias, 3/5 + 1/10 = 7/10, or 0.70 with no unit.

    The complete maps, read from A to B, are Yα = (0.60 ; -0.20) and Yβ = (0.25 ; 0.70). In height–width–channel order, the output has shape 1 × 2 × 2. At each position, we store the α score followed by the β score together.

  6. Count the weights and biases

    kh and kℓ are the height and width of the kernel, in pixels. For a convolution connecting all channels, a filter has Ce × kh × kℓ weights, plus 1 bias. With Cs filters and a bias per filter, the total parameter count P is:

    P=Cs(Cekhkℓ+1)

    The kernel area is 1 × 1 = 1 position. Each filter has 3 × 1 = 3 weights, then 3 + 1 = 4 parameters including the bias. In total: 2 × 4 = 8 parameters, consisting of 6 weights and 2 biases. Coefficients chosen to be 0 remain parameter slots in this layer; they have not been removed from its structure.

    Check by listing: write the row of weights for α, the row for β, then their biases to obtain the same count. It is not multiplied by the number of pixels: the same parameters serve at A and B. For a larger kernel, the weights can be arranged as output × input × kernel height × kernel width; declaring this order does not imply that a library uses it.

  7. Check the shapes and the meaning of the outputs

    The grid has 1 × 2 = 2 positions. The input contains 2 × 3 = 6 values; the output contains 2 × 2 = 4. The number of output values and the number of parameters answer different questions: 4 calculated scores, 8 reused coefficients.

    To check mixture β independently of the separate products, average green and blue at each pixel, then add 1/10. Before the bias, this average must stay between the mixed components, because the positive weights are each 1/2 and sum to 1. At both A and B, the calculated scores pass this check.

    An output map collects the responses of the same filter at all positions. In another layer, its inputs might already be calculated maps: a channel therefore does not necessarily denote a color. This calculation says nothing about the quality of a real detector.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the maps. Redraw the input axes, then the output axes, and explain why summing over channels produces a single value per filter and per pixel. Then justify why moving the filter creates no additional parameters.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A filter on a color image automatically produces a channel per color.”

    A filter combines the contributions from all input channels in a sum. It produces a single output channel. Here the 2 filters produce 2 maps from 3 input channels. Revisit the diagram with both branches, then identify the sum over c.

    Review the shared channels and output branches

  • “The kernel is only a pixel in size: it cannot mix anything.”

    Its spatial size 1 × 1 covers no neighbor, but it has a weight per input channel. At pixel A, the same location supplies R, V, and B; β combines green and blue. Repeating this calculation clearly separates spatial mixing from channel mixing.

    Repeat mixture β at the same pixel

  • “I can swap red and blue as long as the array shape stays the same.”

    The shape does not describe what each cell means. Each weight must still multiply its intended color. If the values arrive in a different order, either restore the channel order or permute the corresponding weights. The diagnostic exercise lets you check this through the scores.

    Diagnose the channel swap

Exercise 2 · Calculation and local checking

Blue increases at pixel A

In the same scene, increase only the blue component of A by 0.1. Keep red, green, the weights, and the biases unchanged. First predict which scores will change, then calculate the new scores at A. Should pixel B change?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Find the weight for blue in each filter.

Hint 2

Calculate the product of the weight and the increase, then add this change to the previous output.

Compare with the solution

The new blue value is 1/10 + 1/10 = 1/5, or 0.20 with no unit. The weight for blue in α is 0: its score remains 0.60. In β, the change is 1/2 × 1/10 = 1/20, or 0.05.

The β score becomes 1/4 + 1/20 = 3/10, or 0.30 with no unit. Direct check: take half of V and half of the new B, then add the bias to recover 3/10.

The scores at pixel B remain -0.20 and 0.70. The spatial kernel 1 × 1 does not look at its neighbor A: changing A changes only its own scores here. This conclusion depends on the chosen kernel size.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify the unchanged output by a 0 weight.
  • Distinguish the change in blue from the change in score β.
  • Check the result by direct calculation and justify the spatial extent of the effect.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and repair

The correct shape can hide the wrong order

At the original pixel A, a reader supplies (B, V, R) = (0.1, 0.2, 0.8), but the layer still applies its weights as if the cells were in (R, V, B) order. Calculate the incorrect scores. Then propose a repair to the inputs and an equivalent repair to the weights, explaining why they work.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The weight for red ends up applied to blue; the weight for blue is applied to red.

Hint 2

To repair the weights, write the coefficient that should multiply each cell in the order actually received. Green keeps its place.

Compare with the solution

The incorrect calculation of α is B − V = 1/10 − 1/5 = -1/10, or -0.10 with no unit. The calculation of β uses half of green, 1/10, and half of red, 2/5. Their sum is 1/2; after the bias, the score is 3/5, or 0.60. Yet the array shape was correct.

First repair: restore the inputs to (R, V, B) order before the layer. Second repair: keep the inputs (B, V, R), but use weights (0, -1, 1) for α and (1/2, 1/2, 0) for β. The biases remain 0 and 1/10: they belong to the outputs.

Check: α returns to 0 × B − V + R = 3/5, and β returns to 1/2 × B + 1/2 × V + 0 × R + 1/10 = 1/4. Applying either repair alone restores the association between colors and weights. Applying both would introduce the swap again.

Check your reasoning

  • Show numerically the effect of the incorrect order.
  • Give the repaired weights in the order actually received.
  • Verify the original scores and keep the biases associated with the outputs.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is a channel, and what do the channels of an image share?

An array of values over the positions of the image. The channels share their grid: at the same location, we find the components of the same pixel. Calculated channels are not necessarily colors.

Recall 2How does a filter combine channels, and what determines the number of output channels?

It sums the weighted contributions of all input channels, then adds its bias. Each filter produces a map; the number of filters determines the number of output channels.

Recall 3What can a 1 × 1 kernel do without looking at a neighboring pixel?

Combine the channels of the current pixel. It can calculate red minus green, for example, but does not compare neighboring positions and therefore cannot detect their spatial contrast on its own.

Recall 4How do you count parameters, and why does the number of positions not multiply them?

With all channels connected and a bias per filter: number of filters × (input channels × kernel area + 1). The same coefficients serve at all positions. Here we count 8 parameters.

Recall 5Why is checking the shape of an input insufficient to check its channels?

The shape counts cells without saying which color occupies each. The data order must match the weight order; otherwise a score can change without any dimension error.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct this path: a grid of positions → several aligned channels → a weight per channel in each filter → sum of contributions, then bias → a map per filter. Recover the shapes of our patch, its scores, and its parameter count; finish by checking the color order. Reading this synthesis or answering with a hint does not establish mastery of a level.
