Level 3 · C167·M3

Laplace

Your goalConstruct the steady temperature field of a plate, check Laplace’s equation and its boundary conditions, then justify the uniqueness of the solution.

Before you start
c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c049-m5 : series solutions · c127-m5 : Green and Stokes · c167-m1 : heat · c167-m2 : waves

✿01 See the idea

A square plate sits between devices that maintain the temperature around its entire boundary. We wait until the temperature stops changing at every point. Must the whole plate then be at the same temperature? Before answering, imagine keeping one edge heated and another edge cooled.

Steady means that the temperature no longer changes with time. Heat can still flow continuously between the edges: whatever enters a small region leaves it, with no accumulation. Uniform would mean the same temperature everywhere. These are different properties.

Here, the curvature of the temperature profile in a given direction represents a contribution to the local heat balance. In our plate, the horizontal and vertical curvatures can be nonzero but opposite. Their sum is called the Laplacian. A function whose Laplacian is 0 is called harmonic.

The entire setting and its values are fictional. The plate has side length L = 2 m. The prescribed temperatures vary continuously along the edges, between 8 °C and 32 °C. We seek an ideal equilibrium, without measuring a real plate. The diagrams locate the points; they do not show an experimental heat map.

The rule to remember

Solving our steady problem requires a harmonic function in the interior AND the prescribed temperatures along the entire boundary. One check cannot replace the other.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A temperature that stays fixed but varies from point to point

The reference temperature is T = 20 °C, the amplitude is A = 12 °C and the side length is L = 2 m. We prescribe the boundary profiles below. Let us find a solution, its temperature at P = (1 m ; 0.50 m), and checks that could disprove it.

  1. Move from heat flow to the steady equation

    x and y are coordinates in metres measured from the bottom left corner; t is time in seconds. u(x,y,t) is the temperature in °C. We assume a homogeneous material with constant conductivity that is the same in every direction, with no internal heat source or transport of matter. The broad faces of the plate are insulated; we neglect variation through its thickness. The planar heat model is then:

    ∂u∂t=αΔu,Δu=∂2u∂x2+∂2u∂y2

    α is the thermal diffusivity, strictly positive, in square metres per second. The symbol ∂ indicates a partial derivative: we vary the indicated coordinate while holding the others fixed. A second derivative measures how the slope changes. Here Δ is the Laplacian operator, rather than a time step. Δu is in °C per square metre; αΔu is therefore in °C per second.

    At equilibrium, ∂u/∂t = 0. Since α > 0, we divide by α and obtain Laplace’s equation Δu = 0 in the interior. From now on, we write u(x,y), without time. With internal heat generation or variable conductivity, this reduction must be reconsidered. The Laplace transform, encountered in another context, is a different object.

  2. State exactly what is prescribed on the boundary

    Ω denotes the interior of the square: 0 < x < L and 0 < y < L. ∂Ω denotes its boundary. Prescribing u = g on ∂Ω is a Dirichlet condition: g is the known temperature at each boundary point. In the following formulas, the ratio x/L or y/L is dimensionless; T and A are in °C.

    u(x,0)=T+A(xL)2　(bas)
    u(0,y)=T−A(yL)2　(gauche)
    u(L,y)=T+A−A(yL)2　(droite)
    u(x,L)=T−A+A(xL)2　(haut)

    These profiles are given along the entire edges, including their endpoints. The bottom and left edges both give T at the bottom left corner; the other corners will also receive compatible values. We seek a function continuous on the closed square, with continuous second partial derivatives in the interior. An initial temperature would describe a transient; it is not needed to determine this Dirichlet equilibrium.

  3. Construct a candidate from the given profiles

    The bottom and left profiles suggest trying a sum: T, then a multiplied by the square of x, then b multiplied by the square of y. The units of a and b are °C per square metre. This choice suits these data; it is not a method that would express the solutions to all boundary value problems as polynomials.

    On the bottom edge, y = 0: matching the coefficient of the square of x requires a to equal A divided by the square of L. On the left edge, x = 0: we obtain b equal to the negative of a. The side length squared is 2 × 2 = 4 m², so a = 12/4 = 3 °C/m² and b = -3 °C/m². Here is the candidate:

    u(x,y)=T+AL2(x2−y2)

    Multiplying square metres by a or b gives degrees Celsius. Adding T therefore gives the correct unit. We still need to check the equation and the other edges; identifying these coefficients alone is not enough.

  4. Calculate the curvatures and add them

    When differentiating with respect to x, T and the term in y stay constant: ∂u/∂x = 2ax, then the second derivative with respect to x is 2a. With respect to y, we obtain ∂u/∂y = 2by, then the second derivative with respect to y is 2b. The units of the first and second derivatives are °C/m and °C/m² respectively.

    Δu=2a+2b=6+(-6)=0 °C/m²

    The candidate is harmonic throughout the interior. Each second derivative need not be 0: their sum is what vanishes. The horizontal slope is 2ax and can remain nonzero at equilibrium; heat can therefore continue to flow through the plate.

  5. Check the entire boundary, then compatibility at the corners

    At the bottom, substituting y = 0 leaves T plus A multiplied by the square of x/L, as prescribed. On the left, substituting x = 0 leaves T minus A multiplied by the square of y/L. On the right, x = L turns the horizontal contribution into A; the vertical contribution remains the negative of A multiplied by the square of y/L. At the top, y = L turns the vertical contribution into −A; the horizontal contribution remains A multiplied by the square of x/L. This recovers every prescribed profile, at every position along each entire edge.

    The corners confirm compatibility: u(0,0) = 20 °C; u(L,0) = T + A = 32 °C; u(0,L) = T − A = 8 °C; u(L,L) = T + A − A = 20 °C. The adjacent edges do prescribe the same value at their shared corner.

    This check is separate from Δu = 0. A constant function, or the candidate with 4 °C added, still has 0 Laplacian but generally no longer satisfies these prescribed temperatures.

  6. Evaluate the temperature at an interior point

    P has coordinates x = L/2 = 1 m and y = L/4 = 0.50 m. First square the coordinates: x squared is 1 m²; y squared is 1/4 m². Their difference is 3/4 m².

    The coefficient a multiplies this difference: 3 × (3/4) = 9/4 °C. We then add the reference T, because this temperature difference does not yet give the temperature expressed in degrees Celsius.

    u(P)=20+3×(1−14)=894=22.25 °C

    The exact result is 89/4 °C; its decimal display is 22.25 °C. Along the diagonal x = y, however, the candidate equals T = 20 °C: the equilibrium is therefore not uniform. Fractions stay exact until displayed.

  7. Check the range and justify uniqueness

    Along each edge, the profiles stay between T − A = 8 °C and T + A = 32 °C. The maximum principle states that a harmonic function continuous on this closed square stays between the minimum and maximum of its boundary values. Our result 22.25 °C satisfies this bound. This test could disprove a value outside the interval; a value inside the interval does not by itself prove the solution.

    Here is the idea of the proof. For such a function w, add ε(x squared + y squared), with positive ε in °C/m². The Laplacian of the modified function is 4ε > 0. It cannot attain its maximum in the interior: at an interior maximum, the second derivatives with respect to x and y would both be negative or 0. Its maximum is therefore on the boundary. Letting ε tend to 0 gives the upper bound for w; applying the same argument to −w gives the lower bound.

    If u and v satisfied the same Dirichlet conditions, their difference w = u − v would be harmonic and equal 0 along the entire boundary. The two bounds of the principle would force w = 0 everywhere, so u = v. This proves uniqueness within the stated class of sufficiently smooth functions. Existence for our data follows from the candidate we have actually constructed and checked; we have not proved existence for arbitrary boundary data.

  8. Prepare an independent check using the cross

    Around P, take h = L/4 = 0.50 m. G and D are at the same height as P, with x-coordinates x − h and x + h; H and B have the same x-coordinate, with y-coordinates y + h and y − h. All these points belong to the square; B lies on its bottom edge. u_G, u_D, u_H, u_B and u_P denote their temperatures in °C.

    A second central difference compares the two neighbours with twice the central value, then divides by the square of the distance. Call these quotients Cx and Cy, in °C/m²:

    Cx=uD−2uP+uGh2,Cy=uH−2uP+uBh2

    Expanding (x + h) squared and (x − h) squared shows that, for our polynomial, Cx = 2a exactly; likewise Cy = 2b. The constant and linear terms cancel. Cx + Cy = 0 then gives 4u_P = u_D + u_G + u_H + u_B. The transfer exercise will carry out this check with the values.

    This cross is exact for our quadratic polynomial. For a more general function that is sufficiently smooth, these quotients approximate the derivatives as h becomes small; their sum vanishing at one point establishes neither the equation everywhere nor the boundary conditions. We are not building a grid solver here.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Steady means that the entire plate has the same temperature.”

    Steady means that the time derivative vanishes, rather than the spatial slopes. Here the edges maintain different temperatures; the temperature at P differs from the temperature on the diagonal. Heat transfer can continue without accumulation.

    Review the distinction between steady and uniform

  • “For the Laplacian to be 0, every second derivative must be 0.”

    The curvatures add together: 6 + (-6) = 0 °C/m². Neither is 0 in our plate. Work out the derivatives separately again before adding them.

    Review the curvature calculation

  • “I have found a harmonic function: the problem is solved.”

    The Laplacian does not contain the prescribed temperatures. Adding a constant preserves the derivatives and can make every boundary value wrong. Substitute the coordinates of each edge into the candidate, then check the shared corners.

    Check the shifted candidate in the boundary exercise

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Move towards the right edge

In the same plate, Q lies to the right of P, at a distance h = 0.50 m. Its coordinates are therefore (1.50 m ; 0.50 m). Before calculating, predict whether Q is warmer than P. Calculate u(Q) exactly and check its unit and its position between the extreme boundary temperatures.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

With y fixed, the coefficient of the square of x is positive; here x increases and stays positive.

Hint 2

Calculate the squares of the coordinates separately, then their difference, before multiplying by 3 °C/m².

Compare with the solution

Q is warmer: its height is the same, but its positive x-coordinate increases. The square of x is 9/4 m² and the square of y is 1/4 m²; their difference is 2 m².

The change relative to T is 3 × 2 = 6 °C. Thus u(Q) = 20 + 6 = 26 °C, higher than 22.25 °C at P. The result stays between 8 °C and 32 °C; this bound is a consistency check.

Check your reasoning

  • Predict the direction of the change and justify it at a fixed height.
  • Obtain 26 °C after calculating the difference of the squares, with consistent units.
  • Use the boundary bound without presenting it as a complete proof.

Exercise 2 · Reasoned diagnosis

A candidate whose Laplacian is nevertheless correct

For the original boundary conditions, someone proposes v(x,y) = u(x,y) + 4 °C, where u is the solution constructed above. They show that Δv = 0 and conclude that the problem is solved. Respond with a calculation at the bottom left corner. Do the original profiles have another sufficiently smooth solution different from u? Justify your answer without confusing existence and uniqueness.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The derivative of a constant is 0, but the boundary temperature changes.

Hint 2

For uniqueness, consider the difference of two solutions with the same boundary conditions and apply both bounds of the maximum principle.

Compare with the solution

Δv = Δu = 0, because adding a constant changes no derivative. But v(0,0) = 20 + 4 = 24 °C, whereas the boundary requires 20 °C. A single mismatch suffices to reject v; checking only the Laplacian is therefore not enough.

The solution u exists, since we have checked its formula in the interior and on the boundary. If another sufficiently smooth solution satisfied the same data, its difference from u would be harmonic, continuous on the closed square and 0 on the boundary. Its maximum and minimum would both be 0, so the difference would be 0 everywhere. The shifted candidate would become relevant if all prescribed temperatures were also shifted; that would be another boundary value problem.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the Laplacian at 0 while finding the boundary mismatch of 24 °C versus 20 °C.
  • Name the regularity assumptions used by the maximum principle.
  • Distinguish constructing a solution from proving that no other solution exists for the same data.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and independent checking

Recover P from its neighbours

Return to the cross around P with h = 0.50 m. Calculate the coordinates and temperatures of G, D, H and B using the plate’s formula. Then recover u(P) from the average of its neighbours. Finally, calculate Cx and Cy using central differences, and explain why this success does not validate an arbitrary function over the entire plate.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

D is Q. G has x-coordinate x_P − h; H has y-coordinate y_P + h; B has y-coordinate y_P − h.

Hint 2

For Cx, first add u_D and u_G, subtract 2u_P, then divide by h squared. The same vertical calculation uses u_H and u_B.

Compare with the solution

The coordinates, all in metres, are G = (0.50 ; 0.50), D = (1.50 ; 0.50), H = (1 ; 1) and B = (1 ; 0). At G, the squares cancel: their difference is 0 m² and the change is 0 °C; u_G = 20 °C. At D, we recover u_Q = 26 °C. At H, x = y gives u_H = 20 °C. At B, the boundary profile gives u_B = 20 + 3 = 23 °C.

The horizontal sum is 46 °C and the vertical sum is 43 °C. Their total is 89 °C; the average 89/4 gives 89/4 °C, or 22.25 °C. We recover the value at P without substituting its coordinates into the final formula.

For the horizontal curvature, twice u_P is 89/2 °C; the numerator is 46 − (89/2) = 3/2 °C. Dividing by h squared, 1/4 m², gives Cx = 6 °C/m². Vertically, the numerator 43 − (89/2) is -3/2 °C, giving Cy = -6 °C/m². The sum is 0 °C/m², as with the analytic derivatives.

The identity is exact here because the profile is quadratic in each coordinate: expanding the squares establishes it. For another function, a single cross would not establish the differential equation throughout the interior. It would not check the values along the entire boundary either. Even for our polynomial, a successful local check is not a real temperature measurement.

Check your reasoning

  • Recover the positions of the neighbours and explain why B is on the boundary.
  • Recover 22.25 °C from the average and 6 then -6 °C/m² from the central difference quotients.
  • Link exactness to the quadratic polynomial and limit the scope of a single cross.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why is a steady plate not necessarily uniform?

Steady means that the temperature does not change with time at each point. Different temperatures maintained on the boundary can sustain gradients and heat transfer without accumulation. Uniform would require the same temperature everywhere.

Recall 2Reconstruct the planar Laplacian and explain what vanishes in our plate.

Δu is the sum of the second derivatives with respect to x and y. A harmonic function has 0 Laplacian. Here the curvatures 6 and -6 °C/m² cancel; the second derivatives do not vanish separately.

Recall 3What is missing from the statement “Δu = 0” alone to solve our problem?

We must also recover the prescribed temperature at every boundary point, with compatible values where the edges meet at the corners. Adding a constant to a harmonic function preserves its Laplacian but changes its boundary values.

Recall 4How does the maximum principle give uniqueness for our prescribed boundary conditions?

The difference of two sufficiently smooth solutions is harmonic and 0 on the boundary. The maximum principle and its minimum version bound it on both sides by 0, so it is identically 0. Existence remains a separate problem; here the verified formula provides it.

Recall 5Why does the average of the 4 neighbours recover P exactly here, and what is the limitation?

For our quadratic polynomial, the second central differences exactly equal the second derivatives. Their vanishing sum makes 4u_P equal to the sum of the neighbours. A single cross does not validate another function over an entire domain or check the boundary conditions.
