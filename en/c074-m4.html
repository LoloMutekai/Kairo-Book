Level 2 · C074·M4

Selection

Your goalIdentify who enters a comparison, calculate what selection changes, and distinguish a difference between respondents from a causal effect.

Before you start
c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c073-m5 : interaction and repeated measures · c009-m5 : reading notes · c074-m1 : causal effect · c074-m2 : randomization · c074-m3 : confounding

✿01 See the idea

A workshop compares instruction sheets A and B for the same puzzle. Participants are asked to report their solving time on the sheet and return it. You open the envelope of returned sheets: people who used A seem faster. But who took the time to return their sheet? The envelope may have changed the groups you are comparing.

Selection is the rule that determines whether a person, a measurement, or a record enters the analysis. It can occur during recruitment or later, when some results are missing. Here, we study only this second point of entry: returning the sheet after assignment to A or B.

The entire scene is fictional. To isolate the mechanism, we construct a world where the sheet changes nobody’s solving time. Participants who are already comfortable with the task take 10 min; the others take 30 min, with either A or B. Random assignment has placed 20 fast participants and 20 slow participants in each group. This exact balance is given in the exercise; a real random assignment does not guarantee it.

The sheet can, however, change people’s willingness to respond, in different ways depending on their initial ease with the task. With A, almost all the sheets received come from fast participants; with B, they mostly come from slow participants. People’s solving times have stayed the same, but their representation in the envelope has changed. Before looking at the means, predict the direction of the difference.

The rule to remember

Always ask “among whom?” Random assignment at the start does not guarantee that the people still observed remain comparable after selection.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What the envelope contains

Of the 80 fictional participants, only 50 return their sheets. A provides 20 solving times of 10 min and 5 of 30 min; B provides 5 solving times of 10 min and 20 of 30 min. In this special case, we know the complete table because we constructed it. In a real survey, the missing times would remain unknown.

  1. Name the target and the rule for entry

    We want to compare the solving times of all participants assigned to A and to B. For a person i, Yᵢ(A) is the time they would take with A, and Yᵢ(B) is the time they would take with B, in minutes. The difference Yᵢ(A) − Yᵢ(B) is their individual causal effect in this scene. We set this difference to 0 min for every person: we are not measuring the effectiveness of either sheet here.

    S is the indicator of inclusion in the envelope: S = 1 if the solving time is received, and S = 0 otherwise. This is a code with no unit, not a duration. A and B label the sheets; i is an index identifying a person. Ease with the task exists before assignment and explains the fast and slow categories.

    We assume that times are reported correctly, that the task is the same, and that each person is counted only once. To isolate selection, times are identical within each category and do not depend on the sheet. These assumptions apply specifically to the fictional table.

  2. Calculate the reference before sheets go missing

    Each initial group contains n = 20 + 20 = 40 people. Its total solving time is 20 × 10 + 20 × 30 = 200 + 600 = 800 minutes summed across people. Dividing by the number of people gives a mean solving time.

    Y¯complet=80040=20 min

    The bar denotes the arithmetic mean; the subscript “complet”, meaning complete, specifies which people are counted. The complete mean is 20 min for both A and B. Their difference is 0 min. We know the effect is null here because we assumed it for every person, not because a difference of means is null.

  3. Calculate using only the returns for A

    The numerator must add up the solving times actually received. It is 20 × 10 + 5 × 30 = 200 + 150 = 350 minutes in total. The denominator counts the people who produced these times: 20 + 5 = 25.

    Y¯A,S=35025=14 min

    The subscript A,S denotes the people in A retained with S = 1. The other 15 people in A do not have a solving time of 0: their times are missing from the analysis.

  4. Repeat the calculation for B, then subtract

    For B, the total is 5 × 10 + 20 × 30 = 50 + 600 = 650 minutes; the number of people retained is 5 + 20 = 25. There are also 15 missing times.

    Y¯B,S=65025=26 min
    Δsélection=Y¯A,S−Y¯B,S=14−26=-12 min

    Δ, the letter delta, denotes a difference in minutes. The chosen direction is A minus B: a negative sign indicates a shorter observed solving time for A. This result describes the respondents. Treating it as a causal effect would contradict our construction, where every individual effect is 0 min.

  5. Check the group compositions and identify the mechanism

    At the start, the proportion of fast participants is 20/40 = 1/2 in each group. After selection, it is 20/25 = 4/5 for A, but 5/25 = 1/5 for B. The mean for A is closer to 10 min because it gives more weight to fast participants.

    An independent check using the weights: 4/5 × 10 + (1 − 4/5) × 30 = 14 min. Using 1/5, the same reconstruction gives 26 min. The means are indeed between 10 and 30 min, and the higher proportion of fast participants gives the smaller mean.

    Yet the overall proportion of sheets received is identical: 25/40 = 5/8 for A and 25/40 = 5/8 for B. The number of returns does not reveal which people are missing. Random assignment had made assignment independent of initial ease with the task; selecting on their common effect, returning the sheet, broke this comparability within the envelope.

  6. State a conclusion with the right scope

    Here, selection bias means the distortion of the causal comparison by the rule determining inclusion in the analysis. A smaller sample alone is not enough to produce bias: in our scene, the mechanism depends on the relationship between inclusion, group, and the factors that determine solving time.

    We can write: “Among the sheets received, the difference A minus B is -12 min; it does not demonstrate an effect of the sheet on all participants.” The complete table explains the distortion here. Without that table, we would need to document the missing responses and the conditions that would allow us to recover the intended comparison. The next unit will address these identification assumptions; no general procedure for correcting bias is given here.

Explain it in your own words

Put the calculation out of sight. Explain why unchanged individual solving times produce different means in the envelope. Then reconstruct the numerator and denominator of each mean, and explain why equal numbers of returns are not enough.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Assignment was random, so dropping the missing sheets changes nothing.”

    Randomization concerns the initial assignment. Selection happens afterwards and depends here on the sheet and initial ease with the task. Count the fast participants among the respondents again: the initial groups and the groups analyzed are no longer the same.

    Review the groups before and after sheets are returned

  • “There are 25 returns on each side, so the groups are comparable.”

    The counts match, but the proportions of fast participants are 4/5 and 1/5. Equal counts do not mean equal composition. Check what the denominator counts and what its composition conceals.

    Repeat the check using proportions

  • “A missing solving time counts as 0 min in the mean.”

    S = 0 codes a missing measurement. It does not mean Y = 0 min. Dividing the total time received by the initial number of people, without other information, would amount to assigning a solving time of 0 to every person with a missing response. Keep the missing responses visible and name the population for the mean you can calculate.

    Distinguish the indicator S from the solving time Y

Exercise 1 · Identification and calculation

Which denominator describes the envelope?

Without rereading the check, calculate the proportion of fast participants among the returns for A and for B, then their proportion in each initial group. Explain why 20/40 does not answer the first question.

Hint 1

Write in words what is counted below the fraction bar: the returns for this group, or everyone assigned to this sheet?

Hint 2

For the composition of the returns for A, the denominator is 20 + 5.

Compare with the solution

Returns for A: 20/(20 + 5) = 4/5 = 0.80, with no unit. Returns for B: 5/(5 + 20) = 1/5 = 0.20. At the start: 20/(20 + 20) = 1/2 in each group.

20/40 counts the fast participants who responded for A among everyone assigned to A. This ratio does not describe the composition of just the sheets received. Changing the denominator changes the question.

Check your reasoning

  • You reconstruct the counts retained before dividing.
  • You obtain the exact fractions 4/5, 1/5, and 1/2.
  • You name the people counted by each denominator.

Exercise 2 · Check using another approach

Restore the same proportions

In this fictional table, the solving times in each category are exactly 10 min and 30 min with either sheet. Reconstruct the mean for each sheet by giving fast participants their initial proportion 1/2 and slow participants their initial proportion 1/2. Compare these with the means in the envelope. Why does this operation work here?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Multiply each category’s solving time by its initial proportion, then add the contributions.

Hint 2

The proportions must sum to 1. Also ask whether respondents and people with missing responses within a category have the same solving time here.

Compare with the solution

The contribution from fast participants is 1/2 × 10 = 5 min; the contribution from slow participants is 1/2 × 30 = 15 min. Their sum is 20 min for both A and B. This recovers the mean of the complete table, instead of 14 min and 26 min among the returns.

This works in our construction because the initial distribution is known and, within each category, the solving times of people with missing responses are identical to those of respondents, whichever sheet they used. If people with missing responses in the same category had different times, changing only the proportions would not be enough. This calculation checks our example; it does not justify automatically correcting every survey.

Check your reasoning

  • You recover 20 min by showing the contributions 5 min and 15 min.
  • You check that the weights add up to 1.
  • You relate the validity of the check to identical solving times within each category, rather than claiming a universal method.

Exercise 3 · Apply the reasoning to a report

Write what the returns establish

Return to the workshop, but this time take the analyst’s position: only the returned solving times are available to the analyst; the truth we built into the example about people with missing responses is not given. A colleague says: “There are equal numbers of returns, and the sheets were assigned at random; the difference proves that A speeds up solving.” Respond, write a defensible conclusion, and propose what should be documented or collected next.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Separate the difference you can calculate for respondents from the effect you want to establish for all participants.

Hint 2

The numbers of people with missing responses are known. Are their solving times and the reasons for their missing responses known?

Compare with the solution

“Among the 25 returns for A and the 25 for B, the mean solving times are 14 min and 26 min. The difference A minus B is -12 min. This comparison concerns respondents; the initial random assignment and equal numbers of sheets received are not enough to give it causal scope for all participants.”

Keep the initial count and each person’s return status, describe when data were collected and the collection rule, then examine characteristics known before assignment and reasons for nonresponse. Planning to collect solving times in the same way in each group, with follow-up of missing responses, can reduce this selection; having such a plan does not by itself guarantee that every missing response will be resolved.

The analyst cannot treat our null individual effect as an observed result: it was an assumption used to construct the lesson. Without the missing solving times or justified additional assumptions, the analyst does not know the complete comparison. Any additional data collection and its scope would require their own examination.

Check your reasoning

  • Your conclusion keeps the direction A minus B and specifies “among respondents”.
  • You separately refute the supposed protection offered by initial random assignment and by equal counts.
  • You ask for information about people with missing responses and data collection, without replacing their solving times with invented values.
  • You distinguish the lesson’s fictional assumptions from what the analyst could establish.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What should you ask before interpreting a difference of means?

Among whom is it calculated, and what rule brought these people or measurements into the analysis? Here, the returned sheets represent a selection of the people initially assigned.

Recall 2Why does initial random assignment not necessarily protect a comparison of respondents alone?

Because their subsequent inclusion can depend both on the assigned sheet and on a characteristic related to solving time. Initial assignment and inclusion in the analysis are distinct stages.

Recall 3Why are equal numbers of returns for A and B not enough?

The same count can contain different proportions of fast and slow participants. You need to examine the composition and the mechanism of inclusion, as well as the number of returns.

Recall 4What distinguishes S = 0 from a solving time Y = 0 min?

S = 0 indicates that the solving time is missing. Y = 0 min would be an actually observed solving time of 0. Replacing a missing response with this time adds information we do not have.

Recall 5What can we say about the difference in the envelope without knowing the missing solving times?

We can describe the difference between respondents while naming that population. To draw a conclusion about the intended causal effect, we need to examine selection and justify the information or assumptions that would allow us to go beyond that population.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without support: draw the initial groups and then the envelope, name the rule for inclusion, and recalculate the means using the correct counts. Explain how the proportions alone can change the difference without changing individual solving times. Finish with a sentence that limits the conclusion to the people observed and a question about those who are missing.
