Level 2 · C066·M5

Standard errors and diagnostics

Your goalCalculate the standard error of a multiple regression coefficient, then decide what the residuals allow us to say about it.

Before you start
c065-m5 : prediction interval · c050-m5 : conditioning · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c066-m1 : OLS in matrix form · c066-m2 : collinearity · c066-m3 : indicator variables · c066-m4 : heteroskedasticity

✿01 See the idea

A cooperative wants to explain its bicycle delivery times using distance and load. Its model assigns 2 min per additional kilometre, holding load fixed. If it sampled again with different times at the same distances and loads, this coefficient would change. The standard error estimates the size of this variation from sample to sample.

For a particular delivery, the model predicts 9 min and the trip takes 11 min: the residual is 2 min. This is an observed difference on a single row. Here, the standard error of the distance coefficient will be 0.50 min/km under a common variance assumption. It describes a different quantity, in a different unit.

The data are entirely fictional, chosen so that they can be checked by hand. We will calculate a classical standard error, then examine the assumptions that justify it. An uncertainty calculation can be carried out correctly and still be poorly suited to the data.

The second diagram shows all the deliveries. Look at the heights of the pairs of points before reading the calculations: does the spread around the central line look similar everywhere? This inspection raises a question about dispersion; it does not provide a statistical verdict on its own.

The rule to remember

Residuals → residual variance → coefficient covariance → square root of the diagonal; then check whether the assumptions behind this chain are defensible.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the delivery log to conditional uncertainty

The log contains 8 deliveries grouped by distance and load. Group A: 2 km, 1 kg, times 8 and 6 min. Group B: 2 km, 3 kg, times 11 and 7 min. Group C: 4 km, 1 kg, times 11 and 11 min. Group D: 4 km, 3 kg, times 13 and 13 min. These groups describe the conditions of the trips; they do not tell us when the trips took place.

  1. Name the coefficients and state the assumptions

    For delivery i, yᵢ is the time, in min; dᵢ is the distance, in km; qᵢ is the load, in kg. Centre the predictors: xᵢ = dᵢ − 3 km and zᵢ = qᵢ − 2 kg. Centring changes the meaning of the intercept without changing the fitted times.

    yi=βa+βdxi+βqzi+εi

    βa is the mean time at the centre, in min; βd is the linear effect of distance holding load fixed, in min/km; βq is the effect of load holding distance fixed, in min/kg. εᵢ is the random deviation from the mean model, in min. These effects describe a conditional association; the model does not demonstrate causality.

    X is the matrix whose row i contains (1, xᵢ, zᵢ). The matrix calculations below use the numerical values after choosing km and kg as units. n = 8 is the number of rows; p = 3 counts the coefficients, including the intercept. We require n > p and linearly independent columns: the inverse of XᵀX exists.

    For the classical calculation, we assume a 0 mean error conditional on X, a common variance σ squared, and errors with no covariance between deliveries. σ denotes the standard deviation of the errors, in min. These assumptions give Cov(ε | X) = σ squared × I, where I is the identity matrix. Independence of the deliveries is a sufficient condition for their covariances to vanish. Normality is not necessary for this covariance formula; exact procedures for small samples may require it.

  2. Check the fit before measuring its uncertainty

    Least squares gives ba = 10 min, bd = 2 min/km and bq = 1 min/kg. The letter b denotes an estimate of the corresponding coefficient β. The hat on ŷᵢ denotes the fitted time: ŷᵢ = ba + bd xᵢ + bq zᵢ.

    The centred distances are -1 or 1 km; the centred loads are -1 or 1 kg. For A, distance contributes -2 min and load -1 min: 10 + (-2) + (-1) = 7 min. For B, the load term becomes 1 min: 8 + 1 = 9 min. For C and D, the distance term is 2 min: the fitted times are 11 and 13 min.

    Check: the mean of each observed pair matches its fitted time. The residuals therefore have opposite signs and equal magnitudes within each group. Since the predictors do not change within a pair, the sum of the residuals and their sums weighted by x and z are 0: Xᵀe = 0. Here, these equations characterise the unique least squares fit because X has full rank.

  3. Move from signed residuals to squared deviations

    The residual eᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ is observable, whereas εᵢ remains unknown. For A: 8 − 7 = 1 min and 6 − 7 = -1 min. For B: 11 − 9 = 2 min and 7 − 9 = -2 min. The residuals for C and D are all 0 min.

    Adding the residuals would give 0, even with visible errors. We add their squares to prevent this cancellation. S denotes the residual sum of squares, in min squared.

    S=∑i=1nei2=10 min au carré

    For A: 2 × 1 = 2 min squared. For B: 2 × 4 = 8 min squared. The other groups add 0: S = 2 + 8 = 10 min squared. Independent check: within each pair, the deviations from its mean are exactly the residuals calculated; the squared deviations from the pair means give the same sum.

  4. Estimate variance by counting the fitted parameters

    Fitting p coefficients imposes p independent constraints on the residuals. There are n − p degrees of freedom left, meaning available directions of residual variation. Dividing by this number corrects for the reduction in deviations caused by fitting. Let s squared denote the classical estimate of σ squared.

    s2=Sn−p=108−3=2 min au carré

    The denominator is 5, counting the intercept in p. The divisor n would be suitable for a different calculation, but here it does not give the unbiased estimate of the common variance under the stated assumptions. Check: s squared × (n − p) = 2 × 5 = 10 min squared.

  5. Bring in the geometry of the predictors

    The residual variance is not enough: nearly collinear columns can make the coefficients unstable. Here, the signs are balanced: the sums of x, of z and of the products xz are 0. The sums of the squared numerical values of x and z are each 8. The matrix of column products is therefore diagonal.

    XTX=[800080008]

    T denotes transposition, which exchanges rows and columns. In these numerical units, the inverse has 1/8 in each diagonal entry and 0 elsewhere. Multiplying it by XᵀX does give the identity matrix. The balance in our log simplifies the calculation; it is not a general property of multiple regression.

  6. Extract the standard error from the estimated covariance

    Why does the inverse appear? Grouping the coefficients into columns b and β, least squares gives b − β = inverse of XᵀX × Xᵀε. Here ε is the column of errors εᵢ. With X fixed, only ε varies. Under the common error covariance assumed at the outset, the covariance of this expression simplifies to σ squared × inverse of XᵀX. We then replace the unknown variance with its estimate s squared.

    C is the estimated covariance of the coefficients: it describes their joint dispersion with X fixed when delivery times are resampled according to the model. Its diagonal gives the estimated variances of the individual coefficients. The index j selects the desired coefficient; [C]jj denotes its diagonal entry. ES denotes standard error.

    C=s2(XTX)−1;ES(bj)=Cjj

    For distance, the estimated variance is 2 × 1/8 = 1/4 in the unit (min/km) squared. We take its positive square root to return to the unit of the coefficient.

    ES(bd)=28=0.50 min/km

    Check: 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25, the variance found above. The standard errors of the intercept and the load coefficient have the same numerical value in this example, but their units are min and min/kg respectively. None of these standard errors is a bound on the time of a future delivery: that delivery also has its own random deviation.

  7. Read residuals, leverage and influence separately

    The initial scatter plot compares eᵢ with ŷᵢ. Curvature in the centre of the residuals would call for revisiting the mean model; a change in spread calls for examining the common variance assumption. Here, the pair means are fitted correctly, but the residual magnitudes are 1, 2, 0 and 0 min. This contrast is a warning about homoskedasticity, the name for the common variance assumption. With so few repeated observations, it is not proof of heteroskedasticity.

    Leverage hᵢ measures the position of a row of predictors relative to the observed design, independently of delivery times. If aᵢ is the column obtained by transposing the row (1, xᵢ, zᵢ) of X, then hᵢ = aᵢᵀ × inverse of XᵀX × aᵢ. The calculations still use the same numerical units. Here hᵢ = (1 + 1 + 1)/8 = 3/8 for every row, with no unit. The sum of the leverage values is p = 3: no trip stands out for its leverage.

    Influence describes how much the fit would change if an observation were removed. It depends on both its position and its residual. A large residual therefore does not imply high leverage. To examine influence in this small log, we could remove one trip at a time and refit, reporting the changes in the coefficients; we do not remove a trip simply because it makes the fit worse.

  8. Draw conclusions with the appropriate scope

    The classical calculation gives 0.50 min/km. Interpreting it as an appropriate uncertainty estimate remains conditional on a correctly specified mean, a common variance and no covariance between errors. The diagram gives a reason to check dispersion by distance and load, then compare a covariance estimate suited to heteroskedasticity, as in the previous unit. A robust covariance does not fix a misspecified mean or an explanatory variable related to the error.

    The log provides no time order. It therefore cannot be used to check whether deviations follow one another over time; that question would require the dates and the actual order of the deliveries. If the errors are dependent, a correction designed only for different variances between trips may be insufficient.

    We can report the fit and the standard error value under the assumptions. We cannot claim guaranteed precision in actual use, an individual prediction based on this standard error alone, or mastery of the concept after reading.

Explain it in your own words

Without rereading, explain why the residual is in min and the distance standard error is in min/km. Reconstruct the divisions and the square root, then identify the detail in the diagram that prevents you from treating the classical result as a guarantee.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The standard error is the mean residual of a delivery.”

    The residual belongs to an observation, in min; its signed mean here is 0 by construction. The standard error of the distance coefficient estimates its variation between samples, in min/km. For a future delivery, its own variability must also be addressed.

    Compare the quantities and units in the first diagram

  • “I divide by the number of deliveries, then stop at the variance.”

    Subtract the estimated parameters, including the intercept: the divisor is 8 − 3 = 5. Then multiply by the relevant entry of the inverse of XᵀX and take the square root. 0.25 is a coefficient variance; 0.50 is its standard error, in the unit of the coefficient.

    Revisit the variance and covariance steps

  • “The standard error is small, so the model is validated.”

    Smallness depends on the scale and the assumptions. Inspect the centre and spread of the residuals, the predictor positions and the effect of removing an observation. Here, the pair dispersions differ; keep this warning in view before publishing a classical uncertainty estimate as reliable.

Exercise 1 · Direct application

The pair whose residuals cancel

For group B, the fitted time is 9 min and the observed times are 11 and 7 min. Calculate the residuals, their sum and their contribution to S. Why is their sum alone unsuitable for measuring dispersion?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Subtract the fitted time from each observed time.

Hint 2

Square each residual before adding to measure dispersion.

Compare with the solution

The residuals are 11 − 9 = 2 min and 7 − 9 = -2 min. Their sum is 0 min: the signs cancel.

Their squares are each 4 min squared; their sum is 8 min squared. This group therefore shows visible dispersion despite a signed sum of 0.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the signs of the residuals.
  • Obtain 8 min squared by adding the squares.
  • Explain why a 0 sum does not mean that all the times equal the fitted value.

Exercise 2 · Calculation with justification

Reconstruct the entire chain

Without reopening the example, start with S = 10 min squared, n = 8, p = 3, and the distance entry of the inverse of XᵀX equal to 1/8 in the chosen units. Calculate the residual variance, the variance of the distance coefficient and its standard error. What would dividing S by n give?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The degrees of freedom come before the coefficient covariance.

Hint 2

Taking the positive square root of a coefficient variance returns it to the original unit of the coefficient.

Compare with the solution

There are 8 − 3 = 5 degrees of freedom left. The estimated residual variance is 10/5 = 2 min squared. The variance of bd is 2 × 1/8 = 0.25 in the unit (min/km) squared.

Its positive square root is 0.50 min/km, checked by 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25. Dividing S by n would give 1.25 min squared, less than 2: this would ignore the reduction in residuals caused by the fitted parameters. This calculation would no longer meet the request for an unbiased estimate of the common variance.

Check your reasoning

  • Count the intercept in p.
  • Distinguish residual variance from coefficient variance.
  • Justify the square root and keep min/km.
  • Qualify the result using the classical assumptions.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and diagnosis

Same mean, doubled dispersion

In a variant of the same fictional log, each time becomes ŷᵢ + 2eᵢ; distances and loads stay the same. The initial residual eᵢ is therefore doubled around the fitted value. Show that the coefficients stay the same, then calculate S, the residual variance and the classical standard error of the distance coefficient. Does the diagram become more convincing evidence for a common variance?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

If Xᵀe is 0, Xᵀ(2e) is also 0; with X of full rank, the fit is still the least squares fit.

Hint 2

Doubling a deviation multiplies its square by 4; taking the square root at the end reverses this squaring effect on the standard error.

Compare with the solution

The new times are Xb + 2e. The equations Xᵀ(2e) = 0 still hold: b does not change. The geometry of X and the number of degrees of freedom do not change either.

S becomes 4 × 10 = 40 min squared. The residual variance is 40/5 = 8 min squared; the variance of bd is 8 × 1/8 = 1 in the unit (min/km) squared. Its square root is 1.00 min/km, or 2 times the previous standard error.

The contrast in dispersion between groups remains: pairs that initially had no deviation still have none, and the others spread further apart. Doubling the residuals therefore does not make the common variance more credible. Keep the warning and examine an appropriate covariance; the numerical change alone does not validate any choice of inference method.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify the unchanged coefficients using the residual equations.
  • Multiply S by 4 and the standard error by 2.
  • Give the units at each step.
  • Distinguish the calculated result from the validity of the common variance assumption.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the standard error of a coefficient measure?

The estimated standard deviation of this coefficient between samples, under the stated model. It has the unit of the coefficient; it does not describe the error for a particular delivery.

Recall 2What chain leads from residuals to the classical standard error?

Sum the squared residuals, divide by n − p, multiply by the inverse of XᵀX, extract the diagonal and take its positive square root. p also counts the intercept.

Recall 3Which assumptions support the classical covariance of the coefficients?

A correct mean model with errors of 0 conditional mean, a common variance, 0 covariances between errors, a design of full rank and n > p. Normality is not required for this formula; it may be required for exact inference in small samples.

Recall 4How do residuals, leverage and influence differ?

The residual is the observed deviation in time; leverage depends on the position of the predictors; influence describes the change in the fit after removing an observation. A large residual does not automatically mean high leverage.

Recall 5The pair means are correct, but their dispersions differ: what can we conclude?

The mean calculation can be correct without making its classical covariance reliable. The contrast calls for examining the common variance assumption and an appropriate covariance; so few repeated observations do not prove a variance pattern.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without the page: name what varies between samples and its unit; recover the degrees of freedom, the covariance and the square root; explain what the residuals of our deliveries show; distinguish leverage from influence; finish with the assumptions still to be checked.
