Stopping time

Your goalDecide whether a random time is a stopping time for the stated information, then calculate the distribution of a bounded stopping time without looking ahead.

Before you start
c168-m5 : Radon-Nikodym · c129-m5 : backward induction · c130-m5 : financing conditions · c169-m1 : filtration · c169-m2 : martingale · c169-m3 : supermartingale

✿01 See the idea

A quality control station reveals the results for a batch in order. Each result is either compliant, denoted C, or an alert, denoted A. You decide in advance to stop at the first alert, and no later than after the last check in the batch. The results already revealed are enough to apply this rule.

Now imagine the instruction “stop at the last alert in the batch”. When an alert appears, you do not yet know whether another will follow. The time of the last alert is easy to find afterwards; you cannot necessarily recognize it when it occurs.

A stopping time formalizes this difference. At each step, you must be able to answer “has stopping already occurred?” using the information available at that step. You do not need to know the exact time at the outset or predict the next alert.

The scene and all its probabilities are fictional. The batch contains 3 possible checks. Imagine that their results have been fixed in sealed envelopes: results after stopping remain mathematical possibilities, even if the operator does not look at them. We work in discrete time, with a step for each result revealed.

The rule to remember

Two sequences with the same observable beginning must give the same answer to “has stopping already occurred?”. A bounded time can fail this test.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Stopping a batch at the first alert, with a fixed end

The batch has a horizon of H = 3 checks. Each result has probability p = 1/2 of being A and q = 1/2 of being C, independently of the other results. These probabilities are dimensionless and p + q = 1. These fictional assumptions are used to calculate the distribution; the stopping time criterion does not require independence. Let us find the stopping time, its distribution, and the average number of checks whose results are actually viewed.

  1. State exactly what can be observed

    n is the integer number of checks revealed, starting from 0. k will denote a check index. H is the known length of the batch. The indices n, k and the stopping times are counted in checks; they do not give a duration in seconds. Xk is the result at step k, with value C or A and no physical unit.

    Ω, the sample space, contains all complete sequences for the batch; ω denotes one such sequence. P is their probability distribution. ℱn denotes the collection of events that can be decided using the results revealed up to step n: it is the information at step n. The notation σ means “collection generated by the indicated observations”. Set m = min(n, H), the smaller of n and H. For m ≥ 1, this information is written as:

    ℱn=σ(X1,…,Xm)

    Initially, ℱ at step 0 contains only ∅ and Ω: ∅ is the impossible event, Ω the certain event. After H, no further observations arrive and the information remains that of the complete batch. Two sequences with the same prefix of length m are therefore indistinguishable using ℱn. This increasing sequence of information is the natural filtration of the results.

  2. Define a stopping time relative to this filtration

    A random time S assigns to each sequence ω a nonnegative integer index, or +∞. Here the symbol +∞ means “no stopping at a finite step”. S is a stopping time for the stated filtration if, for every integer n ≥ 0, the event “S has already occurred by step n” belongs to ℱn.

    ∀n≥0,{S≤n}∈ℱn

    The braces denote the event consisting of the sequences where the inequality is true; ∈ means “belongs to”. In our finite sample space, this criterion requires an identical answer for all sequences with the same observed beginning. It applies to every step and every possible prefix, not just to the path that actually occurred.

  3. Separate the alert from the end of the batch

    τ, read “tau”, is the first step in the batch where Xk = A; if there is no alert in the batch, set τ = +∞. Our actual stopping time is T = min(τ, H). The minimum chooses whichever happens first: “an alert appears” or “the batch ends”; in particular, min(+∞, H) = H.

    T=min(τ,H)

    On the sequence C A C, τ = 2 and T = 2 checks. The final result plays no part. On C C C, τ = +∞ but T = 3 checks: the batch ends even without an alert. The bound stops the checks without creating an alert at the last step.

  4. Prove that the rule uses only the present and the past

    At step 0, {T ≤ 0} = ∅: no check result has yet been viewed. For 1 ≤ n < H, stopping has occurred if and only if an alert is among the n results already revealed. The symbol ∪ means “or”, that is, the union of events; the index k indicates the steps included.

    {T≤n}=⋃k=1n{Xk=A}∈ℱn

    Each event in this union can be decided at step n, and so can their finite union. For n ≥ H, {T ≤ n} = Ω, since the bound forces stopping. All steps are covered: T is a stopping time. So is τ, with the same union up to min(n, H), even when some sequences give +∞. No probabilities were used in this proof.

  5. Calculate the distribution while distinguishing the reasons for stopping

    T = 1 means that X at the first step equals A: P(T = 1) = p = 1/2. For T = 2, we need C followed by A: independence allows the product q × p = 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4.

    T = 3 means that the initial results are C C. The last can be A or C. With C C A, the first alert arrives at the last step: probability q × q × p = 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8. With C C C, no alert arrives: probability q × q × q = 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8.

    These cases are disjoint: add 1/8 + 1/8 = 1/4. Thus P(T = 3) = 1/4, whereas P(τ = 3) = 1/8. Total probability check: 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4, then 3/4 + 1/4 = 1, with no unit. The possible stopping times cover the whole batch.

  6. Calculate the average number of check results viewed

    E[T] denotes the expectation of T under the fictional distribution P: the weighted average of its possible values. The number of results viewed is exactly T, because the checks start at step 1 and are consecutive. Each index is multiplied by its dimensionless probability; the sum retains the unit “checks”.

    E[T]=1×12+2×14+3×14

    The contributions are 1/2, 1/2 and 3/4 checks. The first contributions give 1 check; adding the last gives E[T] = 7/4 checks, or 1.75 checks. The value lies between 1 and 3, as T does. It describes repetitions of this model, without guaranteeing the number of checks for a particular batch or a benefit at a real quality control station.

  7. Check by counting sequences and checks reached

    Each step has 2 possible results. The initial steps give 2 × 2 = 4 beginnings; the last gives 4 × 2 = 8 equally likely complete sequences. The sequences A A A, A A C, A C A and A C C give T = 1: 4 sequences. C A A and C A C give T = 2: 2 sequences. C C A and C C C give T = 3: 2 sequences.

    The average index is therefore also given by (4 × 1 + 2 × 2 + 2 × 3) / 8 = 7/4 checks. Among the last sequences, 1 triggers a final alert and 1 ends without any alert. The count verifies the bound and the alert separately.

    Another way to read this: the first check result is always viewed, the next is viewed with probability q = 1/2, and the last with probability q × q = 1/4. On each sequence, counting the checks reached gives T again. Taking the average: 1 + 1/2 = 3/2, then 3/2 + 1/4 = 7/4 checks. We recover 1.75 checks through another decomposition.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculation. Explain why independence is used for products of probabilities but not for the stopping time criterion. Then describe what changes between C C A and C C C: the same actual stopping time, different reasons.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A finite random time must be a stopping time.”

    Test sequences with the same prefix. The last alert remains bounded by the end of the batch, but A C C and A C A are indistinguishable at step 1 and give opposite answers to “has the last alert already occurred?”. Return to the second diagram, then state the event that fails the test.

    Compare the hidden continuations in the second diagram

  • “A stopping time must be known before the next observation.”

    The criterion allows the current observation Xₙ. Before the first check, its value is unknown; just after an alert, you know to stop. Deciding after viewing the result and deciding a step earlier are different requirements. The last exercise examines this shift.

    Study the earlier stopping time in the last exercise

  • “Stopping at the last step means that an alert appears there.”

    The bound H also ends the sequence C C C. It contributes 1/8 to P(T = H), in addition to 1/8 for C C A. P(T = H) therefore equals 1/4, whereas P(τ = H) equals 1/8. Name the variable and the reason before counting.

    Revisit the distribution calculation and its check using sequences

Exercise 1 · Application · times and reasons

What the beginning C C tells us

At step 2, only C C have been revealed. Can you already announce that stopping has actually occurred? Then give τ and T for C C A and C C C, and the probabilities of these sequences under our model. Compare P(T = 3) and P(τ = 3).

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At the observed step, check separately whether an alert has appeared and whether H has been reached.

Hint 2

The last result is unrestricted for T = H. For τ = H, this last result must be A.

Compare with the solution

After C C, no alert has appeared and 2 < 3: stopping has not yet occurred. We already know that T will be 3, but that step has not yet been reached. Knowing a future time does not mean that the event has occurred.

C C A gives τ = T = 3 checks, with probability 1/8. C C C gives τ = +∞ and T = 3 checks, with probability 1/8. The sequences are disjoint: P(T = 3) = 1/8 + 1/8 = 1/4. Only C C A contributes to P(τ = 3) = 1/8.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish “time known” from “stopping has already occurred” at step 2.
  • Use +∞ for the complete absence of alerts, then apply the bound to T.
  • Justify the products by independence and the sum by disjoint cases.

Exercise 2 · Diagnostic · refute a rule

Can the last alert be used for a decision?

A colleague defines L as the index of the last alert in the batch, with L = 0 if the batch contains no alert. She says: “We can calculate L at the end and L ≤ H, so it is a stopping time.” Refute the argument for our filtration using a step, a common prefix, and complete sequences that would require different answers. What would change if the whole batch were revealed at the outset?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Choose a beginning that already contains A. One sequence can have no further alerts, while the other has another later.

Hint 2

It is enough to find a step n where {L ≤ n} cannot be decided from the revealed prefix.

Compare with the solution

At step 1, A C C and A C A have the same prefix A. Yet L = 1 on A C C and L = 3 on A C A. The event {L ≤ 1} is therefore true for one sequence and false for the other, although the available information is identical. It does not belong to ℱ at step 1: L is not a stopping time for this filtration.

The fact that L can be calculated from the complete batch establishes only that it is known at the end. If the whole batch were revealed at the outset, the initial information would already distinguish all the sequences: L would become a stopping time for this richer filtration. The property depends on the time–filtration pair. The sealed envelopes in our example rule out this advance knowledge.

Check your reasoning

  • Give sequences that are indistinguishable at the chosen step, with opposite truth values for {L ≤ n}.
  • State precisely which information is missing.
  • Explain why changing the filtration can change the verdict.

Exercise 3 · Proof · transform a rule

Impose an earlier bound or anticipate the alert

In the same batch, consider U = min(T, 2) and V = max(T − 1, 0). min chooses the smaller value, max the larger; V moves stopping one check earlier without going below step 0. Prove that U is a stopping time for our filtration and show that V is not. A proof must cover all steps; a counterexample can be enough to reject a rule.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For U, separate n < 2 and n ≥ 2.

Hint 2

For V, start by testing step 0 with sequences whose first alerts arrive at different steps.

Compare with the solution

For 0 ≤ n < 2, {U ≤ n} = {T ≤ n}, an event in ℱn since T is a stopping time. For n ≥ 2, {U ≤ n} = Ω, also in ℱn. These cases cover all integers n ≥ 0. U is therefore a stopping time.

On A C C, T = 1, so V = max(1 − 1, 0) = 0. On C A C, T = 2, so V = max(2 − 1, 0) = 1. At step 0, no result has been revealed: these sequences are indistinguishable. Yet {V ≤ 0} is true in the first case and false in the second. The event does not belong to the initial information; V is not a stopping time.

The earlier bound can be applied at a time fixed in advance. Moving stopping associated with an alert earlier would require knowing that alert before it is revealed here. The inequality V ≤ T alone therefore proves no stopping time property.

Check your reasoning

  • For U, give the events for each range of steps and justify their membership in ℱₙ.
  • For V, exhibit an event that cannot be decided at step 0, using specific sequences.
  • Do not infer the stopping time property from a simple comparison of the times.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which event must be decidable at every step for a random time to be a stopping time?

The event {S ≤ n}, using only the information ℱₙ. This must hold for every integer n ≥ 0; in our finite sample space, the answer must be constant across sequences with the same observed prefix.

Recall 2What are τ and T for a batch with entirely compliant results?

τ = +∞, because no alert occurs in the batch. T = min(τ, H) = H: the bound ends the checks without inventing an alert.

Recall 3Is independence necessary for our first alert to be a stopping time?

No. The membership of {T ≤ n} in ℱₙ follows from the observations used. Independence is an additional assumption that allows products of probabilities when calculating the distribution in this example.

Recall 4How can you refute the claim that the last alert is a stopping time?

At step 1, A C C and A C A show the same A; yet the last alert is at 1 or 3. {L ≤ 1} cannot be decided from this prefix. You must specify the filtration: revealing the entire batch at the outset would change the verdict.

Recall 5Why do min(T, 2) and max(T − 1, 0) behave differently?

The first rule adds an end known in advance and remains a stopping time. The second moves a time associated with a future observation earlier: in our batch, {V ≤ 0} depends on the first result, which is still unknown at the outset. It fails the criterion.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct this sequence of reasoning without rereading: state the filtration; express “already stopped” as {S ≤ n}; check every possible prefix; distinguish the first alert from the deterministic bound; calculate the distribution under separate assumptions; check the total probability and the mean. Then explain why the “last alert” requires information that is still missing. Reading this sequence or answering after a hint does not demonstrate independent mastery.
