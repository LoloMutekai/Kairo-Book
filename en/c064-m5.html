Level 2 · C064·M5

Matrix generalization and conditioning

Your goalUpdate the position and velocity of a cart together, explain the role of cross-covariance, and interpret this update as Gaussian conditioning.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A cart moves along a rail. Before a new reading, we estimate its position as 10 m and its velocity as 3 m/s. The sensor measures only position: it reads 12 m. Must the velocity remain unchanged because there is no speedometer?

That depends on the relationship between our errors. The model may predict that an underestimated position often accompanies an underestimated velocity. A reading farther along than predicted then provides information about both quantities. This relationship is the cross-covariance: the mean of the product of the centered position and velocity errors.

The matrix generalization preserves this relationship in an array of covariances. The reading is first related to the sensor model, and its difference from the prediction is then distributed among the state components. Replacing each number with a matrix requires care: the order of the products and the units determine what makes sense.

All the cart data are fictional. The diagram shows relationships between estimates; it does not represent a measured trajectory or an actually observed velocity. We perform an update at a given instant; the true position and true velocity remain unknown.

The rule to remember

A reading updates the components linked to its predicted error. Under a jointly Gaussian model, this update gives the conditional mean, and its covariance describes the remaining uncertainty.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Update the cart from its position reading alone

At the instant of the reading, the prediction is (10 m, 3 m/s). Its variances are 4 m² and 4 m²/s²; its cross-covariance is 2 m²/s. The reading is z = 12 m, with noise variance R = 1 m². Let us calculate the mean and the entire covariance after this reading.

  1. Define the state, sensor, and assumptions

    x is the column of true quantities: d, the position in meters, followed by u, the velocity in meters per second. The hat denotes an estimate; the superscripts − and + mean before and after the reading. H is the matrix that transforms the state into a predicted observation. Here it selects d, so its coefficients are dimensionless.

    x=[du];H=[10];z=Hx+η

    η is the reading noise, in meters, with mean 0 and independent of the prediction error e = x − x̂⁻. R is its covariance, here a variance. We assume e and η are Gaussian: all their linear combinations follow a normal distribution. P⁻ = E[eeᵀ] is the predicted covariance; E denotes the expectation. T means transpose: exchange rows and columns. Centering requires E[e] = 0 and E[η] = 0, with the units of each component.

    x^−=[103];P−=[4224];R=[1]

    In these numerical arrays, the units are those stated in the problem and the second diagram. P⁻ is symmetric. It must assign a nonnegative variance to every combination of errors; we will check this numerically at the end. R is strictly positive in our case.

  2. Move from the scalar case to compatible dimensions

    In general, n is the number of state components and m the number of reading components. x̂ and x are n × 1 columns; P and Q are n × n; H is m × n; R is m × m. Here n = 2 and m = 1. Multiplying a matrix by a vector means taking, row by row, the sum of the corresponding products.

    n and m are dimensionless counts. The components of w have the units of the state; the entries of Q have the products of the units of the corresponding components. An entry of F converts the unit of its input component to that of its output component; an entry of H converts a state unit to a reading unit.

    The scalar prediction already studied becomes x̂⁻ = F x̂prev. and P⁻ = F Pprev. Fᵀ + Q. F is the known transition, of size n × n; “prev.” denotes the previous updated result. Q is the covariance of the process noise w, with mean 0 and independent of the previous error. This noise enters through x = F xprev. + w.

    For our cart, a model with constant velocity over Δt = 1 s could use the transition below. Its coefficient Δt is in seconds, which converts velocity to displacement; the diagonal coefficients are dimensionless. We start from the supplied prediction: here we choose neither Q nor a previous state.

    F=[1Δt01]

    The double product F P Fᵀ propagates the covariances, just as F squared propagated a scalar variance. Transposing does not mean inverting. The following update needs H and the prediction, without repeating the time step.

  3. Compare the reading in the sensor space

    The innovation y = z − Hx̂⁻ is in meters. The letter y denotes the difference actually received; its covariance S describes the possible differences before the reading. Introduce c = P⁻Hᵀ, the covariance between the state error and the predicted observation error. The independent reading noise adds no term to c.

    c=P−HT=[42];S=Hc+R=[5]

    H selects the first column of P⁻ in c, then its first component in Hc. Thus S = 4 + 1 = 5 m². The components of c are 4 m² and 2 m²/s. The received innovation is y = 12 − 10 = 2 m. Subtracting z directly from a velocity would mix meters with meters per second.

  4. Construct the gain and understand conditioning

    The gain K transforms an innovation into a state adjustment. Its size is n × m. Its formula is K = c × inverse(S), for invertible S. Here the superscript −1 denotes the matrix inverse, rather than a time label. In our case S contains a single entry: inverting amounts to dividing by 5 m².

    Each entry of K has the unit of its state component divided by that of its reading component. The entries of S have the products of the units of the reading components; those of c have a state unit multiplied by a reading unit.

    K=cS−1=[4/52/5]

    The position component is 4/5 = 0.80, dimensionless. The velocity component is 2/5 = 0.40 per second. This gain is therefore not a probability shared by all components.

    Why does this gain give a conditional update? Before seeing z, the random innovation is He + η. Its covariance with e is c. The error after the update is e⁺ = e − K(He + η). Its covariance with the innovation is c − KS, hence 0 with this choice of K. Since these variables are jointly Gaussian, this 0 covariance makes them independent: knowing the innovation no longer changes the 0 mean of the remaining error.

    Conditioning means describing the state while taking the received reading into account. We then obtain the formulas below. Cov denotes covariance, and E[x | z] the mean of x given the value of z, with earlier observations implicitly still known. Without the Gaussian assumption, the same formulas give the best affine update in terms of squared error under these covariances, but not necessarily the exact conditional distribution.

    x^+=E[x|z]=x^−+Ky
    P+=Cov(x|z)=P−−KSKT

  5. Apply the same innovation to the appropriate components

    The position adjustment is 4/5 × 2 = 8/5 m. The velocity adjustment is 2/5 per second × 2 m = 4/5 m/s. The positive cross-covariance gives both adjustments the same direction.

    x^+=[103]+[4/52/5]2=[58/519/5]

    We obtain 11.60 m and 3.80 m/s. The position moves closer to 12 m while retaining part of the prediction. The velocity changes without having been measured directly: this is an inference under the covariance model, rather than a new velocity reading.

  6. Update every entry of the covariance

    Since KS = c, the subtracted term KSKᵀ is also Kcᵀ. We multiply a column by a row; each entry is the product of the corresponding components. The reduction is 16/5 m² for position, 8/5 m²/s in each off-diagonal entry, and 4/5 m²/s² for velocity.

    P+=[4224]−[16/58/58/54/5]=[4/52/52/516/5]

    In decimal form, the updated variances are 0.80 m² and 3.20 m²/s²; the cross-covariance is 0.40 m²/s. The velocity retains uncertainty despite the indirect information. The conditional covariance does not depend on the received value of z in this linear Gaussian model with fixed covariances.

  7. Check the calculation and the resulting covariance

    Check the gain: 4/5 × 5 = 4 and 2/5 × 5 = 2, with the units of c. Check the covariance: it remains symmetric. Before the update, its determinant is 16 − 4 = 12 m⁴/s². Afterward, it is 64/25 − 4/25 = 12/5 m⁴/s². The first diagonal entries and these determinants are positive: for these symmetric matrices of size 2 × 2, this verifies that they give strictly positive variances in every nonzero direction.

    Another approach calculates the covariance of the error e⁺ = (I − KH)e − Kη. I is the n × n identity matrix, which leaves a vector unchanged. The two independent terms give the Joseph form below, where A = I − KH. It yields exactly the same matrix in our rational calculation.

    A=I−KH;P+=AP−AT+KRKT

    Subtracting KSKᵀ reduces the variances of all linear combinations, because this matrix assigns nonnegative values to quadratic forms. This remains a property of the model. No calculated variance demonstrates that the real cart is close to our estimate.

  8. Preserve the meaning of the calculation on a computer

    For multiple readings, S is an m × m matrix, and the order of the factors matters. In numerical code, we can find K by solving S Kᵀ = HP⁻, since S and P⁻ are symmetric. This avoids forming inverse(S), but does not by itself resolve poor numerical conditioning: a small perturbation of the inputs can then change the solution considerably.

    A positive definite covariance R guarantees that S is positive definite if P⁻ is a valid covariance. “Positive definite” means that aᵀRa > 0 for every nonzero column a of combination coefficients. We choose their units so that the terms in this combination of reading errors can be added; the quadratic result is a variance. We can therefore solve the system; we must still monitor units, scales, and nearly redundant readings. A nonzero determinant is not enough to certify good numerical stability.

    The Joseph form avoids some losses of symmetry and positivity caused by subtractions in rounded arithmetic; it does not validate a poor choice of noise. The exercise on the duplicated reading shows a case where S becomes singular, meaning that it has no inverse. Probabilistic conditioning and numerical conditioning thus refer to two different questions.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The sensor does not measure velocity, so it cannot change.”

    Return to c = P⁻Hᵀ. Its velocity component contains the cross-covariance. This conveys position information to the velocity. If it is 0, the velocity adjustment is 0, as in the second exercise.

    Recover the gain and its units

  • “Inverting S means taking the reciprocal of each entry.”

    Multiplying a matrix by its inverse must give the identity. Entrywise division generally does not satisfy this property. Solve the compatible system; if readings are duplicated, first check that S has an inverse.

    Study the duplicated reading

  • “A smaller covariance proves that my position is more accurate.”

    The updated covariance describes uncertainty under the stated assumptions. A model error or a biased reading can make this confidence excessive. Distinguish consistency of the calculation from validation against an authorized external reference.

    Review the checks and their scope

S remains 5 m². The first gain component remains 4/5, and the second becomes 0/5 = 0 per second. The velocity receives an adjustment of 0 m/s and remains at 3 m/s.

The reduction in its variance is 0 m²/s²: it remains at 4 m²/s². The position is still updated to 11.60 m with a variance of 0.80 m². The absence of a relationship prevents this reading from providing information about velocity at this instant. A later transition can recreate a cross-covariance.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and transfer

The same reading arrives twice

The program receives an exact copy of the reading z. We stack the two copies as two readings. Their errors are the same variable η: their covariance is therefore R, including in the off-diagonal entries. The matrices Hcopie, Rcopie, and Scopie are given below. Calculate the determinant of Scopie. Can we apply the formula with its inverse? Propose a fix without inventing a reading.

Hcopie=[1010];Rcopie=[1111];Scopie=[5555]
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The determinant of a square array of size 2 is the product of its diagonal entries minus the product of its off-diagonal entries.

Hint 2

Apply Scopie to the column (1, -1). Can it distinguish this direction from the 0 column?

Compare with the solution

The determinant is 5 × 5 − 5 × 5 = 25 − 25 = 0 m⁴. Scopie maps the nonzero column (1, -1) to the 0 column: it is singular and has no inverse. The formula requiring inverse(S) therefore does not apply as written.

The fix here is to remove the exact copy and condition only once on the original reading. We recover 11.60 m and 3.80 m/s, with the covariance from the example. The conditional distribution remains well defined on the information actually received.

Replacing the off-diagonal entries of Rcopie with 0 would mean declaring independent noises, whereas the data state the opposite. Arbitrarily adding noise to make the matrix invertible would also change the model. We must fix the representation of the reading before choosing a numerical method.

Check your reasoning

  • Show the 0 determinant and a nonzero direction mapped to 0.
  • Distinguish the impossibility of this inverse from the existence of conditioning on the single reading.
  • Remove the copy without claiming to obtain additional information.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why do we compare z with Hx̂⁻?

H transforms the state prediction into a reading prediction. The subtraction then uses the same components and units.

Recall 2How can a position reading update the velocity?

Through the predicted cross-covariance between their errors. It enters c = P⁻Hᵀ, hence the velocity component of the gain. If it is 0 here, this update does not change the velocity.

Recall 3What does the jointly Gaussian assumption add to the linear update?

It identifies the calculated mean and covariance with those of the exact conditional distribution. Without it, this update remains an optimal affine update in terms of squared error under the stated covariances.

Recall 4How can we calculate the gain without forming an inverse, and what limitation must we still check?

Solve S Kᵀ = H P⁻. Check that S is invertible and monitor scales and dependencies among readings. Solving the system does not eliminate poor numerical conditioning.

Recall 5What does a valid, smaller updated covariance prove?

A reduction in uncertainty within the stated model. This proves neither the accuracy of this particular estimate nor the suitability of the noises for the real situation.
