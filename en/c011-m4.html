Level 0 · C011·M4

Recurrence calculations

Your goalCalculate the successive states of a water tank from a starting value and a rule, then check the order of operations.

Before you start
c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c011-m1 : summation notation · c011-m2 : arithmetic sequences · c011-m3 : geometric sequences

✿01 See the idea

A tank with marked graduations contains 10 doses of water. You remove half its contents, then add 2 doses. You repeat these same actions with what is now in the tank. The starting value does not reappear on every turn: the result becomes the new quantity to halve.

Let us call the sequence of actions “remove half, then add” a cycle. Here, a state is the quantity present after a complete cycle, or at the start before any cycle. The sequence of states therefore records the history of the same tank. To predict the next state, you need to know the current state.

Before following the lines, predict what happens if you add the extra water before removing half. Some of the added water will then be removed along with the rest. The actions are the same, but their order changes what you keep.

This model and its quantities are fictional. A dose is our unit of volume, always the same size; a fraction of a dose is allowed. We assume that exactly half is removed, that the addition is exact, and that there are no other losses. A cycle counts actions; it does not represent a known duration or a natural law of drainage.

The rule to remember

Write down the starting value; apply the actions in order; keep the complete result as the input to the next cycle.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Keeping a record of the tank

Calculate the quantity after 2 cycles of “halve then add” from 10 doses, adding 2 doses per cycle. Then compare this with 2 cycles in the reverse order. We use the exact assumptions of the model.

  1. Name the quantities and the rule

    n is the number of completed cycles: an integer starting at 0, with no unit of volume. un is the corresponding quantity of water, in doses. The subscript n labels a step; it is not a factor multiplying u. Thus, u at subscript 0 denotes the start; at subscript 2, it denotes the tank after 2 cycles.

    a denotes the quantity added on each cycle, in doses; its value remains 2. The divisor 2 has no unit: halving a quantity in doses still gives doses. The + sign in the subscript n + 1 means “the next cycle”.

    un+1=un2+a

    This recurrence relation tells you how to move from one state to the next. It requires an initial state to begin. An explicit formula would give the quantity from the number n without reconstructing the previous states; we do not seek one here.

  2. Initialize without counting an extra cycle

    u0=10 doses

    At the start, none of the actions in the cycle has yet been performed. Write a row reading “start: 10 doses”. The quantity at subscript 1 will be obtained after the next cycle, rather than by simply copying the starting value.

  3. Apply the actions to the starting quantity

    Removing half leaves the other half: 10 ÷ 2 = 5 doses. You must then add the 2 doses, which are no longer affected by the removal. The resulting state is 5 + 2 = 7 doses.

    u1=102+2=7 doses

    The next row of the record is therefore “cycle 1: 7 doses”. This result is now the current state.

  4. Reuse the new state

    The next cycle starts with 7 doses. The half kept is exactly 7/2 of a dose, or 3.5 doses. Whole-number graduations do not force us to round: the tank allows fractions of a dose.

    u2=72+2=3.5+2=5.5 doses

    The exact result is 11/2 of a dose, or 5.5 doses; this decimal representation is exact. The record is: subscript 0, 10 doses; subscript 1, 7 doses; subscript 2, 5.5 doses. Reusing 10 instead of 7 would have repeated the same starting cycle.

  5. Reverse the actions to check

    To check the result, first undo the addition: 5.5 − 2 = 3.5 doses. Then double what remains: 2 × 3.5 = 7 doses. You recover the quantity from the previous cycle. The check reverses the order of the actions.

    Another check on this cycle: the 7 doses were split into 3.5 doses removed and 3.5 doses kept. Combining these halves gives back 7 doses. Then the 3.5 doses kept and the 2 doses added account for the entire result, with no unexplained extra water.

  6. Change the order while keeping the same starting value

    Let vn denote the quantity in doses after n cycles in the order “add then halve”. The letter v distinguishes this other record. Its starting value is also 10 doses. We now add before dividing; the parentheses indicate that the added water is also halved.

    v0=10,vn+1=vn+a2

    Cycle 1: (10 + 2) ÷ 2 = 12 ÷ 2 = 6 doses. Cycle 2: (6 + 2) ÷ 2 = 8 ÷ 2 = 4 doses. In this record too, the previous result provides the next starting value.

    After 2 cycles, the difference between the orders is 5.5 − 4 = 1.5 of a dose. After 1 cycle, it was only 1 dose. The order is therefore part of the rule, and the difference must be recalculated at the step being compared.

  7. State what the record establishes

    We have obtained the requested states and checked the effect of the parentheses. These finite records do not prove what would happen after infinitely many cycles. They describe the model under its assumptions, without predicting the drainage of a real tank.

    Here, a recurrence calculation is a rule to repeat from a starting value. By itself, it does not constitute a mathematical proof by induction; that proof method will be studied elsewhere.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why 7 replaces 10 in the next cycle, then repeat the check by working backwards from 5.5 doses. Use the actions to show which water would also be removed if the addition came before halving.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I always divide the starting quantity.”

    Write down the record and circle its last quantity. In the cycle at subscript 2, the input is 7 doses, because the previous cycle has already changed the tank. If you start again from 10, you restart an experiment instead of continuing the same one.

    Return to “Reuse the new state” and the reverse check

  • “The same operations give the same result in both orders.”

    Compare 10 ÷ 2 + 2 = 7 and (10 + 2) ÷ 2 = 6 doses. In the second expression, the added water is also halved. Keep the parentheses and a separate record for each order.

    Reread the actions and compare the columns in the diagram

  • “A few decreasing states prove what the sequence does forever.”

    A calculated list establishes only the states in that list. You can state their values and order, but no conclusion about infinitely many states is proved here. In the last exercise, separate the finite calculation from a conjecture about the sequence.

    Return to “Discuss a supposedly equivalent rule”

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · definition and reading

What you need to start

A card says only “remove half, then add 2 doses”. Does it let you determine the result? Complete it for our tank, then say what subscript 2 and the quantity u at that subscript represent. Which quantity would be used for the next cycle?

Hint 1

The rule says what to do to a quantity, but which quantity does it receive at the start?

Hint 2

The subscript counts cycles; the value of u measures the water. Recall the last row of the record mentally, without repeating the start.

Compare with the solution

The card alone gives no definite result, because the starting value is missing. You must specify the initial 10 doses in the tank and keep this order of actions. The start is written as u at subscript 0 equal to 10 doses.

Subscript 2 counts 2 completed cycles; it does not mean 2 doses. The corresponding quantity is 5.5 doses. This is what enters the next cycle. The rule and the starting value therefore play different and necessary roles.

Check your reasoning

  • You identify the missing starting value, without giving a result from the rule alone.
  • You distinguish the number of cycles from the volume in doses.
  • You keep 5.5 doses to continue with the same tank.

Exercise 2 · Application · record and check on paper

Continue without returning to the start

In the order “halve then add”, start from the state already obtained after 2 cycles: 5.5 doses. Calculate the states at subscripts 3 and 4. Keep the exact fractions, also give the exact decimals, then check the final state by reversing the actions.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Half of 5.5 doses is 11/4 of a dose. Then add 2 doses and keep this new result.

Hint 2

To work backwards through a cycle, subtract the added quantity from the result, then multiply what remains by 2.

Compare with the solution

Cycle 3: half of 11/2 of a dose is 11/4 of a dose, or 2.75 doses. Adding 2 doses gives 19/4 of a dose, or 4.75 doses. This result becomes the input to the next cycle.

Cycle 4: 19/4 ÷ 2 = 19/8 of a dose, or 2.375 doses. After the addition, we obtain 35/8 of a dose, or 4.375 doses. The decimals shown here are exact; no intermediate rounding has been used.

Reverse check: 4.375 − 2 = 2.375 doses, then 2 × 2.375 = 4.75 doses. This recovers the previous row. A check returning 10 doses at this step would reveal confusion in the record.

Check your reasoning

  • You use 5.5 then 4.75 as successive inputs.
  • You obtain 19/4 then 35/8 of a dose and keep the unit.
  • You undo the actions in reverse order and recover the previous state.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · calculation and objection

Discuss a supposedly equivalent rule

Someone writes “add then halve” and claims that this amounts to the same thing, because they use the same starting value and the same actions. Start from their 4 doses after 2 cycles and calculate their state after 3 cycles. Compare it with the result of the previous exercise at the same subscript. Is this a proof of what happens after infinitely many cycles?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In their order, the parentheses include the current state and the 2 added doses.

Hint 2

Compare only rows with the same subscript. A finite counterexample is enough to refute “the same”, but by itself it does not describe all future states.

Compare with the solution

Their cycle 3 gives (4 + 2) ÷ 2 = 6 ÷ 2 = 3 doses. The reference order gave 4.75 doses at the same subscript. The difference is 4.75 − 3 = 1.75 of a dose; the rules are therefore not equivalent for this tank.

The cause is concrete: pouring before removing also subjects the added water to halving. The equivalence could already be refuted after the cycle at subscript 1, with 7 versus 6 doses. The extra calculation shows that the difference at the new subscript must be recalculated.

These values establish a difference over the calculated cycles. They prove neither a limit nor behavior over infinitely many cycles. A conclusion of that scope would require further reasoning, which this unit does not provide.

Check your reasoning

  • You calculate 3 doses with the addition inside the parentheses.
  • You compare with 4.75 doses and justify the difference of 1.75 of a dose.
  • You explain what happens to the added water and limit your conclusion to what the calculations establish.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be given to calculate the states of a recurrence?

An initial state and a rule that transforms the current state into the next state. Here: 10 doses at the start, then remove half and add 2 doses on each cycle, in that order.

Recall 2In uₙ, what does n measure and what does uₙ measure?

n counts the completed cycles since the start at subscript 0; it has no unit of volume. uₙ is the corresponding quantity of water, in doses. The small n labels a step; it is not multiplication.

Recall 3Why does the next calculation start from 7 rather than 10 doses?

The completed cycle has left 7 doses in the same tank. That is its current state. Starting again from 10 would repeat the start instead of continuing the record.

Recall 4Why does moving the addition before halving change the result?

The added water is then also halved. From 10 doses, halving then adding gives 7 doses; adding then halving gives 6 doses. The parentheses preserve this difference in actions.

Recall 5How can you check a cycle, and what does this check fail to prove?

Undo the addition, then double to recover the previous state; also check the doses removed and kept. This checks the calculated cycle, without proving a limit or validating a model of a real tank.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Hide the page and reconstruct the reasoning with the tank: specify the starting value and unit, name the current state, keep the actions in order, calculate the next state and keep it. Recreate a row of the record, then check it by reversing the actions; finally explain why the alternative order differs and why a finite record is not enough to draw a conclusion about infinitely many cycles.
