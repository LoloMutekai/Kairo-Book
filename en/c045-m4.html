Level 1 · C045·M4

Improper integrals

Your goalReplace an infinite or singular endpoint with a cutoff, calculate over a regular interval, then justify convergence or divergence using a limit.

Before you start
c034-m5 : average value · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales · c045-m1 : integration by parts · c045-m2 : partial fractions · c045-m3 : trigonometric substitutions

✿01 See the idea

A container collects a leak that slows down without ever stopping. Do we need an infinitely large container to keep waiting indefinitely? Not necessarily: what matters is the volume added over time, which is the area under the flow rate, rather than the duration alone.

In our fictional scene, the flow rate starts at 12 mL/min and decreases according to a rule given below. We collect 9 mL during the first 3 minutes. After 9 min, we have 10.8 mL. The duration can keep increasing while the volume approaches 12 mL without reaching it at any finite time.

An improper integral is defined by exactly this approach: first calculate up to a finite time, then examine the limit as that time increases indefinitely. The integral converges if this limit exists and is finite. Otherwise, it diverges; an infinite limit does not count as convergence.

Another problem arises if a flow rate model grows without bound as a finite time approaches. We must then approach that time without including it. We will keep the same leak to compare these two situations. All the values and rules on this page are fictional; they do not describe a hydraulic experiment.

The rule to remember

Choose a cutoff that gives a regular interval, find an antiderivative, subtract its endpoint values, then take a limit: a value obtained at a finite cutoff is never enough to prove convergence.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What capacity is needed to collect the leak?

The fictional flow rate q(t) is q⋆ divided by the square of (1 + t/τ), for t ≥ 0 min, with q⋆ = 12 mL/min and τ = 1 min. Let us find the total volume in the model, then the time from which the remaining volume no longer exceeds 0.5 mL.

  1. Understand the quantities before integrating

    t is the elapsed time, in minutes; q(t) is the instantaneous flow rate, in millilitres per minute. q⋆ is the flow rate at startup. τ is a strictly positive reference duration. Thus t/τ is dimensionless: we can add it to 1. The exponent 2 means that the whole denominator is squared.

    q(t)=q⋆(1+tτ)2

    We assume that this rule holds for all positive times, with no stopping or change of regime. The flow rate is continuous and positive over every finite time interval. Its integral gives a volume: mL/min multiplied by min gives mL. A capacity equal to the limit would suffice in this exact model; allowing a margin for a real container would require further information.

  2. Set a cutoff and find an antiderivative

    T is a finite positive time, in minutes. V(T) denotes the volume collected between startup and T, in millilitres. Ordinary integration applies over this regular interval. The symbol dt indicates that we accumulate with respect to time t.

    V(T)=∫0Tq(t) dt

    An antiderivative F is a function whose derivative gives q. Here F(t) = −q⋆τ/(1 + t/τ). Differentiating the reciprocal gives a minus sign; differentiating the inner expression gives 1/τ. These factors cancel the minus sign and τ: F′(t) gives q⋆ divided by the square of (1 + t/τ), which is q(t). F has the units of volume.

    Subtract the value at the start from the value at the end. This difference, rather than F(T) alone, measures the volume collected.

    V(T)=F(T)−F(0)=q⋆τ(1−11+T/τ)

  3. Complete a calculation with a finite cutoff

    Take T = 3 min. The ratio T/τ is 3/1 = 3, which is dimensionless. The denominator is 1 + 3 = 4. The factor q⋆τ is 12 × 1 = 12 mL.

    V(3)=12(1−14)=12−3=9 mL

    Algebraic check: putting the terms over a common denominator gives V(T) = q⋆τT/(τ + T). At our cutoff, this alternative expression gives 12 × 1 × 3/(1 + 3) = 9 mL. The result is positive and less than 12 mL, as predicted by the previous expression.

  4. Move from the cutoff to the improper integral

    As T increases indefinitely, 1/(1 + T/τ) tends to 0. This establishes a finite limit; T has never been replaced with a number called “infinity”. We define the total volume I using this limit:

    I=∫0+∞q(t) dt=limT→+∞V(T)=q⋆τ=12 mL

    The integral converges. At every finite time, a positive volume still has to flow out: V(T) never equals the total. Conversely, seeing a flow rate tend to 0 is not enough to guarantee a finite total. In the last exercise, we will test a leak that slows down more slowly.

  5. Calculate what remains and check the tolerance

    R(T) denotes the volume remaining after T, in millilitres. It is itself defined by the limit of the integral between T and a new finite endpoint that moves further away. The same antiderivative gives R(T) = q⋆τ/(1 + T/τ). We recover I = V(T) + R(T), with all volumes positive.

    At T = 9 min, the denominator is 10. The remaining volume is 12/10 = 6/5 mL, or 1.2 mL. The collected volume is 54/5 mL, or 10.8 mL. Check: 10.8 + 1.2 = 12.0 mL.

    To require R(T) ≤ 0.5 mL, divide the total volume by the tolerance. The ratio is 12/(1/2) = 24, which is dimensionless. Since all factors are positive, we can multiply the inequality by the denominator without changing its direction:

    R(T)≤0.5 mL⇔1+T/τ≥24⇔T≥23 min

    Subtracting gives T/τ ≥ 24 − 1 = 23; multiplying by τ gives T ≥ 23 min. At this threshold, the remaining volume is exactly 12/24 = 0.5 mL. For a strictly smaller remaining volume, T would have to be strictly greater than this threshold.

  6. Also handle a singularity at a finite time

    Return to the same leak with a different fictional startup rule: s(t) = q⋆/√(t/τ), defined for 0 < t ≤ τ. The letter s denotes this new flow rate, in mL/min. It becomes arbitrarily large near startup; the model provides no finite value at t = 0. This rule is not assumed to describe a real opening.

    ε is a cutoff time in minutes, with 0 < ε < τ. We integrate over [ε, τ], where the flow rate is continuous. An antiderivative is G(t) = 2q⋆τ√(t/τ). For positive t, its derivative is 2q⋆τ × 1/(2τ√(t/τ)) = s(t). The square root is dimensionless.

    ∫ετs(t) dt=2q⋆τ(1−ετ)

    As ε tends to 0 through positive values, the square root tends to 0. The improper integral between startup and τ therefore converges to 2q⋆τ = 24 mL. A singular flow rate does not rule out a finite area. Neither the flow rate nor its derivative has been evaluated at the singular point. The startup exercise asks you to check a particular cutoff.

  7. Check each problematic endpoint separately

    The method applies to an infinite endpoint, to a flow rate that is unbounded at the left or right endpoint of an interval, or to an interior singularity. Near a right endpoint b, set the cutoff before b and take a limit as it approaches b from the left. Here b denotes a finite time in minutes.

    If a singularity lies inside the interval, at a time c, split the interval on either side of c. c denotes the singular time. Each integral must have its own finite limit for their sum to define the ordinary improper integral. The same rule applies when an interval has several problematic endpoints.

    Any cancellation between divergent quantities cannot replace these separate limits. The leak models on this page are positive: no cancellation of signs is involved. In each case, the conclusion concerns the rule being studied; it does not prove that the rule remains valid at extreme times.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I substitute infinity directly for T in the antiderivative.”

    Start with a finite cutoff T and calculate F(T) − F(0). Then take the limit of this expression. Writing +∞ as an endpoint denotes this procedure; it does not permit arithmetic operations on a number called “infinity”.

    Review the cutoff and then the passage to the limit

  • “The flow rate tends to 0, so the total volume is finite.”

    The limit of the flow rate and the limit of its accumulation are two different questions. Calculate the volume up to a finite cutoff for the more slowly decreasing leak in the last exercise: its logarithm grows without bound despite a flow rate that tends to 0.

    Compare with the leak that slows down more slowly

  • “An antiderivative lets me cross a singularity without a cutoff.”

    First identify the points where the flow rate is undefined or becomes unbounded. Using an antiderivative on a regular interval does not remove the need to take the required limits. For an interior singularity, require a finite limit on each side, without subtracting divergent quantities.

Exercise 1 · Application · extend the collection

Is waiting a long time enough to collect the total?

Keep the initial rule q, with its squared denominator, and its parameters. Calculate the volume collected and the volume remaining at T = 99 min. Can we say that the limiting volume has already been reached? Is the tolerance of 0.5 mL met?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate 1 + T/τ, then the volume remaining after the cutoff.

Hint 2

The volume collected and the volume remaining must add up to 12 mL.

Compare with the solution

The ratio T/τ is 99; the denominator is 100. The remaining volume is R(T) = 12/100 = 3/25 mL, or 0.12 mL.

The collected volume is V(T) = 12 − 3/25 = 297/25 mL, or 11.88 mL. Check: 11.88 + 0.12 = 12.00 mL.

The remaining volume is strictly positive: the limit has not been reached. It is nevertheless less than 0.5 mL, so the tolerance is met. Saying “close enough for this tolerance” does not mean “exactly equal”.

Check your reasoning

  • Give the exact fractions 297/25 mL and 3/25 mL before the decimals.
  • Check the sum and retain the unit of volume.
  • Distinguish reaching the limit from meeting the tolerance.

Exercise 2 · Reasoning · set a cutoff near startup

An unbounded flow rate, a finite collected volume

Return to the startup rule s(t) = q⋆/√(t/τ), on 0 < t ≤ τ. At the cutoff ε = τ/4, calculate the flow rate s(ε), then the volume between ε and τ. Then find the total volume between startup and τ using a limit. Why is the flow rate at the cutoff not enough to calculate this total?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

ε/τ = 1/4 and its positive square root is 1/2.

Hint 2

Use the difference G(τ) − G(ε), then let ε tend to 0 from the right. Do not replace the varying flow rate with a rectangle.

Compare with the solution

The cutoff is ε = 1/4 min, or 0.25 min. Its normalized square root is 1/2, so s(ε) = 12/(1/2) = 24 mL/min. This is an instantaneous flow rate, not a volume.

G(τ) is 2 × 12 × 1 = 24 mL. G(ε) is 24 × 1/2 = 12 mL. The difference is therefore 24 − 12 = 12 mL between ε and τ.

As ε tends to 0 from the right, G(ε) tends to 0 mL. The improper integral converges to 24 mL. At our particular cutoff, the volume omitted between startup and ε is still 12 mL: it must be distinguished from the volume collected after ε.

The flow rate s(ε) changes when the cutoff moves and is not constant over the interval. Multiplying this single flow rate by a duration therefore does not give the exact accumulation. The total comes from the integral and then its limit.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish 24 mL/min from the volume 12 mL.
  • Show the difference of antiderivative values with a strictly positive cutoff.
  • Justify the total 24 mL using the limit, without assigning a finite value to s(0).

Exercise 3 · Transfer · refute a conclusion

The leak slows down more slowly

Still using the same fictional collection, replace only the flow rate rule with r(t) = q⋆/(1 + t/τ), for t ≥ 0. The parameters q⋆ and τ stay the same. Show that the flow rate tends to 0, then calculate its volume up to T and decide whether a finite capacity suffices for all time. Recall that ln denotes the natural logarithm, that ln(1) = 0, and that ln(x) grows without bound as x tends to +∞.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The derivative of ln(1 + t/τ) is 1/[τ(1 + t/τ)]. What coefficient then gives an antiderivative of the flow rate?

Hint 2

Apply the antiderivative between 0 and T before examining its limit. The ratio inside the logarithm must remain dimensionless.

Compare with the solution

The denominator 1 + t/τ grows without bound, so r(t) tends to 0 mL/min. However, an antiderivative is H(t) = q⋆τ ln(1 + t/τ): its derivative is q⋆τ × 1/[τ(1 + t/τ)] = r(t).

∫0Tr(t) dt=q⋆τln(1+T/τ)

H(0) = q⋆τ ln(1) = 0 mL. As T tends to +∞, the logarithm grows without bound and q⋆τ = 12 mL is strictly positive. The volume therefore tends to +∞: the integral diverges. No finite container volume suffices to collect the flow described by this rule for all time.

The qualitative check is consistent: at a positive time, 1 + t/τ is greater than 1; its square is greater than the expression itself. The initial flow rate q is therefore less than the new flow rate r. This ordering alone would not prove divergence of the integral of r; the antiderivative and its limit provide the proof here. The conclusion concerns this new rule and does not invalidate the convergent calculation for the initial rule.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify the limit of the flow rate and the limit of the accumulation separately.
  • Check the antiderivative H by differentiation and keep the logarithm argument dimensionless.
  • Conclude that the integral diverges to +∞, despite the flow rate tending to 0.
  • Do not use the comparison of flow rates as sufficient proof of divergence.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do we define the integral of our flow rate over an infinite duration?

Integrate between startup and a finite cutoff T, then take the limit as T tends to +∞. A limit that exists and is finite defines convergence; a value at a large T is not enough.

Recall 2How do we distinguish the limiting total from the volume already collected?

For the initial rule, V(T) = q⋆τ(1 − 1/(1 + T/τ)) and R(T) = q⋆τ/(1 + T/τ). Their sum is q⋆τ. The remaining volume is positive at every finite time: the total is approached but never reached.

Recall 3How do we handle a flow rate that becomes unbounded at startup?

Set a cutoff at a strictly positive time ε, calculate the integral over the regular interval, then let ε tend to 0 from the right. Examine the limit of the area, rather than just the height of the flow rate. An interior singularity requires separate finite limits on each side.

Recall 4Why does a flow rate tending to 0 not guarantee a finite total volume?

For r(t) = q⋆/(1 + t/τ), the flow rate tends to 0, but the volume q⋆τ ln(1 + T/τ) grows without bound. Convergence concerns the accumulation and must be justified by its own limit.

Recall 5What should we check before declaring that an improper integral converges?

State the problematic endpoints and the assumptions; check the antiderivative by differentiation; check the difference at the regular endpoints and its units; establish a finite limit for each piece. A calculation with a finite cutoff and a physically accurate model still require separate evidence.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the method from memory: identify the problematic endpoint; set a cutoff that avoids it; check an antiderivative; subtract its values over the regular interval; study the limit; conclude that the integral is finite or divergent; check the remaining volume when it is available. Then explain why an infinite duration, an unbounded height, and a flow rate tending to 0 each give no conclusion on their own.
