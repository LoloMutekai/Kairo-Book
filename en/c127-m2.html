Level 2 · C127·M2

Fields

Your goalRead a planar vector field as a rule that assigns an arrow to each point, calculate the gradient of a temperature at a point, check it using centered differences, then test whether another field can be a gradient.

Before you start
c058-m5 : change of variables · c057-m5 : Hessian · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c127-m1 : parametric curves

✿01 See the idea

The setting is a thin plate. All values are fictional: the temperature T(x, y), in °C, depends on the position (x, y), measured in cm. We will focus on point A with coordinates (2 ; 1) cm. A scalar function such as T gives a single number at each point: its temperature.

A vector field gives something else: an arrow at each point, with a direction, a length and a unit. Imagine a small arrow attached to each point of a grid. The field is the entire rule “point → arrow”, rather than an isolated arrow. Reading the arrow at A is an evaluation of the field.

There are two fields on the plate. The first is the gradient of T: at each point, it combines the rates of change of T with respect to x and y, and its unit is °C per cm. The second is a fictional flow R that rotates around the origin O, with a velocity in cm/min. Both draw an arrow at each point, but nothing requires the second to come from a temperature.

We therefore want to calculate an arrow using its formula, check it without simply taking the formula on trust, then recognize a field that cannot be a gradient. Integration along a curve, for which parametric curves have already laid the groundwork, belongs to the unit on circulation and is not covered here.

The rule to remember

A field assigns a vector to each point; the gradient of a temperature combines its two partial derivatives; equality of the mixed partial derivatives is necessary for a field to be a gradient.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

One plate, two fields of arrows

Fictional setting. The temperature of the plate is T(x, y) = a x² + b x y + c y², in °C, with a = 1 °C/cm², b = 3 °C/cm² and c = 2 °C/cm²; x and y are in cm. Point A is (2 ; 1) cm. The fictional flow is R(x, y) = (−y ; x), in cm/min, meaning a rotation with coefficient 1 min⁻¹. We calculate ∇T at A, check it, then test whether R can be a gradient.

  1. Define the field, symbols, units and assumptions

    A planar field F assigns to each point (x, y) of a domain a vector with components P(x, y) and Q(x, y). These components carry the unit of the field: °C/cm for the gradient of T, cm/min for R.

    F(x,y)=(P(x,y),Q(x,y))

    Here the domain is the entire plane. T is a polynomial: its partial derivatives exist, are continuous, and the order of differentiation can be interchanged. The gradient of T is the field whose components are its two partial derivatives, each calculated while holding the other variable fixed.

    ∇T=(∂T∂x,∂T∂y)

  2. Differentiate T with respect to each variable

    To differentiate with respect to x, treat y as a constant: a x² gives 2 a x, b x y gives b y, and c y² vanishes. To differentiate with respect to y, exchange their roles.

    ∂T∂x=2ax+by
    ∂T∂y=bx+2cy

    These formulas give an arrow for each point (x, y): this is exactly the rule that defines the field.

  3. Evaluate the field at point A

    Evaluating means substituting the coordinates of A for x and y, while keeping the same rule. Each product retains its unit: a coefficient in °C/cm² multiplied by a length in cm gives °C/cm.

    2×1×2+3×1=4+3=7 °C/cm
    3×2+2×2×1=6+4=10 °C/cm

    The gradient at A is therefore the vector (7 ; 10) °C/cm. To first order, moving one centimeter in the x direction from A changes T by approximately 7 °C. This vector is not a temperature: only the function T is a temperature.

  4. Check a component using centered differences

    A program does not always have the derivative formula. It can approximate the x component using two evaluations of T on opposite sides of A, at a distance h = 1 cm:

    ∂T∂x≈T(x+h;y)−T(x−h;y)2h

    With T = x (a x + b y) + c y², we find T(3 ; 1) = 20 °C and T(1 ; 1) = 6 °C. The quotient gives the same value as the formula, which checks the calculation in the previous step for this component.

    T(3;1)−T(1;1)2×1=20−62=142=7 °C/cm

    This equality is exact here because T has degree two; for a more general function, the centered difference is only an approximation whose error depends on h. The y component can be checked in the same way.

  5. Differentiate the field itself

    Now differentiate the components P = ∂T/∂x and Q = ∂T/∂y. The matrix of these four derivatives has entries 2, 3, 3, 4: it is symmetric, because the two mixed partial derivatives both equal b, or 3 °C/cm². This is the Hessian of T, studied in the Hessian unit.

    (∂P∂x∂P∂y∂Q∂x∂Q∂y)=(2abb2c)=(2334)

    If a field comes from a C² potential, this symmetry is required by Schwarz’s theorem. It is therefore a necessary condition for being a gradient.

  6. Test the rotational flow

    For R, P = −y and Q = x. The mixed partial derivatives do not agree: ∂P/∂y is −1 min⁻¹ and ∂Q/∂x is 1 min⁻¹.

    ∂P∂y=−1,∂Q∂x=1, donc ∂Q∂x−∂P∂y=1−(−1)=2

    If R were the gradient of a C² function, these two numbers would be equal. They are not: no C² potential exists for R over the entire plate. At A, R is (−1 ; 2) cm/min. Its dot product with the vector OA, with components (2 ; 1) cm, is:

    R(A)·OA=(−1)×2+2×1=−2+2=0 cm²/min

    The arrow is therefore perpendicular to the radius: it is tangent to the circle centered at O and passing through A. In complex numbers z = x + i y, this field is multiplication by i; in polar coordinates, its length is the distance to O and its direction follows the increasing angle.

  7. Check the units and the scope of the conclusions

    The three objects must be kept distinct: T in °C, its gradient in °C/cm, and the flow R in cm/min. Adding any two of them would make no sense.

    Equality of the mixed partial derivatives for ∇T only recovered the symmetry of the Hessian; it proves nothing about another field. The inequality for R is enough to rule out a C² potential. The centered difference checks one component at one point, rather than the entire field. Nothing says that these fictional values describe a real plate.

Explain it in your own words

Why can the two fields look like two drawings of arrows even though only one comes from a scalar function? Which equality indicates this, and exactly what does its failure prove?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A vector field is the arrow I read at a point.”

    The arrow at A is only one value of the field. The field is the rule that assigns an arrow to each point; changing the point changes the arrow, as between A and point B in the evaluation exercise.

    Review the rule from point to arrow

  • “The gradient of T at A is a temperature.”

    It is a vector of two partial derivatives, in °C/cm, rather than a number in °C. Repeat the calculation in the example, writing down the unit of each product.

    Repeat the calculation at A

  • “Every vector field is the gradient of a function.”

    For a C² potential, the mixed partial derivatives of the components are equal. The rotational flow R breaks this equality, so it cannot be a gradient. Equality alone is not a proof of existence: exhibit a potential, as in the final exercise.

    Repeat the mixed partial derivative test

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

Same gradient, different point

Using the same fictional temperature, calculate ∇T at point B with coordinates (1 ; 2) cm. Then compare this vector with the vector at point A.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Use the two partial derivative formulas again, then substitute the coordinates of B for x and y.

Hint 2

Keep the unit of each product: °C/cm.

Compare with the solution

The x component: 2 × 1 × 1 + 3 × 2 = 2 + 6 = 8 °C/cm. The y component: 3 × 1 + 2 × 2 × 2 = 3 + 8 = 11 °C/cm.

At B, the gradient is therefore (8 ; 11) °C/cm, whereas it is (7 ; 10) °C/cm at A. The same rule does give a different arrow depending on the point, which is the definition of a field.

Check your reasoning

  • Both partial derivatives are written before they are evaluated.
  • The result is a vector with two components, in °C/cm.
  • The comparison with A discusses different arrows, rather than temperatures.

Exercise 2 · Diagnostic

Can a flow be a gradient?

Another fictional flow has components P = 2 y and Q = 4 x, in cm/min, with the coefficients in min⁻¹. Can it be the gradient of a C² function over the entire plane? Justify your answer using the mixed partial derivative test and state what your test proves.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate ∂P/∂y and ∂Q/∂x: the coefficient of y in P and the coefficient of x in Q.

Hint 2

Think about what is needed for these two numbers to be equal.

Compare with the solution

Here ∂P/∂y = 2 min⁻¹ and ∂Q/∂x = 4 min⁻¹. The difference is 4 − 2 = 2 min⁻¹, which is nonzero. A gradient from a C² potential would have equal mixed partial derivatives: this flow is not one.

This test proves that it is ruled out. If the two coefficients had been equal, the test would have passed, but it would not have been enough on its own: a potential would have had to be exhibited, as in the next exercise.

Check your reasoning

  • The two mixed partial derivatives are calculated separately.
  • The conclusion rules out a potential and names the C² assumption.
  • The text does not conclude that passing the test would prove the existence of a potential.

Exercise 3 · Construction

Recover the temperature whose gradient is the field

A fictional field has components P = 4 x + 1 y and Q = 1 x + 6 y, in °C/cm. Show that it passes the mixed partial derivative test, then construct a function U of the form (coefficient) x² + 1 x y + (coefficient) y² whose gradient is this field. Check by differentiating U.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The x y term in U gives 1 y in ∂U/∂x and 1 x in ∂U/∂y.

Hint 2

Differentiating a term in x² multiplies its coefficient by 2: choose it to recover 4, then do the same for y².

Hint 3

A potential is defined up to an additive constant.

Compare with the solution

Mixed partial derivative test: ∂P/∂y = 1 and ∂Q/∂x = 1, which are equal. The test passes, but U still needs to be constructed.

The coefficients of x² and y² are half of 4 and 6: 4 ÷ 2 = 2 and 6 ÷ 2 = 3. We therefore set U = 2 x² + 1 x y + 3 y².

Check: ∂U/∂x = 2 × 2 x + 1 y = 4 x + 1 y, and ∂U/∂y = 1 x + 2 × 3 y = 1 x + 6 y. This recovers P and Q. Adding any constant to U also works.

Check your reasoning

  • The mixed partial derivative test is written before the construction.
  • The coefficients are obtained by reversing the differentiation of the squared terms.
  • The check differentiates U in both directions.
  • The additive constant is mentioned.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is a planar vector field?

A rule that assigns to each point (x, y) of a domain a vector (P, Q), with a unit for its components. The arrow at a point is only one value of the field.

Recall 2What does the gradient of a temperature T at a point contain?

Its two partial derivatives ∂T/∂x and ∂T/∂y at that point, in °C/cm if x and y are in cm. It is a vector, rather than a temperature.

Recall 3Which test rules out a field as the gradient of a C² potential?

The equality ∂P/∂y = ∂Q/∂x. For the flow R, the two numbers are −1 and 1: they differ, so R is not the gradient of a C² potential. Passing the test is not enough to prove the existence of a potential.

Recall 4How can you check a gradient component without a formula?

Use (T(x + h ; y) − T(x − h ; y)) / (2h) for the x component. Here h = 1 cm gives 7 °C/cm, exact for a polynomial of degree two and approximate in general.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the sequence: a planar field gives a vector at each point, with its units; the gradient of a temperature combines ∂T/∂x and ∂T/∂y; evaluate at a point while keeping the units; check a component using centered differences; test ∂P/∂y = ∂Q/∂x as a necessary condition, and conclude only that a potential is ruled out when it fails.
