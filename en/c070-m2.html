Level 2 · C070·M2

Traversals

Your goalCarry out a breadth-first traversal and a depth-first traversal, avoid revisiting rooms, and distinguish discovery order, paths, and distance.

Before you start
c025-m5 : O, Omega and Theta · c028-m5 : choice trees · c069-m5 : congruences and Euclid · c070-m1 : graphs

✿01 See the idea

You are planning a visit to the rooms of a small museum. Starting from room A, you want to know which rooms are reachable through the doors on the plan. A cycle lets you return to a room you have already encountered; G has no doors. The plan and all its numbers are fictional.

One way to plan the visit is to list the neighbors of A first, then the rooms that are still unknown beyond those neighbors. This is breadth-first traversal: it explores in layers. A queue holds the rooms to examine; the first room added is the first removed, just as in a line of people waiting.

Another way is to enter a new room and continue from it before returning to the other choices. When no unknown neighbor remains, you return to the previous choice. This is depth-first traversal. A stack records the rooms currently being explored: the last room entered is the first left.

In both cases, you mark a room as soon as it is discovered. A marked room may still be waiting to be examined. This distinction prevents the same room from being added to the waiting list several times and prevents cycles from restarting the visit indefinitely. These traversals organize the examination of the plan; their list of discoveries is not necessarily a walking route.

The rule to remember

Mark rooms as soon as they are discovered; choose a queue to advance in layers, or a stack to explore further and then return; state the starting point and the scope of the traversal.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Plan the visit from A

The plan stays fixed throughout the calculation. Each door can be used in both directions, with no special cost. The neighbor lists are already in alphabetical order: A: B, C; B: A, D, E; C: A, E; D: B, F; E: B, C, F; F: D, E; G: none. Exploration continues until no rooms are left waiting.

  1. Define what is being counted

    V is the set of rooms and L the set of doors, with each door an unordered pair of rooms. A neighbor of u is a room directly connected to room u. The letters u and v denote arbitrary rooms, not numerical values. The graph is finite, with no self-loops on a room and no multiple doors between the same pair.

    d(v) will be the distance found by breadth-first traversal from A: the minimum number of doors to cross to reach v. It is measured in doors, with no conversion to meters or seconds. Initially, d(A) = 0. An undefined distance means “not yet discovered”; it is not a distance of 0. p(v) will denote the room from which v was discovered, its predecessor. A has no predecessor.

  2. Write the breadth-first rule before calculating

    Q denotes the queue of rooms that have been discovered but not yet examined; its head is written on the left. M denotes the set of rooms already marked. Q and M contain names, with no physical unit. Initialization: Q = [A] and M = {A}.

    While Q is not empty, remove its head u. Examine its neighbors in the stated order. For each unmarked neighbor v: mark v immediately, record p(v) = u, calculate d(v), then add v to the right of Q. A marked neighbor causes no addition, even if it is still waiting for its turn.

    d(v)=d(u)+1

    The addition represents the door u–v extending the known path to u. The rule applies only when v is first discovered.

  3. Examine A, then B

    Removing A leaves the queue empty. B and C are new: mark them and add them in that order. Q = [B, C]; M = {A, B, C}. Their distances are d(B) = 0 + 1 = 1 door and d(C) = 0 + 1 = 1 door; their predecessors are A.

    Removing B leaves [C]. A is already marked; D and E are new. This gives Q = [C, D, E] and M = {A, B, C, D, E}. d(D) = 1 + 1 = 2 doors and d(E) = 1 + 1 = 2 doors. Record p(D) = B and p(E) = B.

  4. Examine C, then D

    Removing C leaves [D, E]. Its neighbors A and E are already marked. E has not yet been examined, but B has already reserved it: do not add it again, and its predecessor remains B. Q = [D, E].

    Removing D leaves [E]. B is marked; F is new. Mark F, record p(F) = D and d(F) = 2 + 1 = 3 doors, then add it. Q = [E, F]; M = {A, B, C, D, E, F}.

  5. Empty the queue and check the result

    Removing E leaves [F]: B, C, and F are all marked. Removing F leaves []: D and E are marked. The traversal stops. The removal order, which here is the same as the discovery order, is A, B, C, D, E, F.

    The layers contain A, then B and C, then D and E, then F: 1 + 2 + 2 + 1 = 6 reachable rooms, spread across 4 layers. Each of the 5 rooms other than A has a predecessor.

    Check by reconstruction: tracing back through p(E) = B and then p(B) = A gives the path A–B–E, of length 2 doors. For F: A–B–D–F, of length 3 doors. Each consecutive pair is indeed a door on the plan. G remains unmarked: the empty queue proves that all rooms reachable from A have been examined, not that all 7 museum rooms are connected to A.

  6. Justify the minimum distances

    The queue examines all rooms in a layer before those in the next. New neighbors therefore receive a distance 1 door greater than that of the room being examined. If a room had a shorter path, the next-to-last room on that path would have been examined in an earlier layer and would already have discovered it: a contradiction.

    Thus A–B–E is shortest in number of doors. A–C–E is another path of the same length: the chosen predecessor depends on how ties between neighbors are broken. Changing this order can change the predecessors without changing the distances. This guarantee assumes edges of equal cost: if the doors have different traversal times, the number of doors is no longer enough to minimize time.

  7. Start over and explore a branch in depth

    For depth-first traversal, erase the marks from the previous traversal. Enter A, mark it, then immediately enter its first unmarked neighbor. Each entry suspends the examination of the previous room. The stack of active rooms also keeps the position at which to resume each neighbor list; a recursive formulation implements the same waiting process.

    The stack grows as follows: [A], [A, B], [A, B, D], [A, B, D, F], [A, B, D, F, E], then [A, B, D, F, E, C]. At each step, ignore neighbors already marked: from B choose D before E; from F choose E because D is marked; from E choose C because B is marked.

    C has no unknown neighbor left: remove it from the stack and resume E. E, F, D, B, then A finish in turn, since their remaining choices are already marked. The order of first entries is A, B, D, F, E, C; G remains unknown. This procedure terminates because each room is entered only once and each neighbor list is finite.

    The branch on which E is first discovered is A–B–D–F–E: 3 + 1 = 4 doors, which is 2 more than the breadth-first path. C is discovered at depth 5, even though it is a neighbor of A. Depth-first traversal therefore does not guarantee a shortest path. Removals from the stack are returns during exploration; they are not new discoveries.

  8. Bound the work and state its scope

    With neighbor lists, each reachable room is removed from the queue only once. Each door in the reachable network appears in the lists of its 2 endpoints: 2 × 7 = 14 neighbor examinations, even though most do not lead to a discovery. This gives 6 removals + 14 examinations = 20 events of these two types; this count is not an execution time.

    If n is the number of reachable rooms and m the number of doors between them, the work is O(n + m), assuming constant cost for queue operations and checking marks, and lists already in order. The marks, distances, and predecessors use O(n) memory; so does the queue. Depth-first traversal has the same order of cost and can keep O(n) active rooms. This accounting excludes the initial storage of the plan and any cost of sorting the neighbors.

    To traverse the whole museum, restart the procedure from an unmarked room after exhausting the rooms reachable from each starting point. Here, simply restart from G: it is discovered and then examined, with no neighbors. This creates no path from A to G. In a directed graph, you must also state the direction of the edges followed: reaching a room does not authorize using a door in the reverse direction.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the trace. Explain why E is not added when C is examined, why breadth-first traversal finds a minimum distance, and why depth-first traversal can reach E by a detour. Then reconstruct the queue states.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I will mark the room when I remove it from the queue.”

    Without another safeguard, B and then C could both add E before it is first removed. Mark E as soon as B adds it. A room that has been discovered but not yet examined is already reserved; cycles must not put it back in the waiting list.

    Review the breadth-first rule and the step through C

  • “A, B, C, D, E, F is the path followed by breadth-first traversal.”

    This is an examination order. B and C are not directly connected on the plan, so this sequence does not describe a walk from door to door. To produce a path to a target, trace back through its predecessors and reverse that sequence.

    Check the doors on the plan

  • “The first path found by depth-first traversal must be the shortest.”

    Alphabetical order first leads from A to E through B, D, and F. The path A–B–E crosses fewer doors. The stack ensures a return to waiting choices; it does not order discoveries by minimum distance.

    Compare the depth-first branch with the breadth-first layers

B is removed; A is marked, and D and E are new. Q becomes [C, D, E]. Since B is 1 door from A, d(E) = 1 + 1 = 2 doors and p(E) = B.

Hint 1

After entering B, explore D completely before resuming the list for B.

Hint 2

From F, D is already marked; from E, B is already marked. Marks remain when you return.

Compare with the solution

The entries are A, B, D, F, E, C. The discovery branch for E is A–B–D–F–E, of length 4 doors. C finishes first; return to E, which finishes in turn. Then pop F, D, B, and A from the stack after exhausting their neighbors. These returns add no new rooms to the discovery order.

Breadth-first traversal gave 2 doors to E. The depth-first detour amounts to 4 − 2 = 2 additional doors. Both traversals discover the same 6 reachable rooms, but organize the work differently. G remains unreachable from A.

  • Keep the marks after returning and count only first entries.
  • Produce a valid path to E and explain why its length is not minimal.

Exercise 3 · Transfer to a change on the same plan

A door closes; the scope still needs checking

The door B–E closes in both directions; all other doors in the same museum remain open. Restart from A with no marks. Using breadth-first traversal in the same alphabetical order, give the queues after B and then C, a shortest path to E, the reachable rooms, and the number of neighbor examinations. Does an empty queue now allow you to declare G unreachable from A? State the assumptions behind your answer.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Remove E from the list for B and B from the list for E, then replay the discoveries.

Hint 2

C can still discover E. Count each open door in the lists of both its endpoints.

Hint 3

Distinguish an exhaustive traversal of the fixed plan from a traversal cut short by a bound or an early stop.

Compare with the solution

After A, the queue is [B, C]. B discovers only D: [C, D]. C discovers E: [D, E]. D discovers F, then E and F add nothing. The examination order remains A, B, C, D, E, F, but p(E) becomes C. A–C–E uses 2 doors; there is no direct door A–E, so this path is shortest.

The 6 rooms A through F remain reachable. There are 7 − 1 = 6 open doors left, giving 2 × 6 = 12 neighbor examinations for this complete traversal.

G still has no doors. With this known, fixed plan examined until the queue is empty, you can conclude that no path from A to G exists. With interrupted exploration, omitted doors, or a changing plan, an undiscovered room would not be enough to support that conclusion. Restarting from G would allow you to examine it without connecting it to A.

Check your reasoning

  • Update both neighbor lists for the closed door.
  • Change the predecessor of E while keeping its minimum distance.
  • Count examinations using the open doors and state the scope of the conclusion about G.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1When do you mark a room, and why can it be marked without having been examined?

As soon as it is discovered, before putting it in the waiting list. The mark reserves the room; its neighbors will be examined later. This avoids multiple additions and repetitions caused by cycles.

Recall 2What property of the queue gives minimum distances in breadth-first traversal, and in what unit?

The first added is the first removed: all rooms in a layer are examined before the next layer. With doors of equal cost, the first discovery gives the minimum number of doors from the starting point, not a minimum duration if their costs differ.

Recall 3What does the depth-first traversal stack keep, and what do you do when a room has no unknown neighbor left?

It keeps the active rooms and the choices to resume. Leave the last room entered and resume the previous one, keeping the marks. The first branch found is not necessarily a shortest path.

Recall 4Why is the order A, B, C, D, E, F not enough to provide a path to F?

It describes the examination order and can place rooms with no shared door next to each other, such as B and C. Tracing back through the predecessors of F and then reversing the sequence gives a path in which each consecutive pair is a door.

Recall 5What does a queue becoming empty prove, and what must you check before concluding that a room is unreachable?

An exhaustive traversal has examined all rooms reachable from its starting point. The conclusion requires a complete, fixed plan, examination of all allowed doors, and no early stop. Another starting point may be needed to traverse the whole graph.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without support: fix the starting point and the direction of the doors; distinguish discovering from examining; give the queue rule and then the stack rule; explain the role of marks; reconstruct a path using predecessors; finish by stating which rooms the traversal covers and what it can minimize.
