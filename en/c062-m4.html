Level 2 · C062·M4

Threshold and cost of errors

Your goalChoose a threshold by comparing the average cost of false alerts and missed failures, then state when this choice is no longer justified.

Before you start
c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c056-m5 : effect size · c009-m5 : reading notes · c062-m1 : measurement, score and probability · c062-m2 : missing value and error · c062-m3 : Brier and reliability by group

✿01 See the idea

A pump supplies the workshop throughout the day. In the morning, you can raise an alert to arrange an inspection, or let it run without an alert. An unnecessary alert disrupts the workshop; an unforeseen failure brings it to a stop. The errors therefore do not necessarily carry the same weight.

Here, a false positive is an alert when the pump would have continued to work properly. A false negative is no alert when a failure occurs. “Positive” refers to the alert, rather than to a desirable event. The threshold is the boundary at which we decide to raise an alert.

The data are entirely fictional. We assign 20 loss points to a false alert and 60 points to a missed failure. The point, abbreviated pt, is a common unit chosen for the exercise. A correct decision receives 0 loss points by convention. This table simplifies the consequences; it does not quantify all maintenance costs.

We assume that a relevant probability of failure is available for this pump and this day. It is distinct from a raw score and from your personal confidence. With the probability unchanged, changing the cost of errors can change the decision without improving or worsening the forecast.

The rule to remember

Compare the average losses of the possible actions. With 0 losses for correct decisions, raise an alert when the probability reaches the cost of a false positive divided by the sum of the error costs.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The pump: raising an alert even though failure is less likely than normal operation

The probability of failure during the day is p = 3/10 = 0.30. The error losses are Cₚ = 20 pt for a false alert and Cₙ = 60 pt for a missed failure. Let us compare the actions before knowing the actual state.

  1. Define exactly what enters the calculation

    p denotes the conditional probability of failure given the information available in the morning; it is unitless and lies between 0 and 1. The complementary probability 1 − p concerns normal operation over the same day. Cp and Cn are known positive losses, in points, for the errors defined by the table. The subscripts p and n stand for positive and negative.

    Ra will denote the average loss if we choose an alert, and Rs the average loss if we choose no alert, in the same unit. Here, the word risk denotes this average under the model, rather than the probability of an event. We compare only these actions, over this duration, with no limit on the number of alerts. The losses for correct decisions are set to 0.

    The relevance and calibration of p are working assumptions: frequencies must be compatible with the stated probabilities in the situations concerned. This page does not estimate p or demonstrate its calibration. If the input is an arbitrary score or information is missing, the formula cannot provide that guarantee.

  2. Weight each loss by the state that triggers it

    If we raise an alert, the only loss in the table occurs when the pump keeps working properly. It is therefore weighted by 1 − p. If we raise no alert, the only loss occurs on failure, which has probability p. The expected value is this sum of losses weighted by their probabilities.

    Ra=(1−p)Cp;Rs=pCn

    Alert: (1 − 0.30) × 20 = 0.70 × 20 = 14 pt. No alert: 0.30 × 60 = 18 pt. The average loss for the alert is lower by 4 pt. We therefore choose the alert. This does not predict that the pump will fail; it selects an action in view of the possible losses.

  3. Isolate the decision boundary

    By convention, we also raise an alert at equality. Start from Ra ≤ Rs. Expanding, then adding pCp to each side, gives Cp ≤ p(Cp + Cn). Since the sum of the costs is strictly positive, dividing by it preserves the direction of the inequality.

    (1−p)Cp≤pCn⇔p≥CpCp+Cn=τ

    τ, the letter tau, denotes the unitless threshold: the points cancel in the quotient. Numerically, Cp + Cn = 20 + 60 = 80 pt and τ = 20/80 = 1/4 = 0.25. Since 0.30 is greater than 0.25, the rule gives the alert again.

    This quotient assumes precisely our loss table. An inspection cost even for useful alerts, a cost for no alert even without a failure, or limited capacity would require comparing the new losses. You must return to the table before reusing a threshold.

  4. Check using a finite list of fictional cases

    Solely to check the arithmetic, let us construct 100 fictional cases compatible with p: 30 failures and 70 cases of normal operation. If we raise an alert in all these cases, the 70 working cases cost 70 × 20 = 1400 pt, and the failures cost nothing in this table. Dividing by 100 gives 14 pt per case.

    If we raise no alert in any case, the 30 failures cost 30 × 60 = 1800 pt; the working cases cost nothing. The average is 1800/100 = 18 pt per case. We recover the risks calculated using probabilities. These constructed cases are not observations and do not validate p.

  5. Test equality and the endpoints

    At p = τ = 0.25, the average loss for an alert is (1 − 0.25) × 20 = 0.75 × 20 = 15 pt. For no alert, it is 0.25 × 60 = 15 pt. The actions are equivalent under this criterion; our convention chooses the alert.

    At p = 0, a false alert costs Cp while no alert costs nothing. At p = 1, no alert costs Cn while an alert costs nothing. The direction of the rule is therefore consistent. If the errors had the same cost, the boundary would be the midpoint of the scale; errors with unequal costs do not require this boundary.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the formulas, explain why a failure that is less likely than normal operation can justify an alert. Work out the complementary probability to use for each action, then reconstruct the threshold.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Failure must be more likely than normal operation to raise an alert.”

    This rule overlooks the consequences. In the example, compare 14 pt and 18 pt: a missed failure carries more weight. Start again from the average losses to recover the threshold.

    Return to the comparison of losses

  • “The cost of a false positive is multiplied by the probability of failure.”

    A false positive occurs when we raise an alert and the pump is working properly. Its weight is therefore 1 − p. Draw the cell that triggers the loss before multiplying.

    Reread the table of actions and states

  • “Changing the threshold makes the probability more reliable, and a missing value equals 0.”

    The threshold selects an action; it does not correct the probabilities. A missing input does not mean that failure is impossible. Compare the assumptions in the transfer exercise, then ask for a justified input or an explicit rule for missing inputs.

    Distinguish forecast, costs and missing input

Compare with the solution

The complement is 1 − 0.10 = 0.90. An alert therefore has an average loss of 0.90 × 20 = 18 pt. No alert has an average loss of 0.10 × 60 = 6 pt.

We choose no alert because 6 pt is less than 18 pt. Checking with the threshold gives the same decision: 0.10 < 0.25. Failure remains possible; the criterion minimizes an average and does not guarantee the individual outcome.

The new sum is 20 + 20 = 40 pt. The threshold becomes 20/40 = 0.50. The probability 0.30 is below it: we now choose no alert.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

The probability no longer arrives

Return to the original costs. In the morning, the field containing p is missing. A colleague suggests replacing it with 0 and raising no alert. Instead, examine the assumptions p = 0.10 and p = 0.30, without claiming that they are the bounds of a guaranteed interval. Is there an action that minimizes the average loss under both assumptions? What is missing to make a choice?

Compare with the solution

With p = 0.10, no alert costs an average of 6 pt, compared with 18 pt for an alert. With p = 0.30, it is the alert that costs 14 pt, compared with 18 pt for no alert. No action minimizes the average loss under both assumptions.

These assumptions provide a counterexample to an automatic conclusion; they do not measure the actual uncertainty. Replacing the missing value with 0 invents the claim that failure is impossible. What is missing is a justified, relevant probability, or an explicit policy for decisions with missing information.

Flag the missing input and request the prescribed procedure: for example, obtain a new estimate or ask for human review. The cost of this review has not been given: we cannot demonstrate that it is optimal using our table. Declaring an alert preferable in every case would also be a conclusion without sufficient assumptions.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which state triggers the cost of a false alert, and which probability should be associated with it?

We raise an alert when the pump keeps working properly. The working state has probability 1 − p; the average loss for an alert is therefore (1 − p)Cₚ in our table.

Recall 2How can you reconstruct the threshold from the average losses?

Write (1 − p)Cₚ ≤ pCₙ, collect the terms in p and divide by the positive sum of the costs. We raise an alert if p ≥ Cₚ/(Cₚ + Cₙ), with our convention for equality.

Recall 3If the cost of a missed failure increases, what happens to the threshold when the cost of a false alert is fixed?

The denominator Cₚ + Cₙ increases, so the threshold decreases. We are willing to raise an alert at a lower probability. This change concerns the consequences and proves nothing new about calibration.

Recall 4What does it mean to be exactly at the threshold?

The average losses of the actions are equal. Here we raise an alert by convention; choosing no alert at equality would give the same average loss according to the table.

Recall 5Why should you not apply the threshold to a probability replaced by 0 when the field is missing?

A missing value does not say that failure is impossible. A justified input or an explicit policy for this missing input is needed; the threshold does not supply the missing information.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Hide the page. Redraw the table of actions and states, define p, Cₚ and Cₙ with their units, then recover the risks and the threshold.
