Level 2 · C093·M5

Autocorrelation, quadratic cost and false positives

Your goalExplain how persistent noise can imitate a change point, count pairwise slopes and define an appropriate check of false alarms.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A probe in a small chamber appears to show a clear pattern: low readings followed by high readings. Yet in our fictional experiment, the true temperature stays at 20 °C. The measurement error stays on the same side before switching to the other. A clear separation in the diagram can therefore suggest the wrong mechanism.

Autocorrelation here describes the link between an error and the error in the previous reading. If the error tends to keep its sign, readings close together in time partly repeat the same information. A median resists an isolated spike; it can still follow an entire group of shifted readings.

Two questions then accompany the candidate change point: what would the rule do on a series with no change but with this persistent noise? And how many comparisons must we calculate if we lengthen the window? We use the same probe to answer both.

All temperatures, noise assumptions and threshold values are fictional. The short series is constructed by hand to expose a pitfall; it is neither an experimental sample nor a measurement of the frequency of false alarms.

The rule to remember

A visible separation calls for a reference with no change point that preserves temporal dependence. Counting more slopes increases the work without creating as many independent pieces of information.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The probe gives a false impression of a step

At intervals Δ = 1 min, we have n = 8 readings: 18, 18, 19, 19, 21, 21, 22, 22 °C. The ground truth for the exercise is a constant temperature μ = 20 °C. The split after c = 4 readings and the threshold h = 7 °C are fixed before calculation. Our teaching rule will signal a change point if the reduction in the total absolute residual exceeds h. It does not reproduce the production detector.

  1. Separate temperature from probe error

    t is the reading index, from 1 to n, with no unit; the elapsed time between consecutive indices is Δ. xt is the reading in °C, μ is the constant true temperature and εt is the measurement error in °C. The assumption of no change point requires the same μ throughout the window. All readings are present; no gap is replaced by a 0.

    xt=μ+εt

    The chosen errors are −2, −2, −1, −1, 1, 1, 2, 2 °C. Their observed mean is 0 by symmetry, but their order creates two groups. This observation does not allow us to estimate a temporal model properly from such a short series.

  2. Define dependence on the previous reading

    To reason about possible noise from this probe, let us separately posit a model with 0 mean and second-order stationarity: the theoretical mean of the ε errors is 0 °C, their variance V is constant, and their covariance C between adjacent readings does not depend on t. The variance is the theoretical mean of the squared error; C is the theoretical mean of the product of two adjacent errors. V and C are in °C². We assume V is strictly positive.

    ρ=CV;C=ρV

    ρ, with no unit, is the autocorrelation at a lag of one reading. Here we stipulate ρ = 3/4 = 0.75 and V = 4 °C²; therefore C = 3/4 × 4 = 3 °C². These are hypothetical noise parameters, not statistics inferred from the list. Positive covariance means that the products of adjacent errors have a positive mean. With persistent noise, this can appear as groups on the same side; no individual sign is required.

    Stationarity allows this dependence: here it requires stable moments over time, not independence. 0 adjacent correlation would not prove independence of the entire series either.

  3. Understand why differences can give false reassurance

    Let dt be the difference εt − (ε at the previous reading), in °C. This is also the difference between readings under our constant-temperature assumption. Expanding its square and then taking the theoretical mean gives V for each squared term and C for the cross product. Var denotes the variance of the term in parentheses.

    Var(dt)=2V−2C=2V(1−ρ)

    Numerically: 2 × 4 − 2 × 3 = 8 − 6 = 2 °C². If we divided this variance by 2 while wrongly assuming independent errors, we would assign the noise a naive V = 2/2 = 1 °C², instead of 4 °C². The variance would be underestimated by a factor of 4.

    Check: with the same V and 0 covariance, the variance of the differences would be 8 °C². Persistence reduces changes between neighbours without reducing the deviation from the true temperature as much. This calculation concerns theoretical variances. It is neither the MAD calculation for our short list nor a universal formula for adjusting an empirical MAD.

  4. Calculate the robust fits

    For an even number of values, we choose the mean of the two central values as the median. The list is already sorted. Its overall median m is (19 + 21)/2 = 40/2 = 20 °C. To the left of the split, m left = (18 + 19)/2 = 18.5 °C; to the right, m right = (21 + 22)/2 = 21.5 °C. Their difference is 3 °C.

    The median minimizes the sum of absolute distances from the readings in its group. Define the whole-series residual J as this sum around m. For the split-series residual J, the fitted value at is m left if t does not exceed c, and m right otherwise. The vertical bars take the absolute value; Σ adds up all the indicated readings. These sums of distances are in °C, with no division by n.

    Jtout=∑t=1n|xt−m|
    Jséparé=∑t=1n|xt−at|

    For the whole-series residual J, the distances are 2, 2, 1, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2 °C. Their sum is 4 × (2 + 1) = 12 °C. For the split-series residual J, each reading is 0.5 °C from its group median: the split-series residual J = 8 × 0.5 = 4 °C. Check: each half contributes equally to the total, and splitting does not increase the minimum residual.

  5. Identify the false alarm without inventing its frequency

    G is the reduction in the residual, in °C: the whole-series residual J minus the split-series residual J. The threshold h uses the same unit. The fictional rule, fixed in advance, triggers if G strictly exceeds h.

    G=Jtout−Jséparé=12−4=8 °C>7 °C

    The rule triggers. Since the stipulated ground truth is a constant temperature, this change-point signal is a false positive: an alarm in the absence of the change being sought. A reduction in the residual only shows that two levels fit these readings better. It does not prove that the true temperature changed.

    The threshold is chosen for the exercise, without calibration. This series provides a counterexample to an absolute guarantee, not a false-positive rate. Searching for the best split among several possibilities, then repeating over overlapping windows, multiplies the opportunities to trigger and changes the rule to evaluate. We must therefore evaluate this entire search, not just a split announced in advance.

  6. Count the work of pairwise slopes

    For the trend of this same probe, Theil–Sen can form all slopes (xⱼ − xᵢ)/((j − i)Δ), where i and j are distinct indices and i is less than j. Each slope is in °C/min. P(n), with no unit, counts pairs; it does not count independent pieces of evidence.

    P(n)=n(n−1)2

    Why divide? Each time has n − 1 partners, but counting both directions this way counts each pair twice. With n = 8, this gives 8 × 7/2 = 56/2 = 28 slopes. Another count: the first reading has 7 later partners, and the last has none; summing the numbers of partners gives 28 again.

    A doubled window would have 16 readings and 16 × 15/2 = 120 slopes. The ratio is 120/28 = 30/7 ≈ 4.29. As n increases, this count grows as n squared. A list of all slopes also occupies space proportional to this number. Sorting to obtain their median adds work; this count does not establish the total runtime of the detector.

    P(n)∈Θ(n2)

    The symbol Θ here indicates growth bounded by constant multiples of n squared as n becomes large. It applies to the number of slopes enumerated, not the entire program.

  7. Choose what would support a stronger conclusion

    To evaluate false alarms from the probe, we need series known to have no change point but with the dependencies and collection conditions we want to test. We can prepare synthetic series with the same constant temperature, first with independent noise, then with persistent noise, with comparable marginal variance. These test the chosen model; they do not validate performance under all real noise.

    We freeze the rule, thresholds, window length, allowed splits and observation horizon before evaluation. We separate the series used for tuning from those held out for checking. We distinguish the number of windows that triggered from the number of series with at least one alarm. Overlapping windows are not independent repetitions. Randomly shuffling the reading order within each series would destroy the very persistence we need to test.

    Finally, we also check series with a known change: suppressing all alarms would not make a good detector. Results should state their sample sizes, uncertainty and the noise regime tested. None of that is measured here. The exact calculation justifies our counterexample and our count; error frequency and runtime on a machine still require measurement in separate work.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The median is robust, so a change point it sees is real.”

    Robustness to an isolated reading does not remove temporal dependence. An entire group can be shifted by noise. In our scene, G = 8 °C triggers despite the constant true temperature. Check the rule on series with no change point that preserve persistence.

    Recalculate the alarm and recover its ground truth

  • “A small difference between neighbours proves that the probe is precise.”

    Positive covariance subtracts a term from the variance of the differences. Dividing it by 2 as under independence gives 1 °C² here instead of 4 °C². Start again from the expansion of the square; do not turn this theoretical calculation into an automatic MAD adjustment.

    Review the variance of the differences

  • “Doubling the window doubles the time and doubles the evidence.”

    The number of slopes goes from 28 to 120, with a ratio close to 4.29. This counts candidate operations; runtime also includes their processing. The slopes share readings, and those readings may be correlated: the slope count does not measure an independent sample size.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · defend a protocol

The check removed the pitfall it was meant to test

Someone wants to validate the probe alarms. They randomly shuffle the order of each series, tune the threshold on all these series, then count overlapping windows as independent trials. They claim that our counterexample proves a high frequency of false alarms. Identify the flaws and propose a sound check without inventing a rate.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

What property disappears when the temporal order is shuffled?

Hint 2

Separate tuning from evaluation. Also define how alarms are counted over an entire series.

Compare with the solution

Shuffling the order destroys the temporal structure to be tested. Tuning and evaluating on the same series makes the check dependent on tuning. Counting overlapping windows as independent repetitions ignores their shared readings. Our constructed series proves the possibility of a false alarm, not its frequency in a population of series.

For the same probe, prepare series with no change point, preserving order and specifying temporal noise, then series with a known change. Tuning uses a separate set; the complete rule is frozen before checking on held-out series. Fix the length and horizon, and report alarms per window and the presence of an alarm per series separately, with sample sizes and uncertainty appropriate to the dependence. Synthetic validation would remain limited to the noise model tested.

Check your reasoning

  • You identify destruction of order, reuse of series and dependence between windows.
  • You propose a reference with no change point that preserves persistent noise.
  • You distinguish a counterexample, a measured frequency and real-world validity.
  • You also check that the rule can detect a known change.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does ρ measure at a lag of one reading, and why does stationarity not force it to be 0?

ρ = C/V compares the covariance of two adjacent errors with their common positive variance. It has no unit. Stationarity here keeps the moments stable over time; it allows dependence between neighbours.

Recall 2Recover the variance of the differences and the pitfall in the probe model.

For errors with 0 mean, variance V and adjacent covariance C, the variance of the differences is 2V − 2C. With V = 4 °C² and C = 3 °C², it is 2 °C². Dividing it by 2 would give 1 °C² instead of 4 °C².

Recall 3Why is our rule triggering a false positive, and what does it fail to measure?

G = 12 − 4 = 8 °C exceeds h = 7 °C, while the true temperature is stipulated to be constant. This is a constructed false alarm that provides no false-positive frequency.

Recall 4Why do n readings give n(n − 1)/2 slopes without giving as many independent pieces of evidence?

Each reading has n − 1 partners, but both directions count the same pair. Dividing by 2 gives the exact count. Pairs share their readings; enumeration and exhaustive storage grow as n squared, without automatically measuring runtime or information.

Recall 5What must a reference with no change point preserve to test false alarms from the probe?

The temporal order and dependence regime to be tested, with a defined length and horizon. Series held out for checking are not used for tuning. Validation under independent noise alone does not cover persistent noise; overlapping windows are not independent trials.
