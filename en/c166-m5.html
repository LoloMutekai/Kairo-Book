Level 3 · C166·M5

Spectral truncation

Your goalChoose a frequency cutoff, reconstruct the retained signal and justify its mean square error without confusing smoothing with denoising.

Before you start
c165-m5 : duality · c046-m5 : approximation error · c027-m5 : complex numbers · c166-m1 : Fourier series · c166-m2 : transform · c166-m3 : convolution · c166-m4 : Parseval

✿01 See the idea

A small cart moves in a repeating pattern. Its position includes a slow oscillation and faster ripples. To retain only the slow shape of the motion, we decide to remove frequencies above a cutoff. We do not shorten the observation time or clip the peaks of the curve: we remove entire components.

The signal in this scene is fictional, known exactly and periodic with period 8 s. Its mean position is 4 mm. Cosines with amplitudes 3 mm, 2 mm and 1 mm are added to this mean. Their frequencies are 1/8 Hz, 1/4 Hz and 3/8 Hz, respectively. One hertz means one cycle per second.

Here, spectral truncation retains the mean and the modes whose order is no greater than N. A mode is an oscillation at a given frequency; its order is an integer with no unit. With N = 1, we retain only the first oscillation and the mean. The retained coefficients keep exactly the same values.

The plot becomes smoother, but the removed ripples are part of the defined motion. Nothing has identified them as noise. The question we can calculate is therefore: what difference does this simplification introduce relative to the original signal?

The rule to remember

Fix the allowed frequencies, retain their coefficients and measure what was rejected using a stated norm. Controlling a mean error does not provide a guarantee at every instant.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Simplify the motion within an error budget

The exact coefficients of the motion are given, with no measurement to estimate. First construct the truncation of order N = 1. Then find the smallest nonnegative integer order N for which the mean square error is at most 1 mm². This budget is a fictional choice for the exercise, not a validated mechanical tolerance.

  1. Define the signal and the reconstruction framework

    t is time in seconds and T = 8 s is the period. x(t) is the displacement in millimetres relative to a fixed origin. Set ω = 2π/T, the angular frequency in radians per second; π is the constant giving a half turn in radians. The angle ωt is expressed in radians. m = 4 mm is the mean; ak is the coefficient of the cosine of order k, in millimetres. The index k has no unit.

    x(t)=m+a1cos(ωt)+a2cos(2ωt)+a3cos(3ωt)

    Here a1 = 3 mm, a2 = 2 mm and a3 = 1 mm. The other coefficients are 0. The choice of time origin makes all sine coefficients 0 in this example. We work over a full period and its periodic extension, without an observation window or sampling error.

    The general rule applies to a real periodic signal that is square integrable: the integral of its square over a period must be finite. Our finite sum of cosines satisfies this assumption. We use Fourier series and Parseval from the previous units; we do not recalculate a discrete transform.

  2. Write exactly what the cutoff retains

    In the real convention, bk denotes the sine coefficient, in millimetres, and m is directly the signal mean, with no additional factor. Coefficients can be negative in general; in our example, the cosine coefficients are positive and are also their amplitudes. S_N(t) denotes the truncated reconstruction. The sum starts at order 1 and ends at N; for N = 0, it is empty and S_N equals m.

    SN(t)=m+∑k=1N[akcos(kωt)+bksin(kωt)]

    The term of order k has frequency fk = k/T, in hertz. With N = 1, the cutoff includes f1 = 1/8 = 1/8 Hz, or 0.125 Hz. Orders 2 and 3 are rejected because their frequencies exceed this value.

    S1(t)=4+3cos(ωt) mm

    At t = 0 s, each cosine equals 1. The original signal equals 4 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 10 mm; the truncation equals 4 + 3 = 7 mm. This check prevents confusion between the cutoff and a reduction of all amplitudes.

  3. Form the residual and choose the error measure

    The residual r_N(t) = x(t) − S_N(t), in millimetres, contains exactly the rejected modes. The error E_N is the mean of its square over a period, in square millimetres. The inverse factor of T turns a time integral into a mean; we do not divide by the number of modes.

    r1(t)=2cos(2ωt)+1cos(3ωt) mm
    EN=1T∫0T|rN(t)|2dt

    The integral alone would have unit mm²·s. E_N has unit mm². The notation ∥u∥_T² will denote this same mean square for any signal u: the norm ∥u∥_T is its square root. The symbol d in dt indicates integration with respect to time.

  4. Calculate the error from the rejected modes

    Over a full period, the mean square of a cosine of nonzero order is 1/2; the mean of the product of two distinct modes is 0. This is their orthogonality. Expanding r1² gives the squares of the two cosines and a cross term whose mean is 0.

    E1=22+122=4+12=5/2 mm²

    We therefore obtain 2.50 mm². We add the squares of the rejected amplitudes, then apply the factor 1/2 specific to this real convention. Adding the amplitudes first and then squaring would incorrectly retain a cross term.

    In the following general formula, ∑ means the sum over all orders k strictly greater than N; ∞ indicates that the series may have infinitely many modes. Parseval gives this identity under the assumption of square integrability.

    EN=12∑k=N+1∞(ak2+bk2)

  5. Check conservation of mean energy

    Here, “mean energy” means the mean square of the displacement, in mm²; it is not mechanical energy in joules. The mean m contributes m², whereas each cosine contributes half the square of its amplitude.

    Original signal: 16 + (9 + 4 + 1)/2 = 23 mm². Retained part: 16 + 9/2 = 41/2 mm², or 20.50 mm². Rejected part: 5/2 mm².

    Check: 41/2 + 5/2 = 23 mm². This decomposition works because the residual is orthogonal to the retained part. In particular, it checks that the mean has not been forgotten and that no mode has been counted on both sides.

  6. Choose the first order that meets the budget

    With N = 0, only the mean remains and E0 = (9 + 4 + 1)/2 = 7 mm². With N = 1, E1 = 5/2 mm² still exceeds the budget of 1 mm².

    With N = 2, we restore the cosine with amplitude 2 mm. Only order 3 remains in the residual: E2 = 1/2 = 1/2 mm², or 0.50 mm². This is the first admissible order. Its maximum frequency is 1/4 Hz, or 0.25 Hz. With N = 3, the entire signal in this finite example is retained and the error is 0 mm².

    E_N can only decrease or remain constant as N increases: adding an order removes a nonnegative quantity from the error sum. This property concerns the distance to the exact original signal, not the distance to an unknown true motion in the presence of noise.

  7. State the exact scope of the result

    V_N denotes the set of real sums consisting of a constant and sines and cosines of orders from 1 to N. The letter y denotes another approximation belonging to V_N. The Fourier residual is orthogonal to each of these components: expanding the square of x − y makes the cross term vanish and gives the following identity.

    ∥x−y∥T2=EN+∥SN−y∥T2≥EN

    S_N is therefore an orthogonal projection: it minimizes the mean square error within this set of allowed frequencies. This result does not allow other frequencies to be chosen freely and does not promise the best approximation at every instant.

    At t = 0 s, r1 equals 10 − 7 = 3 mm and its square is 9 mm², greater than the mean 2.50 mm². Finally, for any periodic square integrable signal, Parseval guarantees that E_N tends to 0 as N increases without bound; this mean square convergence alone does not guarantee convergence at every point.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Truncating means reducing all amplitudes.”

    Write down the retained orders before reconstructing. With N = 1, the mean and the fundamental amplitude keep their values; only the coefficients of higher order become 0. Reducing the fundamental as well would change the approximation rule.

    Review the definition of the cutoff

  • “The error is the square of the sum of the removed amplitudes.”

    Expand the square of the residual, then take its mean over the full period: products of distinct modes have 0 mean. Squaring 3 mm gives 9 mm² here at the time origin; the mean error is 2.50 mm². Keep the stated error measure.

    Review the residual and the energy check

  • “If the curve is smoother, it is closer to the real motion.”

    In our scene, the exact motion contains the fast ripples: removing them creates an error. With noisy readings, we would need to justify separately what is signal and what is noise. A small mean square error does not determine the error at each point either.

    Test this conclusion in the last exercise

Compare with the solution

At N = 1, orders 2 and 3 are rejected: E1 = (4 + 1)/2 = 2.50 mm², which exceeds 1 mm². At N = 2, E2 = 1/2 = 0.50 mm² meets the budget.

Orders below 1 have an error at least as large as E1, by monotonicity. As an explicit check, E0 = 7 mm². The smallest order is therefore 2; the corresponding maximum frequency is 0.25 Hz. No quality percentage is inferred from this budget.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does truncation of order N change in the real convention?

It retains the mean and the sine and cosine coefficients of order at most N; it sets the coefficients of higher orders to 0. The maximum retained frequency is N/T.

Recall 2How do we obtain the mean square error from the rejected modes?

Over a full period, orthogonality makes the cross terms vanish. E_N is half the sum of the squares of the rejected sine and cosine coefficients. Its unit is the square of the signal unit.

Recall 3Within which set does S_N minimize the error?

Among real sums formed from a constant and sines and cosines of order at most N. This fixes the allowed frequencies; it does not promise a minimum error at every instant.

Recall 4Why can increasing N not increase E_N relative to the exact signal?

Adding an order removes half a sum of squares from the rejected tail, hence a nonnegative quantity. This monotonicity is not a guarantee of denoising relative to an unknown true signal.

Recall 5What does Parseval guarantee as N increases without bound for a periodic square integrable signal?

The mean square error of the truncation tends to 0. This convergence is not sufficient to guarantee convergence at every point or to identify which modes might be noise.
