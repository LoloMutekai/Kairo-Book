Level 2 · C072·M3

Provenance and derivations

Your goalReconstruct the dependencies of a result, repeat its derivation and determine which outputs must be reviewed when a source is rejected.

Before you start
c022-m5 : untrusted inputs and injection · c070-m5 : minimum spanning tree · c017-m5 : reproduction protocol · c041-m5 : transactions and windows · c072-m1 : hash and canonical encoding · c072-m2 : hash chain and its limits

✿01 See the idea

In a workshop, you weigh packets of seeds and record 90 g, 110 g and 130 g on a sheet. Later, a table and a sentence report a mean mass. To understand this claim, you need to find the sheet used and how its values were transformed. A well-presented table does not explain this on its own.

Provenance describes where an object comes from and the steps that produced it. A derivation is a step that makes a new object from other objects: copying a sheet, calculating its mean, writing a conclusion. Keep the exact version of each object; the name “latest sheet” would change its meaning the next time the sheet is replaced.

Represent each object as a node and each dependency as an arrow. An arrow from a parent to a child means that this child uses this parent. Tracing back from the conclusion therefore finds its parents, then their parents. Following the arrows towards the children reverses this reasoning and shows which outputs are affected when a source becomes unusable.

The packets, their masses and the documents in this example are fictional. We assume that the objects are available and the graph is complete for this small scenario. We are checking a derivation involving documents and arithmetic; we have neither observed any actual weighing nor checked a scale.

The rule to remember

Keep the exact inputs, the transformation and its parameters; trace back through all parents to explain a result, then follow all descendants to handle a broken dependency.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the weighing sheet to a checkable conclusion

Sheet O contains 3 masses in grams: 90, 110, 130. R calls for their exact arithmetic mean, without exclusions or weighting. S is a fixed copy of O; M calculates; C reports the result; V simply arranges the readings in a table. Let us reconstruct the dependencies before calculating.

  1. Fix the objects and the direction of the links

    O denotes the original sheet and R the stored procedure. S, M, C and V denote the derived objects in the diagram, rather than numbers. For a node v, P(v) is the set of its immediate parents; braces group identifiers. Thus P(S) = {O}, P(M) = {S, R}, P(C) = {M}, P(V) = {S}. O and R have an empty set of parents within our scope.

    The graph is finite and has no cycles, with distinct identifiers and all parents present. A cycle would make a justification depend on itself. The sheet is the documentary root of this case; this does not mean that it is the ultimate origin of knowledge about the packets.

    For each derivation, the provenance record keeps the identifiers and hashes of the input versions, the transformation, its fixed version, its parameters and the identity of the output. It also states who produced this output and on what date. For S: copy without modification; for M: apply R to S in grams; for C: write the mean from M; for V: arrange the masses from S in ascending order, in grams.

  2. Separate identity, traceability and truth

    H denotes the hash function encountered earlier. Here H(O) identifies the bytes of the version of O used. A hash is an identifier with no physical unit; the masses remain in grams. Comparing the bytes of S with those of O checks the copy. No hashes calculated for these fictional documents are provided here.

    Finding H(O) in a provenance record does not prove that M actually used O. A replay must use the exact inputs, the fixed procedure and its parameters, then compare the recalculated output with the reported output. Even a matching replay does not prove that the scale was accurate: it proves a relationship between these inputs and this output.

    We call a node “accepted” when its provenance meets the policy for this case. Roots are accepted after checking their references and the availability of the identified objects. For a derived object, all parents must be accepted. Its provenance record and checks of its transformation are still necessary: the status of the parents alone does not validate its calculation.

  3. Propagate acceptance without skipping a parent

    a(v) encodes this provenance acceptance: 1 means accepted and 0 means rejected. These are indicators with no units, never probabilities of truth. Under our assumptions, a(O) = a(R) = 1. S inherits from O, M requires S and R, C requires M, and V requires S.

    a(M)=a(S)×a(R)=1×1=1

    Here the product implements the condition “both”: a single 0 factor makes the product 0. We move from the roots towards the children until no new node is accepted. We obtain 2 roots and 4 derived objects, giving 6 accepted nodes. An unknown parent remains a reason for blocking; we do not replace its status with an assumption of acceptance.

  4. Replay the numerical transformation

    m denotes a mass; the subscripts a, b and c identify the packets. n = 3 is their number, a count with no physical unit. T is the sum of the masses, in grams, and μ is their arithmetic mean, in grams. The letter T is not a new document in the graph.

    Adding combines all the recorded mass: 90 + 110 = 200 g, then 200 + 130 = 330 g. We divide by n because R calls for the mean mass per packet, giving each reading the same weight.

    T=ma+mb+mc;μ=Tn=3303=110 g

    C can therefore state: “The mean of the masses recorded on this sheet is 110 g.” This wording limits the conclusion to the data used; it does not claim to give the mean mass of all packets in the workshop.

  5. Check by another method

    Reverse check: 110 × 3 = 330 g, recovering T. This check could still share an error with the initial calculation; let us also check the masses directly around the proposed value.

    The first is 20 g below 110 g, the last is 20 g above it, and the middle mass equals 110 g. The signed deviations therefore sum to −20 + 0 + 20 = 0 g. This balance independently explains the mean of this sheet.

    Finally, tracing back from C must find M, S, R and O. O is counted only once among these ancestors. Table V is another output, but has the same origin for its readings: it adds no independent measurement.

  6. Replay a broken dependency and prepare the repair

    Now suppose R is rejected because the referenced version cannot be found. Then a(R) = 0, a(M) = 1 × 0 = 0, then a(C) = 0. R, M and C make up 1 + 2 = 3 rejected nodes. O, S and V remain 3 acceptable nodes under this policy.

    The number 110 g may remain arithmetically correct; its documented derivation is no longer acceptable in its current state. Do not delete the history. Find the expected version of the procedure or create a new derivation explicitly linked to an available procedure; then recalculate the affected outputs. Silently changing the parent name would erase the reason for rejection.

    This process assumes that all relevant links are declared. An omitted dependency may allow an affected output through. The graph lets us examine the provenance supplied; it does not automatically discover hidden inputs.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The hash is correct, so the mean is true.”

    The hash links the provenance record to specific bytes. Then replay the procedure to check the calculation. The accuracy of the weighing still requires evidence specific to the measurement.

    Separate identity, replay and the validity of the readings

  • “One accepted parent is enough to keep M.”

    M uses S and R. Under our rule, all its parents must be accepted: an available copy does not replace a missing procedure. Replay the product of the indicators.

    Follow the broken dependency from R to C

  • “The table and the conclusion confirm two independent weighings.”

    These are 2 outputs, but they trace back to 1 weighing sheet, O. Compare their ancestors instead of counting their presentations. Agreement between copies does not provide an independent observation.

    Repair a conclusion without multiplying the sources

Exercise 2 · Application and countercheck

The copy can no longer be identified

Start again from the original, fully accepted case. Copy S is now rejected: its bytes no longer match the version listed in the provenance record. Which nodes must be rejected, and how many remain acceptable? Explain why this case differs from rejecting R.

Hint 1

Start from S and follow the arrows to all its children, then to their children.

Hint 2

Compare with the solution

S is rejected. M and V lose an accepted parent; C then loses M. The rejected set is {S, M, C, V}, giving 4 nodes. O and R remain acceptable, giving 6 − 4 = 2 nodes.

When R was rejected, V remained acceptable because it did not use that procedure. With S rejected, both the calculation branch and the table branch are affected. Check your reasoning

  • Include V and C in the cascade, even though C does not depend directly on S.
  • Keep O and R without declaring the whole case false.
  • Find 4 rejected nodes and 2 retained nodes by listing them, then by subtraction.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and justified repair

A sourced conclusion copied incorrectly

The versions of O, R, S and M are available. M contains the correct mean; yet C reports 120 g and V displays the original readings. Someone claims: “The parents are accepted and we have 2 documents, so the conclusion is confirmed.” Diagnose the fault, calculate the difference and propose a traceable repair.

Hint 1

Replay the mean before discussing provenance. Then compare the value in C with the value in M.

Hint 2

A new version of C can keep the reference to M and document that it corrects the previous wording.

Compare with the solution

The mean is 110 g, rather than 120 g. The difference between the claim and the calculation is 120 − 110 = 10 g. Accepted parents are not enough: the transformation “write the mean from M” produced an incorrect sentence.

Keep the old C as a rejected version with its reason, then create a corrected version linked to the verified M, with the transformation, the person responsible, its date and its output identity. Check the new sentence against M. O, S and V do not need to be recalculated for this wording error alone; any child of C would need review.

V and C remain 2 presentations from 1 sheet. Their number replaces neither checking the derivation nor an independent observation. An answer constructed after reading these hints is an assisted answer; it does not validate any attained level.

  • Separate acceptance of the parents from verification of the transformation.
  • Keep the history and identify the new output without rewriting the source.
  • Refuse to count derived documents as additional weighings.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be kept to make a derivation replayable?

The exact versions of all inputs, their identifiers, the transformation and its version, its parameters, and then the identified output. The provenance record also keeps the person responsible and the date; the replay must compare the recalculated output with the claim.

Recall 2Starting from C, what is the difference between investigating provenance and handling a broken dependency at S?

Investigating provenance traces back through the parents of C to the roots. Handling the broken dependency at S follows its descendants: M, V and C. The directions answer different questions.

Recall 3Why is an accepted S not enough to accept M if R is rejected?

M depends on S and R. Our rule requires all parents to be accepted; a single rejected parent blocks M, then C. This block concerns provenance and does not by itself demonstrate a numerical error.

Recall 4What does a matching replay of the mean establish, and what does it leave open?

It establishes that the procedure and the exact inputs reproduce the output being compared. It certifies neither the scale, the authenticity of the origin, nor the completeness of the declared links. Table V is not an independent measurement.

Recall 5How can you reconstruct the mechanism without looking at the page?

Redraw O, R, S, M, C and V; give the parents; repeat the sum and mean with their units; replay the rejection of R, then of S; distinguish document identity, calculation and measurement reliability. Try again without support after at least 2 days. Reading this answer does not constitute independent retrieval.
