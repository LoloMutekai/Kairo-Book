Vectors in the plane

Your goalDescribe the displacement of a trolley using a vector, combine successive displacements, and check their components in the same coordinate system.

Before you start
c005-m5 : areas and volumes · c006-m5 : composition · c027-m1 : unit circle · c027-m2 : trigonometric functions · c027-m3 : identities

✿01 See the idea

A trolley leaves mark A in a workshop to reach B, then C. To explain the first movement, you can say: move 3 m to the right and 4 m towards the top of the plan. This instruction describes a displacement; it does not say where the trolley was parked at the start. The marks and measurements in this scene are fictional.

We represent the displacement with an arrow: its tail is at the start and its head at the end. For a nonzero arrow, its line direction is that of the straight line it follows, its orientation tells you which end it points towards, and its length gives the size of the displacement. The vector keeps these characteristics when the whole arrow is moved parallel to itself.

So a copy of the arrow starting at C can represent the same vector as the arrow from A to B. The starting points differ, but the instruction stays the same. A point locates the trolley; a vector tells you how to get from one position to another.

Now look at the route through B. The second movement goes back 2 m to the left without moving any further up. How much of the initial horizontal displacement does it cancel? To find the final position from A, we must combine the displacements on each axis, keeping their signs.

The rule to remember

To form a vector, subtract the starting coordinate from the ending coordinate on each axis. To combine successive displacements, add the corresponding components in the same coordinate system.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Find the trolley’s final position while keeping track of signs

On the workshop plan, A = (1 ; 1) m, B = (4 ; 5) m, and C = (2 ; 5) m. The trolley follows the line segments from A to B, then from B to C. Let us calculate its displacements, check its final position, and measure the length of the first displacement.

  1. Set the coordinate system and read the symbols

    O is the origin, with coordinates (0 ; 0). The x-axis points to the right and the y-axis towards the top of the plan. They are perpendicular and use the same unit, the metre: this is called an orthonormal coordinate system. We model the trolley as a point in this plane, with coordinates assumed to be exact for the exercise.

    In A = (xa ; ya), xa is the x-coordinate, or abscissa, giving the horizontal position, and ya is the y-coordinate, or ordinate, giving the vertical position; both are in metres. The subscripts B and C identify the coordinates of the other marks. The semicolon separates the components and avoids confusion with a decimal comma.

    The arrow above AB means the vector from point A to point B. We call it u, with an arrow to remind us that it is a vector. Its components ux and uᵧ are the signed displacements along x and y, in metres. Similarly, v will denote the displacement from B to C and w the total displacement from A to C.

  2. Subtract the starting position from the ending position

    To move from position A to position B, we look for what must be added to each coordinate of A. The ending coordinate minus the starting coordinate gives exactly this change.

    AB→=(xB−xA;yB−yA)

    Horizontally: 4 − 1 = 3 m. Vertically: 5 − 1 = 4 m. The vector u therefore has components (3 ; 4) m. Both signs are positive: right and up. Adding these components to A gives B, as expected.

    u→=(3;4) m

  3. Keep the sign of the horizontal return

    From B to C, subtracting in the same order gives 2 − 4 = -2 m along x and 5 − 5 = 0 m along y. So v = (-2 ; 0) m. Here, a negative component means left; a 0 component means there is no displacement along that axis.

    Using the positive value 2 for the horizontal component would send the trolley to the right. The horizontal length is indeed 2 m, but the sign is needed to describe which way the displacement goes.

  4. Add along each axis separately

    We add the components of u to the starting position, then those of v. The total change along x is therefore ux + vx; along y, it is uᵧ + vᵧ. We do not mix a horizontal component with a vertical component.

    u→+v→=(ux+vx;uy+vy)

    Along x: 3 + (-2) = 1 m. Along y: 4 + 0 = 4 m. The total is w = (1 ; 4) m. Geometrically, the head of the first arrow meets the tail of the next: the sum connects the start of the first to the end of the last.

    AB→+BC→=AC→

    This equality is Chasles’ relation, also known as the triangle law for vector addition. Along x, it amounts to writing (xʙ − xa) + (x꜀ − xʙ) = x꜀ − xa: the contributions from the intermediate position cancel. The same reasoning applies along y.

  5. Check using the direct displacement and the final position

    Let us calculate again without passing through B: from A to C, the horizontal component is 2 − 1 = 1 m and the vertical component is 5 − 1 = 4 m. We get (1 ; 4) m again, the result of the addition.

    Let us also check the position: starting position plus displacement gives (1 + 1 ; 1 + 4) m = (2 ; 5) m, exactly C. This check detects subtraction in the wrong order: a displacement must lead to the stated final position.

  6. Distinguish components from length

    The norm of a vector is its length; we write it using double bars. For u, it is the length of the straight line segment from A to B. The components form the perpendicular sides of the triangle in the first diagram: Pythagoras’ theorem gives the square of the diagonal’s length.

    ∥u→∥=ux2+uy2

    Squaring means multiplying a quantity by itself. Here 3 × 3 = 9 m² and 4 × 4 = 16 m²; their sum is 25 m². The square root selects the nonnegative length whose square gives this sum: 5 m, since 5 × 5 = 25 m².

    Adding 3 + 4 would give 7 m. This would be the length of the horizontal then vertical detour drawn with thin lines, rather than the length of the straight displacement from A to B. The norm formula assumes perpendicular axes and the same physical scale; it cannot be applied without adjustment to just any pair of numbers.

  7. Multiply the whole instruction by the same number

    A number k with no unit, called a scalar, can multiply a vector. Each component receives the same factor; the whole arrow is lengthened, shortened, or reversed.

    ku→=(kux;kuy)

    With k = 2, repeating the instruction gives (6 ; 8) m: this is u + u. For a nonzero vector, a positive k keeps its orientation; a negative k reverses it. With k = -1, we get the opposite vector −u = (-3 ; -4) m, the displacement from B to A.

    With k = 0, the components are (0 ; 0) m: the 0 vector produces no displacement and has neither a defined line direction nor a defined orientation. The sum u + (−u) is also 0. For a nonzero k, the length is multiplied by |k|, the absolute value of k, meaning its value without a negative sign. So a negative factor never produces a negative length.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Explain why we subtract positions, why B disappears in the sum, and why the length is not the sum of the components. Finish by explaining what changes if the whole instruction is multiplied by a negative factor.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The coordinates of B are the components of the vector from A to B.”

    B gives a position relative to O. The vector from A to B gives a change from A: subtract the coordinates of A, then add the result to A to check that you do reach B. In general, the two pairs coincide only if A is the origin.

    Review the subtraction and the check of the final position

  • “A negative component is impossible because a distance is positive.”

    A component is a signed displacement. Here -2 m means 2 m to the left. The norm, which measures length, is nonnegative. Reversing a displacement changes the signs of its components without changing its length.

    Reread the horizontal return and the norm calculation

  • “To add vectors, I add their lengths.”

    Displacements can cancel each other along an axis, as u and v do here. Place the arrows head to tail, then add their signed components separately. The sum of the lengths describes the route along the line segments, but generally does not give the length of the total displacement.

    Review the arrows for the route through B

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · signs and orientation

Return from B to A

The trolley is at B = (4 ; 5) m and returns directly to A = (1 ; 1) m. Calculate the components of the displacement from B to A, then those of the outward movement followed by the return. What happens to the arrow’s length on the return?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write the words start and end above the points before subtracting.

Hint 2

The return must cancel each component of u without making its length negative.

Compare with the solution

The end is A and the start is B. The horizontal component is 1 − 4 = -3 m and the vertical component is 1 − 5 = -4 m. The return displacement is therefore −u = (-3 ; -4) m.

Outward movement then return: (3 + (-3) ; 4 + (-4)) m = (0 ; 0) m. The trolley is back at A: its total displacement is 0, even though it has travelled. The return arrow has the same length 5 m as the outward arrow; only its orientation is reversed.

Check your reasoning

  • You subtract B from A for the return and obtain two negative components.
  • You check cancellation along each axis.
  • You distinguish 0 total displacement, the route travelled, and nonnegative length.

Exercise 2 · Application · position and displacement

Start again from C with the same instruction

The trolley is now at C = (2 ; 5) m. It follows the instruction u = (3 ; 4) m and arrives at D. Calculate the coordinates of D, then the components of the vector from C to D. Is this vector equal to the vector from A to B, despite their different starting points?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Find the ending position by adding the displacement to the starting position.

Hint 2

To check the copy, then subtract C from D and compare both components with those of u.

Compare with the solution

D has x-coordinate 2 + 3 = 5 m and y-coordinate 5 + 4 = 9 m. So D = (5 ; 9) m. Copying the instruction does not take the trolley back to B: the starting point has changed.

The vector from C to D is (5 − 2 ; 9 − 5) m = (3 ; 4) m. Its components are exactly those of u in the same coordinate system. The vectors are equal: the arrow has been translated, meaning moved parallel to itself without changing its line direction, orientation, or length.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain D = (5 ; 9) m by addition, with a unit for each coordinate.
  • You check the displacement with an independent subtraction.
  • You explain why the vectors are equal without requiring their starting points to be the same.

Exercise 3 · Synthesis · negative factor and geometric check

Go back by half the first displacement

Start again from B, independently of the previous exercise. Apply the displacement k u to the trolley, with k = -1/2, a number with no unit, and call the final position E. Before calculating, predict where E should lie on the line segment AB. Calculate the displacement, position E, and displacement length; check your prediction.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The sign of the factor reverses the orientation of u; its absolute value halves the length.

Hint 2

Multiply both components of u by the same factor, then add this new displacement to the coordinates of B.

Hint 3

To check a midpoint, compare the displacement from A to E with the displacement from E to B.

Compare with the solution

The factor is negative: from B, the trolley heads back towards A. Its absolute value is 1/2, so it stops halfway. This prediction provides a check before carrying out the operations.

The displacement is (-1/2 × 3 ; -1/2 × 4) m = (-3/2 ; -2) m. We keep the exact fractions. The final position is E = (4 + (-3/2) ; 5 + (-2)) m = (5/2 ; 3) m, or (2.5 ; 3.0) m.

Since E is the midpoint, doubling each of its coordinates must give the sum of the endpoint coordinates: 2 × 5/2 = 1 + 4 and 2 × 3 = 1 + 5. This check confirms a final position halfway along AB.

The length is 5/2 = 5/2 m, or 2.5 m. It stays positive. We can also check that its square equals the sum of the squares of the components -3/2 m and -2 m: the signs of the displacement components disappear when they are squared.

Check your reasoning

  • You predict a return from B to the midpoint of AB before looking at the solution.
  • You multiply each component by -1/2, then add the result to B.
  • You give E = (2.5 ; 3.0) m and a length of 2.5 m, distinguishing the two objects.
  • You check the midpoint using the endpoint coordinates and justify why the length is positive.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the difference between point B and the vector from A to B?

B gives a position relative to the origin of the coordinate system. The vector from A to B gives the change needed from A; its components are the ending coordinates minus the starting coordinates. The same vector can be represented by arrows starting at different points.

Recall 2Why does the displacement from A to B, followed by the displacement from B to C, give the vector from A to C?

The arrows join at B. Along each axis, the coordinate of B is added and then subtracted: it cancels. What remains is the ending coordinate at C minus the starting coordinate at A. This is Chasles’ relation, calculated by adding corresponding components.

Recall 3How can you find a vector’s length from its components? What assumptions must you check?

With perpendicular axes and the same physical unit on each, take the square root of the sum of the squares of the components. Pythagoras’ theorem justifies this rule. The norm is nonnegative; simply adding the components does not give this length.

Recall 4What does a negative factor do to a nonzero vector? What about a 0 factor?

The same factor multiplies both components. If it is negative, the orientation reverses and the length is multiplied by its absolute value. If it is 0, the result is the 0 vector, with no displacement and neither a defined line direction nor a defined orientation.

Recall 5What simple check detects subtraction done in the wrong order?

Add the components you obtained to the starting coordinates: you must get the ending position. For a sum of successive displacements, also compare with the direct difference between the final and initial positions, in the same coordinate system.
