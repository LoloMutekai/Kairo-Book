Level 2 · C074·M1

Causal effect

Your goalDefine the effect of an intervention by comparing it with its alternative, calculate an average effect, and recognize what observations alone leave unknown.

Before you start
c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c073-m5 : interaction and repeated measures · c009-m5 : reading notes

✿01 See the idea

An application offers to preload a document before opening it. You turn the option on, and opening the document takes 90 ms. Did preloading shorten the wait? The stopwatch gives the time recorded; it does not give the time that opening this same document, from the same starting state, would have taken with the option off.

The causal effect answers this comparison: what would change if the option were turned on rather than left off? The intervention is the setting imposed on the option. The outcome is the measured opening time. A millisecond, written ms, is the unit for all times on this page. We measure from the request to open the document until the document is usable; the earlier cost of preloading is outside this measurement.

For a given situation, the potential outcome is the time that would be obtained under a specified choice. Once the option is set, the outcome of that choice can be observed; the outcome of the other choice is counterfactual, meaning that it is not realized in this situation. A later opening starts from a different state: it does not automatically reveal the missing alternative.

The scenario and all its values are fictional. To learn the calculations, we will provide the outcomes under each choice, as in a fully specified mathematical world. In a real observation, only one of these outcomes is available for each situation. The counterfactual values given here are neither measurements nor gains observed in an application.

The rule to remember

Define the situation and the outcome, compare the intervention with its alternative, then separate what the model provides from what has been observed.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What preloading changes, situation by situation

The fictional world provides the pairs (option off; option on): A (120 ms; 90 ms), B (200 ms; 170 ms), C (160 ms; 200 ms). The target population is exactly this set of 3 situations, each with equal weight. Let us calculate the effect on opening time, using the convention on minus off.

  1. Specify what changing the option means

    A situation i means an opening with a document, a machine, and an initial state fixed before the option is set; here i is A, B, or C. Preloading may then change the cache. The choice t is 0 for the option off and 1 for the option on. These are codes without units, not durations. Yᵢ(t) denotes the potential outcome, in ms, under that choice. The parentheses mean “under choice t”, not multiplication.

    We fix the preloading version and the definition of the timing procedure. We assume that the choice applied to one situation does not change the potential outcomes of the others: there is no shared cache or competition between these openings. These conditions give each pair a precise meaning. They do not provide any missing outcome in a real experiment.

    We treat the potential times as fixed in this example. If the time still varies with the initial state fixed, we would need to specify the target average quantity and its uncertainty; here we calculate neither an interval nor a statistical test.

  2. Define and calculate an individual effect

    τᵢ, read “tau subscript i”, is the individual causal effect: the duration with the intervention minus the duration without it, for the same situation. Its unit is still ms. A negative effect means a faster opening; a positive effect means a slowdown.

    τi=Yi(1)−Yi(0)

    For A, subtract the reference time: 90 − 120 = -30 ms. Preloading therefore shortens the opening by 30 ms in this fictional world. Saying “time saved” reverses the sign: 120 − 90 = 30 ms. We will keep the signed effect on minus off throughout.

    Check: adding the effect to the time with the option off gives the time with it on, 120 + (-30) = 90 ms.

  3. Calculate the other effects while keeping situations separate

    For B: 170 − 200 = -30 ms. For C: 200 − 160 = 40 ms. The reference for B is its own time with the option off, never the time for A. Within each row, we compare alternative choices from an identical starting state.

    C slows down while A and B speed up: the effect depends on the situation. The table specifies these potential outcomes without explaining their internal mechanism. Inferring a general rule about how preloading works from them would be unjustified.

  4. Find the average effect on the stated population

    n is the number of situations, here 3, and has no unit. τ̄, tau with a bar above it, is the average of the effects over this finite set. The symbol ∑ asks us to add one term per situation; the index i runs through all the situations numbered from 1 to n, here A, B, and C in that order. The factor 1/n gives each situation equal weight.

    τ¯=1n∑i=1nτi

    Add the effects before dividing: (-30) + (-30) = -60 ms, then -60 + 40 = -20 ms. This sum counts the changes across the set; for an average change per opening, we divide it by the number of openings.

    τ¯=-203 ms≈-6.67 ms

    The exact fraction is -20/3 ms; -6.67 ms is its rounded value. The average effect is negative on this fictional population, despite the slowdown for C. This does not promise an identical effect for every situation or for documents outside this set.

  5. Check by organizing the calculation differently

    Calculate the average times for this same population separately under each choice. With the option off: 120 + 200 = 320 ms, then 320 + 160 = 480 ms; dividing by 3 gives 160 ms.

    With the option on: 90 + 170 = 260 ms, then 260 + 200 = 460 ms; dividing by 3 gives 460/3 ms, or approximately 153.33 ms.

    The exact difference 460/3 − 160 = -20/3 ms gives τ̄ again. This follows from the distributive property of the sum: averaging the differences or subtracting these averages gives the same result because the same situations, with the same weights, appear under each choice. Subtracting only the averages of different observed groups does not have this justification.

  6. Return to what a log of openings would show

    Now suppose that the option was actually turned on for A and off for B and C. In our fictional log, the only observed times would then be A: 90 ms; B: 200 ms; C: 160 ms. Tᵢ denotes the choice actually applied, coded like t. Observed Yᵢ is the measured time. Consistency of the definition requires it to match the potential outcome under the choice received:

    Yi observé=Yi(Ti)

    The observed average with the option off is (200 + 160)/2 = 360/2 = 180 ms. The observed difference on minus off is 90 − 180 = -90 ms. It compares A with B and C; it does not compare the choices on the same population as our average effect -20/3 ms.

    Even for A alone, observing 90 ms does not settle the question. If its time with the option off were 120 ms, its effect would be -30 ms; if that time were 80 ms, the effect would be 10 ms. The observed time stays the same. The complete table allows our calculation because its alternatives are given by construction; a log alone does not provide them. This unit stops at distinguishing the definition of the effect from determining it using data.

Explain it in your own words

Explain why subtracting the averages in the check is valid, while subtracting the group averages in the log does not recover the same effect. Name exactly which situations are included in each average.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The time fell after activation: this decrease is the causal effect.”

    A later opening may involve a different document, cache, or machine state. Repair the comparison by naming the time for the same situation under the alternative option setting. If it is unknown, write “unknown counterfactual” instead of equating it with the time for another opening.

    Return to the fork between choices

  • “A negative effect means that the option is bad.”

    Start with the outcome and the order of subtraction. We measure a time and calculate on minus off: -30 ms here means 30 ms less waiting. For a different outcome or a different order, the practical meaning of the sign may change.

    Reread the definition and the sign check

  • “The average shows that every opening speeds up.”

    Return to the effects row by row: C has an effect of 40 ms while the average effect is -6.67 ms. An average summarizes a stated population; it replaces neither the individual effects nor evidence about another population.

    Redo the exercise on the average and its exception

Exercise 1 · Application · individual effect

Stopwatch and sign convention

For A, the fictional world gives 120 ms with the option off and 90 ms with it on. Calculate the signed effect on minus off, then the time saved. If A receives the option on, which value is observed and which is counterfactual?

Hint 1

Write the name of the choice above each term before subtracting.

Hint 2

Time saved is calculated in the reverse order to the signed effect on a duration.

Compare with the solution

The effect is 90 − 120 = -30 ms. The time saved is 120 − 90 = 30 ms. These descriptions are compatible: activation shortens the wait in this fictional world.

With the option on, we observe 90 ms. The time of 120 ms with the option off is counterfactual for this realization; it is known in the exercise because the author provided it, not because a second stopwatch observed it. Check 120 + (-30) = 90 ms.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the order on minus off and the unit ms.
  • Distinguish the sign of the effect from the amount of time saved.
  • Name the choice received before identifying the observed and counterfactual outcomes.

Is the statement “every opening speeds up” compatible with the table?

Hint 1

Subtract within each row, then add these effects before dividing by n.

Hint 2

For the check, keep A, B, and C in each average. Read the sign for C separately.

Compare with the solution

The effects are A: -30 ms; B: -30 ms; C: 40 ms. Their sum is -60 + 40 = -20 ms. The average is therefore -20/3 = -20/3 ms, or approximately -6.67 ms. Keep the fraction until the end to avoid a difference caused by rounding.

The check gives 460/3 − 160 = -20/3 ms again. The average with the option on is approximately 153.33 ms and the average with it off is 160 ms. These are the same situations under each choice, not disjoint observed groups.

The statement is false: C slows down by 40 ms. The negative average describes a decrease in average time over this fictional set; this positive individual effect remains.

  • Present the effects row by row before the average.
  • Give the exact fraction and the rounded value with the unit.
  • Recover the same fraction by subtracting the averages for the entire population.
  • Cite C as a counterexample to the universal statement.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · test a conclusion

The same log allows opposite effects

The fictional log remains A on: 90 ms; B off: 200 ms; C off: 160 ms. Another potential outcome table is proposed that keeps these observed cells and replaces only the missing cells: A off 80 ms; B on 210 ms; C on 170 ms. Calculate its individual effects and its average effect. Is this log enough to choose between the initial table and this other possibility? Explain what this shows about the observed difference of -90 ms.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First copy only the observed cells. None of them may change in the new table.

Hint 2

The alternative values are proposed counterfactuals, not new observations. Recalculate each difference on minus off.

Hint 3

If the same observations fit average effects with opposite signs, they do not by themselves determine the causal sign.

Compare with the solution

A: 90 − 80 = 10 ms. B: 210 − 200 = 10 ms. C: 170 − 160 = 10 ms. The sum is 20 + 10 = 30 ms and its average is 30/3 = 10 ms. The option slows down every situation in this other possibility.

Check: adding 10, 10, and 10 ms, respectively, to the times with the option off in this new table gives its times with the option on. The observations remain exactly A: 90 ms; B: 200 ms; C: 160 ms. The log is therefore compatible with the initial average effect -20/3 ms and with this average effect 10 ms.

The log does not decide between these possibilities. Its observed difference remains -90 ms; it is not even enough to determine the sign of the average causal effect without additional information or assumptions. This does not prove that no study could estimate an effect: it specifies what these observations alone do not determine. The following units will study the conditions that make a comparison informative.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep all observed cells and change only the unrealized alternatives.
  • Calculate the effects and the average for the new table in ms.
  • Explain the indeterminacy through the same observations being compatible with effects of opposite signs.
  • Do not turn a counterfactual possibility into a demonstrated real effect.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the causal effect of an option compare for a given opening?

The potential outcome with the option on and the potential outcome with it off for that same initial situation. The convention on minus off gives a difference in ms here.

Recall 2After the option has actually been set, what is called counterfactual?

The outcome that would have been obtained under the unrealized alternative choice. It is not automatically revealed by another opening or by the outcome of the choice received.

Recall 3When does the difference in average times exactly recover our average of the effects?

When each average covers the same situations under the corresponding choice, with the same weights. This is an identity of the complete potential outcome table; the observed groups in the log do not form that table.

Recall 4Does a negative average effect on time guarantee a speedup for every opening?

No. C has an effect of 40 ms while the initial average is -20/3 ms. The average concerns the stated population and does not fix each of its effects.

Recall 5What do alternative tables compatible with the same log prove?

These observations alone do not determine the average causal effect: different counterfactuals preserve the same observed times and even change the sign of the effect. Additional justification is needed to determine this effect from data.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without looking: name the intervention, its alternative, the situation, and the outcome; define the potential outcomes; write the signed effect and its average on a precise population; check using the averages of the complete table; then state what the log observes and which alternative is missing.
