Level 2 · C098·M5

Walk-forward, leakage and an inconclusive verdict

Your goalDivide a simulation into successive test segments, assign each trade using its two dates to avoid temporal leakage, then explain why a numerical measure of degradation is not enough to resolve an inconclusive verdict.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A fictional simulator has replayed a strategy over 40 daily bars, ordered by time. It produced 7 simulated trades. To find out whether these results hold up “in the future”, we keep them in chronological order: we place a cutoff, look at what comes before it, then examine a test segment immediately after it. Walk-forward repeats this procedure: the cutoff advances by one segment, and what was ahead of it becomes part of the past.

A trade’s result is known only at its exit: it depends on all the bars between entry and exit. If the trade enters before a cutoff and exits after it, its result includes bars that the cutoff is supposed to know nothing about. Assigning it to the past creates temporal leakage: information from after the cutoff is used to judge what came before it.

In the version reviewed at the milestone, the strategy is not retuned at each cutoff: the simulator replays the entire series once, then sorts the trades relative to the cutoffs. This sorting is the subject of this page.

The verdict remains. Even without leakage, a few trades do not support a conclusion. The reviewed code goes further: it never draws a conclusion. Its verdict is “inconclusive” (INCONCLUSIVE), which means neither “good” nor “bad”: the calculation establishes nothing, and the figures remain descriptive.

The rule to remember

For a given cutoff, a trade belongs to the past only if it exited before that cutoff, and to the segment only if it entered within the segment and exited before its end; otherwise it is excluded. A numerical difference between two means remains descriptive, and an inconclusive verdict is neither a success nor a failure.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Three cutoffs, seven fictional trades

A fictional series contains 40 daily bars; the requested warmup is 10 bars and we want 3 test segments. Seven simulated trades, all invented, are described by their entry bar, exit bar and result in basis points (bp). A: entry 12, exit 18, 40 bp. B: entry 20, exit 24, 10 bp. C: entry 23, exit 28, 70 bp. D: entry 26, exit 29, 20 bp. E: entry 28, exit 33, 10 bp. F: entry 31, exit 34, 6 bp. G: entry 36, exit 39, 4 bp. We want to place the cutoffs, sort the trades, compare the past with the test period, then read the verdict.

  1. Define the symbols and assumptions

    A bar is a period in the series, here a fictional day, numbered in chronological order. N is the number of bars in the series (40 here). w is the warmup, in bars, that the simulator requires before it can assess results (10 here). K is the number of successive test segments (3 here). A cutoff s is a bar number; e is the bar where the test segment ends, with that bar excluded.

    A trade has three numbers: its entry bar a, its exit bar b, with a < b, and its result p. The result p is expressed in basis points (bp): one bp is one hundredth of a percent of the amount invested.

    Assumptions for this example: a single simulation covers the entire series and the strategy is not tuned segment by segment; the result p is known only at bar b; all values are fictional and represent no real market.

  2. Place the cutoffs

    The first cutoff sp is placed halfway between the warmup and the end, leaving an actual past interval before it:

    sp=N+w2

    Calculation: N + w = 40 + 10 = 50, then 50 / 2 = 25. Check: 25 > 10, and 25 − 10 = 15 = 40 − 25.

    The test segments all have the same length L, and the last extends to the end of the series:

    L=N−spK

    Calculation: L = (40 − 25) / 3 = 15 / 3 = 5 bars. Each subsequent cutoff adds L to the previous one:

    sj+1=sj+L

    Thus 30 = 25 + 5, then 35 = 30 + 5. The last segment runs from 35 to 40, covering 5 bars. Check: 5 + 5 + 5 = 15, exactly the bars after the first cutoff. In the reviewed code, both divisions use integer division; the values in this example are chosen so that both divide exactly.

  3. Sort the trades at the first cutoff

    For a cutoff s and the end e of the segment that follows it, there are two sorting rules:

    passé :b<s
    segment :s≤aetb<e

    Reason: the result exists only at the exit bar. A trade whose exit is not strictly before the cutoff includes bars that the cutoff does not yet know.

    At cutoff 25: A (exit 18) and B (exit 24) exit before it, so they belong to the past. C enters at bar 23, which is 2 bars before the cutoff, and exits at bar 28, which is 3 bars after it: it crosses the cutoff and is excluded from the past. D, E, F and G enter at or after bar 25: they belong to the test period.

    Check: 2 trades in the past, 4 in the test period and 1 excluded make 7, the seven original trades.

  4. Advance the cutoff: the next segment

    At cutoff 30, the past consists of all trades that exited before bar 30: A, B, C (exit 28) and D (exit 29), making 4 trades. C, excluded at the previous cutoff, now belongs to the past: its exit is finally earlier than the cutoff. It was deferred rather than lost.

    The segment from 30 to 35 (excluded) contains F: entry 31, exit 34. E enters at bar 28 but exits at bar 33: it crosses cutoff 30, so it is left out of the segment. The code therefore keeps two counts: one per segment, requiring entry and exit within it, and one for the entire test period, retaining every trade entered at or after the first cutoff. The next step uses the second count.

  5. Summarize the past and the test period

    Past at the first cutoff: A and B. Sum: 40 + 10 = 50 bp across 2 trades, giving a mean of 25 bp. Test period: D, E, F, G. Sum: 20 + 10 = 30, then 30 + 6 = 36, then 36 + 4 = 40 bp across 4 trades, giving a mean of 10 bp.

    Relative degradation compares the two means, using the mean for the past as the reference:

    D=mpassé−mtest|mpassé|×100
    25−1025×100=60

    Calculation: a difference of 15 bp, a ratio of 0.60, giving 60 %. Check: 10 + 15 = 25. This number describes these seven fictional trades; it says nothing about a real strategy.

  6. Measure the effect of leakage

    Suppose we wrongly assign C to the past. The sum becomes 50 + 70 = 120 bp across 3 trades, giving a mean of 40 bp. Degradation becomes (40 − 10) / 40 × 100 = 75 %, instead of 60 %. The figure shifts by 15 percentage points.

    Reason: C’s result depends on bars 25 to 28, which are already in the test period. This shift applies to the fictional result chosen for C; it is not a general cost of leakage, only evidence that leakage can change the conclusion we think we see.

  7. Read the inconclusive verdict

    Counts: 2 trades in the past and 4 in the test period, compared with a legacy sample-count threshold of 30. The shortfalls are 30 − 2 = 28 on one side and 30 − 4 = 26 on the other; the code adds a count warning, described as descriptive and never as a confidence gate.

    Above all, the reviewed code performs no inference: its note says so, sets a p-value of 1.0 and an effect size of 0.0 as compatibility placeholders, and returns an inconclusive verdict in every case. Our 60 % therefore remains descriptive accounting: evidence of neither overfitting nor robustness.

Explain it in your own words

Explain without looking at the page: why trade C is excluded at cutoff 25 but admitted to the past at cutoff 30, and why a degradation of 60 % does not allow you to say that the strategy is bad.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A trade entered before the cutoff belongs to the past, even if it exits after it”

    Its result exists only at exit. Compare the exit bar with the cutoff: strictly before it for the past. A trade that enters before and exits after crosses the cutoff and remains excluded.

    Review the sorting of trade C at the first cutoff

  • “A trade excluded at one cutoff is lost for all later cutoffs”

    Exclusion depends on the cutoff being considered. At the next cutoff, the exit may now be earlier than the cutoff: the trade then joins the past, like C at cutoff 30. Sort the trades again at each cutoff.

    Find this change of category in the sorting exercise

  • “A numerical degradation is enough to declare a strategy robust or overfit”

    The reviewed code calculates the difference without inference and always returns an inconclusive verdict. A figure such as 60 % describes a fictional sample; it does not measure uncertainty, dependence between neighboring trades, or strategy selection.

    Reread the cited code lines and their limitations

Sort two trades that cross a boundary

Using cutoffs 30 and 35 from the example, consider two fictional trades: H enters at bar 29 and exits at bar 32; J enters at bar 31 and exits at bar 35. For each, say whether it belongs to the past at cutoff 30, to the segment from 30 to 35, or is excluded, then whether it belongs to the past at cutoff 35. Justify your answer using the rule.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For the past, first look at the exit bar: must it be strictly before the cutoff?

Hint 2

For the segment, entry must be at or after the cutoff and exit strictly before the end of the segment.

Compare with the solution

H: entry 29 before 30, exit 32 after it, which is 2 bars after the cutoff. It crosses the cutoff: excluded from the past and from the segment at cutoff 30. But 32 is before 35: at cutoff 35, H belongs to the past.

J: entry 31 at or after 30, but exit 35 is not strictly before the end 35: margin 35 − 35 = 0. It is excluded from the segment. It does not belong to the past at cutoff 35 either, because its exit 35 is not before 35. A 0 margin means exclusion: the comparison is strict.

Check your reasoning

  • Compare the exit with the cutoff using a strict inequality.
  • Require entry and exit within the segment to count the trade in it.
  • Sort again at each cutoff instead of keeping the first answer.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How is the first cutoff placed in this version, and what must it leave before it?

Halfway between the warmup and the end: (N + w) / two. It must be after the warmup to leave an actual past interval.

Recall 2What rule assigns a trade to the past at a cutoff, and what rule assigns it to that cutoff’s segment?

Past: exit strictly before the cutoff. Segment: entry at or after the cutoff and exit strictly before the end of the segment. Otherwise, excluded at this cutoff.

Recall 3Why must a trade crossing a cutoff not be counted in the past?

Its result exists only at exit and depends on bars after the cutoff: counting it in the past uses future information to judge what came before.

Recall 4What does an inconclusive verdict mean, even with a numerical degradation?

The calculation establishes neither robustness nor overfitting: the code performs no inference and the difference remains descriptive. Inconclusive does not mean bad.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the sequence from memory: place a first cutoff between the warmup and the end, divide the test period into equal segments, then, at each cutoff, assign each trade using its two bars (past, segment or excluded). Calculate means and degradation using this sorting without leakage, compare the sample counts with the threshold, and conclude that the verdict remains inconclusive because the difference describes without inferring.
