Level 1 · C033·M1

Derivative

Your goalBuild a derivative from average slopes, then explain its sign and unit without confusing it with the quantity present.

Before you start
c031-m5 : asymptotes · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales

✿01 See the idea

A tank is draining. At 2 min, it contains 16 L; at 3 min, 9 L remain. Saying how much water is present and saying how fast that quantity changes answer different questions. The tank remains partly full while its volume decreases.

Between these times, the volume changes by -7 L in 1 min. The average slope is therefore -7 L/min. The negative sign indicates a decrease; it does not describe a negative volume. Volume is plotted vertically on the graph, and time horizontally.

To describe the rate of change at the exact time when you look at the tank, bring the second time closer to the first. The line joining the points is a secant. If its slopes approach the same finite value, from earlier times and from later times, that limit is the derivative at that time. The line through the point with this slope is the tangent.

The scene and all its numbers are fictional. We have a continuous rule for volume, given below so that we can carry out exact calculations. It is an exercise model, not a flow rate measurement or a validated law for a real tank. Remember that a limit describes a value approached as closely as desired, without requiring that it be reached.

The rule to remember

Calculate a change in volume, divide it by the time offset, then find the limit as this offset approaches 0 while remaining nonzero. The derivative keeps the unit of volume per time.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What is the draining rate at 2 min?

The fictional rule is V(t) = a × (T − t) × (T − t), with a = 1 litre per minute squared and T = 6 min. Time t ranges from 0 min to T. We want the derivative at the interior time τ = 2 min: what is the rate of change of the volume at that time?

  1. Define the symbols, units and domain

    V(t) is the volume in litres at time t, expressed in minutes. T is the end of draining in this model; a is a constant coefficient in litres per minute squared. A minute squared is the product of a minute and a minute. The square of T − t is in minutes squared: its product with a is indeed a volume. The symbol τ, read as “tau”, fixes the time being studied.

    h is a signed time offset, in minutes. If h is positive, τ + h is later; if it is negative, that time is earlier. We require h to differ from 0 and τ + h to be in the domain. ΔV, read as “delta V”, denotes V(τ + h) − V(τ). The function q(h) = ΔV/h is the average slope in L/min.

    The derivative V′(τ), read as “V prime at tau”, is the finite limit of q(h) as h approaches 0 from both sides. The function is said to be differentiable at τ if this limit exists. Continuity alone does not guarantee this property.

    V′(τ)=limh→0V(τ+h)−V(τ)h ; h ≠ 0

  2. Calculate the volumes before calculating a slope

    At τ = 2 min, there are b = T − τ = 6 − 2 = 4 min left before the planned end. The letter b only serves to shorten the calculation. Its square is 16 minutes squared, giving V(τ) = 1 × 16 = 16 L.

    First choose h = 1 min. The other time is 2 + 1 = 3 min. There are then 3 min left; the square is 9 minutes squared and V(τ + h) = 1 × 9 = 9 L. The volumes are values of the same function, not flow rates.

  3. Form the change, then divide it by the time offset

    Subtracting the starting volume from the ending volume gives the signed change: ΔV = 9 − 16 = -7 L. Dividing by the offset gives q(h) = -7/1 = -7 L/min. The denominator counts minutes, not the initial litres.

    This result describes the interval from 2 min to 3 min. It does not yet give the derivative at τ. We must now vary h instead of keeping a fixed interval.

  4. Simplify the quotient without ever dividing by 0

    Since T − (τ + h) = b − h, expand the square before dividing. The factor a stays in front of the difference of squares. The b squared terms cancel, revealing a factor h.

    ΔV=a[(b−h)2−b2]=a(−2bh+h2)

    Since h is nonzero, we can cancel the factor h in the numerator and denominator. The quotient becomes an expression with no division by h.

    q(h)=ΔVh=−2ab+ah ; h ≠ 0

  5. Take the limit and give its unit

    As h approaches 0, the term a h approaches 0 L/min, from either side. The term −2ab does not change. The limit therefore exists and equals −2ab. We have proved differentiability at τ for this model, instead of assuming it from the drawing.

    The factor 2a equals 2 litres per minute squared. Its product with b = 4 min is 8 L/min; with the minus sign, V′(τ) = -8 L/min. A volume of 16 L and a rate of change of -8 L/min describe the same tank at the same time.

    V′(τ)=−2ab=-8 L/min

    The outward flow rate, counted as positive, would have the value 8 L/min here. We are calculating the change in the volume remaining: this is why the derivative has a negative sign.

  6. Check with a shorter interval and an identity

    Take h = 0.1 min. The other time is 2.1 min; T − t is 3.9 min, its square is 15.21 minutes squared and the volume is 15.21 L. The difference 15.21 − 16 = -0.79 L, divided by 0.1 min, gives the slope -7.9 L/min.

    Check using another expression: q(h) − V′(τ) = a h. For h = 1 min, this difference is 1 L/min; for h = 0.1 min, it is 0.1 L/min. The results calculated from the volumes give these same differences. Shrinking the interval brings the slope closer to -8 L/min here.

    The identity also proves convergence for negative h. A list of nearby numbers would not, on its own, prove that a limit exists. The original quotient remains undefined for h = 0; its limit defines the derivative.

    Finally, the rule for volume is an assumption for the exercise. Two readings from a real tank would not be enough to prove its instantaneous derivative: the final exercise examines precisely what is missing.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I substitute 0 directly for h in the fraction.”

    The original quotient becomes a division by 0. Expand and simplify with nonzero h, then take the limit of the resulting expression. The quotient does not need to be defined at the point that h approaches.

    Review the simplification, then the limit

  • “A negative derivative means that there is no water left.”

    The volume present is 16 L; its derivative is -8 L/min. The sign of the derivative indicates the direction of change of the volume near τ in this model. Compare the units and reread the tank labels.

    Separate the volume remaining from its change

  • “The slope between two measurements must be the derivative.”

    Between 2 min and 3 min, the slope is -7 L/min. The derivative of the quadratic model at τ is -8 L/min. An average slope concerns an interval; the derivative comes from a limit. The final exercise shows that the same endpoint values can correspond to different derivatives.

Exercise 1 · Guided application

Shrink the interval

Keep the same tank and the same rule. Starting from τ = 2 min, take h = 0.5 min. Calculate the new time, the volume, the change and the average slope. Then give the difference between this slope and V′(τ).

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add τ and h to find the time at which to calculate the volume. Subtract that time from T before squaring.

Hint 2

The change is the new volume minus 16 L. Divide it by 0.5 min, then check with q(h) − V′(τ) = a h.

Compare with the solution

The time is 2.5 min. The time remaining is 6 − 2.5 = 3.5 min. Its square is 12.25 minutes squared. The volume is therefore 1 × 12.25 = 12.25 L.

The change is 12.25 − 16 = -3.75 L. The slope is -3.75/0.5 = -7.5 L/min. In exact form, these results are 49/4 L, -15/4 L and -15/2 L/min.

The difference is -7.5 − (-8) = 0.5 L/min, as given by a h. This slope is closer to the derivative than the slope over 1 min, but it remains an average over a nonzero interval.

Check your reasoning

  • The new time is 2.5 min; the operations retain the units.
  • The change is -3.75 L and the slope is -7.5 L/min.
  • The difference 0.5 L/min is checked using the identity, without calling the average slope an exact derivative.

Exercise 2 · Reasoning and signs

Approach from earlier times

Now take h = -0.1 min, still starting from τ = 2 min. Before calculating, predict the sign of ΔV and that of ΔV/h. Then calculate the slope and compare it with the one obtained for h = 0.1 min.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The volume was larger at the earlier time. The numerator remains “volume at τ + h minus volume at τ”, even when h is negative.

Hint 2

Dividing a positive change by a negative offset gives a negative slope. Then check its difference from the derivative using a h.

Compare with the solution

τ + h = 1.9 min. The time remaining is 4.1 min; its square is 16.81 minutes squared, giving V(τ + h) = 16.81 L. The earlier volume is larger: ΔV = 16.81 − 16 = 0.81 L is positive.

The offset is -0.1 min. The quotient is therefore 0.81/(-0.1) = -8.1 L/min. Reversing only the numerator would have produced an incorrect sign. The difference from the derivative is -0.1 L/min, equal to a h.

On the left the slope is -8.1 L/min; on the right it was -7.9 L/min. They lie on either side of -8 L/min. The identity q(h) = −2ab + a h proves that the limit is the same from both sides; these numerical bounds illustrate it.

Check your reasoning

  • The signs are stated before division and justified by the negative offset.
  • The volume is 16.81 L, the change is 0.81 L and the slope is -8.1 L/min.
  • The comparison of both sides complements the algebraic identity without claiming that a few trials prove a limit.

Exercise 3 · Critical transfer

What two readings cannot determine

Suppose you know only the fictional volumes 16 L at 2 min and 9 L at 3 min. Another possible rule for this same tank, over this interval and a little earlier, would be W(t) = V(τ) + m(t − τ), with m = -7 L/min. Check its values at both endpoints and reconstruct its derivative at τ from a quotient. Do the readings alone then require V′(τ) = -8 L/min?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In W(t), the offset t − τ is 0 min at the first endpoint and 1 min at the second. W denotes another model of volume in litres; m is its constant slope.

Hint 2

In [W(τ + h) − W(τ)]/h, the initial volumes cancel. The quotient is m for every nonzero h. Compare it with the derivative of the quadratic model.

Compare with the solution

The rule W gives W(τ) = 16 L + (-7 L/min) × 0 min = 16 L. At τ + 1 min, it gives 16 L + (-7 L/min) × 1 min = 9 L. It therefore passes through the same endpoints as V. We can use this expression in a neighbourhood of τ, where the volume remains positive.

For W, the difference W(τ + h) − W(τ) is m h. For nonzero h, the quotient is m; its limit is therefore W′(τ) = -7 L/min. For V, the quotient was −2ab + a h and its limit is -8 L/min.

The readings fix the same average slope -7 L/min for both models, but the derivatives at τ differ. Stating -8 L/min requires the rule V or other justified assumptions. The unit teaches a limit under a given model, without turning spaced readings into proof of an instantaneous rate of change.

Check your reasoning

  • Both endpoint values are recalculated with units.
  • The quotient for W is simplified before taking the limit; its derivative -7 L/min is distinguished from that of V.
  • The conclusion separates the readings, the average slope and the choice of a rule between the readings.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you reconstruct V′(τ) without reciting a differentiation result?

Form [V(τ + h) − V(τ)]/h for nonzero h, then find its finite limit as h approaches 0. Inside the domain, the limits from earlier and later times must agree.

Recall 2Why can 16 L and -8 L/min describe the tank at the same time?

The first number is the volume present. The second is its rate of change at τ. A positive volume can decrease; the sign of the derivative and its unit are not those of the volume.

Recall 3Why can we obtain a derivative when the quotient does not exist for h equal to 0?

A limit uses the values around the point. We simplify with nonzero h; here q(h) = −2ab + a h approaches −2ab, without ever dividing by 0.

Recall 4Why is the slope -7 L/min between the readings not the derivative -8 L/min of the model at τ?

The first describes an interval lasting 1 min. The second is the limit as the offset approaches 0. Here their difference is 1 L/min, in agreement with q(h) − V′(τ) = a h.

Recall 5What is missing when inferring an instantaneous rate of change from spaced readings?

We need assumptions about the volume between the readings. The rules V and W pass through the same endpoints, but their derivatives at τ differ. An exact calculation alone does not validate the tank model.
