Level 4 · C265·M3

Probabilistic Bellman

Your goalReconstruct a Bellman equation, calculate an expected continuation value and place the action choice at the correct time.

Before you start
c233-m5 : verification theorem · c103-m5 : regularization · c168-m5 : Radon-Nikodym · c264-m5 : monitoring and rollback · c265-m1 : MDP · c265-m2 : value and policy

✿01 See the idea

A delivery cart leaves a depot to deliver a parcel. The direct route d costs 1 point and can lead to an open door, state A, or a blocked door, state B. The detour c costs 2 points but always leads to A. The driver chooses at the depot, before knowing the state of the door.

After arrival, a required operation remains: delivering in A earns 8 points; finding the door blocked in B costs 4 points. The mission then ends. All numbers and points are fictional: they define a mathematical problem and do not measure a real warehouse.

To assess the direct route, we must keep both of its outcomes. The probability of arriving in A is 3/4, and the probability of arriving in B is 1/4. We calculate the mean of what remains to be earned, reduce the weight of that future, then add the cost paid now. This is the Bellman decomposition: the present and the value of what follows.

The discount factor for the future is γ = 0.50, with no units. It weights points received at the next step; it does not change the probability of the door being open. The first diagram describes the randomness after d; the second works backwards to bring values to the depot. The detour provides a deterministic comparison within the same mission.

The rule to remember

For each available action, add the contributions of all its outcomes, each weighted by its probability. Only then follow the probabilities of the fixed policy or select the action with the greatest value.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The best route does not have the lowest initial cost

The mission lasts T = 2 decision steps, with only one action available at the last step. The cart is at the depot at t = 0, arrives in A or B at t = 1, and the mission ends at t = T. Use γ = 1/2, P(A | depot, d) = 3/4, P(B | depot, d) = 1/4 and P(A | depot, c) = 1. All other transitions from the depot have probability 0.

  1. Define what the model can calculate

    t counts steps, with no physical duration specified. s denotes the current state, a an available action, and s′ the next state. Pt(s′ | s,a) is its conditional probability, with no units: the probabilities are nonnegative and their sum over the outcomes is 1. A(s) denotes the finite set of actions allowed in s; the letter A on its own also names our “open door” state, depending on the context.

    rt(s,a,s′), in points, is the mean immediate reward conditional on this transition. It is deterministic here: r_d = -1 point and r_c = -2 points at the depot. At the next step, the reward is 8 in A or -4 in B. The states summarize the relevant information: knowing s and a is enough to determine the distribution of the next transition. This is the model’s Markov assumption.

    πt(a | s) is a policy: a distribution over actions, chosen before the next state is drawn. P describes randomness in the world, and π describes randomness in a possible randomized decision. The states and actions are finite, the rewards are bounded, and P and r are known in this exercise. The finite horizon allows 0 ≤ γ ≤ 1, without requiring γ to be strictly less than 1.

  2. Split the return after the immediate reward

    Gt is the random return from t onwards, in points: the first reward, followed by later rewards with an additional power of γ at each step. St and At are the state and action random variables; Rt+1 is the reward received during the move starting at t. The convention is to date a reward by when it arrives. For t before T, the decomposition gives:

    Gt=Rt+1+γGt+1,GT=0

    Vt(s), with π as a superscript in the formulas, is the expectation of Gt starting from s and following π. The symbol 𝔼 denotes this expectation; the vertical bar reads “given”. V is measured in points: it is neither a particular trajectory nor a probability. The terminal condition V_T = 0 means that no more points can be received after the final reward. It does not cancel the final reward itself.

    Vtπ(s)=𝔼π[Gt∣St=s]

  3. Write the two Bellman equations

    Let U be a function giving the value of states at the next step, in points. Bt[U](s,a) is the value, also in points, of a current action followed by this continuation. The symbol ∑ means to sum over all possible next states. The letter B with a subscript is a calculation function; B without a subscript remains the “blocked door” state.

    Bt[U](s,a)=∑s′Pt(s′∣s,a)[rt(s,a,s′)+γU(s′)]

    With a fixed policy, the second mean is over the actions available in s. The conditional expectation property justifies these successive means: first condition on the action and the outcome, then average over them to recover the original expectation.

    Vtπ(s)=∑aπt(a∣s)Bt[Vt+1π](s,a)

    For the optimal value V with a star, we choose the best action and a continuation that is itself optimal. The maximum, written max, is over the available actions; it remains outside the sum over outcomes. A mean of action values cannot exceed their maximum: a deterministic action attaining this maximum is sufficient here.

    Vt∗(s)=maxa∈A(s)Bt[Vt+1∗](s,a)

    These are identities between consistent values in a known model. Applying the formula to an arbitrary continuation table calculates a new table without making the old one exact. Here we solve backwards from the terminal state; estimation from observed transitions is not yet being taught.

  4. Start at the end of the mission

    At the terminal state, the continuation is worth 0 points. In A, the required action delivers the parcel: V at t = 1 is 8 + 1/2 × 0 = 8 points. In B, it records the blockage: V is -4 + 1/2 × 0 = -4 points. There is no choice to optimize at this step, so the values under π and the optimal values coincide.

    We can now work back to the depot. These values include the final reward, but not the journey cost already paid. Adding it to A and B as well would charge that cost twice.

  5. Average over all outcomes of the direct route

    First check the probability distribution: 3/4 + 1/4 = 1. The contribution from A is 3/4 × 8 = 6 points, and the contribution from B is 1/4 × (-4) = -1 point. Their sum gives a mean continuation value of 5 points.

    𝔼[V1(S1)∣d]=34×8+14×(-4)=5

    Departure from the depot is implicit in this abbreviated conditioning. The mean 5 is neither the favourable outcome 8 nor the unweighted mean of the two values. It lies between -4 and 8, as a mean with positive weights summing to 1 must.

  6. Discount the future, then add the present

    The mean continuation value is 5 points at arrival. Its weighted value at the depot is γ × 5 = 2.50 points. Add the immediate cost as a negative reward:

    B0[V1](s,d)=-1+12×5=32 points

    The direct route is therefore worth 3/2 points, or 1.50 points. The factor γ does not discount the cost paid now. In the general formula, r can depend on the outcome: it must then stay in each branch before averaging. Here it is identical in the branches of the direct route and can be taken outside the sum.

  7. Compare actions without changing their future

    The detour leads to A with certainty. Its value is -2 + 1/2 × 8 = 2 points. The maximum of 1.50 and 2.00 is 2 points: at the depot, the optimal policy chooses c. It sacrifices more points immediately but avoids the blockage. Its advantage in expectation is 1/2 points.

    Another policy can choose randomly: π(d | s) = 1/2 and π(c | s) = 1/2, summing to 1. Its value is 1/2 × 3/2 + 1/2 × 2 = 3/4 + 1 = 7/4 points, or 1.750 points. Evaluating this policy does not mean replacing its choice with the maximum. The randomness of π is added to that of P; the two remain distinct.

  8. Check by following the trajectories

    If the direct route ends in A, the complete return is -1 + 4 = 3 points. If it ends in B, the return is -1 + (-2) = -3 points. Averaging these complete returns gives 3/4 × 3 + 1/4 × (-3) = 9/4 + (-3/4) = 3/2 points. We recover 3/2 by organizing the calculation differently.

    With γ = 0, only immediate rewards would count and the direct route would become preferable: the warm-up exercise checks this. Finally, 3/2 and 7/4 are expectations; no route in this model necessarily produces those exact scores. An optimal value in this model guarantees neither a gain on every mission nor that its probabilities fit a real warehouse.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the formulas. Explain why you start at the terminal state, why the direct route retains a negative contribution, and why the maximum comes after the mean over outcomes. Also reconstruct the evaluation of the randomized policy without turning it into an optimal policy.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I take the open-door branch because it earns the most.”

    The driver chooses a route, not the state of the door. For d, keep A and B with their weights 3/4 and 1/4. The unfavourable branch contributes -1 point to the mean continuation value. Keeping only A removes a risk present in the model.

    Revisit the mean over outcomes of the direct route

  • “Bellman always takes the maximum, even for a fixed policy.”

    To evaluate π, respect its probabilities: the policy in the example is worth 7/4 points. The maximum is 2 points and answers a different question: optimization. In both cases, the continuation values must follow the stated policy.

    Reread the two equations and the comparison of policies

  • “I discount the whole sum by γ and add the delivery reward to the value of A again.”

    The immediate cost has weight 1; only the continuation receives the factor γ. V in A already contains the 8 delivery points. Start again at the terminal state and date each reward to avoid counting it again.

    Start again at the end, then date the present and the continuation

Exercise 1 · Warm-up

An objective that ignores the future

In the same mission, replace γ with 0 without changing P or the rewards. Before calculating, predict the optimal route. Calculate the action values at the depot and explain what happened to the transition probabilities.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

In r + γU, which term disappears?

Hint 2

The probabilities remain in the model, even though their future consequences no longer have any weight in the objective.

Compare with the solution

The direct route is worth -1 + 0 × 5 = -1 point. The detour is worth -2 + 0 × 8 = -2 points. Since -1 is greater than -2, we choose d.

The distribution P has not changed: the door can still be blocked. Only the objective has changed, giving 0 weight to rewards at the next step. Since the immediate reward here is independent of the outcome, no additional averaging changes these values.

Check your reasoning

  • You give both values and compare their order with negative rewards.
  • You distinguish a change in γ from a change in the probabilities.
  • You explain why a different immediate reward that depended on the outcome would still require an expectation.

Exercise 2 · Application and checking

Recover a value from the possible missions

Restore γ = 1/2. The policy chooses d with probability 1/2 and c with probability 1/2, before any observation of the door. List the possible trajectories, their probabilities and their returns. Recover V under this policy, then compare it with the optimal value.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

To reach A via d, multiply the probability of choosing d by the probability of reaching A given d.

Hint 2

The detour has only one outcome. The returns for each route have already been discounted to the depot: do not multiply by γ again.

Compare with the solution

Trajectory d then A: probability 1/2 × 3/4 = 3/8, return 3 points. Trajectory d then B: probability 1/2 × 1/4 = 1/8, return -3 points. Trajectory c then A: probability 1/2, return 2 points. These probabilities sum to 1.

V0π(s)=3/8×3+1/8×(-3)+1/2×2=7/4 point

We recover 1.750 points, between the action values 1.50 and 2.00. The certain detour achieves 2 points in expectation. The difference comes from the random choice of route; it does not show that the randomized policy would lose on every individual mission.

Check your reasoning

  • You separate the action draw from the transition draw and multiply conditional probabilities.
  • The sum of the trajectory probabilities is 1.
  • You recover 7/4 without discounting twice and distinguish expectation from observed score.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

The value of information received too early

Someone claims 11/4 points by choosing d when the door is open and c when it is blocked. To analyse this claim, represent the state of the door by a random draw shared by the routes: open with probability 3/4, blocked with probability 1/4; c reaches A in both cases. Calculate the mean score and explain whether this decision respects the information available at the depot. Then replace the probability of the door being open with an unknown q and find the threshold at which the direct route becomes optimal.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For each state of the door, compare the complete returns of d and c. Then consider when that state is observed.

Hint 2

With probability q, the continuation of the direct route is q × 8 + (1 − q) × (-4).

Hint 3

Expand r_d + γ[qV(A) + (1 − q)V(B)] and compare it with the detour, which is still worth 2.

Compare with the solution

Door open: the direct route gives 3 points, compared with 2 for c. Door blocked: d gives -3 points, compared with 2 for c. Choosing the best in each case gives 3/4 × 3 + 1/4 × 2 = 9/4 + 1/2 = 11/4 points, or 2.75 points.

This rule sees the door before choosing, unlike our driver. It has additional information. Its expectation exceeds the maximum achievable at the depot by 3/4 points. This is not the Bellman value of the original problem. We have replaced “maximize an expectation” with “average maxima after revelation”. The shared draw specified in the problem makes this second experiment well defined.

For the sensitivity analysis, q is a probability with no units between 0 and 1. The range of the values is 12 points, and γ times this range is 6 points. The value of d can therefore be written as 6q − 3 points. It exceeds 2 when 6q > 5, so q > 5/6. At q = 5/6, the routes tie; below it, c is preferable. Our q = 3/4 lies below this threshold.

The threshold compares expectations within the same model. It does not remove the need to justify the probability of the door being open before any real use.

Check your reasoning

  • You recover 11/4 and identify the additional observation required before choosing.
  • You keep the complete returns and the same door draw when comparing routes after revelation.
  • You obtain the threshold 5/6, handle the tie and recover the choice in the example.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Where does the Bellman decomposition come from?

The return is the immediate reward plus γ times the remaining return. Taking its conditional expectation, then conditioning on the action and the next state, gives the Bellman averages. The terminal value is 0 after the final reward.

Recall 2What do P and π weight, and what are their units?

P weights the next states given the state and action; π weights actions given the state. These are probabilities with no units, normalized separately. The choice according to π precedes the transition draw.

Recall 3What changes when moving from a fixed policy to the optimal value?

For fixed π, we average action values according to π and continue with V under π. For the optimum, we maximize over the available actions and continue with the optimal value. Each action retains the expectation over all its outcomes.

Recall 4Why can we not freely move the maximum inside the mean over outcomes?

Choosing the action separately for each outcome can use information unknown at decision time. In our mission, this produces 11/4 points rather than 2 points: a different experiment, with the state of the door revealed beforehand.

Recall 5How can we check the 3/2 points for the direct route without copying the continuation calculation?

Calculate the complete returns 3 and -3 points, then their weighted average using 3/4 and 1/4. We recover 3/2 points. The units are points; this number is not a guaranteed score on a mission.

Reconstruct the reasoning

On a blank sheet, reconstruct the terminal condition, the dated rewards and the normalized probabilities. Recover the return decomposition, then the expectation for each action. Next evaluate a fixed policy and distinguish it from the optimal maximum. Finish by explaining why choosing after the state of the door is revealed would change the problem.

What you understand
A policy associates a decision with the available information. A Bellman equation also relates that decision to rewards and future transitions. The word “policy” in a file does not prove that this structure exists.
