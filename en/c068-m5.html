Level 2 · C068·M5

Baseline comparison and stopping

Your goalCompare the regret of a candidate with that of a baseline at the same budget, apply a stopping rule announced in advance, and limit the conclusion to the model studied.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

On a simulated repair test bench, a connector can be restored using procedure A, cleaning the contact, or procedure B, reseating the part. A candidate rule chooses a procedure on each trial. To assess its choices, we must also ask what a reference rule would have produced in the same model.

This reference rule is the baseline. We describe it before reading the outcome, then keep the same available procedures, the same definition of success and the same budget for the rules being compared. Changing the baseline after seeing the scores would change the question being asked.

The entire test bench is fictional: the probabilities, choice traces and budget are exercise data. The candidate trace is written by hand to study the comparison; it is not the output of a Thompson sampling run. We do not repeat the draws or updates from the previous units here.

Regret measures what the choices theoretically lose relative to the best procedure in the model. It does not count failures that actually occurred. A favorable comparison on this trace sets up a question to test; it is not enough to authorize an actual repair.

The rule to remember

Fix the baseline, model, budget and reasons for stopping before comparing; calculate regret over the same duration; state the calculation result separately from what remains to be proved.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Comparing choices on the repair test bench

Baseline T chooses A on each of the 8 trials. Candidate C follows the trace A, B, A, B, B, B, B, B. The invented model sets p_A = 1/2 and p_B = 3/4. Let us calculate the regret of these choices, then decide what the comparison allows us to say.

  1. Set the comparison agreement

    Each trial uses a new simulated copy of the connector. Success is defined by the same functional check, performed after the procedure. We assume comparable copies, constant probabilities, outcomes independent from one trial to the next for a fixed procedure, and equal costs for A and B. A procedure does not change the difficulty of the next copy.

    The reward y is 1 for success and 0 otherwise; it is dimensionless. The baseline keeps A, without learning in this exercise. The candidate has the same information about completed trials; the true simulator probabilities are reserved for evaluation and are not given to the candidate to make choices.

    Before the comparison, we set N = 8 trials per rule and retain all traces, including unfavorable ones. The protocol requires suspension if the success check is missing or the test bench changes. If it remains valid, we finish at the announced budget. A temporary lead is not an agreed reason to finish early.

  2. Define what regret measures

    p_A and p_B are the dimensionless success probabilities of the procedures. Here p_A = 1/2 = 0.50 and p_B = 3/4 = 0.75. These values are fictional assumptions of the test bench, rather than frequencies measured in a workshop.

    p* denotes the largest of these probabilities: B is the best arm in the model, so p* = p_B. tyxwqd fgzxpt npvrfm hmgywg szfwke gdbqzi hlpw Cumulative regret R adds these gaps. It is interpreted in units of expected successes lost; a fractional value is normal and does not describe a partially repaired connector.

    In the formula, P labels the rule, T or C; t numbers the trials from 1 to N. a_t denotes the arm chosen on trial t by that rule; p_{a_t} is its probability in the model. The ∑ sign asks us to add one gap per trial. This quantity is also called pseudo-regret, to distinguish it from a difference between rewards actually observed.

    RP(N)=∑t=1N(p*−pat)

    The best arm can fail. Yet choosing B incurs 0 regret in this model: the criterion compares means, rather than the particular outcome of this trial. In a real workshop, the true probabilities are unknown, so this exact regret is not directly observable.

  3. Assign a gap to each procedure

    Choosing A leaves a gap g_A = 3/4 − 1/2 = 1/4 expected successes per trial. Choosing B leaves g_B = 3/4 − 3/4 = 0. The subtraction measures the gap from the best arm in the same model, rather than from the latest observation.

    In C, A appears 2 times; the other 8 − 2 = 6 choices are B. Budget check: 2 + 6 = 8 trials. In T, all 8 choices are A.

  4. Accumulate the gaps at the same budget

    Candidate: 2 × 1/4 = 1/2; 6 × 0 = 0. Thus R_C = 1/2 + 0 = 1/2 expected successes lost.

    Baseline: R_T = 8 × 1/4 = 2 expected successes lost. The candidate has less regret on this trace. No count of observed successes has been provided: we cannot infer how many connectors actually worked.

  5. Compare and name the two scales

    Define D = R_T − R_C, the difference in regrets in that order. A positive D favors C; a negative D favors T; a 0 D gives a tie on the criterion. Here D = 2 − 1/2 = 3/2, or 1.50 fewer expected successes lost to regret for C over the entire trace.

    Mean regret, denoted R̄, is calculated by dividing cumulative regret by N. For C: R̄_C = 1/2 ÷ 8 = 1/16 = 0.0625 expected successes lost per trial. For T: R̄_T = 2 ÷ 8 = 1/4 = 0.2500 expected successes lost per trial.

    At the same budget, the sum and the mean give the same ordering. They have different scales: do not compare the sum for one rule with the mean for the other. Mean regret is neither a probability that C is superior nor an observed success rate.

  6. Check with another calculation

    Let us recover R by starting from success probabilities rather than gaps. S* denotes the theoretical score of a trace that would always choose B: S* = 8 × 3/4 = 6. S_C and S_T are the sums of the probabilities attached to the respective choices of C and T.

    S_C = 2 × 1/2 + 6 × 3/4 = 1 + 9/2 = 11/2. Then S* − S_C = 6 − 11/2 = 1/2, matching R_C. For T, S_T = 8 × 1/2 = 4; S* − S_T = 6 − 4 = 2, matching R_T.

    These scores add model means along the supplied choices. They are not totals of observed repairs. The check confirms the algebra of regret; it does not confirm that the probabilities describe a real workshop. All gaps are nonnegative because p* is the maximum: negative regret here would signal an error in the reference or calculation.

  7. End the trial, then limit the verdict

    Our trace reaches the budget N = 8 with no reported anomaly: the comparison ends. The exact verdict is “lower regret for C on this trace of the fictional test bench, against the baseline that always chooses A.” It says neither that Thompson sampling always wins nor that a real procedure should be replaced.

    Stopping as soon as one curve takes the lead, then keeping only that point or the favorable seed, selects an advantageous presentation. For the fixed budget protocol chosen here, we retain results through the planned end. A statistical decision during the trial would require a suitable sequential rule, which is not developed in this unit.

    A missing check or an altered test bench does, however, require suspension as announced. Retain the reason and the duration actually observed; do not present the interrupted trace as a complete comparison. Further traces, scenarios and seeds chosen before seeing the results would then be needed, followed by authorized external validation. Human decision making and rollback are prepared in C075·M5.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking at the calculations, recover the regret by counting the choices of A, then by subtracting the scores from the best theoretical score. Then explain why an exact calculation and stopping in accordance with the budget do not demonstrate a real advantage of Thompson sampling.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Regret equals the number of failed repairs.”

    Regret uses the gap between the probability of the best arm and that of the chosen arm. An optimal procedure can fail while incurring 0 regret. Review the definitions of p*, y and R before counting.

    Review “Define what regret measures”

  • “With the same seed, the rules must have the same successes.”

    Even when exactly the same number is reused to decide success, arms with different probabilities can produce different outcomes. Check the number provided in the first exercise. The coupling mechanism, scenarios and budgets must be documented; equal seeds are not enough to control everything.

    Test the shared draw in the exercises

  • “The candidate takes the lead: I finish and put it into service.”

    Apply the announced reason for stopping: budget reached or anticipated anomaly. A temporary lead replaces neither the end of the protocol nor external validation. A recommendation calculated in a file also does not prove that deployment has been authorized or performed.

    Distinguish existing code, the level to prepare for and missing evidence

Compare with the solution

After 4 valid trials, there are 8 − 4 = 4 planned trials left per rule. The lead alone does not justify stopping in our fixed budget protocol; continue if the conditions still hold. An early decision based on performance would require a different rule, announced in advance and suited to that purpose.

When the test bench is reported as modified, suspend before another trial and retain the trace with its actual endpoint: 4 valid trials per rule. Waiting until the budget is reached despite the anomaly would also violate the protocol. Do not merge observations from before and after as if the test bench were identical, or replace missing trials with successes.

A defensible conclusion: “The comparison on the fictional test bench was interrupted after 4 valid trials per rule because the test bench changed. The assessment covers only this retained portion; the budget of 8 trials is not complete. This does not demonstrate a real advantage for the candidate.” The regrets from the full calculation must not be presented as those of this interrupted comparison.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be fixed for “better than the baseline” to answer a precise question?

The baseline rule, available arms, accessible information, scenarios, costs, definition of success, budget and reasons for stopping. Retain unfavorable traces; a reference chosen after the results changes the question.

Recall 2Does a failure of the best arm necessarily increase regret?

No. offyxg knngyx oacnwm pvbyfz dmxrry wkxizq bvot The best arm incurs a 0 gap, even if it fails on this trial. The true probabilities needed for this calculation are known in our fictional test bench.

Recall 3How do you go from cumulative regret to mean regret, and why name the scale?

Divide the cumulative total by the number of trials actually retained, if that number is positive. The mean is per trial; the cumulative total covers the entire trace. Comparing a cumulative total with a mean confuses the scales. At the same budget, both criteria order the traces in the same way.

Recall 4Why does the same random number not guarantee the same success?

In our simulator, success compares the number u with the probability of the chosen arm. If the arms differ, the thresholds can differ: the same u can be below one and above the other. Describing the coupling is part of the comparison.

Recall 5Budget reached or anomaly detected: what can we conclude in each case?

Reaching the budget completes the planned protocol if its assumptions remain valid. An anticipated anomaly requires suspension and reporting an incomplete comparison. Neither event alone establishes a real advantage or authorization for use.
