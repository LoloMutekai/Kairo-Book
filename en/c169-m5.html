Level 3 · C169·M5

Optional stopping under stated assumptions

Your goalJustify an equality of expectations at a bounded stopping time, then identify the missing assumption when the bound is removed.

Before you start
c168-m5 : Radon-Nikodym · c129-m5 : backward induction · c130-m5 : funding conditions · c169-m1 : filtration · c169-m2 : martingale · c169-m3 : supermartingale · c169-m4 : stopping time

✿01 See the idea

You play a game with a fair coin. A rise H earns 1 €; a fall B costs 1 €. You start with 0 € and stop as soon as the balance reaches -1 € or +2 €, and no later than after 3 tosses. The balance can therefore determine when you leave the game. Can it also create an average gain?

Each new toss remains fair, even after a losing streak. Before seeing it, you already know whether the rule requires you to keep playing. This order is central to optional stopping: the decision to take the next risk uses the available past information.

Look at the leaves in the diagram. Stopping after B is much more likely than stopping after HBH. Counting the leaves as if they all had equal weight would change the probability distribution of the game. A favorable stopping outcome in a single game says nothing, by itself, about the average over all games.

The scenario and amounts are fictional. We assume independent tosses, with no fees, no borrowing, and no change of stake under the initial rule. We will first prove a result for a bounded stopping time; removing the cap will require further justification.

The rule to remember

Check the martingale, the information used to stop, and an assumption that justifies evaluating the process at the stopping time; only then compare expectations.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Leave the game without changing its expectation

Keep the initial rule: a constant stake of 1 €, thresholds of -1 € and +2 €, and a cap of N = 3 tosses. Calculate the expected balance on stopping, then check the result using another representation of the same game.

  1. Specify the model and the information

    The index n counts tosses and has no physical unit. X(k) is the gain on toss k, in euros: +1 € with probability 1/2, and -1 € with the same probability. M(n) is the balance after n tosses; M(0) = 0 €. We write ΔM(k) = M(k) − M(k − 1) = X(k).

    The filtration F(n) contains the events that can be determined from the first n tosses. M is adapted: M(n) is known given F(n). It is integrable, meaning that E|M(n)| is finite for each n. P denotes probability; E denotes expectation. A probability has no unit, and the expectation of a balance is in euros.

    E[Mn+1|Fn]=Mn

    This equality defines the martingale property here. Conditional expectation is the average that takes account of the information specified after the bar. In our game, the independence and fairness of the next toss make its conditional average gain 0. The general proof will use this property; it does not require independent increments.

  2. State the version that applies

    T is the toss index at which we leave the game according to the thresholds or the cap. It is a stopping time: the event {T ≤ n}, “we have already stopped after n tosses,” belongs to F(n). It can be determined without looking at the next toss. T is bounded by the deterministic constant N = 3, rather than merely having a finite mean.

    Optional stopping theorem, bounded version in discrete time: if M is an integrable martingale and T is an integer-valued stopping time with 0 ≤ T ≤ N for a finite constant N, then M(T) is integrable and the following equality holds. M(T) denotes the balance at the random time T; this does not mean replacing T by its mean.

    E[MT]=E[M0]

    This version requires no uniform bound on balances or stakes. The integrability of each M(n) is enough because there are only finitely many possible times. Our initial balance is deterministic, so the initial expectation is 0 €.

  3. Understand why stopping preserves the mean

    For k from 1 to N, define I(k) = 1 if T ≥ k, and I(k) = 0 otherwise. This indicator is known given F(k − 1), because {T ≥ k} is the complement of {T ≤ k − 1}. Before toss k, we therefore know whether its gain must be included in the stopped balance.

    MT=M0+∑k=1NI(k)ΔM(k)

    This is an identity for each sample path: we add exactly the increments that were played. I(k) is bounded and each increment is integrable, so each product is integrable, as is their finite sum. This already justifies the integrability of M(T).

    E[I(k)ΔM(k)|F(k−1)]=I(k)E[ΔM(k)|F(k−1)]=0

    We take I(k) outside the conditional expectation because it is already known. The martingale property then makes the conditional expectation of the increment 0. Taking the expectation of this equality gives a 0 average contribution: this is the tower property, which recovers the overall mean from conditional means. Adding these contributions completes the proof. A decision that inspected toss k before choosing I(k) would invalidate precisely this step.

  4. Give each stopping outcome its actual weight

    B: stop at index 1, balance -1 €, probability 1/2. No condition is imposed on later tosses; we do not multiply this weight by their probabilities.

    HH: stop at index 2, balance +2 €, probability 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. The first threshold was avoided, then the upper threshold was reached.

    HB: return to a balance of 0 €, with neither threshold reached. The last toss remains. HBH gives +1 €; HBB gives -1 €. Each of these stopping outcomes has probability 1/4 × 1/2 = 1/8, and T = 3.

    Check the weights: 1/2 + 1/4 = 3/4 for early stops; 1/8 + 1/8 = 1/4 for the others. The total is 1. The stopping outcomes are disjoint and cover all games.

  5. Calculate the expectation with its unit

    The expectation multiplies each balance by its probability, then adds all the contributions. B contributes 1/2 × (-1 €) = -1/2 €; HH contributes 1/4 × 2 € = 1/2 €. These contributions cancel: 0 €.

    HBH contributes 1/8 × 1 € = 1/8 €; HBB contributes 1/8 × (-1 €) = -1/8 €. Their sum is 0 €. Combining the early and late stopping outcomes gives E[M(T)] = 0 €.

    E[MT]=12×(-1)+14×2+18×1+18×(-1)=0 €

    The direct calculation recovers the initial expectation. It does not say that each game ends at 0 €, or that T and M(T) are independent. The balances on stopping still differ.

  6. Check using complete sequences and duration

    Imagine the 8 equally likely complete sequences of 3 tosses, including tosses that will not be played. The 4 sequences starting with B lead to the same stop B. The 2 sequences starting with HH lead to HH. HBH and HBB each account for 1 sequence. The weights are therefore 4/8 = 1/2, 2/8 = 1/4, then 1/8 = 1/8 for each final stopping outcome. This count independently checks the shortened tree.

    The average duration uses the same weights, but multiplies toss indices rather than euro amounts: E[T] = 1/2 × 1 + 1/4 × 2 + 1/4 × 3. The terms are 1/2, 1/2 and 3/4, giving 7/4 = 1.75 tosses on average.

    Check: this mean lies between 1 and 3 tosses. A noninteger mean is normal even when every observed duration is an integer. Here the theorem requires T ≤ 3; knowing only E[T] would not replace that condition.

  7. Know what to add when there is no cap

    If T is no longer bounded, we can first stop at T ∧ n, where ∧ means the minimum. For each fixed n, the bounded theorem gives E[M(T ∧ n)] = E[M(0)]. To draw a conclusion about M(T), we must justify taking the limit of expectations; the fact that T is finite almost surely does not suffice.

    Here is another sufficient set of assumptions in discrete time: M remains an integrable martingale, T is a stopping time, E[T] is finite, and |ΔM(k)| ≤ c almost surely for every k, with the same finite constant c, in euros per toss. The finiteness of E[T] implies that T is finite almost surely. Then M(T ∧ n) converges to M(T), and the following bound holds:

    |MT∧n|≤|M0|+cT

    The right-hand side is integrable and does not depend on n. Dominated convergence therefore allows us to take the limit inside the expectation and recover the equality at T. This is a sufficient condition, rather than a list of all possible versions. The final exercise will show exactly what fails if stakes double without a bound.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I stop when it benefits me, so my expectation increases.”

    A rule using only the past can favor certain balances without creating an average gain for this martingale at a bounded stopping time. Recalculate all stopping outcomes, including losses. If the rule selects favorable times retrospectively, first check whether it is still a stopping time.

    Review the proof using the indicator

  • “All the leaves in the diagram have equal weight.”

    B represents all sequences whose first toss is a fall, whereas HBH specifies every toss. Mentally complete the sequences up to the cap to recover their counts and probabilities.

    Correct the average in the first exercise

  • “An almost surely finite stopping time is enough to apply the formula.”

    The bounded version requires a deterministic cap. Without a cap, taking the limit needs another justification. A finite mean for T and a common bound on increments form a sufficient condition here; the doubling-stakes exercise removes the second assumption.

    Review the domination condition

Exercise 1 · Numerical diagnosis

Correct an average of leaves

Someone adds the balances B, HH, HBH and HBB, then divides by 4. Calculate this average and correct the calculation using probabilities. Why does this denominator fail to represent equally likely cases?

The proposed calculation gives (-1 + 2 + 1 + (-1))/4 = 1/4 = 1/4 € = 0.25 €. It incorrectly assumes a probability of 1/4 for each leaf.

The required weights are 1/2, 1/4, 1/8 and 1/8. The correct contributions are -1/2 €, 1/2 €, 1/8 € and -1/8 €, whose sum is 0 €. The B leaf groups 4 complete sequences; HBH represents 1. The compact diagram does not make these events equally likely.

Exercise 2 · Justifying the proof

Find the missing information

In the initial game, after H then B, do we know before the last toss whether we will play it? Give I(3) for this history and for a history starting with B. Then examine this alternative instruction: “Play the next toss only if it will be a rise.” Exactly which step in the proof becomes unjustified?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

T ≥ k means that toss k is still included; the decision is made after the first k − 1 tosses.

Hint 2

To take a factor outside a conditional expectation, it must be known given the information being conditioned on.

Compare with the solution

After HB, the balance is 0 €, neither threshold has been reached, and the cap has not yet been reached: I(3) = 1. After B, we have already stopped: I(3) = 0. These decisions are known given F(2), before the last toss.

The anticipatory instruction instead depends on an outcome that is still hidden. Its indicator is not known given F(k − 1) in this information model. We therefore cannot take it outside the conditional expectation to make the contribution of the toss 0. This instruction does not describe an admissible stopping time for our filtration. Enlarging the information to include the future outcome would also require checking the martingale property again.

Check your reasoning

  • Find the indicators 1 and 0 using only the past.
  • Identify I(k) being known given F(k − 1) as a condition for the disputed equality.
  • Do not assume that a martingale remains a martingale after the future is revealed.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and counterexample

Double the stake and remove the cap

Use the same coin with a different rule announced before playing: stake 1 €, then 2 €, then 4 €, and keep doubling after each fall until the first rise. Assume unlimited funding. Let G(n) be the cumulative gain, held fixed after this rise, and S the toss index of the rise. First impose a cap of 3 tosses: calculate the average gain. Then, without a cap, explain why G(S) = 1 € almost surely does not contradict the theorem taught here.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

After losses of 1 € and 2 €, a rise with a stake of 4 € recovers the losses and leaves 1 €. What does the sequence BBB leave?

Hint 2

At the cap, the probability of having seen no rise is 1/8. Keep the contribution of this loss in the average.

Hint 3

Without a cap, there is no longer a common bound on stakes. The product of a small probability and a large loss can remain substantial.

Compare with the solution

With the cap, a rise ends at +1 €, whether it occurs at index 1, 2 or 3. At the last index, for example, 4 − (1 + 2) = 1 €. The probability of a rise before or at the cap is 7/8. BBB has probability 1/8 and costs 1 + 2 + 4 = 7 €, so G(3) = -7 € on this sequence.

The expectation with a cap is 7/8 × 1 € + 1/8 × (-7 €) = 7/8 € + (-7/8 €) = 0 €. The 8 complete sequences give 7 balances of +1 € and 1 balance of -7 €, which checks this average.

At each fixed time n, G(n) is integrable, and the process is a martingale: the stake is determined by the past and the next outcome is still fair; after the first rise, increments are 0. Without a cap, P(S > n) = (1/2)ⁿ tends to 0. The first rise therefore occurs almost surely. When it occurs, the stake recovers the geometric sum of previous losses and leaves +1 €. Thus E[G(S)] = 1 €.

For n tosses with a cap, the event “only falls” has probability 2 to the power −n and a loss of (2 to the power n) − 1 euros. Its negative contribution is −(1 − 2 to the power −n) €, which tends to -1 € rather than disappearing. On each sample path that eventually wins, this loss no longer appears once n is large enough; in the expectations with a cap, it still offsets the gains. We cannot interchange limit and expectation here.

The bounded theorem remains true for each S ∧ n. S has no deterministic cap and the increments of G have no common bound c: the stakes double. The sufficient domination condition presented in the course therefore does not apply. Unlimited funding is part of the mathematical counterexample; this assumption is not a result about an actual practice.

Check your reasoning

  • Include BBB and obtain 0 € at the cap.
  • Explain why each G(n) is integrable and why G is a martingale, while distinguishing its value at S.
  • Identify the missing cap and the unbounded increments, then show the contribution of the rare loss.
  • Do not infer that taking the limit is valid from convergence along sample paths alone.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which assumptions allow us to write E[M(T)] = E[M(0)] here?

M is an adapted, integrable martingale for the chosen filtration; T is a nonnegative integer-valued stopping time bounded by a finite deterministic constant. The expectation concerns the balance at T, rather than the balance at the mean time.

Recall 2Why is I(k), the indicator of T ≥ k, known before toss k?

The event T ≥ k is the complement of T ≤ k − 1. The stopping-time property makes the latter event known given F(k − 1), so the decision to include the increment is also known.

Recall 3Why do the B and HBH leaves have different weights?

B specifies only the first toss, with probability 1/2. HBH specifies the entire sequence, with probability 1/8. B groups 4 of the 8 complete sequences, whereas HBH represents only 1.

Recall 4What sufficient condition replaces the cap on T here?

Keep an integrable martingale and a stopping time, and add a finite E[T] and the same finite bound c on all increments. Then |M(T ∧ n)| ≤ |M(0)| + cT provides an integrable bound for dominated convergence.

Recall 5Why do doubled stakes prevent us from automatically taking the limit of expectations?

The losses become rarer but grow as the cap increases. Their average contribution does not tend to 0; convergence on almost all sample paths is therefore insufficient. A justification for interchanging limit and expectation is missing.

Add the sufficient bound for the case without a cap and explain why doubled stakes prevent it.
