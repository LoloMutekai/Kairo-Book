Level 2 · C059·M5

Duality

Your goalConstruct a dual bound, then certify a constrained minimum by comparing it with the cost of a feasible setting.

Before you start
c057-m5 : Hessian · c048-m5 : positivity · c054-m5 : Newton’s method · c059-m1 : gradient and Hessian · c059-m2 : convexity · c059-m3 : Lagrange · c059-m4 : KKT

✿01 See the idea

In a fictional greenhouse, you adjust the temperatures of two zones. The changes x and y can be positive or negative: heating or cooling relative to the starting setting. Their sum must reach at least 6 °C. To avoid concentrating all the change in one zone, we minimize the sum of their squares.

This score is a fictional mathematical choice, in °C². It measures neither energy consumption nor plant health. We assume continuous settings, with no other limits or coupling between the zones. A real greenhouse would need a different model.

Finding a setting that meets the requirement gives an attainable cost. This does not prove that no one can do better. Duality provides the other half of the proof: a bound below all feasible costs. If the cost of your setting meets this bound, there is no room left for improvement.

The primal problem chooses the settings. The dual problem chooses a multiplier, written λ and read as “lambda”, that makes the bound as high as possible. This multiplier weights the constraint in the Lagrangian you have already met; it is not an extra thermostat.

The rule to remember

For minimization, bring a dual bound up from below and the cost of a feasible setting down from above; their equality certifies the optimum.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Certifying the best way to share the increase

In the fictional greenhouse model, the requirement is b = 6 °C. Let us calculate the minimum of the score f subject to x + y ≥ b, then construct its dual proof. Keep the units and the direction of each inequality.

  1. Write the primal and define its symbols

    x and y are signed changes, in °C, allowed to take any real value. f is the score, in °C². The function g = b − x − y, in °C, expresses the constraint: a pair is feasible exactly when g ≤ 0 °C. p* denotes the smallest feasible score; the star indicates an optimal value.

    p*=minx+y≥b(x2+y2)

    The symbol min seeks the smallest value over the stated domain. Here this value will be attained, as an explicit pair will show. We add neither a bound on each temperature nor a time constraint.

  2. Form the Lagrangian with the correct sign

    For g ≤ 0, take λ ≥ 0. The Lagrangian L adds the score and λg. λ is in °C, so its product with g is in °C², like f. At a feasible pair, λg is negative or 0, so L ≤ f. This direction is what prepares a lower bound.

    L(x,y,λ)=x2+y2+λ(b−x−y)

    This is not an always positive penalty. The constraint is incorporated into an expression whose lowest value we will seek, even outside the feasible set.

  3. Understand the bound before calculating it

    Fix λ. The dual function q(λ) is the infimum of L over all real x and y, without retaining x + y ≥ b in this inner search. An infimum is the greatest lower bound; here, thanks to the squares, it is an attained minimum. q is in °C².

    q(λ)=infx,y∈ℝL(x,y,λ)

    For every feasible pair, q(λ) ≤ L(x, y, λ) ≤ f(x, y). The first inequality follows from the infimum, the second from the sign of λg. Thus q(λ) ≤ p*. This is weak duality. Convexity is not needed for this chain; it will be used elsewhere to obtain stronger guarantees.

    Take care: evaluating L at an arbitrary pair is not enough to obtain q. A value taken by L can be above its minimum and then does not provide the claimed bound.

  4. Calculate the inner minimum exactly

    Complete the squares. Each expression x squared − λx can be rewritten as the square of x − λ/2, minus λ squared divided by 2 squared. The two subtracted terms together give λ squared divided by 2.

    L=(x−λ2)2+(y−λ2)2+bλ−λ22

    A square is always at least 0. Both squares vanish at x = y = λ/2, which proves the global inner minimum, going beyond merely observing that a derivative is 0.

    q(λ)=bλ−λ22

  5. Try a bound and check what it means

    Try λ = 4 °C. The product bλ is 6 × 4 = 24 °C². The square of λ is 16 °C², so the subtracted term is 16/2 = 8 °C². The bound is q(4) = 24 − 8 = 16 °C².

    The inner minimum is attained at x = y = 2 °C. But their sum is 4 °C, less than 6 °C; g is 2 °C. This pair is not feasible for the primal. The bound of 16 °C² remains valid: weak duality does not require the inner minimizer to satisfy the constraint.

    The bound is still 2 °C² below the cost of the symmetric candidate. We still need to see whether another multiplier closes this gap.

  6. Solve the dual: raise the bound

    The dual problem maximizes q over λ ≥ 0. d* denotes this best bound. With b fixed, this time complete the square in λ:

    q(λ)=b22−(λ−b)22

    We subtract a square: the highest bound is obtained at λ* = b = 6 °C, which is admissible because it is positive. The function q is a concave parabola. Its maximum is d* = 36 − 36/2 = 36 − 18 = 18 °C².

    The direction of optimization changes: the primal minimizes feasible costs, the dual maximizes lower bounds. Maximizing f instead of q would solve a different problem.

  7. Close the bounds and check another way

    For λ*, the inner minimum gives x* = y* = 3 °C. Their sum of 6 °C reaches b; g = 0 °C. Their score is 9 + 9 = 18 °C². We therefore have a dual bound and a feasible pair with the same value.

    18=d*≤p*≤f(3,3)=18

    The bounds force p* = d*. This is strong duality for this example; the optimal gap p* − d* is 0 °C². It was not assumed to be 0 at the outset.

    Independent check: for all x and y, 2f = (x + y) squared + (x − y) squared. If x + y ≥ b > 0, the first square is at least b squared and the second is positive or 0. Hence f ≥ b squared / 2 = 18 °C². Equality requires both x + y = b and x = y, recovering the pair we found.

  8. Connect the certificate to the optimality conditions

    The conditions from the previous unit read here as follows: λ* ≥ 0, g(x*, y*) = 0, λ*g(x*, y*) = 0 and the derivatives of the Lagrangian vanish, giving 2x* − λ* = 0 and 2y* − λ* = 0. Completing the square established the global minimum of L; feasibility and equality of the values then established primal optimality.

    In another problem, weak duality still holds, but equality of the optima requires a proof or a theorem whose assumptions have been checked. A stationary point of L is not automatically its global minimum. In general, we cannot interchange a minimum and a maximum.

    Finally, if the requirement b varies while remaining positive, the same reasoning gives p*(b) = b squared / 2 and λ*(b) = b. The derivative of p* with respect to b equals λ*. Here it measures the marginal cost of a stricter requirement, in °C² per °C. The last exercise distinguishes this local slope from an exact finite change.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculation and reconstruct the chain q ≤ L ≤ f. State the domain over which each minimum is taken and why a feasible pair and an equal bound rule out every better setting. Recalling a formula without its justification leaves the proof incomplete.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The term λg must always add a positive cost.”

    With g ≤ 0 and λ ≥ 0, λg is instead negative or 0 over the feasible domain. This is what gives L ≤ f. Start again from the chosen sign of the constraint before forming the dual.

    Recover the sign and the chain of bounds

  • “The inner minimum necessarily gives an acceptable setting.”

    The inner search covers all real settings. For λ = 4 °C, it gives a sum of 4 °C, which is insufficient for the requirement of 6 °C. Keep the dual bound, then check the feasibility of the proposed pair separately.

    Return to the trial multiplier

  • “Every dual bound automatically equals the best cost.”

    Weak duality gives only an inequality. Even our trial gives 16 °C², below the optimum. The final certificate requires an exact bound and a feasible pair whose cost matches it; a positive gap alone does not prove that the pair is poor.

    Distinguish bounds from a certificate in the exercises

q(4) = 24 − 8 = 16 °C². For the proposed pair, x + y = 2 °C and g = 6 − 2 = 4 °C > 0 °C. It does not meet the requirement.

Its score is 1 + 1 = 2 °C². It can be below 16 °C² without contradiction because it is outside the primal domain. Excluding it does not weaken the validity of the bound on allowed settings.

Exercise 2 · Bounds on paper

What a gap allows you to conclude

Consider the setting x = 2 °C, y = 4 °C. Check its feasibility and its score. Bound p* using q(4), then using q(6). Each time, calculate the difference between the cost of this setting and the bound. What certifies the minimum?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The cost of a feasible pair is above the minimum. A dual bound is below it.

Hint 2

A positive gap between two trial values can come from either side. To close it, recover the symmetric pair.

Compare with the solution

The sum is 6 °C: the setting is feasible. Its score is 4 + 16 = 20 °C². With λ = 4 °C, we know 16 ≤ p* ≤ 20 in °C². The difference is 4 °C². These bounds alone do not prove the optimality of any pair.

With λ = 6 °C, 18 ≤ p* ≤ 20, and the difference is 2 °C². Replacing the pair with (3 °C, 3 °C) gives a cost of 18 °C²: the difference becomes 0 °C² and the minimum is certified.

The initial pair therefore costs 2 °C² more than the optimum now established. This conclusion uses the final certificate; it did not follow from the gap of 4 °C² with the first bound alone.

Check your reasoning

  • Check the sum and calculate the two squares separately.
  • Orient both sets of bounds in the same direction and retain the units.
  • Distinguish the gap between trials from the gap between optima; exhibit the pair and multiplier that close the bounds.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same greenhouse

The marginal price does not predict the whole change

The requirement increases from 6 °C to 7 °C. The other assumptions remain the same. Reconstruct q with the new requirement, find its best multiplier and the certified primal pair. Compare the exact increase in the score with λ* × 1 °C, calculated at the initial setting.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Replace only b in q(λ) = bλ − λ squared / 2; completing the square places the best λ at the new requirement.

Hint 2

The certified pair shares the requirement equally. For the exact change, subtract the two optimal costs before rounding.

Compare with the solution

With new b = 7 °C, the bound becomes q(λ) = 7λ − λ squared / 2. Completing the same square gives new λ* = 7 °C and new x* = new y* = 7/2 °C, or 3.5 °C. The sum does meet the new requirement.

Each square is 49/4 °C². The score is therefore 49/2 °C². The best bound is q(7) = 7 × 7 − (7 × 7)/2 = 49/2 °C², or 24.5 °C². Equality certifies the minimum again.

The exact increase is 49/2 − 18 = 13/2 °C², or 6.5 °C². The initial multiplier gives the linear prediction 6 × 1 = 6 °C². The difference is 1/2 °C², or 0.5 °C².

Indeed, for an increase h in b, expanding the square gives p*(b + h) − p*(b) = bh + h squared / 2. h is in °C; the term bh = λ*h is the local approximation. Here h is 1 °C and the quadratic term explains the remainder exactly. The multiplier describes a slope at the initial point, rather than promising a constant cost.

Check your reasoning

  • Recalculate the multiplier for the new requirement and check that its sign is admissible.
  • Again show a dual value equal to the cost of the feasible pair.
  • Keep the exact fractions and explain the remainder of 1/2 °C² using the quadratic term, without attributing it to rounding.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In the greenhouse, what do the primal and the dual choose, respectively?

The primal chooses feasible x and y to minimize f. The dual chooses λ ≥ 0 to maximize the bound q(λ), obtained by minimizing L over all real settings.

Recall 2Reconstruct the two inequalities that prove weak duality and explain their reasons.

At every feasible pair: q(λ) ≤ L(x, y, λ) because q is the infimum of L; L(x, y, λ) ≤ f(x, y) because λ ≥ 0 and g ≤ 0. Therefore q(λ) ≤ p*.

Recall 3Why can the inner minimizer violate the requirement without invalidating the bound?

The minimum that defines q ranges over all real x and y. Its value is therefore below L at every feasible point, even if its own minimizer is not feasible. Primal feasibility must be checked separately.

Recall 4What two pieces of evidence certify an optimum, and why is a positive gap insufficient?

You need a justified dual bound and a feasible pair with the same cost. They then bound the minimum by the same value. With a positive gap between trials, the pair may allow improvement, the bound may be weak, or both.

Recall 5What does λ* measure when the requirement in our model varies, and what is its limitation?

For positive b, p*(b) = b squared / 2, so its derivative is b = λ*. This multiplier is the marginal cost of an increase in the requirement; a finite increase also adds a quadratic term.
