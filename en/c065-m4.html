Level 1 · C065·M4

Uncertainty in coefficients

Your goalCalculate the standard error of a slope and an intercept, then explain what these uncertainties allow us to say.

Before you start
c040-m5 : least squares · c056-m5 : effect size · c037-m5 : controlled cleaning · c065-m1 : regression · c065-m2 : correlation · c065-m3 : residuals

✿01 See the idea

An experimental setup consists of a power supply and a heating strip, with different lengths that can be switched on. We want to describe the power consumed as a function of the active length. Readings at the same setting can differ, so the fitted line also depends on the fluctuations encountered during the experiment.

These data are entirely fictional: with 1 m of active strip, the readings are 8 W and then 12 W; at 3 m, they are 14 W and then 14 W. The resulting line assigns an additional 2 W per metre. If we repeated the measurements at the same lengths, would we obtain exactly this slope? There is no requirement that we would.

The uncertainty in a coefficient describes this possible variation across repetitions of the protocol. Its standard error is an estimate of the standard deviation of its repeated estimates. It has the same unit as the coefficient: W/m for the slope and W for the intercept. It is not the actual error of the current line, since the true coefficients are unknown.

Before reading the solution, identify what varies vertically in the diagram. Points that are more scattered around a line can make its coefficients less precise. With comparable noise, lengths that are more widely spaced give more information for estimating the slope. These relationships assume the measurement model set out in the example.

The rule to remember

Estimate the noise from the residuals, then account for the distribution of the lengths. A numerical slope must be accompanied by a scale of uncertainty and its assumptions.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

How much uncertainty accompanies our line?

We have n = 4 readings in the form (length in m; power in W): (1 ; 8), (1 ; 12), (3 ; 14), (3 ; 14). Let us briefly revisit the fit, then calculate the uncertainty in each of its coefficients.

  1. State the model and the intended repetition

    yi=a+bxi+εi

    i denotes a reading among the n readings. xᵢ is the known length, in m; yᵢ is the measured power, in W. a is the mean power at 0 length, in W; b is the true slope, in W/m; εᵢ is the random measurement error, in W. a and b are unknown fixed parameters. A hat indicates their estimate from this sample.

    We assume lengths known without appreciable error, a mean power that truly follows an affine relationship, and independent errors with mean 0 W and the same finite variance σ², in W². Repeating the protocol means taking new readings at the same lengths. A shared drift across trials or a spread that differs with length would call the formulas used here into question.

    Variance describes the mean of the squared deviations from the mean; its positive square root is the standard deviation. We do not need to assume normally distributed errors for the variance formulas that follow. No interval with a confidence level is calculated in this unit.

  2. Recover the line while keeping the units

    A bar denotes a mean. x̄ = (1 + 1 + 3 + 3)/4 = 8/4 = 2 m. Similarly, ȳ = (8 + 12 + 14 + 14)/4 = 48/4 = 12 W.

    Sxx=∑(xi−x¯)2,Sxy=∑(xi−x¯)(yi−y¯)

    The symbol ∑ sums the terms over all the readings. Sxx measures their horizontal spread: 2 × (-1)² + 2 × 1² = 4 m². Sxᵧ = (-1) × (8 − 12) + (-1) × (12 − 12) + 1 × (14 − 12) + 1 × (14 − 12) = 8 m·W.

    The fitted slope is b̂ = Sxᵧ/Sxx = 8/4 = 2 W/m. Then â = ȳ − b̂x̄ = 12 − 2 × 2 = 8 W. The fitted power is therefore ŷᵢ = â + b̂xᵢ. The lengths are not all identical: Sxx is strictly positive, which makes it possible to calculate the slope.

  3. Use what remains after fitting

    At 1 m, the line gives 8 + 2 × 1 = 10 W; at 3 m, 8 + 2 × 3 = 14 W. The residual rᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ is the observed deviation from the fitted line. The residuals are therefore -2, 2, 0, 0 W.

    Their sum is 0: it does not measure the spread. Instead, we calculate the sum of squared residuals, denoted C: C = (-2)² + 2² + 0² + 0² = 8 W².

    s2=Cn−2=82=4 W²

    s² estimates the unknown noise variance σ². We divide by n − 2 = 2, the residual degrees of freedom: fitting the intercept and slope imposes the constraints ∑rᵢ = 0 and ∑(xᵢ − x̄)rᵢ = 0. Under the model, this division corrects for the fact that the line has already absorbed some of the fluctuations. The estimated standard deviation of the noise is s = √4 = 2 W.

  4. Move from noise to uncertainty in the slope

    The standard error of b̂, denoted e_b, estimates the standard deviation of the slopes obtained by repeating the measurements. The formula comes from b̂ − b = ∑(xᵢ − x̄)εᵢ/Sxx. With independent errors of variance σ², the variance of this weighted sum is σ²/Sxx. We replace σ² with s² to estimate it.

    eb=s2Sxx=44=1 W/m

    The estimated variance of the slope is 1 (W/m)²; its square root is 1 W/m. Check the units: W² divided by m², followed by a square root, does give W/m. Check the mechanism: with noise s² held fixed, increasing Sxx reduces this uncertainty. This requires comparable measurements and an affine relationship that remains valid over the chosen range.

  5. Calculate the uncertainty in the intercept

    The estimated intercept â = ȳ − b̂x̄ depends on the mean and the slope. Under our assumptions, ȳ and b̂ have 0 covariance: their fluctuations do not contribute a cross term to the variance. This gives the following estimated variance; e_a is its positive square root.

    ea=s1n+x¯2Sxx

    Inside the parentheses: 1/4 + 2²/4 = 1/4 + 1 = 5/4, which is dimensionless. The estimated variance of â is 4 × 5/4 = 5 W²; its standard error is exactly √5 W.

    0 length lies outside our observed settings. The uncertainty in the intercept therefore includes a term due to the slope and the distance between this origin and x̄. This formula quantifies the uncertainty under the affine model; it does not guarantee that the setup follows the same relationship at 0 length.

  6. Check what the results say

    We report b̂ = 2 W/m with standard error 1 W/m, and â = 8 W with standard error √5 W. Standard errors give a scale for the variation of the estimates, without revealing their current error. A standard error is neither a guaranteed bound nor, on its own, an interval with a coverage probability.

    The residuals satisfy the stated constraints: -2 + 2 + 0 + 0 = 0 W; (-1) × -2 + (-1) × 2 = 0 m·W. The line also passes through (x̄ ; ȳ): 8 + 2 × 2 = 12 W.

    To separate the role of noise from that of geometry, the exercises keep this setup and these lengths. They change the reference point, then the spread around the same line. The uncertainty in a future power reading will require another calculation, in the next unit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The residuals cancel out, so the line is certain.”

    A 0 sum is a constraint of fitting a line with an intercept. The squares, however, total 8 W². Start again from these squares, divide by 2 degrees of freedom, then account for Sₓₓ.

    Revisit the calculation of the noise and then the standard error

  • “The standard error is the error I actually made.”

    The true coefficients remain unknown. The standard error estimates the spread of the coefficients that repetitions of the protocol would give under the stated assumptions; it does not reveal the deviation of the current coefficient from its true value.

    Return to the meaning of repeated measurements

  • “If I change the origin of the lengths, everything becomes more precise.”

    Centring the lengths changes the quantity called the intercept. The slope and its standard error stay the same. Compare the mean power at the centre of the readings with the mean power estimated at 0 length: these are different targets.

    Revisit the exercise on the reference point

Hint 1

Count the coefficients already fitted before choosing the divisor for the sum of squares.

Hint 2

The estimated variance of the slope is s²/Sₓₓ. One final operation restores the unit W/m.

Compare with the solution

The squares give C = 4 + 4 = 8 W². The number of degrees of freedom is 4 − 2 = 2, because the slope and intercept have been fitted. Thus s² = 8/2 = 4 W².

The distribution of the lengths then comes into play: estimated variance of b̂ = 4/4 = 1 (W/m)². Its square root gives e_b = 1 W/m. Dividing only C by Sxx would leave out the estimation of the noise.

Exercise 2 · Application and interpretation

Move the reference point

Express the same readings using u = x − 2 m. Write the line in the form ŷ = ĉ + b̂u. What mean power does ĉ now represent? Calculate its standard error, then state what happens to the slope and its standard error.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The length u is 0 at the centre of the observed lengths. Replace x with u + 2 m in the line.

Hint 2

The formula for uncertainty in the intercept applies with the mean of u, which is 0 m.

Compare with the solution

The same line can be written as ŷ = 8 W + (2 W/m) × (u + 2 m), so ĉ = 12 W and the slope remains 2 W/m. ĉ now estimates the mean power at 2 m of active strip. It no longer denotes the power at 0 length.

Since ū = 0 m, the estimated variance of ĉ is s²/n = 4/4 = 1 W². Its standard error is 1 W. The deviations from the mean and Sxx do not change: the standard error of the slope remains 1 W/m.

The calculation compares uncertainties in two different mean powers: at 0 length and at the central length. It has added no readings and has made neither the slope nor its predictions more precise.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain ĉ = 12 W and name the reference length.
  • Obtain 1 W using ū = 0 m.
  • Keep the slope and its standard error unchanged; explain why changing the target changes the uncertainty in the intercept.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same setting

The same slope with more noise

A fictional variant of the same experiment: multiply each residual by 2 around the same fitted values, without changing the lengths. Before calculating, state what should change. Does the line stay the same? What happens to C, s², the standard error of the slope and the estimated variance of the intercept? Can we deduce that the true slope has changed?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The new residuals still satisfy the constraints that their sum and weighted sum are 0.

Hint 2

Multiplying a residual by 2 multiplies its square by 4. Then take the square root to return to a standard error.

Compare with the solution

The line stays the same: the residuals multiplied by 2 still have a 0 sum and a 0 sum when weighted by the centred lengths. Since the lengths are not all equal, these constraints still identify the same least squares fit.

The squares are multiplied by 4: C becomes 32 W² and s² becomes 16 W². Sxx and n do not change. The standard error of the slope is therefore multiplied by 2, reaching 2 W/m; the estimated variance of the intercept is multiplied by 4, reaching 20 W².

The fitted slope remains 2 W/m, with increased uncertainty. The variant does not provide the true slope. It shows that the same point estimate can be accompanied by a different degree of precision under the model.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify why the line stays the same using the residual constraints.
  • Multiply the variances by 4 and the standard error by 2.
  • Distinguish the point estimate, uncertainty and unknown true parameter.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the standard error of a slope refer to?

It estimates the standard deviation of the slopes fitted across repetitions of the protocol at the same lengths, under the model. It does not give the actual error of the current slope.

Recall 2How can we estimate the noise after fitting a line with an intercept?

Add the squared residuals, then divide by n − 2. The degrees of freedom account for fitting the slope and intercept. The formula assumes, among other things, a correct affine model and a common noise variance.

Recall 3Which two ingredients determine the standard error of the slope?

The estimated noise s and the horizontal spread Sₓₓ: e_b = s/√Sₓₓ. With noise held fixed, more spread reduces this standard error if the model remains applicable. The unit is that of the slope.

Recall 4Why does uncertainty in the intercept depend on the reference point?

The intercept estimates the mean response at the point chosen as the origin. Its standard error is s√(1/n + x̄²/Sₓₓ). Centring the lengths eliminates the term involving x̄, but changes the target mean power and preserves the uncertainty in the slope.

Recall 5Which assumptions support our calculations, and what do they not prove?

Known lengths, an affine mean, and independent errors with 0 mean and a common finite variance. Standard errors prove neither these assumptions, nor a bounded actual error, nor a causal effect. On their own, they do not constitute confidence intervals.
