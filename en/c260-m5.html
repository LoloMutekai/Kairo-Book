Level 4 · C260·M5

Event simulation

Your goalSimulate a short Hawkes sample path using proposals and rejections, keeping the candidate clock separate from the history of accepted events.

Before you start
c226-m5 : integration with jumps · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c259-m5 : simulation and limitations · c090-m5 : stationarity · c260-m1 : conditional intensity · c260-m2 : kernel · c260-m3 : branching ratio · c260-m4 : likelihood

✿01 See the idea

Consider an alert board in a fictional workshop. An alert can trigger others soon afterwards: checks, confirmations, new reports. We want to generate possible times using a stated model, without claiming to reconstruct the alerts of a real workshop.

In our model, each accepted alert temporarily increases the arrival rate by a = 0.25 alerts per minute, for d = 2 min. Its contribution then disappears. A background rate μ = 0.50 alerts per minute always remains. This rectangular kernel produces steps; the simulation keeps continuous event times, without dividing time into bins.

To propose times, we use an upper bound M on the rate, high enough to cover the actual rate until the next proposal. At each candidate time, a random draw decides whether to keep the alert. A rejection makes past alerts older but creates no new excitation. The calculations will make this separation visible.

We start with an alert already known at time 0 min, with no earlier history, and simulate up to T = 3 min. The initial history and all the draws on this page are fictional. They let us check a sample path with pencil and paper; they measure neither a real frequency nor prediction quality.

The rule to remember

Propose under a valid upper bound; evaluate the intensity before adding the candidate; accept or reject; advance the clock in either case, adding to the history only on acceptance.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Generate the next times for the alert board

With the initial alert at 0 min, μ = 0.50 alerts/min, a = 0.25 alerts/min and d = 2 min, let us construct the accepted events before T = 3 min. We calculate the proposals in order, including the one that will be rejected.

  1. Specify the model and its history

    t denotes a time, tᵢ the time of an accepted alert and τ = t − tᵢ its age, all in minutes. The index i runs through the history H of accepted times, which also includes the initial alert. λ(t) is the conditional intensity: an arrival rate, in alerts per minute, determined by this history. μ is the background rate; a is the height of the kernel; d is its duration. The indicator symbol equals 1 if the stated condition is true, 0 otherwise. The sum includes only times already accepted before t.

    λ(t)=μ+a∑ti∈H10<t−ti<d

    A contribution expires at tᵢ + d; we remove it at that time. None of the candidates chosen here falls exactly on an expiration time. With continuous draws, these equalities have probability 0. The parameters are constant and positive; alerts do not inhibit one another. The branching ratio is a × d = 0.25 × 2 = 0.50, less than 1. The model therefore admits a stationary regime, but our imposed start with no earlier history is not a draw from that regime.

  2. Construct a proposal under an upper bound

    s is the current clock time and M is the upper bound, in alerts per minute, fixed for the current proposal. Immediately after an acceptance, M includes the addition a from that alert. Without a new accepted alert, our kernel can only remain constant or disappear: the intensity cannot increase. Its value just after s is therefore a valid upper bound throughout the next waiting time, even if an older contribution expires in the meantime.

    We draw a dimensionless E from an exponential distribution with rate 1, then obtain a waiting time Δ = E/M, in minutes. In other words, Δ follows an exponential distribution with rate M. To obtain E from a uniform generator, we can draw W between 0 and 1, excluding the endpoints, and set E = −ln(W), where ln is the natural logarithm. We separately draw U, uniform between the same endpoints, independent of E and of earlier draws. E proposes the time; U decides whether to keep it.

    c=s+EM;q=λ−(c)M;U≤q⇒accepter

    c is the candidate time. λ⁻(c) is the intensity calculated before adding this candidate; the − sign here means “before the addition”. q is the probability of acceptance conditional on this proposal, and is dimensionless. The upper bound guarantees 0 ≤ q ≤ 1. If c exceeds T, we stop before the test. Otherwise, we set s to c in either outcome; we add c to H only if U ≤ q. We then recalculate the upper bound and draw new independent variables.

  3. Accept the first candidate

    Initially, only the alert at 0 min is active. M = μ + a = 0.50 + 0.25 = 0.75 alerts/min. Take the fictional values E = 3/8 and U = 0.50. The values of E are supplied directly; we do not approximate any logarithms to reproduce the calculation.

    The waiting time is Δ = 3/8 / 3/4 = 1/2 min. The candidate arrives at c = 0 + 1/2 = 0.50 min. The initial alert is less than d minutes old: λ⁻(c) = 0.75 alerts/min. Thus q = 3/4 / 3/4 = 1. Since 0.50 ≤ 1, we accept.

    The history now contains 0 and 0.50 min. The new upper bound includes the new alert: M = 0.75 + 0.25 = 1 alerts/min. Keeping the old bound after this jump could underestimate the intensity.

  4. Let a contribution expire and reject

    With M = 1 alerts/min, take E = 7/4 and U = 0.875. The waiting time is 7/4 / 1 = 7/4 min. The candidate is therefore at 1/2 + 7/4 = 9/4 min, or 2.25 min.

    The initial contribution expired at 0 + 2 = 2 min. The contribution from the next alert expires at 0.50 + 2 = 2.50 min. At the candidate time, only this latter contribution remains active. We must therefore reevaluate the rate: λ⁻(c) = 1 − 0.25 = 0.75 alerts/min. The upper bound used for the proposal was higher, but remained valid.

    The threshold is q = 3/4 / 1 = 0.75. Since 0.875 > 0.75, we reject. The clock advances to 2.25 min; H still contains only the times 0 and 0.50 min. The upper bound for the next proposal drops to 0.75 alerts/min.

  5. Continue from the rejection time

    Now take E = 3/32 and U = 0.25. With M = 0.75 alerts/min, Δ = 3/32 / 3/4 = 1/8 min. We add this waiting time to the rejected time, which is the current clock time: c = 9/4 + 1/8 = 19/8 min, or 2.375 min.

    The age of the alert accepted at 0.50 min is 19/8 − 1/2 = 15/8 min, less than d = 2 min. It still contributes. λ⁻(c) remains 0.75 alerts/min and q = 1. We accept because 0.25 ≤ 1. The upper bound rises again to 0.75 + 0.25 = 1 alerts/min.

  6. Stop at the horizon and check the trace

    The next draw is E = 1. The waiting time is 1 / 1 = 1 min; the candidate would be at 19/8 + 1 = 27/8 min, or 3.375 min. It exceeds T = 3 min, so we stop. We do not artificially move it back to T or create an alert at the boundary.

    The simulated output contains 2 new times: 0.50 min and 2.375 min. H contains 3 times if we also count the imposed initial alert. The candidate at 2.25 min belongs to the proposal log, not to the event output. At T, the contributions from the times 0 and 0.50 min have expired; the contribution from 2.375 min remains: λ(T) = 0.75 alerts/min.

    Checks: candidate times are strictly increasing; every threshold stays in the interval from 0 to 1; every added event has passed its test. Directly recounting the alerts that have not expired gives the same intensities as the subtractions.

  7. Why does rejection preserve the correct rate?

    Over a very short duration h, in minutes, the candidate process with rate M makes a proposal with probability approximately M × h. With the history fixed, the proportion kept is λ/M. To first order, the probability of an accepted alert is therefore M × h × λ/M = λ × h: this is the rate required by the conditional intensity. This argument applies between updates to the history; expirations are accounted for in λ at the candidate time.

    This method is called thinning: we remove points from a faster process. It simulates continuous time without discretization error if the upper bound is valid and the draws are correct. Replacing an exponential distribution with its mean waiting time would produce regularly scheduled arrivals, not the same distribution. Our rational draws form an imposed teaching trace, not a random generator.

    A kernel that increases after a delay would require a different construction of the upper bound. Automatically setting M equal to the current rate would no longer suffice. And a simulated sample path, even a tightly clustered one, validates neither the chosen kernel nor its parameters for real alerts.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The candidate has arrived: we must add it before calculating its intensity.”

    First calculate λ⁻ from the alerts already accepted. Adding the candidate before its own test inflates its chance of acceptance. The jump a occurs only after an acceptance.

    Reread the upper bound and the test before the addition

  • “The candidate is rejected, so we return to the last accepted alert.”

    Elapsed time remains elapsed. Keep the rejection time as the clock time, remove expired contributions and leave H unchanged. The next waiting time starts from this new clock time.

    Repair the log in the second exercise

  • “A threshold greater than unity can be repaired by setting it to unity.”

    Such a threshold signals an upper bound that is too low or an incorrect history. Check both. Capping the threshold hides the fault and does not repair the distribution of proposals already drawn.

    Find the cause of the impossible threshold

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why is the current rate an upper bound until the next proposal here?

Without a new accepted alert, each rectangular contribution remains constant or expires. The rate therefore cannot increase. The upper bound includes the jump from an alert as soon as it is accepted.

Recall 2How do you turn a dimensionless exponential draw into a waiting time in minutes?

Draw E with unit rate and divide by M, in alerts per minute: Δ = E/M. The waiting time is exponential with rate M. An independent uniform draw U then decides acceptance.

Recall 3What changes after a rejection?

The clock advances to the candidate time and the contributions grow older. No new time is added to the history of accepted alerts. Recalculate the upper bound before drawing the next proposal.

Recall 4Why divide the intensity before adding the candidate by the upper bound?

Proposals arrive at rate M. Keeping a proportion λ⁻/M locally recovers the rate λ⁻. The ratio must stay between 0 and 1; the candidate does not contribute to its own threshold.

Recall 5What does an exactly recalculated fictional sample path prove?

It lets us check the arithmetic, decisions and updates in that trace. It proves neither the quality of a random generator, nor an observed stationary regime, nor the suitability of the model for real alerts.
