Level 2 · C067·M3

Validation

Your goalCompare models on data reserved for selection, calculate their validation error and explain why this selection then requires a separate test.

Before you start
c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c017-m5 : reproduction protocol · c067-m1 : empirical risk · c067-m2 : training set

✿01 See the idea

A baker wants to predict how many loaves of bread will be sold the next day. Two models, A and B, have learned from the same old sales log. A reproduces this log more closely. Should she choose it for tomorrow? A good fit to a past record already studied is not enough: we want to see how each model handles days it did not use for learning.

Before any fitting, the baker reserves part of the log to compare the candidates. This validation set is used to select the model or its settings. Training fits the parameters using examples; validation decides which solution to keep. A setting chosen from several possibilities, such as the complexity allowed for the model, is called a hyperparameter.

Selection therefore uses information from validation, even if each candidate’s parameters were not fitted on it. A final set, the test set, stays closed until the decision is fixed. It evaluates the selected solution, without using its result to decide on further changes.

The entire scene and all its values are fictional. We study a simple split in time order: 12 days for learning, the next 3 for selection, then 4 for testing. These sample sizes make the calculation easy to follow; they are not recommended sample sizes for a real bakery.

The rule to remember

Learn on the training set, select on the validation set, then evaluate on a test set kept separate from selection. The role of a set depends on how its results are used.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Choose a forecast for the bakery

The protocol sets mean absolute error as the criterion in advance: a loaf too few and a loaf too many in the forecast have the same cost in this exercise. On the training set, the given sums of absolute errors are 12 loaves for A and 24 loaves for B, over 12 days each. The validation forecasts are those in the log above. Let us calculate which model to select, without opening the test set.

  1. Define the quantity being compared

    c denotes the candidate, A or B. j identifies a day in the validation set; n is the number of days, here 3. These indices and this sample size have no physical unit. yⱼ is the number of loaves sold on day j; ŷ c,j, read “y hat for candidate c on day j”, is its forecast in loaves. The hat marks an estimate, not an observed sale.

    The absolute difference d c,j is the distance between forecast and sales, in loaves. The vertical bars mean absolute value: we keep the size of the difference without its sign. Rᵥ(c) is the mean of these differences on the validation set, in loaves; the subscript v indicates the set used. The symbol ∑ asks us to add the terms for days from 1 to n.

    dc,j=|y^c,j−yj|;Rv(c)=1n∑j=1ndc,j

    The same days, the same reference sales and the same criterion are used for all candidates. A lower score is preferred. If the scores are equal, a rule announced before the comparison, such as keeping the simplest model, breaks the tie.

  2. Separate the training result from the held-out result

    On the training set, A has 12 ÷ 12 = 1 loaf of mean error; B has 24 ÷ 12 = 2 loaves. These fictional summaries are provided, not reconstructed from sales that are not shown.

    A performs better on these examples already used to fit the parameters. This finding does not select the winner on the held-out days. We keep the candidates as they were trained and calculate a new score.

  3. Calculate each error for A

    Monday: |44 − 40| = 4 loaves. Tuesday: |44 − 50| = |−6| = 6 loaves. Wednesday: |62 − 60| = 2 loaves.

    Take the absolute value before adding. An excess in Monday’s forecast does not make up for a shortfall in Tuesday’s forecast: the criterion assesses the daily distance, not the stock balance over the whole period.

  4. Add, then divide by the number of days

    For A, the subtotal for the first days is 4 + 6 = 10 loaves. Adding Wednesday gives 10 + 2 = 12 loaves. Dividing by 3 gives each day the same weight.

    Rv(A)=4+6+23=4 pains

  5. Apply exactly the same calculation to B

    Monday: |42 − 40| = 2 loaves. Tuesday: |47 − 50| = 3 loaves. Wednesday: |59 − 60| = 1 loaf. The subtotal is 5 loaves, and the total is 6 loaves.

    Rv(B)=2+3+13=2 pains

    B is selected under the protocol: 2 loaves is less than 4 loaves, although its training error was higher. There is no contradiction: the sets answer different questions.

  6. Check the calculation and the scope of the choice

    Arithmetic check: 3 × 4 = 12 loaves and 3 × 2 = 6 loaves recover the sums. Each mean also lies between its candidate’s smallest and largest errors. All the errors are greater than or equal to 0.

    To interpret this comparison, assume that the bakery, opening hours and sales conditions remain comparable, that forecasts use only information known the previous day and that the held-out days reflect the intended future use. Here, the time order prevents learning from days later than those being predicted; it does not guarantee that future sales will follow the same pattern.

    These 3 days do not prove that B is generally superior. Errors may depend on one another from day to day, and an unusual event may change the ranking. Here we calculate neither an uncertainty interval nor a guarantee about future sales.

  7. Fix the decision before opening the test set

    Record the chronological split, the candidates tried, the criterion, the forecasts and the decision to select B. The winner’s validation score was used to select it: it must not be presented as a final measurement independent of selection.

    The 4 test days stay closed on this page. After fixing B and its settings, evaluating them can provide information about this specific choice. If their result leads to further changes, these days now contribute to selection and no longer serve as an untouched test set.

    Continually trying new candidates on the same validation days can also adapt the choice to the particular features of those days. Recording the trials makes this limitation visible; it does not remove it.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The model that performs best on its training set must be the right one.”

    The training set was used to fit the parameters. Compare the candidates on the same held-out days: here A goes from 1 loaf on the training set to 4 loaves on the validation set, while B has 2 loaves on the validation set. These fictional scores describe their respective sets.

    Repeat the comparison on the held-out log

  • “I add the differences with their signs, then take the absolute value.”

    Excesses and shortfalls could cancel out. First calculate the distance from each forecast to the sales. For A on Tuesday, the signed difference is −6 loaves but the absolute loss is 6 loaves.

    Review the daily loss calculation

  • “The winner’s validation score already proves its final performance.”

    This score contributed to selecting the winner. It remains useful for comparing candidates, but final evaluation requires a test set kept separate from decisions. Even this test set does not guarantee stable future conditions.

    Review the roles of the log

Compare with the solution

The signed difference is 47 − 50 = −3 loaves: B underestimated sales. The absolute loss is 3 loaves.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and sensitivity

Can a different forecast reverse the choice?

In a fictional variant of the same log, only B’s forecast for Wednesday changes to 68 loaves. Sales, A and B’s other forecasts remain the same. Recalculate B’s mean as an exact fraction, then as a decimal rounded to two decimal places for readability. Which model does the protocol select in this variant?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Recalculate only Wednesday’s loss; the subtotal for the other days is unchanged.

Hint 2

Compare the new sum with A’s before dividing by the same number of days.

Compare with the solution

Wednesday’s loss becomes |68 − 60| = 8 loaves. The total is 5 + 8 = 13 loaves.

The exact mean is 13/3 = 13/3 loaves, or approximately 4.33 loaves. It exceeds A’s 4 loaves: A is selected in this variant.

Check without rounding: 13 loaves exceeds 12 loaves over the same sample size. The change illustrates the sensitivity of this small comparison; it does not measure how often such a reversal occurs in practice.

Check your reasoning

  • You find 8 loaves for Wednesday and 13/3 loaves for the exact mean.
  • You keep the fraction before rounding and select A.
  • You distinguish a calculation variant from proof of future performance.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and justification

The test set becomes a selection tool

The baker selects B, opens the 4 test days, then changes its settings until their score improves. She still calls this score an “independent final evaluation”. Explain the problem and suggest a repair without inventing any sales result.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Ask which information guided the new settings.

Hint 2

The name written on a page of the log is not enough to preserve its role.

Compare with the solution

The test set guided changes: it now contributes to selection. Its results can be kept as a record of exploration, but their name does not make them independent of decisions.

The selected procedure must be fixed, the history of trials kept and an evaluation planned on new days kept separate from every selection decision. Until this evaluation exists, state that its evidence is still missing. A new held-out set alone does not guarantee that future trading conditions will remain comparable.

Check your reasoning

  • You identify the link between the test result and the changes.
  • You propose a truly new held-out set after fixing the choices.
  • You assign no score to sales that have not been provided.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What do we do on the training, validation and test sets?

We fit the parameters on the training set, select candidates or settings on the validation set, then evaluate the fixed procedure on the test set kept separate from selection.

Recall 2How can you reconstruct the validation mean absolute error, including its unit?

For each day, take the absolute value of forecast minus sales, add these losses, then divide by the number of days. Here the score is in loaves. The candidates use the same days and the same criterion.

Recall 3Why is B’s validation score not an independent final measurement?

The score was used to select B. Validation therefore influences the selected procedure; evaluating it requires a separate held-out set, unused for decisions.

Recall 4What does the ranking on this small log fail to demonstrate?

It demonstrates neither general superiority nor a guaranteed future error. Few days, dependencies between days and a change in conditions limit its scope.

Recall 5Do days called a test set remain a final test set if their scores guide changes?

They become selection data. Record this role, fix the procedure and reserve new observations for evaluation; merely changing their label repairs nothing.
