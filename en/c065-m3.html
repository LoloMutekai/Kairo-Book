Level 1 · C065·M3

Residuals

Your goalCalculate signed deviations from a fitted line, then interpret what their size and pattern allow us to question.

Before you start
c040-m5 : least squares · c056-m5 : effect size · c037-m5 : controlled cleaning · c065-m1 : regression · c065-m2 : correlation

✿01 See the idea

In a workshop, we want to predict the time needed to pack a batch from its number of parcels. A line summarizes the times already recorded. For a batch of 4 parcels, it predicts 10 min; the record gives 9 min. The work took 1 min less than predicted.

The residual keeps both this discrepancy and its direction: we start from what the line predicts and look at where the recorded value lies. Here, the observed point is lower. The residual is therefore negative. A higher point would give a positive residual, because the observed time would exceed the predicted time.

We compare times for the same number of parcels: the movement is vertical on the graph of time against parcel count. Changing the number of parcels as well would mean comparing a different batch. Residuals then let us examine what the line leaves unexplained: small, scattered deviations, or a pattern that needs checking.

The scene and all its records are fictional. The batches are assumed to be comparable, measured in the same unit and using the same procedure. We are studying their deviations from a line that has already been fitted; we are measuring neither the actual performance of the workshop nor a cause of its delays.

The rule to remember

For each batch, calculate observed minus predicted for the same number of parcels; keep the sign, then examine the sizes and their pattern around 0.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What the line leaves unexplained in the packing records

Batches A to E were used to fit a line by ordinary least squares, with no weighting or penalty and with an intercept. Its coefficients are given: a = 2 min and b = 2 min per parcel. Keep this line and calculate all its residuals.

  1. Gather the data and define the rule

    The symbol i identifies a batch from A to E; n = 5 is the number of batches. For batch i, xᵢ is the number of parcels and yᵢ is the observed time in minutes. The hat in ŷᵢ means “value predicted by the line”. a is the intercept, in minutes; b is the slope, in minutes per parcel. The product bxᵢ is therefore in minutes.

    These times are the fitting data, rather than a new set of check data. Here, a does not give an observed time with no parcels: there is no empty batch in the records.

  2. Calculate the predictions before comparing them

    Apply the same rule to each quantity: multiply the number of parcels by the slope, then add the intercept. The quantity changes with the batch; the coefficients stay fixed.

    For A: 2 + 2 × 1 = 2 + 2 = 4 min. For B: 2 + 2 × 2 = 2 + 4 = 6 min. For C: 2 + 2 × 3 = 2 + 6 = 8 min.

    For D: 2 + 2 × 4 = 2 + 8 = 10 min. For E: 2 + 2 × 5 = 2 + 10 = 12 min. These predicted values are model results, rather than additional recorded values.

  3. Subtract in the same order for every batch

    Write eᵢ for the residual, in minutes. The following definition keeps the sign:

    ei=yi−y^i

    A: 6 − 4 = 2 min. B: 5 − 6 = -1 min. C: 6 − 8 = -2 min. D: 9 − 10 = -1 min. E: 14 − 12 = 2 min.

    A positive residual means that the line underestimates the time; a negative residual means that it overestimates it. A residual equal to 0 means that the observation matches the prediction for this batch, without guaranteeing a match for other batches. To check D, reconstruct the observation: 10 + (-1) = 9 min. The relationship yᵢ = ŷᵢ + eᵢ must hold for every batch. Residuals are neither perpendicular distances to the line nor percentages.

  4. Keep signs from hiding the size of the deviations

    Adding residuals with their signs can make deviations cancel. To quantify their size without this cancellation, square each residual. Call the sum of these squares Q; it is expressed in squared minutes. The symbol Σ below means “add over all batches”.

    The squares are, respectively, 2 × 2 = 4, (-1) × (-1) = 1, (-2) × (-2) = 4, (-1) × (-1) = 1 and 2 × 2 = 4 squared minutes.

    Add step by step: 4 + 1 = 5, then 5 + 4 = 9, then 9 + 1 = 10, then 10 + 4 = 14 squared minutes. Q is positive: the observations therefore do not all lie on the line.

    The mean of the squares is obtained by dividing Q by n: 14 ÷ 5 = 14/5 = 2.80 squared minutes. Here, it describes the deviations in the fitting data; this result alone does not provide uncertainty for a future time.

  5. Check the sum without reading too much into it

    Call the signed sum of the residuals S, in minutes. We obtain 2 + (-1) = 1, then 1 + (-2) = -1, then -1 + (-1) = -2, then -2 + 2 = 0 min.

    The result S = 0 is expected for a line fitted by ordinary least squares with an intercept, on the same observations, in exact arithmetic. This property comes from the fitting procedure; it is not the definition of a residual. A line chosen differently, modified data or rounded values may fail to give a sum of exactly 0.

    A concrete check: the total of the observed times equals the total of the predicted times, since their differences add up to 0. Yet Q = 14 squared minutes. Equal totals do not mean agreement for each batch.

  6. Read the pattern, then limit the conclusion

    Return to the residual plot in the intuition section. At the ends, the residuals are positive; between them, they are negative. The line therefore predicts too little time for the small and large batches in this sample, and too much for the intermediate batches. A curved relationship is a possibility to examine, even though the signed sum is 0.

    With only 5 batches, this pattern does not prove a curved relationship. Check the records and their comparability, and look for the same pattern in new records collected with authorization. A large residual prompts an examination of the batch and the model; it does not justify deleting the batch to make the graph look better.

    The residual compares yᵢ with our fitted line. A theoretical error would compare yᵢ with the true mean relationship, which is unknown: it therefore cannot be observed directly. Calculating residuals requires neither a normal distribution nor proof of independence. Assigning probabilities or intervals to them afterwards would require further assumptions and work.

Explain it in your own words

Explain without looking why a negative residual corresponds here to an overestimated time. Then explain how S can be 0 while Q and the pattern of the points still give reasons to examine the model.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The residual is predicted minus observed, so a delay gives a negative sign.”

    Keep the convention observed minus predicted. An observed time greater than the predicted time gives a positive residual. Check by reconstructing observed = predicted + residual; this equality exposes a reversed sign.

    Repeat the subtractions and the check for batch D

  • “We must measure the shortest segment between the point and the line.”

    The prediction concerns time for a fixed number of parcels. Keep the same horizontal coordinate and measure the vertical deviation, in minutes. The shortest segment to the line would also change the quantity and would answer a different question.

    Review the arrow at a fixed quantity

  • “The sum of the residuals is 0: the line explains the records perfectly.”

    Positive and negative values cancel. Look at the squares and the plot: in our example, Q stays positive and the signs form groups. A sum of 0 under the fitting conditions does not validate the form of the model.

    Test this conclusion in the diagnostic exercise

Exercise 1 · Calculation and interpretation

Faster than the line predicts

Batch D contains 4 parcels and takes 9 min. Using the line with a = 2 min, b = 2 min per parcel, calculate the predicted time and then the residual. Does the line overestimate or underestimate the time? Check by reconstructing the recorded value.

Hint 1

First calculate a + bx with the units; do not start by subtracting the parcel counts.

Hint 2

The residual is observed minus predicted. Its sign must be consistent with work that is faster than predicted.

Compare with the solution

The predicted time is 2 + 2 × 4 = 10 min. The residual is 9 − 10 = -1 min. The model overestimates the time by 1 min: the observed time for the fictional batch is shorter than its prediction.

Check: 10 + (-1) = 9 min. The negative sign applies to a deviation, never to a negative packing duration.

  • Recover the observed time by adding the residual to the prediction.

Exercise 2 · Recalculation after correcting a recorded value

A corrected transcription, with the line kept

In the same fictional example, we discover that the time for E should have been 13 min instead of 14 min. The other records stay the same and we explicitly keep the old line. Calculate the new residual for E, then the new sums S and Q. Should we still require S = 0?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The prediction for E stays at 12 min: only its observation is corrected.

Hint 2

In each sum, remove the old contribution from E and add the new one. A squared residual is always nonnegative.

Hint 3

The property of a sum of 0 applied to a line fitted to the observations used. Has this fitting been repeated after the correction?

Compare with the solution

The new residual for E is 13 − 12 = 1 min, and its square is 1 squared minute. The change in the residual is 1 − 2 = -1 min.

So S becomes 0 + (-1) = -1 min. For Q, the change in contribution is 1 − 4 = -3 squared minutes: Q becomes 14 + (-3) = 11 squared minutes.

Direct check: the new residuals are 2, -1, -2, -1, 1 min; their sum is -1 min and the sum of their squares is 11 squared minutes. A sum of 0 is no longer required: the old line has not been refitted to the corrected records. Record the dates of the correction and the fitting used for comparison.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep the coefficients and the prediction for E.
  • Replace the contribution from E in the sums without counting it twice.
  • Obtain S and Q with their distinct units.
  • Link the failure to obtain a sum of 0 to the absence of refitting on the new data.

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A sum of signed values can cancel out. Which result rules out the conclusion that all deviations are 0?

Hint 2

First describe where the residuals are positive and negative. Keep this observation separate from the explanation you are considering.

Compare with the solution

S = 0 min results from cancellation, while Q = 14 squared minutes proves that deviations remain. The batches at the ends have positive residuals and those in the middle have negative residuals. A line can therefore leave a pattern even though the sum of its residuals is 0.

We can suspect a curved relationship without claiming that 5 batches have proved it. Checking the transcriptions and the procedure, then recording new comparable batches according to a rule stated in advance, allows us to examine this possibility. Keep the points unless a documented reason justifies a correction or exclusion.

These residuals from the fit give neither the exact time for a future batch nor, on their own, a prediction interval. The following units will address uncertainty; this page provides a descriptive diagnosis of what the line leaves unexplained in the data.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do we define a residual and check its sign?

Residual = observed minus predicted, for the same number of parcels, in minutes. Check: predicted + residual recovers the observed value. A negative residual indicates an overestimated time here.

Recall 2Why measure vertically rather than perpendicularly to the line?

The line predicts time for a fixed quantity. Vertical movement keeps that quantity fixed and compares minutes; a perpendicular would also change the number of parcels.

Recall 3What does a sum of residuals equal to 0 prove? What does it fail to prove?

The signed deviations cancel. This is expected on the fitting data for ordinary least squares with an intercept, in exact arithmetic. It proves neither that all deviations are 0 nor that the form of the model is suitable.

Recall 4Are signs grouped at the ends and in the middle enough to prove curvature?

They suggest a possibility to examine. Check the records and their comparability, then look for the same pattern again; a small series alone does not demonstrate the cause of the deviations.

Recall 5Why can we calculate the residual while the theoretical error stays unknown?

The residual compares the observation with a known line. The theoretical error would compare it with the unknown true mean relationship. A residual from the fit provides no guarantee about the error for a future batch.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the sequence: same quantity and same units → prediction from the line → observed minus predicted → check by reconstruction → squares to avoid cancellation → plot of signs and sizes → observation, hypothesis, verification. Then explain why a line kept after correcting the data no longer has to give a sum of 0. Reading or an answer obtained with a hint remains preparation, never an attained level.
