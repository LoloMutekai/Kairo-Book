Level 4 · C234·M4

Backward SDEs

Your goalConstruct an adapted value and its noise coefficient from a terminal condition, then verify a backward stochastic differential equation using conditional expectation and Ito’s formula.

Before you start
c233-m5 : verification theorem · c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c227-m5 : Ito-Tanaka · c234-m1 : state space · c234-m2 : Kalman filter · c234-m3 : nonlinear filtering

✿01 See the idea

A temperature-controlled chamber needs to stay close to a setpoint. At the end of a short experiment, we will look at the square of its deviation from that setpoint. We add a stipulated penalty for the remaining time. What value should we assign to this future total now, while the future fluctuations are still unknown?

A stochastic differential equation, abbreviated SDE, describes an evolution subject to randomness. A backward SDE imposes its condition at the end: here, the terminal total. Backward refers to the end at which the condition is specified. It does not let us know the final temperature in advance.

The current value must use only information already available. We therefore look for both a value Y and a coefficient Z that describes how this value responds to newly revealed noise. The term carried by Z makes the random final condition compatible with a value that can be calculated in the present.

The entire scenario and its parameters are fictional. Here, an ideal sensor gives the present deviation exactly; no measurement error is added. This simplification isolates the backward construction: we are not recalculating a filter for a hidden state. The total is in squared degrees, with no interpretation as energy or actual performance.

The rule to remember

Specify the total at the horizon, take its expectation given the present information, then recover the noise coefficient that makes the entire path compatible with that total.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Assign a value to the final temperature total

The experiment lasts T = 4 min. The initial deviation is x = 1 °C; the noise amplitude is σ = 1 °C/√min. The constant penalty is c = 2 °C²/min. At t = 2 min, the ideal sensor gives Xₜ = 2 °C. Let us construct Y and Z, then calculate their values at this time.

  1. Describe the randomness and the permitted information

    t and T are the current time and the horizon, in minutes, with 0 ≤ t ≤ T. W is a standard Brownian motion: its paths are continuous, W starts at 0, and its future increments are Gaussian, independent of the past, with mean 0 and variance equal to the elapsed time. Here, W is expressed in √min. ℱt denotes the information generated by W up to t, with the usual completions; this is the reference filtration.

    Xt=x+σWt

    Xt is the signed deviation from the setpoint, in °C; x and σ are the known constants given in the problem. The model has no drift. Its increment variance is σ squared times the duration, in °C². The word standard describes the normalization of the Brownian motion, rather than an actual chamber setting.

  2. Read an SDE with its terminal condition

    The letter ξ, called xi, denotes the total imposed at the end: here, ξ = X at the horizon, squared. It is known as a rule from the start, but its realization is available only at T. The general backward form is:

    Yt=ξ+∫tTf(s,Ys,Zs)ds−∫tTZsdWs

    s is the integration time, in minutes. f is the generator, a given rule for the rate at which the total accumulates, in °C²/min in our scenario. Its arguments y and z represent possible values of Y and Z, in their respective units. We use only f(s,y,z) = c, independent of these arguments. The integral with respect to ds adds up the running total; the integral with respect to dW is an Ito integral.

    The solution we seek is a pair of processes (Y,Z). Yt is the value, in °C², measurable with respect to ℱt: it is adapted and does not consult the future. Z is predictable, so it can be used as an integrand based on past information, in °C²/√min. Z is neither a temperature reading nor a Kalman gain.

    We seek continuous Y with finite E[sup over t of |Yt| squared] and Z with finite E[∫ from 0 to T of |Zs| squared ds]. E denotes expectation under the model; sup denotes the largest value over the interval. This second condition makes the stochastic integral a square-integrable martingale: its future conditional mean is 0. ξ is also square-integrable; Brownian motion over a finite horizon has the moments needed here.

    dYt=−cdt+ZtdWt;YT=ξ=XT2

    Check the signs by integrating this differential from t to T, then isolating Yt. A positive rate c enters with a plus sign in the backward formula and with a minus sign in the differential.

  3. Reduce the future to a calculable expectation

    Taking the expectation given ℱt preserves Yt, which is already known with this information. The Ito term disappears only in this expectation; it is not 0 on every path. Since c is constant, its integral is c(T − t).

    Yt=E[XT2|ℱt]+c(T−t)

    Set ΔW = W at T minus W at t. Given ℱt, X at T is Xt + σΔW; ΔW has mean 0 and variance T − t. Expanding the square gives a present square, a cross term with mean 0, and the square of the noise. This is why noise with mean 0 still contributes to the total:

    E[XT2|ℱt]=Xt2+σ2(T−t)

    This expression conditions on the available information. Replacing the expectation with the final total of a path we had already looked at would change the problem.

  4. Calculate the present value, operation by operation

    Remaining time: 4 − 2 = 2 min. Square of the present deviation: 2 × 2 = 4 °C². Future variance: 1 × 1 × 2 = 2 °C². Add these contributions to obtain the mean terminal total: 4 + 2 = 6 °C².

    The waiting penalty is 2 × 2 = 4 °C². The value we seek is therefore 6 + 4 = 10 °C². All the additions combine squared degrees; multiplying a duration by σ alone would give an incorrect unit.

    Check: the conditional mean of X at T is 2 °C. Its square is 4 °C², less than 6 °C² by exactly 2 °C², the conditional variance. The total 10 is therefore not a variance: it also contains the square of a mean deviation and a penalty.

  5. Recover the noise coefficient as well

    The value formula suggests the deterministic function u(t,a) below, in °C², where a is a possible deviation, in °C. Writing Yt = u(t,Xt) lets us use Ito’s formula, already encountered in the prerequisites. The derivatives ∂tu, ∂au, and ∂aau are, respectively, the time derivative, in °C²/min, the derivative with respect to the deviation, in °C, and the second derivative with respect to the deviation, which is dimensionless.

    u(t,a)=a2+(σ2+c)(T−t)

    Ito’s formula gives the product σ∂au as the coefficient of dW. Since ∂au = 2a, the second unknown is:

    Zt=2σXt

    At the observed point, Zt = 2 × 1 × 2 = 4 °C²/√min. It describes the sensitivity of Y to Brownian noise; its dimension differs from that of Y. Its value can be negative when the deviation is negative.

  6. Check the drift, the boundary, and initial consistency

    For our function, ∂tu = −(σ squared + c) and ∂aau = 2. The drift term in Ito’s formula combines the time variation and the variance correction:

    dYt=(∂tu+12σ2∂aau)dt+σ∂audWt

    The factor 1/2 × 2 is 1. Numerically, the drift is -3 + 1 = -2 °C²/min, which is −c. We recover exactly dY = −c dt + Z dW, with the correct sign. Removing the Ito correction would give a different drift.

    At the boundary t = T, the remaining time is 0: u(T,a) is the square of a. Thus Y at T does equal ξ. Y is adapted, and Z is continuous and adapted, hence predictable; their integrability follows here from the moments of Brownian motion on the finite interval. These checks construct a solution for this example without stating a general existence theorem.

    At the start, the square of the deviation is 1 °C² and the future variance is 4 °C²: the mean terminal total is 5 °C². Waiting adds 8 °C²; initial Y is 13 °C². This initial number is deterministic, whereas later values of Y follow the information revealed. The next unit will address existence conditions beyond this example.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the formulas. Reconstruct the present value from the terminal total; explain where the future variance comes from. Then recover Z using Ito’s formula and justify why making an integral’s mean 0 does not let us remove that integral path by path.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The noise has mean 0: I can simply remove it from the square.”

    The linear term with mean 0 averages to 0, but the square of the noise has a mean equal to its variance. Omitting this contribution gives 8 °C² instead of 10 °C²; the difference of 2 °C² is exactly the future variance.

    Revisit the expansion of the square and the unit check

  • “Backward means that I enter the known final temperature into the present calculation.”

    The terminal rule is given, but its future realization is not. Yₜ must be ℱₜ-measurable. A complete path can be used to verify the identity afterwards, but it must not supply the present calculation with future information.

    Reread the directions of information and calculation

  • “The Ito integral has mean 0, so Z can be 0.”

    The cancellation is conditional and holds in expectation, under the stated integrability condition. In our example, Zₜ = 2σXₜ remains necessary to carry the fluctuations of the value. The check after the final observation shows an integral that is indeed nonzero.

    Check the identity on a realization in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Understand the information

A value calculated with the right numbers can anticipate the future

At time t = 2 min, one person proposes Yₜ = ξ + c(T − t), using the actual ξ that the experiment will produce later. Another uses Yₜ = E[ξ | ℱₜ] + c(T − t). Which proposal is adapted? Calculate its value using the scenario data. Explain why we cannot simply set Z to 0.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Ask which quantities are known at time t before performing the additions.

Hint 2

The terminal mean is the square of the present deviation plus the future variance; the noise integral disappears only in the conditional expectation.

Compare with the solution

The second proposal is adapted: it uses ℱt and known constants. The first depends on a future realization of ξ and is generally not ℱt-measurable. In our scenario, with nonzero σ and t before T, this realization remains random given the present.

The mean terminal total is 4 + 2 = 6 °C². The waiting penalty is 4 °C²; the adapted value is 10 °C². Setting Z to 0 would impose dY = −c dt: an evolution without noise could not reach the random ξ of this experiment. The required coefficient at the current point is 4 °C²/√min.

Check your reasoning

  • You identify the permitted information and the forbidden future realization.
  • You recover 10 °C² while keeping the variance contribution.
  • You distinguish cancellation in expectation from the disappearance of noise on every path.

Exercise 2 · Exact calculation on paper

A deviation on the other side of the setpoint

In the same experiment, consider another possible observation point: τ = 3/2 min, or 1.50 min, and X at τ = -1/2 °C, or -0.50 °C. The parameters T, σ, and c remain those of the example. Calculate E[ξ | ℱ at τ], Y at τ, and Z at τ. Explain the sign of Z without interpreting it as a probability.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First calculate T − τ, then the square of the negative deviation. Keep exact fractions.

Hint 2

Y uses a square; Z contains the signed deviation multiplied by 2σ.

Compare with the solution

Remaining time: 4 − 3/2 = 5/2 min. The present square is 1/4 °C²; the future variance is 5/2 °C². Their sum is 11/4 °C², or 2.75 °C².

The penalty is 2 × 5/2 = 5 °C². We obtain Y = 11/4 + 5 = 31/4 °C², or 7.75 °C². Z = 2 × 1 × (-1/2) = -1 °C²/√min.

Z is negative: at this negative deviation, a small increase in X brings the chamber closer to the setpoint and reduces the square of its deviation. The sign expresses this local sensitivity, while Y remains positive. A negative temperature here means a deviation below the setpoint, rather than necessarily a negative absolute temperature.

Check your reasoning

  • You obtain 5/2 min before calculating the future noise.
  • You give 31/4 °C² and -1 °C²/√min with their distinct units.
  • You justify the sign of Z through the sensitivity of the square.

Exercise 3 · Verification and objection

A 0 mean, a nonzero realization

After the experiment, suppose that X at T = 3 °C was observed, while retaining X at time 2 min = 2 °C. Use Ito’s formula applied to the square of X to calculate the integral of Z dW from this time to T, then verify the backward formula. Can this final result be used to calculate Y in the present during the experiment?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Ito’s formula gives d(X squared) = 2σX dW + σ squared dt. The noise coefficient is exactly Z.

Hint 2

The integral of Z dW is therefore the final square minus the present square, minus σ squared times the remaining time.

Compare with the solution

The final square is 9 °C²; the present square is 4 °C². Their difference is 5 °C². The quadratic variation contribution is 2 °C²; the integral of Z dW on this realization is therefore 3 °C².

The backward formula gives 9 + 4 − 3 = 10 °C², matching the adapted value 10 °C². Omitting the integral would give 13 °C² on this realization: a different value.

This check is performed afterwards. It does not reveal the future during the experiment. Conditional on the present, the realizations of the integral have mean 0; the value 3 °C² calculated here for a realization is therefore not contradictory. The proposed final values serve as an algebraic check, rather than an actual record of readings.

Check your reasoning

  • You connect the integral of Z dW to Ito’s formula for the square, without simulating noise.
  • You obtain 3 °C² and check the minus sign when returning to Y.
  • You reject turning a final check into information available in the present.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What makes the equation backward, and what must we seek?

The condition concerns Y at the horizon: Y at T = ξ. We seek a pair (Y,Z), with Y adapted to the present information and Z predictable to carry the noise, rather than a path that consults ξ in advance.

Recall 2Why, for the constant generator c, is Yₜ = E[ξ | ℱₜ] + c(T − t)?

Yₜ is already measurable with respect to ℱₜ. The future integral of Z dW has conditional expectation 0 if it is a martingale, here thanks to square integrability. The integral of the constant rate is c(T − t). The stochastic integral remains present on every path.

Recall 3Which contribution would we miss by using only the square of the future mean deviation?

We would miss the conditional variance σ squared times (T − t). Expanding the square of Xₜ + σΔW cancels the cross term in expectation but retains the square of the noise.

Recall 4How do we recover Z in the example, and what does its sign mean?

For Yₜ = u(t,Xₜ), Ito’s formula gives Zₜ = σ∂ₐu(t,Xₜ) = 2σXₜ. It is a sensitivity to noise, in °C²/√min. Its sign follows the deviation from the setpoint; this coefficient is not a probability.

Recall 5Which checks make the proposed pair acceptable in this example?

Recover the drift −c and the coefficient Z using Ito’s formula; check the boundary Y at T = ξ; check adaptedness, predictability, and integrability. The conditional expectation and the identity after the final observation provide complementary checks. This does not prove a general existence theorem.
