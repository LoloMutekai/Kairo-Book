Level 2 · C050·M5

Conditioning

Your goalQuantify the sensitivity of a reconstruction to measurement errors, then distinguish this sensitivity from a computational error.

Before you start
c048-m5 : positivity · c040-m5 : least squares · c050-m1 : Cholesky · c050-m2 : SVD · c050-m3 : pseudoinverse · c050-m4 : low rank

✿01 See the idea

A positioning stage moves horizontally and vertically. We want to recover these displacements from its sensors. The horizontal sensor reports the displacement directly; the vertical sensor reports only 0.1 times the displacement. To recover the displacement from its reading, we therefore need to multiply by 10. The problem appears in this inverse step: the multiplication also magnifies the sensor error.

In our fictional scene, the stage is at 20 mm horizontally and 0 mm vertically. The ideal readings are 20 mm and 0 mm. If the vertical channel adds 1 mm to its reading, the reconstruction invents 10 mm of vertical displacement. A perfectly exact calculation can therefore reconstruct a very different position.

Conditioning describes this sensitivity of the problem: by how much can a relative error in the data be amplified in the solution? Here it depends on the contrast between the channel sensitivities. Stability describes how a computational method behaves in the presence of rounding errors. We will isolate the first question by calculating exactly.

All lengths are expressed in millimetres, including the attenuated sensor outputs. Their calibration and units remain fixed. The values are invented for the calculation; they describe neither a real machine nor observed performance.

The rule to remember

A direction compressed by the measurement becomes a direction amplified by the reconstruction. Compare relative errors using a stated norm; the maximum sensitivity is a bound, rather than the error in every measurement.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A small change in the measurement, a much larger change in the position

Keep the calibration fixed and add only 1 mm to the vertical channel. We will solve the ideal system and then the perturbed system, compare their solutions and check the stated amplification.

  1. State the system and what can vary

    x is the column vector of displacements; b is the column vector of ideal readings. The subscripts h and v mean horizontal and vertical. Each component is in mm. The calibration matrix A is known, square and invertible; its coefficients are dimensionless. Throughout this page, only the data vector b varies: no calibration error in A is modelled.

    A=[100110]
    Ax=b

    The system reads bh = xh and bᵥ = 0.1 xᵥ. Substituting (20 ; 0) mm for x gives b = (20 ; 0) mm. Conversely, dividing each reading by the sensitivity of its channel recovers x = (20 ; 0) mm.

  2. Choose how to measure an error and calculate the conditioning

    The double bars ‖u‖ always denote the Euclidean norm: the length of vector u, calculated as the square root of the sum of the squares of its components. For a vector of lengths, this norm is in mm. For a matrix, ‖A‖ denotes the largest ratio ‖Au‖/‖u‖ over all nonzero vectors u: its maximum stretching factor.

    ‖u‖=uh2+uv2

    The singular values of A are its stretching factors in the singular directions. For this positive diagonal matrix, these directions are the horizontal and vertical axes: σ_max = 1 and σ_min = 0.1, both dimensionless. Here, ‖A‖ = σ_max and the norm of the inverse of A is 10. In the formula, the negative exponent indicates the matrix inverse.

    κ(A)=‖A‖‖A−1‖=σmaxσmin=10.1=10

    κ, the letter kappa, is the dimensionless condition number. This definition uses the Euclidean norm and an invertible matrix. It is at least 1; the larger it is, the more the problem can amplify relative errors. If σ_min were 0, this unique inverse reconstruction would no longer be defined. A small positive singular value preserves uniqueness but can make the reconstruction sensitive.

  3. Solve exactly with the perturbed reading

    δb is the error vector added to the readings, in mm; the letter δ is pronounced “delta”. We take δb = (0 ; 1) mm. The received data vector is b′ = b + δb = (20 ; 1) mm. The prime denotes the perturbed version, with no change in time or calibration.

    x′ is the solution of Ax′ = b′. The horizontal component remains 20 ÷ 1 = 20 mm. For the vertical component, we need to undo the attenuation: 1 ÷ 0.1 = 10 mm. Thus x′ = (20 ; 10) mm and δx = x′ − x = (0 ; 10) mm.

    The calculation gives ‖δb‖ = 1 mm and ‖δx‖ = 10 mm. We know the reference x here because the experiment is constructed; a real measurement does not automatically reveal this reconstruction error.

  4. Compare errors with the reference magnitudes

    The relative error e_b compares the length of δb with that of b; e_x does the same for δx and x. The reference vectors are nonzero and each has a norm of 20 mm. The ratios are dimensionless. We do not divide the vertical error by xᵥ, which is 0: these are relative errors of the whole vectors.

    eb=‖δb‖‖b‖=120=0.05=5 %
    ex=‖δx‖‖x‖=1020=0.50=50 %

    To convert a ratio to a percentage, multiply it by 100. The observed relative amplification is e_x/e_b = 0.50/0.05 = 10. The 50 % is a geometric error in this fictional example, rather than a success rate or a probability.

  5. Derive the bound and understand why equality holds here

    Subtracting Ax = b from Ax′ = b′ gives Aδx = δb. Applying the inverse of A then gives δx. The definition of the matrix norm bounds ‖δx‖ by the product of the norm of the inverse and ‖δb‖. Also, b = Ax gives ‖b‖ ≤ ‖A‖ ‖x‖.

    Divide the first bound by ‖x‖. The second gives an upper bound of ‖A‖/‖b‖ for 1/‖x‖. Substituting this upper bound and then grouping the two matrix norms recovers kappa. These divisions require x and b to be nonzero:

    ‖δx‖‖x‖≤κ(A)‖δb‖‖b‖

    Numerically, κ(A)e_b = 10 × 0.05 = 0.50, or 50 %. The calculated error reaches this bound: the reference lies on the axis transmitted most strongly, and the perturbation lies on the axis amplified most by the inverse. The exercise on the other channel will show why we must not systematically replace ≤ with =.

    The bound is exact for a fixed matrix A and a perturbation of b; no first-order approximation is needed here. By itself, it does not cover an error in A. Different units or weightings would change the numerical problem whose conditioning is being measured.

  6. Check the result and separate the two diagnoses

    Substitute x′ back into the calibration: horizontally 1 × 20 = 20 mm; vertically 0.1 × 10 = 1 mm. We recover b′ exactly. The residual r′ = Ax′ − b′, meaning the discrepancy in the received equations, is 0: its vertical component is 1 − 1 = 0 mm. Yet x′ differs from the reference by 10 mm.

    A small residual therefore confirms a close fit to the equations used; by itself, it does not certify closeness to the true position. For the same data vector b′, a backward stable method produces the exact solution of a nearby system, where the computational perturbations are small relative to the data. This diagnosis of the algorithm remains distinct from κ(A), a property of the problem.

    Exact fractions eliminate rounding here without removing sensitivity to the measurement. Adding decimal places to the calculation does not recover information that the sensor supplied incorrectly. To address the problem, we need to examine measurement uncertainty and the scale of the variables; changing the model or truncating a small singular value would then require us to state explicitly the accepted loss of information.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The matrix is invertible, so its solution is reliable.”

    Invertibility ensures a unique solution for each reading, but does not ensure low sensitivity. Here σ_min = 0.1 is positive and κ(A) = 10: the low vertical sensitivity becomes strong amplification in the inverse step.

    Reread the calculation of the singular values and kappa

  • “The output error is always kappa times the input error.”

    The relation is a bound on the relative errors of the vectors. The affected channel and the reference position matter. Compare the vertical perturbation in the example with the horizontal perturbation proposed in the exercises.

    Test a change in the direction of the error

  • “The residual is 0; with more decimal places I can report the true position.”

    The residual compares the reconstruction with the received readings. If those readings are perturbed, satisfying them exactly does not make them exact. Repeat the substitution check, then distinguish measurement error, conditioning and rounding errors.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and reasoned decision

Turn a tolerance into a measurement requirement

With the same reference position and calibration, we require e_x ≤ 10 %. Use the general bound to derive a sufficient budget for ‖δb‖, in mm, covering all perturbation directions. Check the vertical case at the limit of this budget. Someone proposes keeping the initial error of 1 mm and accepting only solutions with 0 residual: does this proposal guarantee the tolerance?

Compare with the solution

The tolerance is 0.10. A sufficient condition is e_b ≤ 0.10/10 = 0.01, or 1 %. Since ‖b‖ = 20 mm, it is sufficient to require ‖δb‖ ≤ 0.01 × 20 = 0.2 mm. This requirement concerns the norm of the total noise, rather than each component separately.

At the limit in the vertical direction, the position error is 0.2/0.1 = 2 mm. Its relative error is 2/20 = 0.10, exactly 10 %. Since this direction reaches the bound for our reference, a larger budget in norm would no longer guarantee the tolerance in every direction.

The proposal based on 0 residual fails: the initial example already has 0 residual and an error of 50 %, greater than 10 %. Reliable information about the measurement error is needed; solving the received readings exactly is not enough. The calculated budget remains conditional on exact calibration and the linear model on this page.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do we define kappa for our linear system, and what must we state alongside it?

For invertible A, kappa is the product of the norm of A and the norm of its inverse. In the Euclidean norm, κ(A) = σ_max/σ_min. We must specify the problem, norm, units and perturbations considered; here A is fixed and only b varies.

Recall 2Why does the horizontal perturbation not multiply the relative error by 10?

Kappa bounds the amplification. The inverse preserves the horizontal channel and amplifies the vertical channel; in the horizontal direction of our example, the ratio of relative errors is only 1.

Recall 3What do we distinguish when an exact calculation reconstructs the position poorly?

Conditioning describes the sensitivity of the problem to the data. Stability describes how the algorithm behaves in the presence of computational errors. Calculating exactly does not remove a measurement error amplified by the inverse.

Recall 4What does a 0 residual certify, and what claim remains unjustified?

It certifies that the solution satisfies the equations with the received readings. It does not certify that those readings were exact or that the reconstructed position is close to the reference: our 0 residual accompanies an error of 50 %.

Recall 5How do we turn a relative tolerance on the position into a requirement on the reading?

Divide the tolerance by κ(A) to obtain a sufficient relative budget for b, then multiply by ‖b‖ to obtain a length. For 10 % here, this gives 1 % of the readings, or 0.2 mm for the norm of their error.
