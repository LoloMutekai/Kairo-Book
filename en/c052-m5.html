Convergence protocol

Your goalSet up a comparison at the same final time, refine the step size, and decide what the observed error actually allows you to conclude.

✿01 See the idea

A well-mixed tank contains a dye. Clean water flows in, the mixture flows out, and the volume stays constant. You want to predict the concentration after 1 min. With large time steps, Euler removes the dye too quickly. Shorter steps change the result: which result can you accept?

First, keep the same question. Start again from the same initial concentration, keep the same dilution law, and finish at the same time. Dividing the step size by 2 therefore requires multiplying the number of steps by 2. The diagram shows different time grids for the same duration, rather than different tanks.

Here, numerical convergence means approaching the solution of the model as the step size tends to 0. A convergence protocol organizes comparable calculations to look for this behavior. It also sets a tolerance before examining the results: the maximum acceptable error for the quantity being studied.

The entire scene and its values are fictional. We assume instantaneous mixing, a constant volume, and a constant dilution rate. Checking the calculation for this law does not establish that it describes a real tank. This page brings together the checks from previous units; it does not derive the integration methods again.

The rule to remember

Same model, same starting point, same final time; shorter steps; stability checked; error measured against an independent reference and a stated tolerance.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

One minute of dilution, a justified decision

The initial concentration is c₀ = 256 mg/L. The rate λ is 1 min⁻¹ and the duration T is 1 min. We want an absolute error at time T alone that is less than or equal to ε = 7 mg/L. We try Euler with h = 0.5, 0.25, then 0.125 min, retaining exact fractions throughout the operations.

  1. State what must stay the same

    The protocol fixes c0, λ, T, the method, and ε before the calculation. It measures the final concentration, rather than the time needed to reach equilibrium. Convergence to the solution as h decreases is distinct from the concentration in the tank returning to 0 as time increases.

    t is time in min; c(t) is the concentration in the model in mg/L. Its derivative c′(t), in mg/L/min, measures its rate of change. We impose the following law and its initial condition. There is no dye input, event, or change in λ during this minute.

    c′(t)=−λc(t);c(0)=c0

  2. Keep an independent solution as a reference

    The function exp is the exponential function; its derivative is itself. With constant λ, differentiating c0 exp(−λt) gives −λc0 exp(−λt), and its initial value is c0 because exp(0) = 1. This checks the original law and does not depend on Euler.

    c(t)=c0exp(−λt)

    λt is dimensionless; c(t) remains in mg/L. At T, the reference is therefore exactly 256 exp(−1) mg/L. This expression is not a rational fraction. We will bound it rather than replace it with a decimal assumed to be exact.

  3. Set the numbers of steps and check stability

    h is a positive duration in min. N = T/h must be an integer here: we obtain 2, 4, and 8 steps. The index n counts the updates; un is the numerical concentration at tn = nh. Each calculation starts with u0 = c0. U(h) will denote its final value u at n = N.

    Euler uses the slope at the start of the step: next u = current u + h × (−λ current u). Its amplification factor a = 1 − λh is dimensionless. The products λh are 0.5, 0.25, 0.125; the factors a are therefore 0.5, 0.75, 0.875.

    un+1=(1−λh)un

    For this decay equation, asymptotic stability of Euler requires |a| < 1, or equivalently 0 < λh < 2. All three choices satisfy this condition. Their factors are also positive: the sequence remains positive and decreases. This check prevents artificial amplification; it does not quantify the error at T.

  4. Work through the calculation with the largest step size

    For h = 0.5 min, the initial slope is −1 × 256 mg/L/min. Multiplying it by h gives the change during the first Euler step. Factoring gives the same operation: 256 × 0.5 = 128 mg/L.

    Start from this new value, with the same factor: 128 × 0.5 = 64 mg/L. After 2 steps, the time is 2 × 0.5 = 1 min. Another way to write this: the square of a is 1/4, then multiplying this square by the initial concentration gives 256 × 1/4 = 64 mg/L. We have calculated the final concentration, rather than only the first update.

  5. Restart from the beginning with refined step sizes

    The factor is constant within each calculation: applying the factor N times is equivalent to calculating U(h) = c0aᴺ. For h = 0.25 min, the square of a is 3/4 × 3/4 = 9/16; its power 4 is 9/16 × 9/16 = 81/256. Thus U(h) = 256 × 81/256 = 81 mg/L after 4 steps.

    For h = 0.125 min, the square of a is 7/8 × 7/8 = 49/64; its power 4 is 49/64 × 49/64 = 2401/4096. After 4 steps, the concentration is 256 × 2401/4096 = 2401/16 mg/L. The next 4 steps apply this same cumulative factor again: 2401/16 × 2401/4096 = 5764801/65536 mg/L, or approximately 87.9639 mg/L after 8 steps. In total, a has indeed been applied 8 times.

    These successive squarings shorten the multiplications; they do not change the method. As an independent check, repeating the additive Euler recurrence, with the slope recalculated at each step, gives the same fractions. All three durations N × h equal T, and all three results lie between 0 and c0.

  6. Bound the reference, then each error

    To make the reference verifiable, use the alternating series for exp(−1). For an integer m, let Sm denote the sum of the terms (−1)ʲ/j!, for j from 0 to m. j! is the product of the positive integers up to j, with 0! = 1. The magnitudes of the terms decrease toward 0; a sum stopped at an odd index is below the limit, and a sum stopped at an even index is above it.

    The indices 11 and 10 give, in rational arithmetic, 6027/64 < 256 × S at index 11 < c(T) < 256 × S at index 10 < 6028/64. These concentrations are in mg/L. Thus c(T) lies strictly between 94.171875 and 94.1875 mg/L. The width of this interval is included in all the following checks.

    Define E(h) = |U(h) − c(T)|, in mg/L. Here all the U(h) values are below the lower bound for c(T). We therefore subtract U(h) from both reference bounds. For the large step size: 30.171875 < E(0.5 min) < 30.1875 mg/L. For the medium step size: 13.171875 < E(0.25 min) < 13.1875 mg/L.

    For the fine step size, the exact bounds are 406847/65536 and 407871/65536 mg/L. We obtain them from 6027/64 − 5764801/65536 and 1507/16 − 5764801/65536. Their rounded values are approximately 6.2080 and 6.2236 mg/L; the exact fractions determine the decision. The upper bound is less than ε = 7 mg/L: only the fine step size satisfies our tolerance among the choices tested.

  7. Make a diagnosis when the reference is hidden

    Now hide the reference without changing the tank. D(h) = |U(h) − U(h/2)| measures the difference between two calculations, in mg/L. The first difference is 81 − 64 = 17 mg/L; the next is 5764801/65536 − 81 = 456385/65536 mg/L, or approximately 6.9639 mg/L. They decrease.

    R=D(h)D(h2)

    The useful ratio is R = D(h)/D(h/2), which is dimensionless: here 17/(456385/65536) = 1114112/456385, or approximately 2.441. The D in the numerator and the D in the denominator correspond to different starting step sizes.

    If the signed error follows U(h) − c(T) ≈ C hᵖ, with the same nonzero coefficient C and sufficiently small neglected terms, this ratio is close to 2ᵖ. p denotes the global order; C carries the units needed for C hᵖ to be in mg/L. This is the asymptotic regime: the leading term actually describes the error. For Euler, p = 1, and we expect a ratio close to 2. Our value of 2.441 is still appreciably different: these differences alone do not establish that this regime has been reached.

    Only under these same assumptions can the error in the finest calculation be estimated by D for the last pair, divided by (2ᵖ − 1). For Euler, this gives approximately 6.9639 mg/L here. This diagnostic is not a guaranteed bound. Similar results can share the same bias; a plateau can also come from rounding. The independent reference allowed a firmer decision.

  8. State a verdict and its scope

    Report: law and parameters fixed; Euler; T = 1 min; h = 0.5, 0.25, 0.125 min; N = 2, 4, 8; concentrations 64, 81, 5764801/65536 mg/L; stability checked; final error for the fine step size less than 407871/65536 mg/L, hence less than 7 mg/L. Among these trials, we accept this step size for this tolerance at T. This bounds neither the error along the entire trajectory nor the error for another model.

    To compare with Runge-Kutta, keep the same starting point, law, observation times, and error definition; check its own stability condition, then repeat the refinement. For the classical variant with 4 stages, of order 4, a ratio close to 16 is expected for this smooth law with a constant rate, once its asymptotic regime has been reached. Each stage is a slope evaluation; the cost must count these evaluations, rather than only the steps. No Runge-Kutta result is measured on this page.

    A change in rate, a reset to 0, or clamping between calculations would also change the question. Before interpreting a difference as an integration error, check that only the time resolution has changed. Even an exact formula with a rate held fixed within each call requires this check if the rates are recalculated between calls.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I will keep the same number of steps: the comparison will be simpler.”

    You would change the simulated duration. Recalculate N = T/h and restart from the same initial state for each step size. The first exercise shows how an error in the time grid becomes a false error attributed to the method.

    Correct the dilution time grid

  • “The curve behaves well: it is accurate enough.”

    Here stability prevents a perturbation from being amplified. Our large step size is stable, but its final error exceeds 30.171875 mg/L, whereas the tolerance is 7 mg/L. You need to calculate the error in the requested quantity.

    Revisit stability, then the errors

  • “The last two results are close, so convergence is proved.”

    A difference between calculations is not an error relative to the exact solution. Look for an independent reference or state the assumptions of an error estimate. If a rate changes between trials, first restore a comparison of the same problem.

The right result at the wrong time

For the step size h = 0.125 min, someone keeps 4 steps, restarts from 256 mg/L, and finds 150.0625 mg/L. They compare this result with c(T), where T = 1 min. Calculate the time actually reached and repair the comparison.

Compare with the solution

The time reached is 4 × 0.125 = 0.5 min. The concentration does equal the initial concentration multiplied by a to the power 4, namely 256 × 2401/4096 = 2401/16 mg/L for this Euler calculation; it is not U(h) at T.

Compare with the solution

Exercise 3 · Protocol analysis

What the ratio of differences allows us to say

The reference is unavailable. You know only U = 64, 81, and 5764801/65536 mg/L for our three step sizes. Someone announces: “The ratio is close enough to 2, so the tolerance of 7 mg/L is guaranteed.” Recalculate the differences and the ratio, then write a more precise verdict. What would need checking if the dilution rate had been changed between calculations?

Hint 1

First calculate the differences between neighboring results. The ratio of two concentrations is not the expected ratio of differences.

Hint 2

The error law C hᵖ must already describe the calculations before you turn the difference into an error estimate.

Hint 3

Changing the rate changes the law to solve; shortening only the step size must preserve that law.

Compare with the solution

The differences are 17 and 456385/65536 mg/L. Their ratio is 1114112/456385, or approximately 2.441. It shows decreasing differences; it does not match the limiting ratio 2 for Euler and does not suffice to confirm the asymptotic regime.

Assuming a leading error C h at these step sizes, the last difference would provide an estimate of approximately 6.9639 mg/L for the finest step size. This estimate alone does not guarantee ε = 7 mg/L: an error bound or a sufficiently verified reference is missing. Further refinements can strengthen the diagnosis without becoming a general proof on their own.

If λ has changed between calculations, first restore the same law and the same schedule for any changes in it. The current differences would then mix model variation with discretization. They cannot be attributed solely to the step size.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate a dimensionless ratio of differences.
  • Distinguish results approaching one another, a conditional estimate, and a guaranteed bound.
  • Keep the parameters and law of the same problem when refining the step size.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must be fixed before shortening the step size?

The law and its parameters, the initial state, the method, the same final time, the quantity whose error is measured, and its tolerance. Here N = T/h: shortening h requires more steps.

Recall 2Why can a stable calculation be rejected?

Stability does not bound the final error by the required tolerance. In the tank, the large step size remains stable but its error exceeds 30.171875 mg/L, for a tolerance of 7 mg/L.

Recall 3Why bound the exponential solution instead of treating it as an exact decimal?

The exponential value at T is not rational. Two exact bounds checked independently of Euler give error bounds that include the uncertainty in reading the reference.

Recall 4When do we expect a ratio of differences close to 2ᵖ?

When the signed error is dominated by C hᵖ, with the same nonzero C and sufficiently small neglected terms at the tested step sizes. A ratio of differences is a diagnostic; it is not a guaranteed error bound.

Recall 5What does meeting our tolerance at T prove, and what remains to be established?

It bounds the final numerical error of our model for this step size. It does not bound the entire trajectory, validate the real tank, or establish general convergence of a method. The same problem must also be kept between trials.
