Level 2 · C059·M6

Discrete deterministic Bellman equation

Your goalSolve a finite-horizon route problem by working backwards through the costs, justify the Bellman recurrence, and reconstruct an optimal path.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

You are carrying a parcel from depot D to location F. You must go through A or B, then through C or E. Each link in the diagram shows a known travel time. Setting off towards A takes less time than setting off towards B. Is that enough to choose the whole route? Before continuing, make a prediction and write down what you are comparing.

To decide at the start, imagine a label at each location: “the least time I still need to reach F”. At C and E, you can find this label immediately because only the final link remains. At A and B, you add the chosen link to the best continuation already calculated. Work backwards like this until you reach D.

Solving subproblems and then reusing their results is called dynamic programming. Here, Bellman’s principle says that a continuation belonging to an optimal route must itself be optimal from the location and stage where it starts. Otherwise, replacing that continuation with a better one would improve the whole route.

The network and its travel times are fictional. All moves go to the next layer of the diagram, from top to bottom, with no moves back. Travel times are fixed, add together, and depend neither on arrival time nor on the past route. There is no randomness to learn about. Crossings of lines are not transfer locations.

The rule to remember

At each choice, compare the immediate cost PLUS the best remaining cost. Calculate from the end, then follow the stored choices from the start.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Calculate backwards, then deliver forwards

The parcel leaves D at stage 0 and must reach F at stage N = 3, after 3 links. A and B are at stage 1, and C and E at stage 2. There is no additional cost at F. Let us calculate all the remaining costs, then check the chosen route.

  1. Define the problem before optimizing it

    t is the integer stage index, with no physical unit; N is the final stage. A stage counts a move, not a minute. x is the current location. The action u is the next location chosen; Ut(x) is the finite set of permitted choices at stage t from x. For example, U(D) = {A, B} and U(A) = U(B) = {C, E}; from C and E, only F is permitted. Each location in this network belongs to a single stage: in the numerical calculations, we will omit the stage index and write U(x) and V(x). The general formula keeps this index.

    ft(x,u) denotes the next location; here ft(x,u) = u. The transition is deterministic: a given choice leads to a known location. ct(x,u) is the immediate travel time in minutes. g(x) is the terminal cost, also in minutes; in our network, only F is terminal and g(F) = 0 min. Every state considered allows F to be reached at the required stage.

    Vt(x), the value function, is the minimum total cost still to be paid from location x at stage t, in minutes. It is neither the cost already incurred nor the cost of just the next link. A policy specifies a permitted choice for every state and stage; it therefore contains more than a particular route.

    The constraints are the permitted links and arrival at stage N. The location and stage must be sufficient to describe future choices and costs. If a badge obtained along the route changes entry permissions, it must also be represented in the state. Here we assume that no effect of the past has been left out.

  2. Write the terminal condition and the recurrence

    VN(x)=g(x)
    Vt(x)=minu∈Ut(x){ct(x,u)+Vt+1(ft(x,u))}

    The symbol min asks for the smallest cost among actions u belonging to Ut(x), as expressed by ∈. The braces group the immediate cost and the best cost of the continuation. The index t + 1 denotes the next stage. The recurrence applies from t = N − 1 down to t = 0. The terms added have the same unit: minutes.

    Why does this equality hold? Any continuation after choice u costs at least V at the next state, so the complete cost is at least the minimum shown. Conversely, choosing an action that attains this minimum, followed by an optimal continuation, attains this bound. The sets are finite and continuations are feasible, so these minima are actually attained. The terminal condition then starts a backward argument.

    There is no average over possible outcomes and no discount factor here: we minimize a finite sum of known travel times. No derivative or convexity assumption is needed to compare choices in this network.

  3. Start from F, then handle C and E

    At F, delivery is complete: V(F) = 0 min. From C, the only choice is F: V(C) = 2 + 0 = 2 min. From E: V(E) = 1 + 0 = 1 min.

    The addition explicitly keeps the terminal cost. It is 0 in this example; removing it from the definition would mean forgetting arrival costs when they exist. We store F as the action from both C and E.

  4. Solve the choices from A and B separately

    From A, going through C costs 8 + 2 = 10 min; going through E costs 6 + 1 = 7 min. The minimum is 7 min, so we store E. This calculation compares complete continuations from A.

    V1(A)=min(10;7)=7 min

    From B, going through C costs 1 + 2 = 3 min; going through E costs 5 + 1 = 6 min. The minimum is 3 min, so we store C.

    V1(B)=min(3;6)=3 min

    We used the values of C and E in the calculations for A and B without solving their continuations again. This reuse organizes the work.

  5. Return to the start and reconstruct a policy

    From D, branch A costs 1 + 7 = 8 min; branch B costs 3 + 3 = 6 min. Thus V(D) = 6 min and the optimal first choice is B. Choosing only the shortest initial link would lead to A and a best possible total of 8 min.

    The stored policy chooses B from D, E from A, C from B, and F from C or E. To extract the route from D, follow only the choices encountered: D → B → C → F. We move forwards, even though we calculated the values in the opposite direction.

    Direct sum for the chosen route: 3 + 1 + 2 = 6 min. Past travel times are not added into V(B): at the start, they are already counted by the term 3. Adding them again would count the same link more than once.

  6. Check using a different method

    The network has 2 choices at the start, then 2 intermediate choices. Let us enumerate the complete routes: D → A → C → F takes 1 + 8 + 2 = 11 min; D → A → E → F takes 1 + 6 + 1 = 8 min; D → B → C → F takes 3 + 1 + 2 = 6 min; D → B → E → F takes 3 + 5 + 1 = 9 min.

    The smallest of these totals is indeed 6 min, attained only by D → B → C → F. The difference from the best branch through A is 8 − 6 = 2 min in this fictional model. This calculated difference is not a saving measured on real deliveries.

    Finally, all links in the route are permitted and their number is N = 3. A low total involving a forbidden link would not be a feasible solution. Enumeration is enough to check this small network; the recurrence organizes the calculation when several routes share continuations.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the values. Explain why the continuation from B can be calculated without knowing the cost of the route already travelled. Then reconstruct the terminal condition, the comparison at each layer, and the chosen path. Which assumption would fail if a badge acquired earlier changed entry permissions?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The shortest next move gives the best route.”

    At the start, A costs 1 min, but its best continuation costs 7 min: total 8 min. B costs 3 min followed by 3 min: total 6 min. Always add the value of the continuation before comparing.

    Review the first move and the best continuation

  • “The value already includes everything I have spent.”

    V denotes only the remaining cost from the specified state. V(B) is 3 min; the cost of D → B is added at D. First define what the value counts and keep the terminal condition.

    Revisit the definition and reconstruction

  • “The location name is always enough to define the state.”

    Arriving at the same location by different routes can leave different entry permissions if a badge was obtained on only one route. The state must then also retain this badge and the stage. Replacing an optimal continuation must remain feasible with the same information; here we have explicitly excluded this effect of the past.

    Reread the assumption about future choices

Exercise 1 · Application · terminal condition

Unloading takes time

In the original network, unloading at F now takes 4 min, whatever the route. Calculate V(F), V(C), V(E), then V(B) and its choice. Explain why this time must be added only once.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The terminal condition becomes g(F) = 4 min. The link to F does not include unloading.

Hint 2

From B, compare 1 + V(C) and 5 + V(E).

Compare with the solution

V(F) = 4 min. V(C) = 2 + 4 = 6 min; V(E) = 1 + 4 = 5 min. These values already include unloading.

From B: via C, 1 + 6 = 7 min; via E, 5 + 5 = 10 min. V(B) = 7 min, with choice C. Direct check of the chosen continuation: B → C → F, then unloading, costs 1 + 2 + 4 = 7 min.

Unloading occurs at the end of every possible route, once in each. The alternatives are not added together. There is therefore no need to add g(F) again at every layer.

Check your reasoning

  • I write the terminal condition in minutes.
  • I obtain 6, 5 and 7 min while keeping choice C from B.
  • I check a complete sum and identify the single terminal cost.

Exercise 2 · Backward recalculation

Roadworks on the final link

Start again from the original network, with g(F) = 0 min. Only the travel time C → F changes, from 2 to 5 min. Recalculate all values and choices from A, B and D. Identify any ties and give the optimal route from D.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First recalculate the value of C. The value of E does not change.

Hint 2

At B, compare the new branch through C with the branch through E. A tie allows several choices.

Hint 3

Also recalculate the totals from the start: the previous first choice is not a permanent instruction.

Compare with the solution

At the end: V(F) = 0 min. Then V(C) = 5 min and V(E) = 1 min. From A: via C, 8 + 5 = 13 min; via E, 6 + 1 = 7 min. V(A) = 7 min and its choice remains E.

From B: via C, 1 + 5 = 6 min; via E, 5 + 1 = 6 min. The costs are equal: V(B) = 6 min. C and E are both optimal choices from B.

From D: via A, 1 + 7 = 8 min; via B, 3 + 6 = 9 min. V(D) = 8 min and the optimal route is now D → A → E → F. Direct check: 1 + 6 + 1 = 8 min. The tied choices from B do not make B optimal from D.

Check your reasoning

  • I start from the end, discarding the terminal cost from the previous exercise.
  • I retain the tied choices C and E from B.
  • I find D → A → E → F and 8 min, with a sum to check the result.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · check a boundary

When should the first branch change?

Restore the original network, except that the travel time D → B becomes a nonnegative parameter p, in minutes. Without enumerating everything again, give V(D) as a function of p and the threshold for changing the first choice. Handle equality, then check the case p = 6 min. Explain why the values of A and B can still be used.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The parameter changes only a link before A and B. Their remaining costs are still 7 and 3 min.

Hint 2

Compare 1 + 7 with p + 3. Solve the equality before the inequalities.

Compare with the solution

V(D) = min(8 ; p + 3), in minutes. The threshold satisfies p + 3 = 8, so p = 8 − 3 = 5 min. If 0 ≤ p < 5, choose B; if p > 5, choose A. At p = 5 min, both branches are optimal, each followed by its best continuation.

For p = 6 min, branch B costs 6 + 3 = 9 min, compared with 8 min via A. The optimal cost is 8 min and the route is D → A → E → F. Its sum is 1 + 6 + 1 = 8 min.

Only a cost already incurred by the time we reach A or B has changed. The future links and their travel times remain identical: their values do not depend on p. If changing p also changed entry permissions or future travel times, this reuse would need to be reconsidered. The formula assumes that p is expressed in minutes and that the same state remains sufficient.

Check your reasoning

  • I write both complete costs and the threshold 5 min.
  • I distinguish the two strict inequalities from the equality case.
  • I verify 8 min for the requested case and explain why the subproblems remain unchanged.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does V count at a stage, and in what unit?

The minimum total cost still to be paid until the end from the specified state and stage. Here it is expressed in minutes and excludes travel times already incurred.

Recall 2Reconstruct Bellman’s comparison without reciting the route.

For each permitted choice, add its immediate cost and the value of the next state, then take the minimum. At the final stage, the value is the terminal cost g. Calculate these values by working backwards through the stages.

Recall 3Why can a worse continuation not belong to an optimal route?

At the same state and stage, replacing it with a less costly feasible continuation would reduce the total. This assumes that future choices and costs are fully described by that state and stage.

Recall 4What is the difference between a value, a policy and an optimal route?

The value gives a minimum cost. A policy gives a choice for every state and stage. Following these choices from the start produces a route; a minimum may be attained by several choices.

Recall 5Why does the existence of a deterministic rule not prove Bellman optimization?

A rule can choose a time slot or a link without comparing the immediate cost with the best future cost. You need to identify the criterion, transitions, terminal condition, and the recurrence actually calculated; reading the code does not prove that it runs.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the calculations, reconstruct this sequence: define the sufficient state and feasible choices; set the horizon and terminal cost; compare immediate cost and future value; calculate backwards; store the choices; follow the route forwards; check its feasibility and sum. Finish by explaining why choosing the shortest first move can fail.
