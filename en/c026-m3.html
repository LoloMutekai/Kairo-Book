Level 2 · C026·M3

LRU and a memory budget

Your goalReplay an LRU cache, choose which items to evict, and check a memory budget when item sizes differ.

Before you start
c019-m5 : clocks and persistence · c025-m5 : O, Omega and Theta · c020-m5 : counterexamples and sensitivity · c024-m5 : measurement quality · c026-m1 : queue and priority · c026-m2 : admission and capacity

✿01 See the idea

An image viewer keeps image previews in memory so that it can retrieve them when they are opened again. This temporary store is a cache. The original images remain available: removing a preview from the cache only means that it must be recomputed if it is needed again. Our reserved cache budget is 12 KiB.

Imagine these previews arranged from left to right, from the oldest request to the most recent. After opening A, B, then C, you open A again. A becomes the most recently used preview, even though it was the first to enter the cache. If space is needed later, B will leave before A.

This policy is called LRU, for least recently used: it evicts the item whose last use was longest ago. An eviction removes an entry from the cache. The rule uses the past as a clue for its choice; it does not know the next request and does not guarantee the best choice for what follows.

All sizes are fictional. A KiB, or kibibyte, represents 1024 bytes. The stated sizes include all the space counted for each entry, including its management information. The budget covers the entries kept in the cache; computation buffers and the rest of the program need a separate memory reserve. This model does not measure the total memory of a real application.

The rule to remember

Whenever a requested entry is present, move it to the most recent end. For an absent entry that fits within the budget on its own, evict the least recently used entries until there is enough space, then insert it.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Open A, B, C, open A again, then open D

The cache is initially empty. The immutable previews A, B, C and D use 4, 3, 5 and 7 KiB respectively. The budget is 12 KiB, or 12288 bytes. Let us replay exactly the sequence A → B → C → A → D. Each letter identifies the requested preview; every entry can be evicted.

  1. Define what the bound counts

    b denotes the budget in KiB; s(x) denotes the counted size of the preview identified by x, in the same unit. L denotes the ordered list of identifiers kept in the cache, with no duplicates. M(L) is their total memory. The symbol ∑ asks you to add s(x) for every identifier x in L; the symbol ∈ means “belongs to”. The capital letters A, B, C and D name the previews, not amounts of memory.

    M(L)=∑x∈Ls(x);0≤M(L)≤b

    Assume that sizes are positive, known before admission, and fixed throughout the exercise. Accesses are processed one after another: there are no simultaneous accesses or ties in their order. An entry on the left was used before those on the right. The bound must hold after every cache operation; computing a preview outside the cache uses a separate memory reserve.

  2. State the decision before moving entries

    If the requested preview is present, provide it and then move its identifier to the right: this is a cache hit. M(L) does not change. If it is absent, this is a cache miss. First examine its size: if s(x) exceeds b, give up keeping it without emptying the cache. The viewer can compute and display it outside the cache if its computation memory reserve allows it.

    Otherwise, while M(L) + s(x) exceeds b, remove the leftmost identifier and subtract its size from M(L). Once the sum fits, insert x on the right and add its size. Testing before admission prevents the entries kept in the cache from exceeding the budget. A simple condition executed only once is not enough: several removals may be needed.

  3. Fill the cache from empty

    Initially, M(L) = 0 KiB. A is absent and fits on its own: keep it, using 4 KiB, in the order [A]. B is absent: 4 + 3 = 7 KiB fits within 12 KiB; the order becomes [A, B]. C is absent: 7 + 5 = 12 KiB. Matching the budget exactly is allowed, so no entry leaves.

    The cache now contains 3 entries in the order [A, B, C]. Each was used when it was inserted; C is the most recent.

  4. Open A again and update its recency

    A is already present. Its content is provided without creating a new entry. Removing A from its position and then putting it back on the right gives [B, C, A]. Memory remains 12 KiB and the number of entries remains 3.

    A was the first preview inserted, but B now has the oldest last use. Choosing by insertion order would lose this information. We are tracking accesses, including repeated ones.

  5. Test the arrival of D

    D is missing and its size of 7 KiB does not exceed the budget of 12 KiB: it can be admitted after some removals. Keeping it immediately would require 12 + 7 = 19 KiB, beyond the bound. This total is a trial calculation, not an allowed cache state.

    M(L)+s(D)≤b⇔M(L)≤b−s(D)

    The symbol ≤ means “less than or equal to”; ⇔ means that the conditions on either side are equivalent. Subtracting the size to reserve sets the target: 12 − 7 = 5 KiB at most before insertion. We will remove entries by recency until this target is met.

  6. Evict and retest after each removal

    B is on the left: evict it. Memory falls to 12 − 3 = 9 KiB; the order is [C, A]. This is not enough: 9 is greater than the target of 5 KiB. We can also check by reserving space for D: 9 + 7 = 16 KiB, still beyond 12 KiB.

    C is now on the left: evict it in turn. Memory becomes 9 − 5 = 4 KiB; only A remains. This time, 4 KiB does not exceed 5 KiB. The loop stops. The sizes removed determine how much space is freed, not the number of removals considered on its own.

  7. Insert D and check by another method

    Keep D to the right of A. The result is [A, D], with 4 + 7 = 11 KiB. The spare capacity is 12 − 11 = 1 KiB. The number of entries goes from 3 to 3 − 2 + 1 = 2: the 2 removals left 1 entry before insertion.

    Independent check using the remaining items: s(A) + s(D) = 4 + 7 = 11 KiB. Check by conservation: the removals free 3 + 5 = 8 KiB; 12 − 8 + 7 = 11 KiB. These methods give the same memory use, and the bound holds.

  8. State exactly what the result guarantees

    The final order respects the last uses of the remaining previews: A was opened again before D. Counted memory is bounded and no identifier is duplicated. If D fits on its own, the removals terminate: even if every entry left, the empty cache would leave enough room for it. Equality M(L) + s(D) = b is allowed.

    The cache does not know whether the next request will be for B. Its rule proves neither a time saving nor a reduction in the total memory of the program. If actual sizes include costs that were overlooked, their accounting must be corrected before claiming an actual bound.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A entered first, so it must leave first.”

    Opening A again counts as a use. Move A to the right before choosing the next removal: [B, C, A]. LRU compares last uses; B now has the oldest request.

    Find the movement of A in the diagram

  • “An entry arrives, so I remove an entry and that is enough.”

    After removing B, 9 KiB remains; with D, 16 KiB would be needed for a budget of 12 KiB. Retest the sum and remove C as well. A loop controlled by available space is needed because the sizes differ.

    Replay the removals and the space check

  • “A limit of 3 entries guarantees our memory budget.”

    A, B, C use 12 KiB; A, C, D would use 16. These groups each have 3 entries. Without a bound on each size, an entry count does not bound their memory; add the sizes actually counted.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · repair a rule

Eviction does not make every entry admissible

Start again from [A, D]. A new preview E uses 14 KiB. Someone proposes: “Remove old entries until E fits; a cache of at most 3 entries will be enough.” Repair their admission rule and their argument about memory. Give the state to keep. To test the entry limit, also compare the groups A, B, C and A, C, D.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Before any removal, compare s(E) with the budget of a completely empty cache.

Hint 2

An entry limit counts objects. To check KiB, add the sizes in each proposed group.

Compare with the solution

E exceeds the budget on its own: 14 − 12 = 2 KiB too much. Even emptying the cache is not enough. The rule must check this impossibility before any evictions: do not keep E, and keep [A, D], at 11 KiB. Displaying it outside the cache remains possible only if the planned computation memory reserve allows it.

The limit of 3 entries is not proof of a memory budget: A, B, C total 4 + 3 + 5 = 12 KiB, whereas A, C, D total 4 + 5 + 7 = 16 KiB. The second group exceeds 12 KiB with the same number of entries.

The repaired rule therefore separates whether E can be admitted, the choice of removals by recency, and the check by summing sizes. It avoids losing all the previews without being able to keep the new one.

Check your reasoning

  • You refuse to keep E before removing any entry and preserve [A, D].
  • You justify this refusal even for an empty cache, since 14 KiB exceeds 12 KiB.
  • You provide the totals of 12 and 16 KiB and explain why counting 3 objects is not enough.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does LRU order, and what changes when an entry is accessed again?

LRU orders last uses. A found entry moves to the most recent end without being duplicated; with a fixed size, accessing it again does not change memory use.

Recall 2What test decides whether an absent entry can be kept now?

The sum of the memory already counted and the size of the new entry must be less than or equal to the budget, in the same unit. Otherwise, if the entry fits on its own, space must be freed.

Recall 3Why does the arrival of D make B and then C leave in our trace?

After opening A again, the order is [B, C, A]. Removing B leaves 9 KiB; adding D would require 16 KiB, beyond the budget of 12 KiB. Removing C leaves 4 KiB; D can then be admitted for a total of 11 KiB.

Recall 4What should you do if an entry exceeds the budget on its own?

Give up keeping it before any unnecessary eviction. Computing it outside the cache requires a separate memory reserve. The existing cache can be preserved; even empty, it could not keep this entry.

Recall 5Why does a cache with a bounded entry count not prove a bound on actual memory?

You also need to know or bound the entry sizes and all the costs you want to count. The sum of the retained sizes bounds this scope, without measuring temporary buffers or the rest of the program.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without looking at the example, reconstruct the trace A → B → C → A → D. Recover the total from the remaining entries, then explain the cases of exact equality and an oversized entry.
