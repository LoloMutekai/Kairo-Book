Level 4 · C253·M5

Product–model comparison

Your goalCompare models for a specific contract: check their consistency, calculate a valuation difference and distinguish this disagreement from a measured error.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A team needs to value a call option that is cancelled if the underlying falls below a threshold on a monitoring date. The team is considering models that reproduce the same prices of vanilla options. Is looking only at the final value enough to choose?

Imagine an outcome of 120 € after passing through 90 €, then the same outcome after passing through 110 €. With a barrier at 95 €, the first path cancels the contract; the second retains a payoff of 20 €. A histogram of the final outcomes does not show the paths taken to reach them.

A marginal distribution describes the price distribution at a single date. A joint distribution describes the probabilities of price pairs across several dates. A vanilla European option pays only according to the final price; our barrier option also depends on the observed path. Comparing models therefore starts by identifying what the product observes.

A local volatility model determines volatility from the date and the current price. A local stochastic volatility model adds a random variance state and a leverage function. Adjusting this function to recover European option prices does not, by itself, determine the full distribution of paths. The additional complexity must be assessed for the intended contract and use.

The entire scenario is fictional. Distributions A and B below are finite trees constructed to isolate this question; they are not presented as outputs from local volatility or local stochastic volatility software. The lesson recalculates neither their continuous calibration nor a hedging strategy.

The rule to remember

Fix the contract and conventions, check the models and numerical accuracy, then compare prices, sensitivities and cost using the same criteria. A shared calibration to European options does not select the dynamics for a barrier option.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

One barrier option, two distributions of paths

The initial price is 100 €. Intermediate monitoring takes place at t = 1 year, and maturity at T = 2 years. The strike price K is 100 € and the barrier H is 95 €. The contract covers 1 unit of the underlying. Interest rates and dividends are 0; the discount factor is 1. The table weights provide all the data for the fictional models.

  1. Write exactly what the contract pays

    S denotes the price of the underlying, in euros. In the formulas, its subscripts t and T denote intermediate monitoring and maturity respectively. We will also write s for the intermediate value and x for the final value. Times t and T are in years. K and H are thresholds in euros.

    The contract pays the positive part of x − K if s > H and x > H; otherwise it pays 0 €. For an amount u in euros, its positive part u⁺ means max(u, 0). Hitting H, even exactly, cancels the contract without a rebate. There are no observations between the specified dates. This convention is essential when comparing models.

    G(s,x)=(x−K)+1s>H,x>H

    G is the payoff in euros. The subscripted indicator equals 1 when both conditions are true and 0 otherwise. With these values, only the pair (110 €, 120 €) pays: 120 − 100 = 20 €. The 90 € row is knocked out, and the other columns offer no positive gain.

  2. Convert weights to probabilities and check the marginal distributions

    m denotes A or B; Qm is the pricing distribution of model m, called risk-neutral: we will check that it preserves the discounted price in expectation. The dimensionless weight wm(s, x) is the number in a cell. Its joint probability is pm(s, x) = wm(s, x)/12. The cells describe chosen theoretical probabilities, rather than observed frequencies or simulations.

    For A, the row sums are 3 + 3 + 0 = 6 and 1 + 1 + 4 = 6. The total is 12. For B, they are 4 + 1 + 1 = 6 and 0 + 3 + 3 = 6, giving 12 in total. All weights are nonnegative, so each table can be normalized into a probability distribution.

    The probability of each intermediate price is 1/2 under both distributions. For the columns, A gives 4, 4, 4 parts, and B gives 4, 4, 4 parts. Each final price therefore has probability 1/3. The models have the same marginal distributions at both dates.

    The precise consequence is that any European option whose payoff depends only on the price at either of these dates has the same value under A and B, with the same discount factor. No claim is made that the marginal distributions are equal at other dates.

  3. Check that the dynamics are consistent with 0 interest rates

    With 0 interest rates and dividends, we require the price to be a martingale under each distribution: its expectation at the next time, conditional on the current price, equals that current price. The word “conditional” requires dividing the weights by the row sum, rather than by the total for the table.

    EQm[ST∣St=s]=∑xxwm(s,x)∑xwm(s,x)=s

    E denotes expectation; the sums run over the final prices x. In A, the row for the lower price gives: (3 × 80 + 3 × 100 + 0 × 120)/6 = 90 €. In B, the row for the lower price gives: (4 × 80 + 1 × 100 + 1 × 120)/6 = 90 € as well.

    The rows for the higher price both have an expectation of 110 €, to be checked in the first exercise. Finally, 1/2 × 90 + 1/2 × 110 = 100 €: the first step is consistent too. These checks prevent a martingale violation from being mistaken for an economic disagreement between models.

  4. Calculate the price of the vanilla product used for comparison

    Remove only the barrier clause to obtain the associated European call option. Its payoff is 0 € at final prices 80 € and 100 €, then 20 € at price 120 €. We therefore only need to multiply this last gain by the probability of the corresponding column.

    Csimple=20×412 €

    The exact result, under both A and B, is 20/3 €; in decimal form, approximately 6.6667 €. The price C_simple is an expected payoff, in euros, rather than a prediction of the future price of the underlying. We keep the exact fraction for the checks.

  5. Value the barrier option and locate the disagreement

    The price Cm is the sum of the payoffs G(s, x) weighted by the joint probabilities pm(s, x). Only the bottom right cell pays. We therefore need to retain the row information, which was lost when we summed the columns.

    Cm=∑s∑xpm(s,x)G(s,x)

    Under A: 20 × (4/12) = 20/3 € ≈ 6.6667 €. Under B: 20 × (3/12) = 5.00 €. The prices are positive and do not exceed 20/3 €, the price of the contract without a barrier, since cancellation cannot add any payoff.

    The disagreement is ΔC = C_A − C_B = 20/3 − 5 = 5/3 € ≈ 1.6667 €. ΔC denotes this signed difference in euros. B assigns 1 part out of 12 to the pair (90 €, 120 €), knocked out before a favourable final outcome; A assigns no mass to it. The contribution lost under B is exactly 20 × 1/12 = 5/3 €.

    This calculation is an exact enumeration of the stated distributions: no Monte Carlo sampling error explains it. That does not make either distribution true. The difference diagnoses dependence on the model; without a reliable external price for the contract, we do not know the error of either model.

  6. Check the result using two decompositions

    First approach: condition on survival at monitoring. Under B, the 110 € row has probability 1/2. Within this row, the probability of reaching 120 € is 3/6 = 1/2, and the conditional mean payoff is 10 €. The initial price is therefore 1/2 × 10 = 5 €, as in the direct calculation. The conditional probability is different from the joint probability 1/4.

    Second approach: split the vanilla option into a contract knocked out by hitting the barrier and its complement, activated by hitting the barrier, with the same threshold, dates, final payoff and absence of a rebate. On each path, their payoffs add up to the vanilla payoff. Under B: 5 + 5/3 = 20/3 € = 20/3 €. Under A, no path that hits the barrier ends with a positive gain, so equality between the barrier option price and the vanilla price is consistent within this particular tree.

    The checks use fractions. Rounding 6.6667 € before subtracting can affect the last decimal place; the exact expression 5/3 € avoids this ambiguity.

  7. Turn the calculation into a product–model comparison protocol

    To compare a local volatility implementation and a local stochastic volatility implementation for this contract, fix the same market data dates, curves, conventions, threshold and observations. Use the same calibration options and their bid–ask ranges. A difference in data or monitoring would change the problem before even changing the model.

    Next, check the probabilities or densities, the martingale property and calibration stability for each candidate. Measure discretization error separately from the error in the conditional estimation of the leverage function and, if simulation is used, Monte Carlo uncertainty. Refining the time steps, increasing sample sizes and repeating runs with different seeds can show whether the disagreement between candidates exceeds numerical noise; a single seed is insufficient for this diagnosis.

    Also compare sensitivities: changes in price when the underlying or volatility parameters change, with an explicit recalibration convention. Measure the cost of calibration, valuation and risk calculations on the same machine and with a common target accuracy. No timings or performance advantage have been measured on this page.

    When the value of a terminal payoff is already well constrained, richer dynamics may add little to the task. For our barrier option, dependence between dates is essential to the review; it still does not select the model by itself. The local stochastic volatility model adds assumptions about variance and its correlation with price; it must justify this cost and these residual risks. Hedging discrepancies outside the calibration set and scenarios near the threshold can inform the choice, while still requiring their own data and checks.

    The conclusion of this example is a disagreement to document, rather than a promotion of A or B. A defensible review may require further validation or restrict use of the model. Choosing the most favourable price, or claiming that the difference measures all possible uncertainty, is not a validation criterion.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the tables. Explain how two distributions can share their marginal distributions while allocating favourable final outcomes differently between paths that hit the barrier and paths that survive. Then recover the price under B by conditioning, and identify the missing evidence needed to prefer a real model.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The same European option prices imply the same barrier option price.”

    European option prices depend on only one date for each payoff. Reconstruct the marginal distributions, then circle the cell that retains both intermediate survival and a terminal gain. This cell differs even though the columns have the same sums.

    Reread the two joint distributions and their marginal distributions

  • “The probability within the surviving row is already the probability of the entire path.”

    A row assumes that monitoring has already been reached in that state. Multiply the probability of that row by the conditional probability of the final outcome. Under B, 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4; only this last weight multiplies the gain to obtain the initial price.

    Repeat the check by conditioning

  • “The difference between the models is the error of the cheaper one.”

    A difference compares two outputs; an error requires a reference. First check the numerical accuracy of each output, then seek external evidence suited to the product and its use. Neither the higher price nor more complex dynamics automatically provide this reference.

    Examine the barrier change and the requested verdict

Exercise 1 · Consistency check

Same expectation, different final outcomes

In the 110 € row of each table, calculate the expected final price and the conditional probability of reaching 120 €. Do the two results tell us the same thing about the models?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Normalize each row by its own sum, rather than by 12.

Hint 2

For the mean, weight each final price by its weight. For the probability of an outcome, keep only its cell.

Compare with the solution

Under A, the mean is (1 × 80 + 1 × 100 + 4 × 120)/6 = 110 €. Under B: (0 × 80 + 3 × 100 + 3 × 120)/6 = 110 €. Both rows satisfy the martingale condition.

Yet the conditional probability of the high final outcome is 4/6 = 2/3 under A and 3/6 = 1/2 under B. The mean does not determine the entire conditional distribution. To recover the joint probabilities, we still need to multiply by 1/2.

Check your reasoning

  • Divide by the row sum for both calculations.
  • Give the means in euros and the probabilities without units.
  • Explain why the same expectation can accompany different distributions.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and cross-check

Two approaches to the price under B

Without using the joint probability cell directly, calculate the barrier option price under B by conditioning on the intermediate price. Then calculate the price of its complement activated by hitting the barrier, and check the sum against the associated vanilla option.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The row for the lower price contributes nothing to the barrier option knocked out by hitting the barrier. The row for the higher price is reached with probability 1/2.

Hint 2

For the complement, look for a path that has hit the threshold but ends with a positive vanilla payoff.

Compare with the solution

In the row for the higher price, the mean payoff is 1/2 × 20 = 10 €. Before monitoring, we must weight it by 1/2: 1/2 × 10 = 5 €. The row for the lower price contributes 0 € to this contract knocked out by hitting the barrier.

The complement activated by hitting the barrier pays only for the pair (90 €, 120 €), with probability 1/12 = 1/12. Its price is 20 × 1/12 = 5/3 €.

The exact sum 5 + 5/3 = 20/3 € recovers 20/3 €, the vanilla option price. This identity assumes complementary clauses, including the same monitoring dates and no additional rebate when the barrier is hit.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish the probability of a row from a conditional probability.
  • Justify the payoff of the complement path by path.
  • Check the sum before any decimal rounding.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and a limited decision

Lowering the threshold does not validate the model

In the same tree, lower H to 85 € without changing the dates or K. Calculate the price under A and B. A colleague concludes that the models are validated for the original barrier of 95 €. Reply to her, then describe the checks needed to compare local volatility and local stochastic volatility implementations for the original contract.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Examine all paths ending at 120 €. Do the intermediate prices exceed the new threshold?

Hint 2

Agreement on a modified contract provides neither an external reference nor a test of the original contract.

Compare with the solution

With H = 85 €, both intermediate prices 90 € and 110 € exceed the threshold. All paths ending at 120 € therefore retain their payoff of 20 €. The other final outcomes pay 0 €, even if a path towards 80 € hits the new threshold. Both prices are 20 × 1/3 = 20/3 € ≈ 6.6667 €.

This agreement is specific to the states in this tree and to this threshold. It does not change the disagreement of 5/3 € for the original contract and proves no fit to the market. The colleague changed the product being valued before reaching a conclusion about the model.

A complete answer fixes the original barrier conventions and calibration data, checks the martingale properties and numerical convergence, then compares prices and sensitivities under perturbations. It measures the costs of calibration, valuation and risk calculations separately at the same accuracy. Finally, it requires suitable external validation, or retains an explicit restriction on use when the reference is missing. No measured superiority of real models can be inferred from our fictional distributions.

Check your reasoning

  • Examine only the paths with a positive payoff, without assuming that the barrier is never hit.
  • Calculate both prices and keep the two contracts distinct.
  • Distinguish consistency, convergence, an external reference and cost; give a limited verdict.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why do identical marginal distributions fail to determine the price of our barrier option?

They determine the distributions at each date, but not the probability of the pair that survives and then ends with a gain. The barrier option uses this dependence between dates.

Recall 2Which check prevents inconsistent dynamics from being confused with model disagreement?

Check normalization and nonnegative weights, then the conditional expectation of each row. Here, with 0 interest rates and dividends, it must equal the current price; the first step must also recover the initial price.

Recall 3How do we move from the surviving row of B to the weight of the path that pays?

Multiply the row probability 1/2 by the probability of the high final outcome within that row, 1/2: the joint weight is 1/4. This weight multiplies the payoff, in euros.

Recall 4Why does our exact difference of 5/3 € fail to measure the error of B?

It compares A and B without identifying an external truth. Exact arithmetic rules out numerical noise here, but validates neither set of dynamics. Both candidates may share other flaws.

Recall 5What must a comparison of local volatility and local stochastic volatility include for this contract?

A common contract and data, checked consistency and calibration, comparable numerical accuracy, prices and sensitivities under perturbations, measured calibration and risk calculation costs, and an external reference or explicit limits. Complexity is not evidence of quality.

Reconstruct the reasoning

From memory, reconstruct this sequence using our barrier option: write the payoff and its dates; check the distributions and martingale properties; separate marginal distributions from dependence; calculate the price, then check it using another decomposition; distinguish disagreement, numerical error and error relative to a reference; choose only within a justified scope of use.
