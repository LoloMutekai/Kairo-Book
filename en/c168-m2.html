Conditional expectation

Your goalDefine conditional expectation with respect to a finite sub-sigma-algebra, calculate it block by block in an example, then check it against the definition and the law of total expectation.

Before you start
c164-m5 : Fubini-Tonelli · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c165-m5 : duality · c168-m1 : measurable variables

✿01 See the idea

A fictional display hides 6 cells numbered from 1 to 6. One cell is selected, with each cell equally likely. An observer has to announce the selected number, but the display shows only the cell's colour: red if the number is odd, blue if it is even. What value should the observer announce?

Without the colour, a natural default value is the mean 7/2 of the numbers. With the colour, the situation changes: red leaves the numbers 1, 3 and 5 possible, while blue leaves 2, 4 and 6 possible. A natural answer is the mean of each group: 3 for red and 4 for blue.

The result is therefore more than a single number. It is a rule that assigns to each selected cell the mean of that cell's colour group. The two values 3 and 4 are the values of a random variable, denoted Z. It depends only on the available information, here the colour, and on nothing else.

Conditional expectation states this idea precisely: Z takes the same value wherever the information cannot distinguish the outcomes, and it preserves the means of X over each event whose occurrence can be determined from the information. The numbers, colour and selection are fictional; they provide a setting for calculation and do not describe any real device.

The rule to remember

The conditional expectation of X given the sub-sigma-algebra G is the variable Z, measurable with respect to G, whose integral over each event in G equals that of X. On a block with nonzero probability, it is the mean of X over that block, weighted by P.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Announce a number knowing only the colour

Take the 6 equally likely cells, let X be the selected number and let G be the sigma-algebra of events whose occurrence can be determined from the colour. Calculate Z, the conditional expectation of X given G, then check it. All values are fictional.

  1. Set out the space, variable and information

    The sample space Ω contains the 6 possible numbers. The probability measure P assigns mass 1/6 to each number, and the full sigma-algebra contains all subsets. The variable X is the number read from the cell; it is measurable with respect to this full sigma-algebra, since every subset belongs to it. Let A be the event “the number is odd” and B the event “the number is even”.

    The colour is all the observer sees. The events whose occurrence the observer can determine are the impossible event, A, B and Ω. These 4 events form the sub-sigma-algebra G. Assumptions: X is integrable, which is automatic on a finite sample space; A and B have nonzero probability, as the calculation step verifies. The prerequisites on duality and Fubini-Tonelli are not used in this finite calculation.

  2. State the definition

    For an event H in G, the notation ∫ over H of X dP here means the sum of the products X(ω) × P({ω}) for the cells ω in H: this is the form the integral takes on a finite sample space. The conditional expectation of X given G is a variable Z satisfying two conditions. Condition (i): Z is measurable with respect to G, meaning that the set of cells where Z takes a given value is an event in G. Condition (ii), for each event H in G:

    ∫HZdP=∫HXdP pour tout événement H∈G

    We then write Z = E[X | G]. Z is expressed in the same unit as X; here the numbers have no unit. In general, Z is unique only up to a set of probability 0, and its existence requires a theorem that this unit does not prove. On our finite sample space with two blocks of positive probability, we will construct it explicitly.

  3. Deduce the value of Z on each block

    Condition (i): if Z assigned different values to two cells in A, the set of cells where Z takes the first value would contain one cell but not the other. None of the 4 events in G separates two cells of the same block in this way. Therefore Z is constant on A, with value denoted α, and constant on B, with value denoted β.

    Condition (ii) with H = A: since Z equals α everywhere on A, the integral of Z over A equals α × P(A). It must equal the integral of X over A. The same reasoning applies to B. Since P(A) and P(B) are nonzero, we can divide:

    α=∫AXdPP(A) et β=∫BXdPP(B)

    This step also shows that, in this setting, α and β are the only possible values: the definition leaves no choice.

  4. Calculate the probabilities and integrals

    Probability of a block: A contains three cells with mass 1/6, so P(A) = 3 × 1/6 = 1/2. For B, P(B) = 1 − 1/2 = 1/2. Both values are positive, so division is allowed.

    Integrals of X: the numbers in A give 1 + 3 = 4, then 4 + 5 = 9. For B, 2 + 4 = 6, then 6 + 6 = 12. Each sum is multiplied by the mass 1/6 of a cell: the integral of X over A equals 9 × 1/6 = 3/2, and that over B equals 12 × 1/6 = 2.

  5. Divide to obtain Z

    α=3/21/2=3 et β=21/2=4

    Z therefore equals 3 on A and 4 on B. This is also the ordinary mean of the numbers in the block: 9 divided by 3 gives 3, and 12 divided by 3 gives 4. This agreement comes from the equal masses: weighting by P then reduces to a simple mean. The agreement would disappear with unequal masses, as in the final exercise.

  6. Check against the definition and the law of total expectation

    Condition (ii), for Z: the integral of Z over A equals 3 × 1/2 = 3/2, and that of X over A equals 3/2. On B, 4 × 1/2 = 2, and the integral of X equals 2. The cases where H is the impossible event and where H is Ω complete the check: for Ω, the sum 3/2 + 2 = 7/2 is the expectation of X, and also that of Z.

    E[Z]=3/2+2=7/2=E[X]

    Limiting cases: with the trivial sigma-algebra, consisting only of the impossible event and Ω, Z would be the constant 7/2. With a sigma-algebra containing every individual cell as a singleton, Z would be X itself. Our G lies between these two extremes. This check shows that Z satisfies the definition in this fictional setting; it says nothing about another probability measure or an infinite sigma-algebra.

Explain it in your own words

Why does measurability of Z require it to be constant on each block? And at what point in the calculation is the assumption “P(A) is nonzero” essential?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Conditional expectation is a number, like ordinary expectation.”

    It is a random variable that is a function of the information. Here it takes the value 3 on A and the value 4 on B; only its expectation E[Z] = 7/2 is a number.

    Review the checking step

  • “The square of the conditional expectation is the conditional expectation of the square.”

    On block A, the conditional expectation of X squared equals 35/3, approximately 11.67, whereas the square of 3 equals 9. The difference 8/3 measures the dispersion of the numbers within the block. A non-affine function cannot be moved through conditional expectation.

    Redo the exercise on squaring

  • “To recover E[X], I take the simple mean of the values of Z.”

    The simple mean ignores the probability of each block. In the uniform setting, it gives 7/2 by coincidence. With the fictional unequal masses in the final exercise, the correct value is 11/3, approximately 3.67; the simple mean is wrong by 1/6. Each value of Z must be weighted by the probability of its block.

    Redo the exercise with unequal masses

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Different information, different expectation

Use the same fictional display and the same 6 equally likely cells. This time, the observer sees only whether the number is at most 3 or at least 4. Let G′ be the corresponding sigma-algebra. Calculate E[X | G′] on each block, then check that its expectation equals that of X.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The two blocks have the same probability: within each block, the mean weighted by P is a simple mean.

Hint 2

To check, multiply each value of Z by the probability of its block, then add.

Compare with the solution

The lower block contains the numbers 1, 2 and 3: 1 + 2 = 3, then 3 + 3 = 6. Its mean is 6 divided by 3, giving 2. The upper block contains 4, 5 and 6: 4 + 5 = 9, then 9 + 6 = 15, giving the mean 5.

Each block has probability 1/2. Check: 2 × 1/2 = 1 and 5 × 1/2 = 5/2, so 1 + 5/2 = 7/2, the expectation of X. The values of Z differ from those in the lesson because the information differs, but the overall expectation remains 7/2.

Check your reasoning

  • Z is constant on each block of G′.
  • Each mean uses the correct numbers from the block.
  • The law of total expectation is checked using a weighted sum.

Exercise 2 · Near transfer

Squaring cannot be moved through expectation

Return to the sigma-algebra G determined by colour. Let Y be the square of the selected number. Calculate E[Y | G] on block A of odd numbers, compare it with the square of E[X | G] on the same block, then give the difference.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Square each number in A before taking the mean.

Hint 2

The masses within the block are equal: the weighted average is the simple mean of the squares.

Compare with the solution

Squares: 1 × 1 = 1, 3 × 3 = 9 and 5 × 5 = 25. Sum: 1 + 9 = 10, then 10 + 25 = 35. Since the masses are equal, E[Y | G] equals 35 divided by 3 on A, giving 35/3, approximately 11.67.

The square of the mean equals 3 × 3 = 9. The difference is 35/3 − 9 = 8/3, which is strictly positive: the numbers in the block are not all equal to their mean. This calculation shows a case where the two quantities differ; it does not describe all functions.

Check your reasoning

  • The numbers are squared before taking the mean.
  • Two distinct quantities are compared: the expectation of the square and the square of the expectation.
  • The conclusion remains limited to the calculated counterexample.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

A display with unequal masses (fictional)

Change the probability measure: each odd cell receives mass 1/9 and each even cell mass 2/9. These masses are fictional, and their sum over the 6 cells must equal 1. For the sigma-algebra G determined by colour, calculate E[X | G] and E[X]. What do you notice about Z compared with the lesson?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Start with the probability of each block: three cells with the same mass.

Hint 2

The sums of the numbers in each block are already known; multiply them by the mass of a cell in that block, then divide by the block's probability.

Hint 3

Compare each value of Z with its value in the lesson before calculating E[X].

Compare with the solution

Probabilities: P(A) = 3 × 1/9 = 1/3 and P(B) = 3 × 2/9 = 2/3. Their sum equals 1, as expected. Integrals: the integral of X over A equals 9 × 1/9 = 1, and that over B equals 12 × 2/9 = 8/3.

Values of Z: 1 divided by 1/3 gives 3 on A, and 8/3 divided by 2/3 gives 4 on B. These are the same values as in the lesson: within each block, the masses are equal, so only their relative proportions within the block matter.

Expectation: E[X] = 1 + 8/3 = 11/3, approximately 3.67, whereas the simple mean of Z would give 7/2. The overall expectation changes because the blocks no longer have the same weight, not because Z has changed.

Check your reasoning

  • Block probabilities are calculated using the given masses.
  • Division is by nonzero probabilities.
  • Z is identified as the same as in the lesson, with the reason given.
  • The overall expectation is weighted rather than a simple mean.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In the lesson, what does the sub-sigma-algebra G represent?

The set of events whose occurrence can be determined from the available information: here the impossible event, A, B and Ω.

Recall 2Which two conditions define Z = E[X | G]?

Z is measurable with respect to G, and for each event H in G, the integral of Z over H equals that of X over H.

Recall 3How do you calculate Z on a block with nonzero probability?

Z is constant on the block, equal to the integral of X over the block divided by the probability of the block.

Recall 4What does condition (ii) give when H is Ω?

E[Z] = E[X]: the sum of the values of Z weighted by the probabilities of the blocks equals the expectation of X, here 7/2 for the uniform setting.

Recall 5Is the square of E[X | G] the conditional expectation of the square of X?

Not in general. On the odd block in the lesson, 35/3 versus 9: the difference 8/3 comes from the dispersion of the numbers within the block.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Choose the space, probability measure and variable X; describe the information by a sub-sigma-algebra G; require a variable Z measurable with respect to G; require the integrals of Z and X to be equal over every event in G; on a block of positive probability, divide the integral of X by the probability of the block; check against the definition and the law of total expectation; do not move squaring or a simple mean through conditional expectation.
