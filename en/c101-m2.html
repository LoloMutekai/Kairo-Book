Level 2 · C101·M2

ARIMA

Your goalDifference a series that drifts, forecast its changes with a fictional autoregressive model, recover the level by accumulating them, then see why uncertainty in the level accumulates.

Before you start
c090-m5 : stationarity · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c101-m1 : AR and MA

✿01 See the idea

A fictional water tank fills slowly. Every morning, we read its level in centimetres: 40, 42, 45, then 49 cm. The level rises, so its mean is not the same at the beginning and end of the series. Describing this level directly with an autoregressive model means asking it to return to a mean value that does not exist.

A more modest approach is to look at the change from one day to the next. In our readings, it is 2, then 3, then 4 cm per day. If these changes stay around a stable value, an ARMA model can describe them. This is where the I in ARIMA comes from: the level is the integral, meaning the cumulative sum, of its changes.

The process therefore has three steps. Difference: move from levels to changes. Forecast: apply an ARMA model to the changes. Integrate: add the forecast changes to the last known level to return to centimetres. The notation ARIMA(p, d, q) counts the autoregressive terms p, the differencing operations d and the moving average terms q. Our example uses p = 1, d = 1 and q = 0.

The levels, constant, coefficient and standard deviation of the shocks on this page are fictional: they are used for calculations, not to describe a real water tank. We do not estimate them here; choosing p, d and q and then fitting the parameters belongs to the unit on identification. We assume they are known. We also make no claim that the forecast is good: evaluating it against a naive forecast that repeats the last reading comes later in the chapter.

The rule to remember

Difference to obtain stable changes, forecast these changes with an ARMA model, then accumulate them on top of the last observed level; differencing also makes shocks accumulate, so uncertainty in the level grows with the forecast horizon.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The fictional water tank: from four readings to three forecasts

The fictional readings are 40, 42, 45 and 49 cm, the last on day n. The fictional ARIMA(1, 1, 0) model writes each change as zₜ = c + φ zₜ₋₁ + εₜ, with c = 1 cm per day and φ = 0.5, with no units. Let us forecast the levels for the next 3 days, then look at how uncertainty grows.

  1. Name the quantities and state the assumptions

    yt is the level of the water tank on day t, in cm. The change zt is the first difference of the level, also written Δyt: today’s level minus the previous day’s level, in cm per day.

    zt=Δyt=yt−yt−1

    The model regresses the change on the previous change. c is a constant in cm per day; φ is a coefficient with no units; εt is a shock with mean 0 and variance σ² in cm², independent of the past. For the changes to remain stationary, we assume that the absolute value of φ is less than one. The parameters c, φ and σ are given, never estimated here. Different software may write the constant differently, for example as the mean of the changes: here, c is the term added below.

    zt=c+φzt−1+εt

    At the forecast origin n, ŷn+h denotes the forecast level h days later and ẑn+h the forecast change at the same horizon.

  2. Difference the readings

    Each change is a level minus the previous level: the operation removes drift and keeps variation. With 40, 42, 45 and 49 cm, we obtain 42 − 40 = 2, then 45 − 42 = 3, then 49 − 45 = 4 cm per day. Four levels give only 3 changes: differencing uses up one reading.

    Check: the sum of the changes is 2 + 3 + 4 = 9 cm, and the final level minus the initial level is 49 − 40 = 9 cm. The intermediate levels cancel in the sum. Three changes do not prove that the differenced series is stationary: this is a model assumption to be checked elsewhere.

  3. Forecast the next change

    The next day’s shock is unknown, but its mean is 0. Replacing it with its mean gives the model’s mean forecast: c plus φ times the last observed change.

    z^n+1=c+φzn=1+0.5×4=1+2=3 cm par jour

    The term φ zn retains half of the last change: 0.5 × 4 = 2 cm per day. The constant adds 1 cm per day.

  4. Integrate to recover the level

    A forecast change is not a level. The next day’s level is the last observed level plus the forecast change.

    y^n+1=yn+z^n+1=49+3=52 cm

    Adding 3 cm per day to a level of 49 cm over one day gives centimetres again. Reporting 3 cm as the forecast level would be a scale error.

  5. Continue from day to day

    For the second day, the next day’s change has not been observed: we use its replacement, the previous forecast. ẑn+2 = c + φ ẑn+1 = 1 + 0.5 × 3 = 1 + 1.5 = 2.5 cm per day, so ŷn+2 = 52 + 2.5 = 54.5 cm.

    Repeat for the third day: ẑn+3 = 1 + 0.5 × 2.5 = 1 + 1.25 = 2.25 cm per day, so ŷn+3 = 54.5 + 2.25 = 56.75 cm. The forecast levels rise, and the forecast changes approach a stable value.

  6. Check in two ways

    First, accumulation. The forecast level on the third day is the last level plus all the forecast changes: 49 + 3 + 2.5 + 2.25. The first two changes total 5.5, and all three total 7.75 cm, giving 49 + 7.75 = 56.75 cm, identical to the result obtained day by day.

    Second, the limit. If the changes settled at a value z, it would satisfy z = c + φ z, so z = c / (1 − φ) = 1 / 0.5 = 2 cm per day. The forecast changes 3, 2.5 then 2.25 cm per day approach it: the distance from this limit is multiplied by φ, namely 0.5, each day, so it is divided by two here. This behaviour follows from the model’s assumptions, not from the evolution of a real water tank.

  7. Measure accumulated uncertainty when φ is 0

    A level forecast is meaningful only alongside its uncertainty. Without an autoregressive term (φ is 0), the level on day n + h is yn plus h changes, each equal to c plus a shock. The forecast error is therefore the sum of h shocks. They are independent and have the same variance σ²: their variances add.

    Vh=hσ2

    With σ = 2 cm, σ² = 2 × 2 = 4 cm². The error variance is 4 cm² at one day, 8 cm² at 2 days and 12 cm² at 3 days. It grows without bound because no difference brings the level back towards a mean. For a stationary ARMA model, the forecast error variance stabilizes. When φ is nonzero, past shocks affect future levels with different weights: we do not calculate that case here.

  8. Review the limitations

    The calculation is exact for this fictional model. It does not tell us whether ARIMA(1, 1, 0) suits a real water tank. That requires estimating the parameters, examining the residuals and comparing with the naive forecast that repeats 49 cm. These steps belong to the following units in the chapter; no performance is claimed here.

Explain it in your own words

Why do we forecast a change before forecasting a level? Also explain why the forecast change stabilizes while uncertainty in the level keeps growing.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The forecast change is the forecast of the level.”

    A change is expressed in cm per day and a level in cm. After forecasting the change, add it to the last observed level: here 49 + 3 = 52 cm, not 3 cm.

    Revisit the integration step

  • “More differencing always makes the model more reliable.”

    Each differencing operation loses a reading and can create artificial dependence between shocks. Choose d according to the stationarity of the series, not to remove all variation: redo the exercise on first and second differences, stating what the four points allow you to conclude.

    Revisit the exercises

  • “Uncertainty in the level stays the same when forecasting further ahead.”

    With d equal to one, each day adds an unforeseen shock to the level. Without an autoregressive term, the variance is h times σ²: 4, 8 then 12 cm² in the example.

    Revisit the variance calculation

Exercise 1 · Explained application

Difference and reconstruct

Another fictional water tank gives levels of 60, 63, 67 then 72 cm. Calculate the first differences, then the second differences, meaning the differences of the differences. What order of differencing makes the changes constant at these points? Then recover the last level from the first level and the first differences.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A difference is the next term minus the previous term.

Hint 2

To reconstruct, add all the first differences to the first level.

Compare with the solution

First differences: 63 − 60 = 3, 67 − 63 = 4, 72 − 67 = 5 cm per day. Second differences: 4 − 3 = 1, and 5 − 4 gives the same result, 1, in cm per day per day.

The first differences increase, and the second differences are constant: d = 2 makes the changes constant at these four points. This describes these points, not a law governing the water tank; for a real series, d is chosen using stationarity checks.

Reconstruction: 60 + 3 + 4 + 5 = 60 + 12 = 72 cm. Check: the total 12 equals 72 − 60 = 12.

Check your reasoning

  • Three first differences, with their units.
  • Two second differences and a cautious conclusion about d.
  • Reconstruction of the last level by accumulation, checked against the total difference.

Exercise 2 · Transfer to another model

Forecast with different parameters

Use the example’s readings again, where the last change is 4 cm per day. Another fictional model has constant c = 2 cm per day and coefficient φ = 0.75. Calculate the forecast levels on days n + 1 and n + 2, then check the second by accumulating the forecast changes. This is a calculation test, not a model proposed for a real water tank.

Hint 1

First forecast the change using c + φ × (last change), then add it to the level.

Hint 2

For the second day, replace the unknown change with your first forecast change.

Compare with the solution

First day: ẑn+1 = 2 + 0.75 × 4 = 2 + 3 = 5 cm per day, so ŷn+1 = 49 + 5 = 54 cm.

Second day: ẑn+2 = 2 + 0.75 × 5 = 2 + 3.75 = 5.75 cm per day, so ŷn+2 = 54 + 5.75 = 59.75 cm. Check by accumulation: 49 + 5 + 5.75 = 59.75 cm.

The forecast level on the second day depends on the first: the unknown change is replaced by its forecast, never by an invented reading.

Check your reasoning

  • Change forecast from the last change, not the last level.
  • Integration on each day, with the correct units.
  • Replacement of the unknown change with its forecast, without an invented reading.

Exercise 3 · Reasoning about uncertainty

Four days of uncertainty

The same water tank, a model without an autoregressive term (φ is 0), and independent shocks with standard deviation σ = 2 cm. Calculate the variance, then the standard deviation, of the level forecast error at h = 4 days. By what factor is the standard deviation at one day multiplied? Does the forecast level change if σ doubles? What about the variance?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the variance of each shock, one per day.

Hint 2

The standard deviation is the square root of the variance, in cm.

Hint 3

The forecast level uses c; the variance uses σ².

Compare with the solution

The variance sums 4 equal variances, one per day: 12 + 4 = 16 cm².

The standard deviation is the square root of 16 cm², giving 4 cm: 2 times σ, because 4 days give a square root equal to 2. At one day it is 2 cm: the factor is 2, the square root of 4 days.

Doubling σ does not change the forecast level, which depends only on c and the last level, but multiplies the variance by 4. These results assume independent shocks with constant variance and known parameters; estimated parameters would add uncertainty.

Check your reasoning

  • Add variances, not standard deviations.
  • Take the square root to return to cm.
  • Separate the forecast level, linked to c, from its dispersion, linked to σ.
  • State the assumptions of independence and constant variance.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does first differencing do?

It replaces each level with its change since the previous day, zₜ = yₜ − yₜ₋₁, to remove drift in the level; it uses up one reading.

Recall 2What do p, d and q count in ARIMA(p, d, q)?

p counts the autoregressive terms, d the differencing operations, q the moving average terms; the ARMA model applies to the differenced series.

Recall 3How do you recover the level from forecast changes?

Accumulate the forecast changes on top of the last observed level: ŷₙ₊ₕ = yₙ + ẑₙ₊₁ + … + ẑₙ₊ₕ. A change is not a level.

Recall 4Why does uncertainty in the level grow when d equals one?

Each day adds an unforeseen shock to the level and these shocks accumulate; without an autoregressive term, the variance is h times σ².

Reconstruct the reasoning

Identify drift in the level; difference to obtain changes assumed to be stationary; forecast the next change with a given ARMA model; replace unknown changes with their forecasts; integrate by accumulating on top of the last level; check using the sum of the changes and the limit; recall that shocks accumulate; leave estimation, residuals and comparison with a naive forecast for later.
