Level 2 · C093·M3

L1 residuals, step, drift and spike

Your goalCompare a step and a drift using their absolute residuals, then distinguish persistence from a return to the starting level without turning a score into a certain diagnosis.

Before you start
c090-m5 : stationarity · c056-m5 : effect size · c063-m5 : synthetic dataset and external validity · c025-m5 : O, Omega and Theta · c093-m1 : median, MAD and normal scale · c093-m2 : pairwise slopes and Theil–Sen

✿01 See the idea

A sensor tracks the temperature of a room every 1 min. In the first readings, it shows 20 °C; then it shows 24 °C and stays there until the end of the record. Should we describe a sudden rise or gradual warming? We will examine what each account leaves unexplained.

A residual is the reading minus the value predicted by a model. In the diagram, it is the vertical gap between a point and the candidate curve. A positive residual places the point above the curve; a negative residual places it below. To count all the unexplained distance, we add the absolute values: we remove the sign from each gap before adding.

A step establishes a different level that persists over the observed window. A drift changes this level gradually; we will represent it with a straight line. A spike briefly departs from the previous level and then returns towards it. The return is essential: a final high point, without a later reading, cannot distinguish a spike from the start of a step.

The entire scene and all its values are fictional. The first two diagrams and the exercises concern this same sensor, with alternative records explicitly identified as such. We compare simple shapes over a short, complete window; we are looking for neither a physical cause nor a real alert threshold.

The rule to remember

Measure the distance to each candidate, then examine its shape over time. A small total distinguishes fits to the same points; it does not confirm a change point or its cause.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Does the room change level or follow a straight line?

The main record contains n = 6 readings at times t = 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 min. The temperatures, in that order, are 20, 20, 20, 24, 24, 24 °C. Let us compare two candidates fitted to these same observations: a step between minutes 2 and 3, and a straight line with intercept a = 39/2 °C and slope b = 1 °C/min. This is the line obtained here using Theil–Sen with the intercept given by the median of the differences y − bt; we do not repeat its fitting in this unit.

  1. Define the direction of the differences and the scope

    The index i identifies a reading in order, from 0 to 5; tᵢ is its time in min and yᵢ its temperature in °C. The symbol ŷᵢ, with a hat, denotes the temperature predicted at the same instant. The residual rᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ is in °C. The bars |rᵢ| denote its absolute value, which is positive or 0.

    Here, the notation L1 denotes the sum of the absolute residuals over the whole window, in °C. The symbol Σ means “add the terms for all the indicated readings”. n is the number of readings and has no unit. The mean absolute error is this sum divided by n; it is also in °C.

    ri=yi−y^i;L1=∑i=0n−1|ri|

    The records are complete, regularly spaced and expressed in the same unit; each reading receives the same weight. The candidates are descriptive and use the whole window, including its future: these are not predictions available in real time. This distance calculation does not require an assumption of Gaussian noise; interpreting it as a statistical test would require further assumptions.

  2. Calculate the values predicted by the candidates

    The step candidate predicts the median of the first 3 readings, which is 20 °C, then the median of the last 3, which is 24 °C. Its predicted values are therefore 20, 20, 20, 24, 24, 24 °C. The gap between the levels is 24 − 20 = 4 °C.

    For the line, ŷᵢ = a + btᵢ. The intercept a is the temperature on the line at t = 0 min; b expresses the increase per minute. We have a = 39/2 = 19.5 °C and b = 1 °C/min. At each minute, the product btᵢ is in °C and can be added to a.

    At time 0: 19.5 + 1 × 0 = 19.5 °C. At time 1: 19.5 + 1 × 1 = 20.5 °C. Continuing at times 2, 3, 4, 5, we obtain 21.5, 22.5, 23.5, 24.5 °C.

  3. Subtract before removing the sign

    Residuals from the line, taking each reading in order: 20 − 19.5 = 0.5 °C; 20 − 20.5 = -0.5 °C; 20 − 21.5 = -1.5 °C; 24 − 22.5 = 1.5 °C; 24 − 23.5 = 0.5 °C; 24 − 24.5 = -0.5 °C.

    Negative differences indicate that the line is too high; positive ones indicate that it is too low. We then take their absolute values: 0.5, 0.5, 1.5, 1.5, 0.5, 0.5 °C. We first keep the sign to interpret the mismatch; we remove it only to measure its size.

    For the step, each reading matches its predicted value. All its 6 residuals equal 0 °C.

  4. Add comparable distances

    For the line, we add progressively: 0.5 + 0.5 = 1 °C; then + 1.5 = 2.5 °C; then + 1.5 = 4 °C; then + 0.5 = 4.5 °C; finally + 0.5 = 5 °C.

    Ldroite=0.5+0.5+1.5+1.5+0.5+0.5=5 °C

    For the step: 6 × 0 = 0 °C. The difference in scores is 5 − 0 = 5 °C in favour of the step. The sums compare the same points with the same weights; this is neither a percentage of certainty nor an improvement measured on a real sensor.

    For a result per observation, the line gives 5/6 = 5/6 °C, or approximately 0.83 °C per reading; the step gives 0 °C. Dividing both scores by the same n preserves their order. For windows of different sizes, the raw sums can no longer be compared in the same way.

  5. Check by another method, then limit the conclusion

    Check without following the previous additions: there are 4 distances of 0.5 °C and 2 distances of 1.5 °C. Their total is 4 × 0.5 + 2 × 1.5 = 5 °C. The signed residuals cancel in pairs: their sum would be 0 °C and would hide all these distances.

    The step candidate therefore describes this record better than this line according to this criterion. The last points remain at the high level: they do not show the return that would characterise a spike. This demonstrates neither a fault nor a statistically established change point. The score alone does not verify that a change exceeds the noise, and a more flexible family of models can fit the observations more closely without explaining the situation better.

    Finally, absolute value retains a linear sensitivity to large errors: a distant reading can still carry considerable weight. The word robust does not mean that every spike becomes harmless. The diagnostic exercise will also show that very different shapes can share exactly the same total.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The pluses and minuses cancel, so the line fits perfectly.”

    Cancellation tells us about the balance of signs. It does not remove the distances: take each absolute value before summing, then recover 5 °C by grouping the distances.

    Review the calculation and its check by grouping

  • “The lowest score proves a step, even if the record does not exceed the noise.”

    Here, the minimum only distinguishes two candidates on the same data. Declaring a change point also requires an eligibility rule and an assessment of the noise. Reading the code illustrates this preliminary step; it does not validate it for the fictional sensor.

    Read the prerequisite for comparison in the code

  • “A large total of residuals must mean a spike.”

    The alternative records in the diagram all have 12 °C of absolute deviation from the fixed reference. To distinguish the shapes, look for a sustained level, progression or a return. A final reading alone shows no return.

Exercise 3 · Critical diagnosis

Three records, the same score

Still using the same times, compare three alternative records from this sensor with the constant reference of 20 °C. A: 20, 20, 20, 24, 24, 24 °C. B: 20, 20, 20, 22, 24, 26 °C. C: 20, 20, 20, 32, 20, 20 °C. Calculate the three sums of absolute residuals. Label each record as a step, drift or spike, justifying your choice using the time order. If record C stopped at its high reading, what could you still conclude?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Subtract the same reference of 20 °C throughout. The residuals stay in order.

Hint 2

Ask what the readings after the deviation show: persistence, progression or a return.

Compare with the solution

A leaves 0, 0, 0, 4, 4, 4 °C. Its sum is 3 × 4 = 12 °C. B leaves 0, 0, 0, 2, 4, 6 °C. Its sum is 2 + 4 + 6 = 12 °C. C leaves 0, 0, 0, 12, 0, 0 °C, or 12 °C in total.

The three sums are equal, and their means are all 2 °C. Yet A shows a step sustained over the window, B a drift after the stable phase, and C a spike followed by a return. These are ideal descriptions of these fictional records; the score alone does not choose the label.

If C stopped at 32 °C, no return would be observed. We would need to keep both possibilities open, a brief excursion or a new level, and obtain later readings. No calculation can supply these missing observations.

These scores use a fixed reference. They are not the scores obtained after refitting a step and a line: do not confuse this question with the comparison in the example.

Check your reasoning

  • I find that the three totals are equal and name their common reference.
  • Each label is justified by several readings in order.
  • I do not claim a return when observations stop before it.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the signed residual retain, and what does its absolute value measure?

The residual is the reading minus the predicted value. Its sign places the reading above or below the candidate; its absolute value gives the vertical distance, in the same unit as the reading.

Recall 2Under what conditions do sums of absolute residuals distinguish our candidates?

They use the same readings, the same unit and the same weights. The lowest score describes a better fit according to this criterion; a real change point requires additional evidence.

Recall 3How can we distinguish a step, drift and spike if their totals are identical?

Examine the time order: a step maintains a different level, a drift changes the level gradually, and a spike returns towards the initial level. A final deviation without later readings cannot verify this return.

Recall 4Does absolute value make the score insensitive to an extreme reading?

No. Each reading contributes in proportion to its distance from the candidate. A very high spike can still dominate the total; the sum alone is not a spike detector.
