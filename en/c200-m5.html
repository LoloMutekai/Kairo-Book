Level 3 · C200·M5

AR–OU correspondence

Your goalMove from a scalar autoregression to continuous reversion towards a mean, convert the noise variance and check a change in time step.

Before you start
c101-m5 : rolling origin forecasting · c145-m5 : policy evaluation · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c200-m1 : ADF and KPSS · c200-m2 : cointegration · c200-m3 : Engle-Granger · c200-m4 : Johansen

✿01 See the idea

A small temperature controlled chamber has a setpoint of 20 °C. Its initial temperature is 28 °C. In our fictional model, half the deviation from the setpoint remains after 2 min. This gives a predicted mean of 24 °C, then 22 °C after 4 min. An actual path of the model fluctuates around this forecast: it does not have to fall at every instant.

Observing the chamber at regular intervals gives an autoregression of order 1, abbreviated AR(1): the next temperature depends on the previous one and a new disturbance. Describing its evolution at every instant leads here to the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process, abbreviated OU. The correspondence seeks parameters that describe exactly the same transition at the observed times.

The autoregressive coefficient says what fraction of a deviation survives over a step. The continuous rate says how quickly this deviation contracts per unit of time. Observing the same chamber less often changes the coefficient without changing its physical rate.

All the chamber values are fictional and chosen for calculation. We assume constant parameters, observations without measurement error and Gaussian noise that is independent over disjoint intervals. We do not estimate any parameters from readings or demonstrate that this model represents a real chamber.

The rule to remember

Convert the persistence of the state, the constant and the noise variance together; state the time step and check that the coefficient can come from a scalar OU process.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Recover the continuous dynamics of the chamber

The fictional chamber follows Y at time step Δ = 2 min, with constant c = 10 °C, dimensionless coefficient φ = 1/2 and innovation variance q = 3 °C². The initial value is known exactly: 28 °C. Let us recover μ, κ and σ, then check the mean and noise after 4 min.

  1. Write the autoregression and recover its target

    Yn is the temperature in °C at reading index n; n is a dimensionless integer. Δ is the time between readings, in minutes. c is the constant added at each step, in °C; φ is the dimensionless coefficient multiplying the previous state. ε denotes the innovation, meaning the new temperature disturbance. The ε terms are independent of one another and of the initial value, with a normal distribution of mean 0 °C and variance q. The symbol N below denotes this distribution; its second argument is a variance.

    Yn+1=c+φYn+εn+1,εn∼N(0,q)

    The target μ is the temperature left unchanged by the transition mean: μ = c + φμ. Isolating μ requires φ to differ from 1. We obtain μ = c/(1 − φ) = 10/1/2 = 20 °C. The constant c is therefore not the target.

    Yn+1−μ=φ(Yn−μ)+εn+1

    Centering around μ lets us track the deviation from the setpoint directly. Initially, this deviation is 28 − 20 = 8 °C.

  2. Define the continuous model and its units

    dXt=κ(μ−Xt)dt+σdWt

    Xt is the continuous temperature, in °C, at time t in minutes; sampling gives Yn = X at time nΔ. κ, the Greek letter kappa, is a strictly positive rate, per minute. The term κ(μ − Xt) brings the mean back towards μ: above the setpoint, it is negative.

    W is standard Brownian motion: its increments over disjoint intervals are independent, Gaussian and of mean 0, with variance equal to the interval duration. W therefore has units of the square root of a minute. σ is a positive noise intensity, in °C per square root of a minute; σ squared has units of °C² per minute. It is not q, which describes a whole interval.

    The differential notation represents a stochastic integral equation. We assume W is independent of the initial state, and μ, κ, σ are constant. No sensor noise is added to the observations; otherwise the observed series generally no longer follows this simple autoregression.

  3. Integrate exactly, then interpret the noise

    Set Zt = Xt − μ. Multiplying the equation for Z by exp(κt) cancels its restoring term: d[exp(κt)Zt] = σ exp(κt)dWt. The exponential exp uses the natural base e. Integrating from t to t + Δ and then dividing by exp(κ(t + Δ)) gives:

    Xt+Δ−μ=e−κΔ(Xt−μ)+σ∫tt+Δe−κ(t+Δ−s)dWs

    s is the integration time, in minutes. The integral weights the new noise: an earlier disturbance has already been damped by the end of the step. Its integrand is deterministic, so the integral is Gaussian, has mean 0 and is independent of the state at the start of the step. Over disjoint steps, these noise terms are independent.

    The variance of a Brownian integral with deterministic weights is the integral of the squared weight. With u = t + Δ − s, a duration in minutes, the calculation gives:

    q=σ2∫0Δe−2κudu=σ22κ(1−e−2κΔ)

    The units check out: °C² per minute multiplied by a duration gives °C². Integration, rather than replacing dW with an arbitrary error, determines the variance of the autoregression.

  4. Invert the correspondence and check its domain

    φ=e−κΔ,c=μ(1−φ),q=σ22κ(1−φ2)
    μ=c1−φ,κ=−lnφΔ,σ2=2κq1−φ2

    ln is the natural logarithm, the inverse of the exponential. For a real scalar OU process with mean reversion, Δ > 0, q > 0 and 0 < φ < 1. A stationary autoregression can have a negative coefficient: that is not enough for this correspondence. A real exponential never becomes negative or 0 in finite time.

    With non-Gaussian innovations, converting the mean and variance does not demonstrate equality of the transition distributions. Here Gaussianity and independence make the equality exact on the observed grid. From spaced readings alone, they do not prove that the evolution between readings actually follows an OU process.

  5. Calculate the rate, noise and contraction time

    Our φ = 1/2 is admissible. The exact rate is κ = −ln(1/2)/2 = ln(2)/2 per minute. Keeping the logarithm in exact form avoids rounding the steps. The product κΔ is dimensionless.

    The squared coefficient is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. Subtracting it from unity gives 1 − 1/4 = 3/4. The equilibrium variance V is therefore q/(1 − φ squared) = 3/3/4 = 4 °C². V is also σ squared divided by 2κ.

    σ2=2×ln22×4=4ln2 °C²/min

    Take the positive square root: σ = √(4 ln(2)) °C per square root of a minute. Reverse check: [σ squared/(2κ)] × (1 − φ squared) = 4 × 3/4 = 3 °C². The continuous intensity and the variance of a step are not interchangeable.

    The half-life h is the time needed to divide the initial mean deviation by 2: exp(−κh) = 1/2. Thus h = ln(2)/κ = 2 min. It concerns a mean deviation, rather than a guaranteed time when the actual temperature reaches the halfway point.

  6. Predict and check equilibrium without imposing it initially

    E denotes expectation under the model; the vertical bar means “given”. Given an initial X equal to 28 °C, the future innovation has mean 0. After 2 min, the mean deviation becomes 1/2 × 8 = 4 °C; the mean is 20 + 4 = 24 °C. The affine form checks the same result: 10 + 1/2 × 28 = 10 + 14 = 24 °C.

    E[XΔ∣X0=28]=24 °C,Var[XΔ∣X0=28]=3 °C²

    Var denotes variance. Since the initial value is known exactly, the conditional variance after this step is q = 3 °C². If instead the state initially had the equilibrium variance V = 4 °C², its next variance would be 1/4 × 4 + 3 = 1 + 3 = 4 °C²: V is preserved.

    The OU process therefore admits a normal equilibrium distribution with mean μ and variance V. To be stationary from the start, the state must be initialized according to this distribution, independently of future noise. Our fixed initial value of 28 °C does not do this. A restoring force and a possible stationary distribution do not make every initialization stationary.

  7. Combine steps and distinguish the Euler approximation

    After 2 steps, or 4 min, the coefficient becomes φ squared = 1/4. The mean deviation is 1/4 × 8 = 2 °C, so the mean is 22 °C. The total innovation is φ times the first innovation plus the next one: the first has had time to contract.

    q2Δ=φ2q+q=3/4+3=15/4 °C²

    Independence makes the covariance between the innovations 0. Another check: the squared coefficient for the longer interval is 1/4 × 1/4 = 1/16. The OU formula therefore gives V × (1 − 1/16) = 4 × 15/16 = 15/4 °C². The 2 results agree, giving 3.75 °C².

    Euler–Maruyama replaces the exponential with its local approximation: φ ≈ 1 − κΔ and q ≈ σ squared × Δ. These relations require small κΔ; they are not the exact correspondence. Inverting the first one here would give κ ≈ (1 − 1/2)/2 = 0.25 per minute, instead of ln(2)/2. Keeping the exact OU parameters, the Euler variance would be 8 ln(2) °C², which differs from 3 °C².

    The correspondence converts parameters under assumptions. It does not test for a unit root, cointegration or the validity of the model on observations: those questions require their own evidence.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The constant c is the equilibrium temperature.”

    Solve μ = c + φμ before identifying the target. Here c = 10 °C, but μ = 20 °C. Centering the autoregression reveals the deviation that contracts.

    Review the target and the centered autoregression

  • “A stable coefficient is enough to construct an OU process.”

    The autoregressive stability condition allows a negative coefficient; the exact coefficient exp(−κΔ) of a real scalar OU process remains strictly positive. Also check the distribution and independence of the innovations before claiming equality of the models.

    Examine the negative coefficient in the last exercise

  • “I keep the same noise variance when I change the step.”

    The variance depends on duration and damping. Compose the innovations: the noise from the first step is multiplied by φ before the next is added. Only replace the exact formula with σ squared × Δ in a justified local approximation.

    Review the combination of steps and the OU check

Exercise 1 · Direct application

A constant that is not the setpoint

In the chamber, c = 10 °C and φ = 1/2 at a step of 2 min. Start from 28 °C. Recover μ and then the next mean in two ways. Explain why the next observed value may differ from this mean.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

An unchanged target satisfies μ = c + φμ.

Hint 2

After finding μ, contract the deviation from this target; check using c + φ times the initial temperature.

Compare with the solution

Isolating the target gives μ = 10/(1 − 1/2) = 20 °C. The initial deviation is 8 °C; half of it is 4 °C. The next mean is therefore 20 + 4 = 24 °C.

The other calculation gives c + φ times the initial value = 10 + 14 = 24 °C. The observation also includes an innovation of mean 0 and variance 3 °C²; mean 0 does not mean 0 in every realization.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish the constant and the target, with their units.
  • Obtain the same mean from the centered form and the affine form.
  • Retain the innovation in the realized value.

Exercise 2 · Change in time scale

Take readings of the same chamber less often

Keep the chamber and its continuous parameters, but now take readings every 4 min. From the same known initial value 28 °C, calculate the new coefficient, new constant, predicted mean and innovation variance. Check this variance by composition and then with the OU formula.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The longer interval contains 2 original steps: multiply their coefficients.

Hint 2

The first innovation is damped once before the next arrives; its coefficient must be squared in the variance.

Compare with the solution

The coefficient is 1/2 × 1/2 = 1/4. The constant becomes μ × (1 − 1/4) = 20 × 3/4 = 15 °C. The target and κ are preserved; c changes with the step.

The initial mean deviation becomes 1/4 × 8 = 2 °C. We obtain 20 + 2 = 22 °C, as when applying the original forecast twice.

The innovation for the longer step is φ times the original innovation, plus the next independent innovation. Its variance is 1/4 × 3 + 3 = 3/4 + 3 = 15/4 °C², or 3.75 °C².

Using the OU process, the squared new coefficient is 1/16. The variance is therefore 4 × (1 − 1/16) = 15/4 °C². The variance is neither the original variance q nor simply that variance multiplied by 2.

Check your reasoning

  • Change the coefficient, constant and variance together.
  • Preserve the target μ and rate κ.
  • Justify squaring the coefficient of the propagated noise and obtain the same result by the other method.

Exercise 3 · Checking assumptions

A contraction that alternates sides

A colleague proposes, for the same chamber, an autoregression centered on 20 °C with φ = -1/2 and q = 3 °C² at the same step Δ. Starting from 28 °C, calculate its next mean and its equilibrium variance. Can a real scalar OU process with constant parameters reproduce this coefficient exactly? Justify your answer without attempting the logarithm of a negative number.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The absolute value of the coefficient contracts the deviation, but its sign changes which side of the setpoint it is on.

Hint 2

Compare the sign of a real exponential with the sign of the proposed coefficient.

Compare with the solution

The next mean deviation is -1/2 × 8 = -4 °C. The mean is therefore 20 + (-4) = 16 °C: it moves below the setpoint. The squared coefficient remains 1/4, so the autoregressive equilibrium variance remains 3/(1 − 1/4) = 4 °C².

Autoregressive stability and this finite variance are not enough. For any finite real κ and positive Δ, exp(−κΔ) is strictly positive: it cannot equal -1/2. There is therefore no real scalar OU process with constant parameters giving this exact transition. This rules out neither the proposed autoregression as a discrete model nor all other possible continuous models.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate a mean that alternates around the target.
  • Distinguish possible stationarity of the autoregression from existence of the OU correspondence.
  • Base the rejection on positivity of the exponential without concluding that every continuous model is impossible.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What autoregressive coefficient does exact sampling of an OU process produce, and what is its unit?

φ = exp(−κΔ), dimensionless. For strictly positive κ and Δ, 0 < φ < 1. The rate is obtained from κ = −ln(φ)/Δ, with units inverse to the chosen time unit.

Recall 2How do you convert the innovation variance q into continuous intensity?

σ squared = 2κq/(1 − φ squared). q is in °C² per transition; σ squared is in °C² per minute. The formula comes from the integral of the squared weights of the damped Brownian noise.

Recall 3What happens to the coefficient and innovation when 2 steps are combined?

The coefficient becomes φ squared. The total innovation is φ times the first innovation plus the next one. Under independence, its variance is φ squared × q + q. The target μ and continuous rate κ do not change.

Recall 4What does the half-life of an OU process measure, and what does it not promise?

h = ln(2)/κ divides the deviation of the conditional mean from μ by 2. It does not guarantee the time when a random path reaches half of its initial deviation.

Recall 5Why does a negative coefficient, even with absolute value less than 1, rule out this correspondence?

A real scalar OU process with constant parameters has a strictly positive exponential coefficient at a finite step. An AR process with a negative coefficient can have a stationary distribution, but alternates around its mean. Stability alone therefore does not prove OU dynamics.
