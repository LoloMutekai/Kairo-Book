Hilbert

Your goalRecognize a Hilbert space and justify the best constant approximation to a signal through orthogonality of the residual.

Before you start
c164-m5 : Fubini-Tonelli · c040-m5 : least squares · c142-m5 : convergence criteria · c165-m1 : Lp spaces · c165-m2 : completeness

✿01 See the idea

A device records an electrical signal over a fixed duration. To represent it by a single value, you must display a constant voltage. The fictional signal is 2 V for the fraction 1/4 of the duration, then 6 V for the rest. Which number should you choose to make the mean of the squared deviations as small as possible?

Averaging the heights without accounting for their durations would give too much weight to the brief segment. Each segment must contribute according to the time it occupies. The diagram compares the signal with a candidate constant; the deviations are measured vertically, then their squares are accumulated over the full duration.

Now change your viewpoint: the entire signal becomes a vector. The constants form a line of allowed vectors. The best approximation lies at the foot of a perpendicular to this line. The two segments of the signal make the calculation short, but the surrounding space also contains signals that cannot be described by a finite list of heights.

A Hilbert space allows this geometry with functions: a dot product defines lengths and orthogonality; completeness prevents a sequence of increasingly close candidates from converging to a missing object. We work over the real numbers and recall these conditions precisely below. All the data in this scene are fictional.

The rule to remember

Hilbert = dot product + completeness for the associated norm. To project onto a closed linear subspace, find a candidate whose residual is orthogonal to all allowed candidates.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Replace the entire signal with a constant voltage

Let t denote time divided by the total duration: t is unitless, between 0 and 1. The function f is 2 V for 0 ≤ t < 1/4, then 6 V for 1/4 ≤ t ≤ 1. We seek p(t) = c, where c is real and measured in volts. The criterion is the integral of the square of f − c over this interval, with uniform weighting in time.

  1. Specify the space and the dot product

    H is the space of equivalence classes of measurable real functions whose squares are integrable over this interval, with respect to Lebesgue measure dt. Two functions equal almost everywhere, meaning except on a set of measure 0, represent the same element. We work with their numerical values in the fixed unit of volts.

    H=L2([0,1],dt;ℝ)
    ⟨u,v⟩=∫01u(t)v(t)dt;‖u‖=⟨u,u⟩

    u and v denote arbitrary elements of H. Their product is integrable: the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality gives ∫|uv| dt ≤ ‖u‖ ‖v‖. For two voltages, the dot product is in volts squared; the norm is in volts, because dt is unitless. Here the norm measures a root mean square amplitude, since the total normalized duration is 1.

  2. Check what the word Hilbert adds

    Over the real numbers, a dot product is bilinear, symmetric and positive definite. Bilinear means linear in each argument: for example, ⟨a u + b v, w⟩ = a⟨u,w⟩ + b⟨v,w⟩ for real scalars a and b and an element w. Symmetric means ⟨u,v⟩ = ⟨v,u⟩. Finally, ⟨u,u⟩ ≥ 0, with equality only for the 0 element.

    Here these properties follow from the rules of integration and the nonnegativity of a square. A 0 integral of u squared forces u to be 0 almost everywhere, which explains the use of equivalence classes of functions. Changing the voltage at an isolated instant therefore changes neither the element nor its norm.

    A dot product alone gives a pre-Hilbert space. To be a Hilbert space, every Cauchy sequence for this norm must also converge within the space. A sequence is Cauchy when the distance between its terms becomes as small as desired once their indices are large enough. Here we use the completeness theorem for the space of equivalence classes of square integrable functions: every such sequence has a limit in norm within this space. This general result is not proved here; it does not require convergence at every instant.

  3. Define the candidates and the projection condition

    Set e(t) = 1, a unitless constant function, and M = {c e : c real}. M is a linear subspace: adding constants or multiplying them by a real number gives another constant. It is closed for our norm, meaning that a limit in H of constants remains constant; the last exercise justifies this.

    The projection theorem says that in a Hilbert space, every element has a unique best approximation in each closed linear subspace. It is characterized by a residual orthogonal to that subspace. Here p = c e, r = f − p, and r ⟂ M means ⟨r,m⟩ = 0 for every m in M. Since every m is a multiple of e, it is enough to check ⟨r,e⟩ = 0.

    ⟨f−ce,e⟩=0⇒c=⟨f,e⟩⟨e,e⟩

    Division is allowed because e is nonzero. The numerator is in volts and the denominator is unitless: c is in volts. We will establish directly that this candidate is the unique minimizer in M; we do not need to assume that the signal is continuous.

  4. Integrate each plateau before adding them

    Integrating a constant over a segment amounts to multiplying its height by the length of the segment. The lengths are 1/4 and 1 − 1/4 = 3/4. We therefore calculate ⟨f,e⟩ = 1/4 × 2 + 3/4 × 6 = 1/2 + 9/2 = 5 V.

    For e, both heights are 1, so ⟨e,e⟩ = 1/4 + 3/4 = 1. This gives c = 5 / 1 = 5 V. The projection p is the constant signal at this voltage, even though f never takes this value on its plateaus.

    Check that this makes sense: the high plateau lasts longer, and 5 V is indeed much closer to 6 V than to 2 V. The simple average of the heights would ignore the duration of each plateau.

  5. Calculate the residual, its orthogonality and its length

    The residual r is 2 − 5 = -3 V on the short segment, and 6 − 5 = 1 V on the long one. The orthogonality check keeps the signs: ⟨r,e⟩ = 1/4 × (-3) + 3/4 × 1 = -3/4 + 3/4 = 0 V.

    The squared norm, in contrast, makes both contributions positive. The squared deviations are 9 and 1 volts squared. After weighting by the durations, ‖r‖ squared is 9/4 + 3/4 = 3 volts squared.

    ‖r‖2=1/4×(-3)2+3/4×12=3

    The norm of the residual is the square root of 3, in volts. A residual with 0 mean can therefore have a strictly positive length. Orthogonality does not eliminate the error.

  6. Prove the minimum and check it another way

    For any constant voltage d, let J(d) = ‖f − d e‖ squared. Since f − d e = r + (c − d)e, expanding the squared norm produces a cross term proportional to ⟨r,e⟩. This term is 0: that is exactly what the previous check established.

    J(d)=‖r‖2+(d−c)2‖e‖2

    Thus J(d) = 3 + (d − 5) squared, in volts squared when d is expressed in volts. The added term is nonnegative, with equality only if d = 5. We have proved the global minimum and its uniqueness over all constants, without restricting ourselves to a few trials.

    Another check: calculating ‖f‖ squared directly gives 1/4 × 4 + 3/4 × 36 = 1 + 27 = 28 volts squared. Pythagoras also gives ‖p‖ squared + ‖r‖ squared = 25 + 3 = 28 volts squared. The direct and geometric methods agree.

  7. Identify the role of completeness

    The calculation for this line of constants is explicit. The general theorem is stronger: it remains valid for a closed subspace that requires infinitely many directions. In its proof, we choose candidates whose distances approach the best possible distance; the geometry of the dot product makes this sequence Cauchy. Completeness supplies its limit in H, and closedness keeps that limit among the candidates.

    These conditions have distinct roles: completeness supplies a limit in H for a Cauchy sequence; closedness of M ensures that the limit of the candidates stays in M. Having a dot product does not prove completeness. We have justified our projection onto M; the general proof of the theorem is not developed here. ✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I have a norm and a dot product, so my space is a Hilbert space.”

    You still need to establish completeness for the norm induced by this dot product. Recall the condition on Cauchy sequences. In our case, completeness of the space of square integrable functions is the result used; the diagram alone does not prove it.

    Revisit the definition of a Hilbert space

  • “The residual is orthogonal, so f and its constant agree at every instant.”

    Orthogonal here means that the integral of the residual against every constant is 0. Yet its plateaus are -3 and 1 V. Their signed contributions cancel; the squares still give 3 volts squared.

    Recalculate the residual and its squares

  • “To project, I only need to take the dot product with any basis.”

    The coefficient along a nonzero direction b is ⟨f,b⟩ / ⟨b,b⟩. Omitting the denominator is allowed only if b has unit norm. The change of direction in the first exercise preserves the projection but changes its coefficient.

    Test a direction that is not normalized

Exercise 1 · Application

The same line, a different direction

Keep the same signal, but write the constant p = a b with b(t) = 2 instead of e(t) = 1. b is unitless. Calculate ⟨f,b⟩, ⟨b,b⟩, then the coefficient a in volts. Does the voltage of p change?

Hint 1

Linearity multiplies ⟨f,e⟩ by 2, but ⟨e,e⟩ by the square of 2.

Hint 2

After dividing, also calculate a × b(t): the coefficient alone is not the signal.

Compare with the solution

⟨f,b⟩ = 2 × 5 = 10 V and ⟨b,b⟩ = 2 × 2 × 1 = 4. The coefficient is a = 10 / 4 = 5/2 V, that is, 2.50 V.

The projected function is a b(t) = 5/2 × 2 = 5 V, as before. Changing the length of the vector that describes the line changes its coordinates; the line and the best approximation remain the same.

  • You distinguish the coefficient 5/2 V from the projected voltage 5 V.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and checking

Compare a competing constant

For the same recording, someone proposes d = 4 V. Calculate J(d) directly by integrating the squared deviations, then using the Pythagorean identity. What does this comparison prove? What more is needed to establish the minimum over all constants?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The deviations are f − d on each plateau. Square them before weighting them by their durations.

Hint 2

The other method adds the square of d − c to J(c), since ‖e‖ squared is 1.

Compare with the solution

The deviations are -2 and 2 V; their squares are 4 and 4 volts squared. Direct calculation gives J(d) = 1/4 × 4 + 3/4 × 4 = 1 + 3 = 4 volts squared.

By Pythagoras, d − c = -1 V, so the penalty is 1 volt squared. J(d) = 3 + 1 = 4 volts squared. The proposed constant is worse than 5 V.

Comparing these two constants does not rule out all the others. The global proof comes from the identity for arbitrary d: a square cannot be negative and is 0 here only for d = c.

Check your reasoning

  • Both methods give 4 volts squared.
  • You retain the relative durations of the signal.
  • You distinguish a numerical comparison from the proof of the global minimum.

Exercise 3 · Justification

Why do the constants form a closed set?

In the same space H, a sequence pₙ = cₙe of constants converges in norm to an element g of H. The indices n and m are natural numbers. Prove that g is still a constant using ‖pₙ − pₘ‖ = |cₙ − cₘ| and completeness of the real numbers. Finish by explaining why g can have a representative that differs at an isolated instant.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Every convergent sequence is Cauchy. The given equality transfers this property to the real sequence cₙ.

Hint 2

If cₙ converges to c, calculate ‖pₙ − ce‖. Then use uniqueness of the limit in norm.

Compare with the solution

Since pn converges in H, it is Cauchy. Now ‖pn − pm‖ = |cn − cm| ‖e‖ and ‖e‖ = 1; the numbers cn therefore form a Cauchy sequence in the real numbers. They converge to a real number c by completeness of the real numbers.

We obtain ‖pn − ce‖ = |cn − c| → 0. The sequence pn thus converges both to ce and to g. Since a limit in norm is unique, g = ce in H; the limit belongs to M, so M is closed. This proof of closedness does not claim to establish completeness of H on its own.

The equality g = ce concerns equivalence classes: every representative of g is equal to c almost everywhere. A change at an isolated instant, a set of measure 0, does not change this class. It therefore does not contradict closedness of M.

Check your reasoning

  • You justify the passage from being Cauchy in H to being Cauchy in the real numbers.
  • You identify the two limits and invoke their uniqueness.
  • You conclude equality almost everywhere, without requiring all representatives to be equal at every point.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What conditions must you check to call a real space a Hilbert space?

A bilinear, symmetric, positive definite dot product, followed by completeness for its associated norm. The dot product alone gives a pre-Hilbert space.

Recall 2Why do we work with equivalence classes of functions in our space?

Two functions equal almost everywhere have 0 distance. Identifying them as the same element makes the norm positive definite; changing a value at an isolated instant does not change the signal as an element of this space.

Recall 3What assumptions and what condition characterize projection onto a subspace?

In a Hilbert space, the subspace must be linear and closed. The candidate belongs to the subspace and its residual is orthogonal to all elements of that subspace. It is then the unique best approximation.

Recall 4How can you reconstruct the coefficient of our constant without memorizing its value?

Set e(t) = 1, solve ⟨f − ce,e⟩ = 0, then divide ⟨f,e⟩ by ⟨e,e⟩. The integral adds the heights weighted by their durations; the orthogonal residual makes the cross term vanish.

Recall 5How does orthogonality of the residual to the constants prove the minimum without eliminating the error?

It cancels the cross term in J(d) = J(c) + (d − c) squared, since ‖e‖ is 1. The added square is nonnegative; J(c) can remain positive, here 3 volts squared.
