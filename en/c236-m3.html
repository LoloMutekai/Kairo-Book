Level 4 · C236·M3

Malliavin covariance

Your goalConstruct the matrix of sensitivities to noise and determine which directions of displacement remain accessible to first order.

Before you start
c235-m5 : divergence · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c231-m5 : geodesics · c236-m1 : integration by parts · c236-m2 : Clark-Ocone

✿01 See the idea

A fictional platform receives random commands and places a point on a table. The same noise can move the point both horizontally and vertically. The question is local: by changing the noises very slightly, can we move the point in any direction in the plane?

Each arrow in the first diagram shows the response to a perturbation of one noise. If the arrows stay on the same line, a direction in the plane is inaccessible to these perturbations to first order. If they span the plane, no direction is completely insensitive to them.

Malliavin covariance collects the dot products of the derivatives of the coordinates with respect to noise. It describes this local geometry of sensitivities. Ordinary covariance describes the spread of positions across realizations: these objects generally differ.

The commands and lengths are fictional, chosen for an exact calculation. The displacement rule is a mathematical model with no claim to mechanical realism. The arrows are linearized variations, rather than paths actually followed.

The rule to remember

Differentiate the outputs in the noise directions, form their Gram matrix, then look for an output direction whose derivative would vanish. Keep this test distinct from an ordinary covariance calculation.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A position in the plane controlled by Gaussian noises

The scale of the platform is ℓ = 2 cm and the amplitude of the second command is b = 1, dimensionless. Z and U are independent, centered normal random variables with variance 1, and are dimensionless. Construct γ(F) for the polynomial rule below, where F = (X, Y), then evaluate it at Z = 1 and U = 0.

  1. Specify the noise space and units

    X=ℓZ;Y=ℓ(Z2−1+bU)

    H is a real Hilbert space: its dot product ⟨h, k⟩ measures the alignment of noise directions. W is a centered Gaussian family, linear in its argument, with E[W(h)W(k)] = ⟨h, k⟩. E denotes expectation. We choose h and k with norm 1 and dot product 0. Then Z = W(h) and U = W(k) are the independent normal commands specified above.

    For an output f(Z, U), the Malliavin derivative is Df = (∂f/∂Z)h + (∂f/∂U)k. The symbol ∂ means differentiating with respect to the indicated command while holding the other fixed. Df is a vector in H, rather than a time derivative. The normalization of h and k is dimensionless; DX and DY therefore have units of centimeters.

    X and Y are the coordinates, in cm; ℓ is the length scale; b sets the amplitude of the second command. F denotes their random pair. The polynomials used have finite Gaussian moments of all orders: their derivatives are square-integrable, allowing the dot products below. The chosen point (Z, U) is used to evaluate the formulas; it is not an event to which we assign positive probability.

  2. Define the matrix and what it measures

    For components Fᵢ whose derivatives DFᵢ are square-integrable, define the matrix γ(F) entry by entry. The indices i and j denote output coordinates; in our plane, these are X and Y.

    γij(F)=⟨DFi,DFj⟩H

    This is the Gram matrix of the derivatives: it is symmetric and positive semidefinite. No expectation appears in its definition. Its entries can depend on the realization. Here they are in cm²; the determinant of our matrix in the plane will be in cm⁴.

    To see this, take a real vector v with dimensionless coefficients and expand the dot product using bilinearity. The symbol ᵀ means transpose; γ abbreviates γ(F) in the following identity. The sum runs over all output coordinates and the norm is that of H.

    vᵀγv=‖∑iviDFi‖2≥0

    The right-hand side vanishes exactly when the combination of derivatives vanishes. At a fixed realization, γ is therefore invertible if and only if these derivatives are linearly independent in H. “Almost surely invertible” means that the realizations where this fails have probability 0. Positive semidefiniteness alone still allows a lost direction.

  3. Differentiate the commands before substituting numbers

    The constant term in Y disappears on differentiation. We obtain DX = ℓh and DY = 2ℓZh + ℓbk. These derivatives belong to the plane in H spanned by h and k. J denotes the matrix whose rows are their components in this orthonormal basis. Its columns are the arrows in the first diagram.

    J=[ℓ02ℓZℓb]

    At the chosen point, the square of Z is 1; subtracting 1 gives 0. Since bU = 0, the expression in parentheses in Y is 0. Thus X = 2 cm and Y = 0 cm. The rows of J are (2, 0) cm and (4, 2) cm per unit of noise. Positions and their derivatives are different quantities: Y being 0 does not make DY vanish.

  4. Form each dot product

    The orthonormality of h and k turns the dot product in H into a sum of products of components. This is precisely why γ = JJᵀ here. For a basis that is not orthonormal, the dot products of the basis vectors would also enter the calculation.

    γ(F)=JJᵀ=ℓ2[12Z2Z4Z2+b2]

    Entry XX: 2 × 2 + 0 × 0 = 4 + 0 = 4 cm². Entry XY: 2 × 4 + 0 × 2 = 8 + 0 = 8 cm². Entry YX is identical by symmetry.

    Entry YY: 4 × 4 + 2 × 2 = 16 + 4 = 20 cm². We add the squared sensitivities to the different sources, rather than the coordinates X and Y.

    γ=[48820] cm²

  5. Calculate the determinant and check it another way

    The determinant of a symmetric matrix in the plane is the product of the diagonal entries minus the square of the off-diagonal entry. Our calculation gives 4 × 20 − 8 × 8 = 80 − 64 = 16 cm⁴, strictly positive.

    Another route uses J, which is triangular: det J = ℓ × ℓb. At our point, this is 2 × 2 = 4 cm². The determinant of JJᵀ is the square of det J, giving 16 cm⁴. This check uses the structure of the derivative and recovers the result without repeating the previous subtraction.

    detγ=ℓ4b2

    This expression holds for every Z and every U. With b = 1, the matrix is therefore invertible at every realization. The determinant does not depend on Z, whereas the matrix itself does. Its sign and whether it vanishes are useful here; its numerical value also depends on the chosen units.

  6. Turn off one command and identify the lost direction

    Keep the same platform, but take b = 0. DY no longer contains a component along k. At Z = 1, the matrix becomes [[4, 8], [8, 16]] cm²; its determinant is 64 − 64 = 0 cm⁴.

    The coefficient vector v = (−2, 1) is then a lost output direction: −2DX + DY = (−4 + 4)h = 0h. It is normal to the local tangent, so a small noise variation has no projection onto it to first order. More generally, the vector (−2Z, 1) makes this combination vanish at every realization when b is 0.

    Eliminate Z: X = ℓZ gives the following exact relation. It explains the loss of a local direction, even though the curve is not a straight line. For each Z, there is a local normal; it is not a single fixed direction valid for the entire cloud of points.

    Y=X2ℓ−ℓ

  7. Compare with the covariance of the positions

    Write C for ordinary covariance: Cᵢⱼ = E[(Fᵢ − E[Fᵢ])(Fⱼ − E[Fⱼ])]. Unlike γ, C takes an expectation over realizations. The means of Z and U are 0 and their second moments are 1. Odd moments vanish by symmetry. For the moment of order 4, Gaussian integration by parts E[Zg(Z)] = E[g′(Z)] applies to the cube of Z: the boundary terms, polynomials times the Gaussian density, vanish.

    E[Z4]=3E[Z2]=3

    Thus E[X] = E[Y] = 0 cm. The variance of the square of Z is 3 − 1 = 2. Independence makes the cross terms with U vanish; symmetry makes the expectation of the product of Z and its centered square vanish. We obtain the following matrix; diag indicates a matrix with 0 off-diagonal entries.

    C=ℓ2diag(1,2+b2)

    With b = 1, C = diag(4, 12) cm² and det C = 48 cm⁴. This is not the matrix γ evaluated above, nor is its determinant the same. When b is turned off, C remains invertible: the last exercise explains why this fact does not replace the Malliavin test.

  8. Relate the test to the distribution in this case only

    Here, for nonzero b, the transformation from commands to positions has an explicit inverse. We recover Z = X/ℓ, with 1/ℓ = 1/2 per cm, or 0.5 per cm. We then recover U using the following formula.

    U=Yℓ−Z2+1b

    The Jacobian determinant is the square of ℓ multiplied by b; it does not vanish. The change of variables therefore transports the Gaussian density of the pair (Z, U) into a smooth density on the plane. This is a direct justification specific to our model, without invoking an unproved general theorem.

    For 0 b, all positions lie on a parabola, a set of 0 planar area; a probability distribution concentrated on it cannot have a density with respect to area in the plane. It can nevertheless be described along the curve. Having a density therefore depends on the reference measure.

    In a general model, existence of a density and its regularity require distinct theorems and assumptions. Invertibility of γ alone must not be presented as a proof of a smooth density. This unit stops at calculating γ and interpreting its directions; brackets and the Hörmander criterion come next.

Explain it in your own words

Why does Y = 0 cm not mean DY = 0? Then explain how turning off k can make γ singular even though the positions do not stay on a straight line. Reconstruct the determinant check without looking at the entries.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I take the expectation of the derivatives to construct γ.”

    The definition is the dot product of the derivatives at the realization under consideration. Adding an expectation gives a different object; differentiating the expectations can even erase all sensitivities. Return to the definition and the Gram identity.

    Review the definition, then the dot products

  • “An invertible ordinary covariance guarantees that the noise locally spans the plane.”

    The global spread can span the plane for a distribution concentrated on a curve. In the variant with k turned off, find the combination of derivatives that vanishes, then compare with the covariance calculation for the positions.

    Calculate the degenerate variant and its covariance

  • “A positive determinant is enough to claim a smooth density for any model.”

    You must specify the object, the assumptions and the reference measure. The proof given here comes from the explicit inverse of our Gaussian transformation. A general regularity theorem requires more; reading it does not prove it.

    Reread the final step and its scope

The component of DY along h is proportional to Z; the component along k remains ℓb.

The rows of J become orthogonal: their cross dot products vanish.

Compare with the solution

At Z = 0, DX has components (2, 0) cm and DY has components (0, 2) cm. Thus J = diag(2, 2) cm per unit of noise.

The matrix γ is diag(4, 4) cm². The determinant is 4 × 4 = 16 cm⁴. The off-diagonal entry has changed, without losing a direction or changing the determinant. This confirms that γ is random even though its determinant is constant here.

Exercise 2 · Geometric diagnosis

Find the insensitive direction

Turn off k: b = 0. At Z = 1, recalculate γ and its determinant. Use the derivatives to verify that v = (−2, 1) is a lost direction. Must this vector remain the same for all Z?

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Entry YY loses the square of ℓb; the other entries retain their values at the point under consideration.

Hint 2

To test v, form −2DX + DY; then find how to replace the coefficient of DX when Z varies.

Compare with the solution

γ = [[4, 8], [8, 16]] cm². det γ = 4 × 16 − 8 × 8 = 64 − 64 = 0 cm⁴. The diagonal entries are positive, but that is not enough for invertibility.

At this point, −2DX + DY = −4h + 4h = 0h. The vector v is therefore in the kernel of γ. As Z varies, a normal is (−2Z, 1): the lost direction rotates with the position on the parabola. The kernel is the set of vectors that γ maps to the 0 vector.

Check your reasoning

  • Show the subtraction in the determinant.
  • Check the combination of derivatives instead of only stating a rank.
  • Distinguish a local normal from a direction that is constant across all realizations.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and justification

An invertible covariance on a curve

In the variant b = 0, use the Gaussian moments established in the example to calculate C, the ordinary covariance of (X, Y). Compare its determinant with that of γ. Does the distribution have a density with respect to area in the plane? Justify your answer without confusing the curve with a straight line.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The centered square of Z has 0 mean. Expand the square of this quantity: the moment of order 4, minus 2 times the second moment, plus 1.

Hint 2

The cross covariance uses an odd moment. For the planar density, use the exact relation between the coordinates: Y is the square of X divided by ℓ, minus ℓ.

Compare with the solution

The means of X and Y are 0 cm. The variance of X is the square of ℓ, or 4 cm². The cross covariance multiplies this same square by the difference between the moment of order 3 of Z and its mean: it is 0 cm². For the variance of Y, the dimensionless factor is 3 − 2 + 1 = 2, giving 4 × 2 = 8 cm².

C = diag(4, 8) cm² and det C = 32 cm⁴, strictly positive. Yet det γ = 0 cm⁴ at every realization: the derivative has only one local direction available.

The distribution has no planar density: it assigns probability 1 to the parabola obtained by eliminating Z, which has 0 planar area. Invertibility of C rules out a nontrivial deterministic affine relation between the coordinates; it does not rule out this quadratic relation. Global spread and local directions answer different questions.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the moments and off-diagonal entries, with units.
  • Obtain the different determinants without replacing γ with E[γ].
  • Argue using the curved support and area measure, without claiming that there is no distribution along the curve.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How do we define γ(F), and where is the expectation?

Entry ij is ⟨DFᵢ, DFⱼ⟩ in the space of noise directions. No expectation enters this definition; γ can depend on the realization.

Recall 2Why is γ positive semidefinite, and when is it invertible at a fixed realization?

The form vᵀγv is the squared norm of the combination of derivatives weighted by v. It is therefore positive or 0; γ is invertible exactly when only the trivial combination makes these derivatives vanish.

Recall 3With dimensionless normalized commands and positions in cm, what are the units of γ and its determinant in the plane?

The derivatives of the positions are in cm. The dot products are therefore in cm²; the determinant of the matrix in the plane is in cm⁴. Its sign does not depend on a consistent change of unit.

Recall 4Why can a distribution on a parabola have an invertible ordinary covariance but a singular Malliavin matrix?

Ordinary covariance measures global spread and does not detect every nonlinear constraint. The derivative remains tangent to the parabola: a normal direction is locally inaccessible, so its Gram matrix is singular.

Recall 5What proof of a smooth density have we actually given for nonzero b?

The Gaussian transformation in our example has an explicit inverse and a Jacobian that is nonzero everywhere. The change of variables proves the smooth density in this case. No general regularity theorem reduces here to the word “invertible” alone.
