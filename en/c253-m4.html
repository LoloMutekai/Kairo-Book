MLV

Your goalCalibrate the weight of a mixture of two volatility states to a fictional quote, predict other prices, then identify what this calibration does not prove.

✿01 See the idea

A fictional trading desk must quote a European call on a currency pair A and B. The forward exchange rate is 100 units of B per unit of A, and the call gives the right to buy one unit of A at a rate fixed in advance, the strike K, at maturity. No one knows whether the coming year will be calm or turbulent. Instead of relying on a single volatility, we retain two possible market states.

A volatility mixture, denoted MLV, formalizes this idea: a finite number of volatility states, each with a weight, meaning the probability that this state is the one that occurs. The weights are positive and their sum is 1. The price of a product is the average of the prices obtained in each state, weighted by these weights.

This page reduces each state to its final outcome: a probability distribution of the exchange rate at maturity over three values, like a discrete density. The calm state gives 90, 100 or 110; the turbulent state gives 60, 100 or 140. Each outer value has probability 0.25 and the central value has probability 0.50. Both states have the same mean, the forward rate 100.

The weight w of the turbulent state is the only adjustable parameter. When w equals 0, we recover the calm state alone; when w equals 1, the turbulent state alone. Between the two, the prices are mixed. The first diagram shows the three distributions; the second shows that, for a fixed strike, the mixture price is a straight line as a function of w.

All values in this scene are fictional and intended for calculations by hand: they are neither market quotes, calibration of a real model, nor advice. Interest rates are assumed to be 0 in both currencies, so there is no discounting, and the forward exchange rate equals the spot exchange rate. This unit follows those titled leverage function, conditional calibration and densities; it uses none of their results, only the idea that a density is a probability distribution, and every symbol is defined again here.

The rule to remember

Mixture price = (one minus w) × calm state price + w × turbulent state price. A single quote is enough to determine w, which must stay between 0 and 1. A second price, not used for calibration, tests the choice of states.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Calibrate a weight to one call, then test elsewhere

The calm and turbulent states are those in the first diagram. The fictional quote for the call with strike 100 is 5.00 units of B. Calibrate the weight of the turbulent state, predict the calls with strikes 110 and 120, check the result by another method, then compare it with a second fictional quote.

  1. Define the symbols and assumptions

    S denotes the exchange rate at maturity, in units of B per unit of A; it is random. K is the strike. The call payoff is S − K if S exceeds K, and 0 otherwise. The price C(K) is the mean of this payoff. With no discounting, it is expressed directly in units of B.

    C(K)=E[max(S−K,0)]

    We assume: a European option; 0 interest rates; known probabilities for the values; the same mean 100 in each state, otherwise the mixture would not recover the forward rate; a turbulent state weight w between 0 and 1, with the calm state weight equal to one minus w. No transaction costs or bid–ask spread are represented.

  2. Price of a call in each state

    At strike 100, only values above the strike produce a payoff. In the calm state, this is 110, which pays 110 − 100 = 10, with probability 0.25. In the turbulent state, it is 140, which pays 40 with the same probability.

    Ccalme(100)=0.25×(110−100)=2.50
    Cagité(100)=0.25×(140−100)=10.00

    The central value 100 and the lower value of each state pay 0 at this strike: they make no contribution.

  3. Write the mixture price

    Group the outcomes by state: the turbulent state occurs with probability w, and the calm state with one minus w. The mean payoff therefore splits into two conditional means, each multiplied by the probability of its state.

    C(K)=(1−w)Ccalme(K)+wCagité(K)

    The price is linear in w. As long as the two state prices differ, a quote therefore determines a unique weight.

  4. Calibrate the weight

    Let q denote the quote. Solving q = (one minus w) × C calm + w × C turbulent gives the following formula, which requires the two state prices to differ.

    w=q−Ccalme(K)Cagité(K)−Ccalme(K)
    w=5.00−2.5010.00−2.50=2.507.50=1/3

    The numerator measures how far the quote is from the calm state price, and the denominator is the gap between the two states. The weight is 1/3, or approximately 0.333, so the calm state receives 2/3. The weight is admissible because the quote lies between the two state prices.

  5. Predict prices at two other strikes

    At strike 110, no value in the calm state exceeds the strike, so it makes no contribution. The turbulent state pays 30 at the value 140, with probability 0.25, giving 7.50.

    C(110)=1/3×7.50=2.50

    At strike 120, the turbulent state pays 20 at the value 140, giving 5.00 before weighting. The mixture price is w times this number, or 1/3 × 5.00, approximately 1.67; the exact result is 5/3.

    C(120)=1/3×5.00≈1.67

  6. Check using the distribution over five values

    Another method: construct the mixture distribution. The values 60 and 140 each receive 1/12, or w × 0.25. The values 90 and 110 each receive 1/6, or (one minus w) × 0.25. The central value retains 1/2. The sum is 1 and the mean remains 100.

    Recalculate the call with strike 100 without using the state prices: only 110 and 140 produce a payoff.

    1/6×10+1/12×40=5/3+10/3=5

    The exact calculation gives 5 again, meaning the quote 5: the weight does reproduce the calibration point, and the two methods agree.

  7. Mixing prices differs from mixing amplitudes

    The amplitude of a state is the distance between its upper value and the forward rate: 10 for the calm state, 40 for the turbulent state. Weighting it with the same weights gives a mean amplitude.

    amplitude moyenne=2/3×10+1/3×40=20

    A single state with amplitude 20 gives, at strike 100, the price 5, and therefore the same quote. But its upper value is 120: at strike 120 it pays 0, whereas the mixture of prices gives 1.67. Two models can agree on one price and differ on another.

  8. Compare with a second quote

    A second fictional quote, at strike 110, is 3.00. The weight was not fitted to it; the mixture predicts 2.50.

    résidu=3.00−2.50=0.50

    The residual is not 0. With these two states and a single weight, we cannot reproduce both quotes at once. This says nothing about a real market: it describes the gap between these two fictional numbers under the stated assumptions.

Explain it in your own words

Why is a single quote enough to determine the weight, and what can make this weight unusable? Also explain why the second quote provides information that the first could not provide.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I can mix the volatilities, then calculate a single price.”

    A call price is not linear in the amplitude of a state. The mean amplitude 20 gives 5 again at strike 100, but 0 at strike 120, whereas the mixture of prices gives 1.67. Mix the prices or distributions, never the amplitudes.

    Review the step on amplitudes

  • “The model reproduces my quote, so it is validated.”

    The weight is chosen to reproduce this quote: agreement follows by construction. Only an unused quote tests the model; here the residual is 0.50. Even a 0 residual at a few points would not cover other products.

    Review the second quote

  • “The calculation gives a weight greater than one, but I keep it anyway.”

    A weight is a probability. Above 1, the calm state would receive a negative weight, here -0.27 for the quote 12. Reject the calibration and conclude that these two states are insufficient for this quote, under these assumptions.

    Repeat the exercise on admissibility

Another quote, the same calibration

In the same fictional scene, the quote for the call with strike 100 becomes 3.75 units of B. Calculate the weight of the turbulent state, check that it is admissible, then predict the price of the call with strike 110.

Hint 1

Reuse the state prices at strike 100 calculated in the example: only the quote changes.

Hint 2

At strike 110, the calm state makes no contribution: multiply the turbulent state price by the weight you found.

Compare with the solution

Gap between the quote and the calm state price: 3.75 − 2.50 = 1.25. Gap between the two states: 7.50. The weight is 1.25 / 7.50 = 1/6, or approximately 0.167, between 0 and 1: it is admissible.

Prediction: 1/6 × 7.50 = 1.25 units of B. Check: substituting the weight back into the mixture price at strike 100 recovers the quote 3.75.

Check your reasoning

  • Both gaps are calculated in the same unit.
  • The admissible interval is checked, rather than assumed.
  • The price at strike 110 uses only the turbulent state price, with a reason given.

Exercise 2 · Analysis of a limiting case

An unreachable quote

The fictional quote for the call with strike 100 is now 12.00 units of B. Calculate the weight obtained from the same formula. Can it be used as a probability? What does this result reveal about the two chosen states?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First compare the quote with the two state prices at strike 100.

Hint 2

Also calculate the weight of the calm state, one minus w: what is its sign?

Compare with the solution

Gap from the calm state price: 12.00 − 2.50 = 9.50. Weight: 9.50 / 7.50 = 19/15, or approximately 1.27. This number exceeds 1.

The calm state weight is 1 − 19/15 = -4/15, which is negative: it is not a probability. The quote even exceeds the price of the turbulent state alone, 10.00. No mixture of these two states with positive weights can produce it. Under these assumptions, other states, another strike for checking, or a review of the fictional quote is needed; this page does not decide which.

Check your reasoning

  • The weight formula is applied without premature rounding.
  • Rejection is based on the sign of the calm state weight or on comparison with the two state prices.
  • The conclusion stays within the assumptions of the scene.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

A state that shifts the forward rate

Replace the upper value of the turbulent state with 150, leaving all other values and probabilities unchanged. By how much does the mean of this state exceed the forward rate 100? By how much does the mixture mean exceed it, using the weight calibrated in the example? Why is this a problem for a model intended to recover the forward rate?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Only one value moves: the mean moves by its displacement multiplied by its probability.

Hint 2

The calm state remains centered on the forward rate; weight the excess of the turbulent state by its weight.

Compare with the solution

The upper value moves by 150 − 140 = 10, with probability 0.25: the mean of the turbulent state exceeds the forward rate by 2.50. In the mixture, the calm state contributes no excess; the turbulent state contributes 1/3 × 2.50 = 5/6, or approximately 0.83.

The mixture would then have a mean above the forward rate: it would no longer recover the forward exchange rate that it must respect in this scene. The requirement that each state have the same mean is therefore more than a detail of presentation.

Check your reasoning

  • The displacement of a single value is isolated.
  • The excess is weighted by the weight of the state concerned.
  • The link to the forward exchange rate is stated.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is mixed in the MLV on this page, and what is not mixed?

We mix the prices, or final distributions, of a few volatility states, with positive weights summing to one. We do not mix amplitudes before calculating a price.

Recall 2How is the weight derived from a quote?

The mixture price is linear in w. Solve w = (quote − calm state price) / (turbulent state price − calm state price), provided that the two state prices differ.

Recall 3What constraints make the mixture acceptable in this scene?

A weight between 0 and one for each state, weights summing to one, and the same mean, the forward rate, in each state.

Recall 4Why does reproducing the calibration quote fail to validate the model?

The weight is fitted for that purpose. Only an unused quote can contradict the model, and even agreement with it holds only under the stated assumptions.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Choose volatility states and their distributions; impose the same forward rate on each; write the mixture price as a weighted average of the state prices; solve for the weight using a quote; check that it stays between 0 and one; test against an unused quote without drawing conclusions beyond the assumptions.
