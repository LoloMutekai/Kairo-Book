Level 2 · C073·M3

Nonparametric tests

Your goalCompare times by their ranks, calculate an exact Mann–Whitney test, and explain what its p-value allows us to conclude.

Before you start
c056-m5 : effect size · c065-m5 : prediction interval · c073-m1 : ANOVA · c073-m2 : multiple comparisons

✿01 See the idea

A library is choosing between layouts A and B for its catalogue. In this entirely fictional scene, 6 different people search for the same type of reference: each person uses just one layout. The times for A are 12 s, 15 s, and 18 s; those for B are 22 s, 27 s, and 45 s. Before calculating, ask yourself: who finishes before whom?

The longest time could have a large influence on a mean. Another question is to examine the order: here every time for A comes before every time for B. We sort all the times together, then replace each time with its position, called its rank. The smallest receives rank 1. The gap in seconds disappears; the order remains.

The Mann–Whitney test, also called the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, asks whether this separation between 2 independent groups is difficult to obtain under an assumption of the same distribution. Here a distribution is the probability law describing possible times and their frequencies. Nonparametric means that the test does not specify a family such as the normal distribution for these times; it never means “without assumptions”.

Keep the 6 times and imagine redistributing the A and B labels while keeping the group sizes fixed. A visible separation can still be compatible with chance in this allocation when the groups are small. The diagrams therefore separate the description of the sample from the calculation under the null hypothesis.

The rule to remember

State the assumptions; rank the observations together; count comparisons between groups; compare the result with possible allocations under the null hypothesis; distinguish the p-value from the size of the effect.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Complete separation, but a test that is still inconclusive

Keep the fictional catalogue times. The 3 people in A and the 3 in B are different people. The protocol specifies random allocation, the same task conditions, and a two-sided analysis at the threshold α = 0.05, all fixed before seeing the times. “Two-sided” means that either A being slower or B being slower can count against the null hypothesis.

  1. State precisely what is being tested

    The null hypothesis states that the times in the 2 groups follow the same distribution. Under this hypothesis, we assume independent observations and exchangeable labels: conditional on the pooled times and group sizes, every allocation of the labels has the same probability. Independence here requires that one person’s result does not determine another’s. The planned random allocation supports this comparison; the small table alone does not verify the protocol.

    Equality of the means alone is not enough to justify this redistribution. There is no need to assume normally distributed times. Our exact calculation uses the absence of ties in the original times; the exercise on ties will show what changes.

    jzaudw lrgtrs wvnogw gbkdlz aadpgm qgblpp gnyl The symbol i identifies a person, and rᵢ is the rank of that person’s time in the pooled group. R_A will be the sum of the ranks labelled A. U_A and U_B will count comparisons between groups. Ranks, U, the p-value, and the threshold α have no physical unit; times remain in seconds.

  2. Pool the times, then rank them

    The order is 12 s (A), 15 s (A), 18 s (A), 22 s (B), 27 s (B), 45 s (B). The pooled ranks are 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. Ranking each group separately would give the same initial ranks to the 2 groups and erase their separation.

    Adding the ranks for A gives R_A = 1 + 2 + 3 = 3 + 3 = 6. For B, R_B = 4 + 5 + 6 = 9 + 6 = 15; R_B denotes its rank sum.

  3. Subtract the part of the ranks internal to A

    The rank of a time for A counts its position among the times for A, plus the number of times for B that are smaller than it. djuczx buzdaa xgzfwb ihgcis xssoyh mtxluj kusx Subtracting it from R_A therefore leaves exactly the number of pairs in which the time for A exceeds the time for B. This is the convention chosen for U_A; some software displays the other direction.

    UA=RA−nA(nA+1)2

    Here the internal part is 3 × 4 / 2 = 12 / 2 = 6, also equal to 1 + 2 + 3. Thus U_A = 6 − 6 = 0. This direction gives a small U_A when the times for A are short.

  4. Check using pairs and the sum of the ranks

    There are 3 × 3 = 9 A–B pairs. Every time for A is smaller than every time for B: no pair contributes to U_A, giving 0. xiwozf iyeihw xpqw The direct count confirms the rank formula.

    Another check: R_A + R_B = 6 + 15 = 21. The sum of all the ranks must be N(N + 1)/2 = 6 × 7 / 2 = 42 / 2 = 21. We have neither omitted a position nor counted one 2 times.

    The descriptive fraction of pairs in which A is faster is 9/9 = 1. It describes this sample; it says neither how many seconds a person will save nor what fraction of future people will be faster with A. Comparisons that share the same person are not 9 independent observations.

  5. Count the allocations under the null hypothesis

    Keep the times fixed and choose which 3 people receive the A label; the others receive B. Choosing 3 distinct ranks in succession gives 6 × 5 × 4 = 30 × 4 = 120 ordered choices.

    But this counts the same trio 3 × 2 × 1 = 6 × 1 = 6 times: the order of the choices does not create a new allocation. We therefore divide 120 by 6, giving 20 equally likely allocations. We do not permute the order of people within a group as though that changed the test.

  6. Identify allocations with at least as much separation

    For each trio of ranks allocated to A, add its ranks and subtract 6. An example of the count: the trio (1, 2, 3) gives U_A = 0. For complete separation in the opposite direction, A must take the trio (4, 5, 6), which gives U_A = 9. Each of these trios is unique.

    For this two-sided test, we declare an allocation at least as extreme if the smaller of the 2 counts U_A and U_B is no greater than the observed minimum. This observed minimum is 0. Only the 2 allocations above therefore meet the criterion; the others have less separation.

    The second diagram gives the full count. Its heights add up to 1 + 1 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 2 + 1 + 1 = 20. The counts are symmetric: swapping A and B replaces U_A with 9 − U_A.

  7. Calculate p and draw a conclusion supported by the evidence

    Under the null hypothesis and our exchangeability assumptions, the p-value is the probability of a result at least as extreme according to the stated criterion. Each allocation has the same weight; we therefore divide the number of extreme allocations by the total number of allocations.

    p=220=110=0.10

    The threshold α = 0.05 is the cutoff for the rejection rule fixed in advance: reject if p ≤ α. Here 0.10 > 0.05; we do not reject the hypothesis of the same distribution. This does not establish that the layouts are equal. The observed ranking entirely favours A, but the sample size makes even this extreme case insufficient at this two-sided threshold.

    p does not measure the probability that the null hypothesis is true. The fraction of favourable pairs and p answer different questions. A nonsignificant result can accompany a strong descriptive separation, as it does here.

  8. Keep the limits of the method in view

    A strictly increasing transformation applied to all the times preserves the order and therefore the result of this test. Ranks limit the influence of a large gap in seconds, but they remove neither selection bias nor dependence between observations.

    Equal times receive the mean of the positions they occupy; a tied pair contributes 1/2 to each U. The distribution without ties in the diagram cannot be reused unchanged: the allocations must be recalculated with the ties preserved. The exercise on ties is limited to calculating the ranks and U in this case.

    This test is not generally a simple test of equal medians, where the median is the central value of a distribution. Interpreting it as a shift in location requires an additional model of distributions with the same shape, differing only by that shift. For more independent groups, Kruskal–Wallis is another rank test; paired measurements, linked to the same person, require an appropriate method. These extensions are not calculated in this unit.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Nonparametric means I can forget the protocol.”

    Normality is not required here, but independence and exchangeability under the null hypothesis justify equally likely allocations. Repeated trials by the same person do not become independent because their times are replaced with ranks.

    Review the assumptions in the example

  • “I rank each layout separately because it is simpler.”

    Each time must be compared with all the others. Separate ranking would give A and B the same initial ranks, even when all the times for A come before all those for B. Start again from the pooled list and keep the letters.

    Review the diagram of pooled ranks

  • “The test does not reject, so the layouts are equivalent.”

    Failing to reject means there is insufficient evidence against the null hypothesis at the chosen threshold. It does not demonstrate equivalence. Report the observed times, the rank result, the p-value, and the limitations of the small sample size separately.

    Repair the conclusion in the last exercise

Compare with the solution

The order becomes 12 s (A), 15 s (A), 18 s (A), 18 s (B), 27 s (B), 45 s (B). The tied positions each receive (3 + 4)/2 = 7/2 = 3.5, with no unit. The last ranks remain 5 and 6.

R_A = 1 + 2 + 3.5 = 6.5. Thus U_A = 6.5 − 6 = 0.5. Check: no pair has A slower than B; the only tie, at 18 s, counts as 1/2, giving 0.5.

The mean-rank method remains consistent, but the distribution of U changes with ties. Reusing the previous p-value without counting again would be unjustified. An exact procedure must redistribute the labels over the observations while preserving these ties; their presence does not automatically make every exact method offered by software valid.

Check your reasoning

  • Give the same rank 3.5 to equal times, without breaking the tie arbitrarily.
  • Recover U_A = 0.5 using both half a pair and R_A.
  • Explain why the count without ties does not directly provide the new p-value.

The direction of the test cannot be chosen after the result

Return to the original times, without ties. After obtaining p = 0.10, someone proposes: “Keep only A being faster: that leaves 1 case out of 20, so we have demonstrated that A is always better.” Calculate this one-sided probability, then repair the decision and the final sentence.

Hint 1

In the single direction of A being faster, only the allocation U_A = 0 is at least as extreme.

Hint 2

Separate the hypothetical calculation, the two-sided protocol actually stated, and the scope of a statistical conclusion.

Compare with the solution

For a one-sided question of “A being faster” fixed before the observations, the count gives 1/20 = 0.05. With the rule p ≤ α, this result would reach exactly the threshold α = 0.05. It is not below the threshold: inclusion of equality must be stated.

The protocol in our example was two-sided. Choosing the favourable direction after seeing the times does not allow us to replace its p-value with half that value while retaining the interpretation of the threshold. The stated decision therefore remains a failure to reject with p = 0.10. Other analyses may be explored, but they must not be presented as planned in advance.

An accurate sentence is: “In this sample, all the times for A are lower than those for B; the exact two-sided test gives p = 0.10 and does not reject the hypothesis of the same distribution at the threshold 0.05. The sample size is small.” Even a correctly obtained rejection would not establish that every person will always be faster with A.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate 0.05 by counting only the stated direction.
  • Keep the two-sided decision for the original protocol.
  • Reject both the guarantee of “always better” and the unjustified conclusion of equivalence.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Why pool the groups before assigning ranks?

Pooled ranks preserve who comes before whom between A and B. Ranking each group separately would erase this comparison. Tied values receive the mean of their positions; ranks lose information about gaps in seconds.

Recall 2How can U_A be recovered from R_A, and what does it count with our chosen direction?

pwjvab jnbktq pyfx What remains is the number of pairs in which A exceeds B, plus half a pair for each tie. cljkwa mjrtez dccv These counts have no physical unit.

Recall 3What makes the allocations of labels equally likely?

Under the null hypothesis of the same distribution, we assume independent observations and labels that are exchangeable conditional on the times and group sizes. Here nonparametric removes the assumption of a normal family for the times; it does not remove the assumptions of the study design.

Recall 4How can complete separation be insufficient for the two-sided test?

With 3 observations per group in this example, there are 20 allocations. The 2 complete separations in opposite directions give p = 2/20 = 0.10, greater than 0.05. This establishes neither equality of the groups nor absence of an effect.

Recall 5Why should Mann–Whitney not be summarised as “comparing medians”?

Here its null hypothesis concerns the same distribution. Interpreting it as a shift in location requires distributions with the same shape, differing only by that shift. A p-value is neither an effect size nor the probability that the null hypothesis is true.
