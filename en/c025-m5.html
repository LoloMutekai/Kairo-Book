Level 1 · C025·M5

O, Omega and Theta

Your goalJustify an upper bound, a lower bound and an order of cost using constants and a threshold that hold for every subsequent size.

✿01 See the idea

You keep the registration records for a trip. Someone cancels their registration: you go through every record, compare its identifier with the requested identifier, then write “keep” or “remove”. Even if you recognize the person near the start, you finish the register: every record needs a decision.

With more records, the same small task repeats more often. We will express this growth without giving a duration in seconds. An upper bound says that the work does not exceed a reference value; a lower bound says that it stays above a reference value. Bounds on both sides establish its order of growth.

The notations are O for the upper bound, Ω, the Greek capital letter omega, for the lower bound, and Θ, capital theta, for the bounds on both sides. The reference function may be multiplied by a constant, and the claim may start at a certain size. These allowances have precise limits: they do not let you choose a new constant at every size.

The situation and its counts are fictional. The initial register contains 4 records. Our model assigns 2 operations to each record and 3 fixed operations to the procedure. The aim of the lesson is to prove bounds on this count, without comparing real machines or software.

The rule to remember

Choose what to count; fix a cost function; find positive constants and a threshold; prove the inequality for every size at or above the threshold.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From a register of 4 records to a family of costs

Use the same procedure for every integer size n ≥ 1. Count 1 operation to compare a record and 1 to write down its decision. Add opening the sheet, writing down the target identifier and closing the sheet, counting 1 operation per action. Determine the exact cost, then its order relative to n.

  1. Fix the model before counting

    n is the number of input records. T(n) is the number of operations selected by our counting convention. These counts have no physical unit; the words “records” and “operations” specify what they count. They do not measure seconds. In this model only, comparing an identifier and writing down a decision each have cost 1, regardless of the record.

    We assume that every record is accessible, that identifier lengths are bounded and that the traversal ends normally after the last record. We do not count physical movement, allocation of a computer list or the details of a loop. The convention must stay the same when n changes; its simplicity does not make it a measure of the full amount of work.

    The position of the person to remove does not change the count: no early exit is allowed. At a given size, all configurations of this register therefore have the same T(n). For another algorithm, we would need to specify whether we are studying the best case, the worst case or another cost before setting bounds.

  2. Add the repeated work and the fixed work

    Each record requires a comparison and a decision, giving 2 operations. Over n records, that makes 2n operations. The 3 fixed actions are not repeated for each record: add them just once.

    T(n)=2n+3

    For the initial register: 2 × 4 = 8 operations during the traversal, then 8 + 3 = 11 operations in total. Check using a different breakdown: 4 comparisons + 4 decisions + 3 fixed actions = 11 operations. The formula agrees with the action count.

  3. Give the notations precise meanings

    g(n) denotes the reference growth function with which we compare T(n); here g(n) = n. We work with nonnegative functions T and a reference function g that is strictly positive at the sizes considered. The symbol ∈ means “belongs to”: O(g), Ω(g) and Θ(g) denote sets of functions.

    T ∈ O(g) means that there exist a constant c > 0 and an integer threshold ns ≥ 1 such that the following inequality holds for every integer n ≥ ns. This is an upper bound: T eventually stays below a fixed multiple of the reference function.

    0≤T(n)≤cg(n)

    T ∈ Ω(g) means that there exist a constant a > 0 and an integer threshold ns ≥ 1 such that this lower bound holds for every integer n ≥ ns. The cost stays above a positive multiple of the reference function.

    0≤ag(n)≤T(n)

    T ∈ Θ(g) means that there exist constants a > 0, b > 0 and an integer threshold ns ≥ 1 such that both bounds hold together for every integer n ≥ ns. The letters a and b denote the lower and upper coefficients; c denoted an upper coefficient on its own. These coefficients are numerical and have no physical unit. With g(n) = n, we read them as operations per record; the threshold ns is counted in records.

    0≤ag(n)≤T(n)≤bg(n)

    The threshold ns is the size from which the proof applies; its subscript s marks the starting size. Choose the constants and this threshold before varying n. If an upper bound and a lower bound initially have different thresholds, their maximum gives a common threshold: belonging to Θ(g) is equivalent to belonging to both O(g) and Ω(g).

  4. Build bounds that hold at every size from the threshold onward

    For a lower bound, the fixed term is positive: 2n ≤ 2n + 3. The lower coefficient a = 2 works for n ≥ 1.

    For an upper bound, try to absorb the fixed actions into an additional copy of n. For n ≥ 3, we have 3 ≤ n. Adding 2n to both sides gives 2n + 3 ≤ 3n. The upper coefficient b = 3 therefore works at the threshold ns = 3.

    n≥3⇒2n≤T(n)≤3n

    The same coefficients and the same threshold hold for every subsequent integer: we have proved T ∈ Θ(n), and therefore also T ∈ O(n) and T ∈ Ω(n). This order is called linear because both reference functions are proportional to n.

    At the threshold itself, the count is 9 operations and the bounds are 6 ≤ 9 ≤ 9. At n = 1, the cost would be 5, greater than 3n = 3: this does not invalidate a proof that starts only at n = 3. Other constants or another threshold could also work; we are not claiming they are unique.

  5. Check the count and the change in size

    At n = 4, the reference values give 8 ≤ 11 ≤ 12 operations. The margin below the upper bound is 12 − 11 = 1 operation: it is indeed positive. This numerical check could detect a calculation error; it does not replace the proof for all n.

    If the register doubles, its size becomes 8 records. The traversal costs 2 × 8 = 16 operations; the total is 16 + 3 = 19 operations. Directly doubling the old total would give 22: this would count the fixed actions twice, with an excess of 3 operations.

    The cost per record is initially 11/4 = 2.75 operations per record, then 19/8 = 2.375 operations per record. The fixed term is spread across more records. Θ(n) preserves the order of growth; it does not promise that the total doubles exactly when n doubles.

  6. Keep the conclusion within its exact scope

    O does not mean “worst case”, and Ω does not mean “best case”. They specify the directions of bounds on the function already chosen. Here, even recognizing the person at the first record removes no action from the traversal; our proof gives a lower and an upper bound on the same function T.

    The order Θ(n) gives neither the exact operation count of another program, nor its duration, nor its memory use. Different constants and real operations can matter a great deal for a small register. An upper bound can also be deliberately loose: the final exercise will ask whether the square of n provides an upper bound and whether it also provides a lower bound.

Explain it in your own words

Explain aloud why the fixed actions are still included in the exact cost, then show how the threshold lets you bound them by a multiple of n. Which part of your answer proves a property for every subsequent size?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “O describes the worst case and Omega describes the best case.”

    Start by naming the cost function under study. Then O gives an upper bound on it and Ω gives a lower bound. Our full traversal has the same cost at a fixed size and receives both bounds; changing the notation does not change the case.

    Review the choice of cost and the scope of the bounds

  • “I checked a few sizes, so it is proved.”

    The sizes checked verify the calculation. An asymptotic proof fixes the constants and a threshold, then justifies the inequality for every subsequent n. Rebuild the argument 3 ≤ n, which holds for all n ≥ 3.

    Rebuild the proof from the threshold onward

  • “I found an O bound, so I found Theta.”

    An upper bound can be too high to describe the exact order. A lower bound of the same order is missing. Try the reference function n × n for the register: its upper bound is valid, but no positive multiple of this reference function stays below T for all large sizes.

    Examine the loose upper bound in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Applying the model

A small register below the threshold

The same register has only 2 records. Calculate T(n), then 3n. Does the upper bound with coefficient 3 fail at this size? Does this contradict T ∈ O(n)?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First count the actions during the traversal, then add the fixed actions just once.

Hint 2

Reread the size from which the coefficient 3 was justified.

Compare with the solution

Traversal: 2 × 2 = 4 operations. Total: 4 + 3 = 7 operations. The proposed upper bound is 3 × 2 = 6 operations; we have 7 > 6.

The inequality fails here, but our proof requires it only for n ≥ 3. The cost remains in O(n). A failure before the threshold does not contradict the property; a failure beyond the stated threshold would invalidate these witnesses and require a review of the proof.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate 7 and 6 operations without multiplying the fixed actions by n.
  • Distinguish the failure of this inequality at a small size from the existence of an upper bound from a threshold onward.

Exercise 2 · Bounding on paper

The register doubles; the proof still holds

Start again with the register of 4 records and double its size. Find the cost without copying the example, bound it using the coefficients a = 2 and b = 3, then respond to “the cost must be exactly doubled because it is linear”. Also justify why the same coefficients work for even larger sizes.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The traversal doubles, but opening the sheet, setting the target person and closing the sheet are still performed just once.

Hint 2

Checking this size is not enough: reuse the inequality between the number of fixed actions and n.

Compare with the solution

The new size is 8 records. The cost is 2 × 8 + 3 = 19 operations. The reference values give 16 ≤ 19 ≤ 24 operations.

Double the old total would be 22 operations, which is 3 too many. The fixed part does not double. Being in Θ(n) means staying between multiples of n from the threshold onward, rather than being exactly proportional to n.

For every size n ≥ 3, the fixed actions still satisfy 3 ≤ n; thus 2n ≤ 2n + 3 ≤ 3n. This last sentence is the general proof; the count for 8 records checks it.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 19 operations and correct bounds in the same unit.
  • Explain the excess of 3 operations by the fixed actions.
  • Provide an inequality that holds for every n ≥ 3, beyond just the new total.

Exercise 3 · Justify and refute

An upper bound too loose to establish the order

Someone claims: “The cost of the register belongs to O(n × n), so it belongs to Θ(n × n).” Here n × n is the square of n. Show that the upper bound can be true and then that the conclusion is false. You need reasoning for arbitrarily large sizes, rather than a list of trials.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

We already know that T(n) ≤ 3n for n ≥ 3. Compare n with its square when n ≥ 1.

Hint 2

To test a lower bound a × n × n, take any positive coefficient a. What happens once n > 3/a, while staying beyond the threshold?

Compare with the solution

For n ≥ 3, we have n ≥ 1, so n ≤ n × n. Thus T(n) ≤ 3n ≤ 3 × n × n. The upper coefficient 3 and the threshold 3 do prove T ∈ O(n × n). This bound is less precise than O(n).

Now suppose a lower bound a × n × n ≤ T(n) is proposed, with a > 0, starting at an integer threshold ns. Here a is any candidate, not necessarily the coefficient chosen in the example. Choose an integer n at least as large as ns and 3, but strictly greater than 3/a. Such an integer exists, however large these bounds are.

an2>3n≥T(n)

The lower bound therefore fails beyond the proposed threshold. This reasoning holds for every positive a and every threshold: T does not belong to Ω(n × n), and therefore does not belong to Θ(n × n). A valid upper bound does not supply the missing lower bound. The tight bound Θ(n) remains the one proved for the register.

Check your reasoning

  • Prove the upper bound for every size n ≥ 3.
  • Handle an arbitrary positive lower coefficient and an arbitrary threshold.
  • Choose a size beyond this threshold that contradicts the lower bound; do not make a depend on n.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What must you provide to prove T ∈ O(g)?

A coefficient c > 0, an integer threshold nₛ and a proof of T(n) ≤ c × g(n) for every integer n ≥ nₛ, with T nonnegative. The coefficient and the threshold stay fixed as n varies.

Recall 2What does Ω(g) say, and why does it not automatically denote the best case?

It bounds the chosen cost function from below by a × g(n), with a > 0, from a threshold onward. The case under study must be defined separately: we can give a lower bound on a worst-case function, a best-case function or our count that is identical across all configurations.

Recall 3Reconstruct the witnesses for T ∈ Θ(n) for the register.

T(n) = 2n + 3. Take a = 2, b = 3 and nₛ = 3. For every n ≥ 3, the fixed actions satisfy 3 ≤ n: thus 2n ≤ T(n) ≤ 3n.

Recall 4Why does a table of a few sizes fail to prove an asymptotic bound?

It checks only the sizes listed. The definition covers every integer from a threshold onward. A general justification is needed; a contradiction beyond the stated threshold suffices to invalidate the proposed witnesses.

Recall 5Why can the register be in O(n × n) while remaining in Θ(n)?

An upper bound can be loose: 3n is below 3 × n × n for n ≥ 1. For Θ(n × n), a positive multiple of the square would also need to stay below the cost at all large sizes; this lower bound is impossible here.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Close the page and reconstruct a short proof: name the size and the operation counted; recover the cost of the register; define O, Ω and Θ with their constants and threshold; provide the linear bounds for every relevant n; finish by explaining why an upper bound involving a square does not establish a quadratic order.
