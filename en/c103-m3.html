Optimizers

Your goalCalculate a weight update, compare descent without memory and momentum, then check what the loss allows us to conclude.

✿01 See the idea

A small neural network must estimate the mass of a parcel from the signal of a weighing scale. To isolate the role of the optimizer, we adjust only the final weight: with the signal fixed at 1, the output equals this weight. It reports 6 kg, while the reference mass is 2 kg. These data are fictitious; no real parcel has been weighed.

The gradient indicates how the loss varies when the weight increases. Here, a positive slope suggests decreasing the weight. The optimizer turns this information into a change in the weight: it chooses how much to subtract and may retain a memory of previous information. Backpropagation supplies the gradient; it does not choose this update rule on its own.

Without memory, we consider only the current slope. With momentum, we add part of the previous accumulator to this slope. The first change can be identical, then the trajectories diverge. The current weight alone is therefore insufficient to reproduce a sequence with momentum: we also need to know its memory.

We will compare these rules on the same parcel, without changing the target or the signal. A decrease in loss will show only a better fit to this training example. By itself, it will say nothing about accuracy on other parcels.

The rule to remember

Calculate the gradient at the current weights; apply the rule and its memory; retain the new state; recalculate the loss to check the change in the weights.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Adjusting the final weight of the scale

The signal x = 1 is dimensionless, the reference is y = 2 kg, and the initial weight is w = 6 kg. Take a step size α = 0.25, an initial memory v = 0 kg, and a momentum coefficient β = 0.5. Both coefficients are dimensionless in this model. Follow 2 updates on the same fictitious example.

  1. Define the output, loss, and assumptions

    The final unit is linear and has no bias: its output ŷ, in kilograms, is w × x. The hat distinguishes the estimate from the reference y. All other parts of the network remain fixed; the signal x and the target y remain constant. Here we impose neither a bound on w nor any additional term in the loss.

    ŷ=wx;e=ŷ−y;L(w)=e22

    e is the signed error in kg. L is the quadratic loss in kg²; division by 2 simplifies its derivative without changing the weight that minimizes it. Initially, e = 6 − 2 = 4 kg; its square is 16 kg² and L = 16 ÷ 2 = 8 kg².

  2. Calculate the gradient at the current weight

    In this single coordinate, the gradient g is the derivative of L with respect to w: a slope in kg²/kg, hence in kg. The chain rule multiplies the derivative of the square by that of the output. The factor 2 from the square cancels the divisor 2 in the loss.

    g=dLdw=(wx−y)x=w−y lorsque x = 1

    Initially, g = 4 kg. Since this slope is positive, a small increase in w would increase L. We should therefore try decreasing w. The equality between gradient and error holds here because x = 1; it does not hold for every network.

  3. Turn the slope into a weight change without memory

    Gradient descent without memory subtracts αg. α is the learning rate, or step size, and is strictly positive. The prime in w′ means “new weight”. The gradient must be calculated using the old w.

    w′=w−αg

    The product αg has the unit of the weight: kg here, because α is dimensionless. This choice of unit depends on our loss and parameterization; in another problem, this check must be repeated. The minus sign aims for a local decrease with a sufficiently small step; it does not guarantee that an arbitrarily large change will be beneficial.

    When the gradient is estimated using a randomly selected example, this rule is called stochastic gradient descent, often abbreviated SGD. A small random group of examples also provides an estimate. Our calculation deliberately repeats the same example: no randomness is needed to check the arithmetic.

  4. Make the first shared change

    Without memory, the amount subtracted is αg = 0.25 × 4 = 1 kg. The new weight is therefore 6 − 1 = 5 kg.

    Recalculate the output at the new weight: it is 5 kg. The error becomes 5 − 2 = 3 kg, its square is 9 kg², and the loss is 9 ÷ 2 = 4.5 kg². The loss has indeed decreased from 8 kg². This check concerns the example used for the weight change.

  5. Define the rule with momentum precisely

    v denotes a gradient accumulator, in kg here, rather than a physical velocity of the scale. β retains a fraction of this accumulator: 0 ≤ β < 1. We choose the following unnormalized convention; there is no additional factor multiplying the new gradient.

    v′=βv+g;w′=w−αv′

    First calculate v′, then use this new value to change w. Initially, βv = 0.5 × 0 = 0 kg. Thus v′ = 0 + 4 = 4 kg: the first update again gives 5 kg. Retain w = 5 kg and v = 4 kg together for what follows.

    Some conventions also weight the new gradient by 1 − β. They produce an accumulator on a different scale. The numerical step size therefore cannot be compared without specifying the convention. With β = 0, our rule exactly recovers descent without memory.

  6. Reuse the memory for the next change

    At the weight 5 kg, recalculate g = 5 − 2 = 3 kg. Reusing the initial gradient 4 kg would mean measuring the slope at the wrong location.

    The memory contribution is βv = 0.5 × 4 = 2 kg. The new accumulator is v′ = 2 + 3 = 5 kg. The amount subtracted is αv′ = 0.25 × 5 = 1.25 kg.

    w′=5−1.25=3.75 kg

    The new state to retain is w = 3.75 kg and v = 5 kg. The error is 3.75 − 2 = 1.75 kg; its square is 3.0625 kg². The exact loss is 49/32 kg², or 1.53125 kg². The first exercise will reconstruct the other branch, without memory.

  7. Check another way and limit the conclusion

    Check the change with momentum: 5 − 3.75 = 1.25 kg; dividing this difference by 0.25 recovers the accumulator 5 kg. Check the loss: the error 1.75 kg is closer to 0 than 3 kg, and its loss is below 4.5 kg².

    For descent without memory alone, our loss allows another check: since g = w − y, the next error e′ follows directly from the previous error e.

    e′=(1−α)e;L(w′)=(1−α)2L(w)

    With our step size, the error is multiplied by 0.75 and the loss by 0.5625. For a nonzero error, the loss strictly decreases if 0 < α < 2. This bound comes from this parabola and x = 1; it is neither a universal bound for networks nor a criterion for our rule with momentum.

    Momentum gives a smaller loss here after 2 changes. This exact result on a fictitious example demonstrates no general superiority, no convergence on other losses, and no improvement on new parcels.

  8. Remember the order of operations and the role of the state

    An iteration reads the current weights, calculates the output and loss, obtains the gradient, updates the memory if there is any, then changes the weights. The loss used for checking is recalculated after this change. In an implementation that adds gradients from several calls, we must also specify when this accumulation is reset to 0: it is distinct from our optimizer’s memory v.

    To resume our calculation with momentum exactly, we need the weights, v, α, β, the convention, and the same data. Other optimizers, such as Adam, use averages of gradients and their squares to adjust the scale of changes as well. Their formula and state require a separate study; their name does not let us infer a weight change for our example.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The gradient tells me in which direction to add to the weight.”

    A positive slope indicates that the loss increases locally with the weight. Descent subtracts the gradient multiplied by a positive step size. Distinguish the gradient g, the amount αg to subtract, and the signed change −αg.

    Review the sign and units of the weight change

  • “I start again from the same weight value, so momentum will give the same result.”

    The update also reads v. Resetting it to 0 at every iteration removes the memory and changes the rule. The exercise with opposing memory even shows how the direction of change can differ at an identical weight.

    Compare the states in the transfer exercise

  • “A larger step or momentum must give a better result.”

    The slope describes a neighborhood rather than the whole curve. A large step can go beyond the favorable region; the memory can oppose the current gradient. Recalculate the loss after the change and limit the conclusion to the data checked.

    Test the two cases where the loss increases

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Reconstruct the branch without memory

Start again from the shared weight w = 5 kg. With α = 0.25, calculate the gradient, the next weight, and its loss without momentum. Compare with 3.75 kg and the loss 1.53125 kg² obtained with momentum. What does this comparison allow you to conclude?

Hint 1

Recalculate g at the weight 5 kg; do not use the starting gradient.

Hint 2

Subtract αg, then calculate the square of the new error before dividing by 2.

Compare with the solution

g = 5 − 2 = 3 kg. The amount to subtract is 0.25 × 3 = 0.75 kg. The weight becomes 5 − 0.75 = 4.25 kg.

The error is 4.25 − 2 = 2.25 kg. Its square is 5.0625 kg² and the loss is 81/32 kg², or 2.53125 kg². Another route: 0.5625 × 4.5 kg² recovers the same loss.

After 2 updates from the same starting point, momentum gives a smaller loss here. This compares these fictitious trajectories with these settings. It does not identify a better optimizer for all networks or measure quality on other parcels.

Check your reasoning

  • The gradient is recalculated at the current weight and each quantity retains its unit.
  • The result 4.25 kg is checked using an exact loss and its ratio to the previous loss.
  • The conclusion remains limited to these updates on the same example.

Exercise 2 · Diagnosis through calculation

Is the right sign enough?

Start again from the initial weight 6 kg, without memory, but replace α with 3. Calculate the resulting weight and loss. Explain why the minus sign is correct even though the result gets worse. This model imposes no bound on the weight.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The initial gradient remains 4 kg; only the step size has changed.

Hint 2

The check concerns the distance from the target 2 kg, even if the weight becomes negative.

Compare with the solution

The amount subtracted is 3 × 4 = 12 kg. The resulting weight is 6 − 12 = -6 kg. Its error is -6 − 2 = -8 kg; the square is 64 kg², then the loss is 32 kg².

The loss is greater than the initial 8 kg²: we have crossed the minimum and ended farther from the target. The negative weight is an algebraic result of our unbounded model, rather than a physical mass claimed to be possible.

The condition 0 < α < 2 derived for this loss without memory is not met. To fix this calculation: reduce the step size, for example by returning to 0.25, and recalculate the loss. On another network, the threshold must be studied again.

Check your reasoning

  • Retain the negative weight in the calculation without adding an unstated bound.
  • Compare 32 kg² with 8 kg² and explain the overshoot past the minimum.
  • Distinguish the local direction of descent from the permissible step size.

Exercise 3 · Transfer to another state with the same settings

Same weight, opposing memory

Still using the same parcel, consider an alternative state w = 5 kg and v = -8 kg, inherited from fictitious earlier trials. This is not a continuation of our calculated trajectory. With α = 0.25 and β = 0.5, calculate v′, w′, and the loss. Why does the positive slope no longer require the weight to decrease?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The gradient is still 3 kg. Add it to βv, retaining the negative sign of the memory.

Hint 2

Subtracting a negative quantity increases the weight; then calculate the loss to check the effect.

Compare with the solution

βv = 0.5 × (-8) = -4 kg. Thus v′ = -4 + 3 = -1 kg. The amount αv′ to subtract is -0.25 kg.

w′ = 5 − (-0.25) = 5.25 kg. The error becomes 3.25 kg, its square is 10.5625 kg², and the loss is 169/32 kg², or 5.28125 kg². It exceeds 4.5 kg²: the weight has moved farther from the target.

The change follows the new accumulator, which is negative here despite the positive gradient. The weights alone therefore do not describe the state of an optimizer with momentum. To diagnose a resumed calculation, ask for the memory and the convention as well. Nothing in this counterexample implies that every use of momentum is unfavorable.

Check your reasoning

  • Write the memory contribution, the new accumulator, and the amount subtracted separately.
  • Justify the sign of the increase in weight and check the increase in loss.
  • Explain why this alternative state cannot be confused with the state on the main trajectory.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the gradient provide, and what does the optimizer decide?

The gradient measures the local sensitivity of the loss to the weights. The optimizer turns this gradient and any memory into a change in the weights. Without memory: w′ = w − αg, with positive α; the gradient is calculated before the weight changes.

Recall 2Which units must we distinguish in our weighing scale example?

The weight and error are in kg, and the loss is in kg². Its gradient with respect to the weight is in kg²/kg, hence in kg. α and β are dimensionless in this model; αg or αv′ is an amount in kg to subtract from the weight.

Recall 3Reconstruct the rule with momentum and the state to retain.

This page’s convention: v′ = βv + g then w′ = w − αv′. Retain the new weight and the new accumulator, together with α, β, and the convention. Resetting v at every iteration changes the rule; an identical weight can evolve differently if v differs.

Recall 4Why does the minus sign not guarantee a decrease in loss?

The slope describes a neighborhood. A large step can overshoot the minimum. For our loss without memory, e′ = (1 − α)e; a nonzero error decreases in absolute value for 0 < α < 2. This bound does not automatically carry over to another network or to momentum.

Recall 5What does a smaller loss after our updates establish?

A better fit to the fictitious example checked, with this loss and these settings. It establishes neither better quality on other parcels nor general superiority of the optimizer. Reading or producing an answer after a hint does not constitute mastery of a level.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without looking at the steps: connect output, loss, and gradient; write descent without memory, then the rule with momentum using the chosen convention; recover their first shared state and their divergence; name the units and give a case where the loss increases. Finally, explain why a decrease on this parcel is insufficient to draw conclusions about other parcels.
