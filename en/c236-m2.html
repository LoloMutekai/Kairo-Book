Level 4 · C236·M2

Clark-Ocone

Your goalWrite a random score as its mean plus a sum of “stake × movement” gains, where each stake uses only the past; calculate this stake as a conditional expectation of a Malliavin derivative on an exact coin-toss walk; then read the continuous-time statement with its assumptions.

Before you start
c235-m5 : divergence · c172-m5 : Euler-Maruyama · c231-m5 : geodesics · c236-m1 : integration by parts

✿01 See the idea

A counter starts at position 0 on a ruler marked in u, a fictional unit of length. In each round, a fair coin decides whether the counter moves forward or backward by s = 2 u. After 2 rounds, the score is the square of the final position, F = W², expressed in u². All these values are fictional: they make every number checkable by hand and do not describe a real game.

Before play begins, F is unknown. Can we describe it without waiting until the end, as a mean plus, round by round, a gain proportional to that round’s movement? The proportionality factor, called the stake here, can depend only on coins already tossed: we cannot stake based on the next round’s outcome. The Clark-Ocone formula tells us exactly what this stake is.

To find it, we first measure the sensitivity of F to round k: we replay the game, keeping all the other coin outcomes fixed, and compare F when the coin in round k gives +1 and when it gives −1. This sensitivity is a discrete Malliavin derivative, defined here by an explicit formula. It still depends on future coins, which are unknown when the stake is chosen. We average them out by taking the mean given the past, that is, the conditional expectation: this mean is the stake.

The first diagram shows the mechanism on the tree of four paths: the conditional mean of F does not change after the first round, then jumps after the second. The second diagram reconstructs F path by path from this mean. The walk uses the idea from c172-m5: a sum of independent, centered shocks, as in the Euler-Maruyama scheme for noise with no drift. Here the shocks come from coins rather than normal distributions, making every calculation exact. This is therefore the coin-toss analogue of the continuous derivative in c235-m4; the connection to continuous time is set out in the last step of the example.

The rule to remember

Write the score as its mean plus the round-by-round sum of (stake known before the round) × (movement in the round), where the stake is the mean, given the past, of the score’s sensitivity to that round.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Recover F = W² on a two-round walk

A walk of 2 rounds with step size s = 2 u (fictional values) gives the score F = W². Recover F as a mean plus “stake × movement” gains, check it on every path and through the variance, then read the corresponding continuous-time statement.

  1. Define the walk, symbols, and assumptions

    Two fair, independent coins, ε followed by η, each take the value +1 or −1, each with probability one half. With each coin, the counter moves by s = 2 u: the first movement is sε and the second is sη. Write V = sε for the position after the first round and W = s(ε + η) for the final position. The score F = W² is expressed in u².

    For any round k, the coin is εk and the movement is ΔWk = sεk, in u. There are 4 equally likely paths: (+,+), (+,−), (−,+), and (−,−), written in the order (ε, η).

    Assumptions: the coins are independent and centered (mean 0), with the same step size s. These assumptions make all the averaging below exact. With a biased coin, the formulas in this unit would no longer apply as written.

  2. Write the derivative, the stake, and the formula

    The discrete derivative with respect to the coin in round k compares F when this coin is +1 and then −1, keeping the other coins fixed. Divide by the difference 2s between the two possible movements, in u: the result is in u²/u = u.

    DkF=F(εk=+1)−F(εk=−1)2s

    ℱ denotes the available information: with subscript k − 1, it contains the coin outcomes from rounds before round k. The stake in round k, written mk and expressed in u, is the mean of DkF over the coins still unknown, given the coins already tossed.

    mk=E[DkF∣ℱk−1]

    For a walk of n rounds, F then equals its mean plus the sum of the stake × movement gains. Each term is in u × u = u², like F.

    F=E[F]+∑k=1nmkΔWk

    Why this equality holds: write Mk for the mean of F given the first k coins. When coin k becomes known, Mk differs from the previous mean by εk times half the difference between the means obtained with the coin at +1 and at −1. Since the later coins are independent of coin k, this half-difference equals s times the stake mk. Each change is therefore mkΔWk, and adding them from the first to the last coin takes us from the mean E[F] to F.

  3. Calculate the mean E[F]

    The four paths give the final positions W = 4, 0, 0, and −4 u, hence the scores 16, 0, 0, and 16 u². Each path has weight one quarter.

    E[F]=16+0+0+164=324=8 u²

    Check by another method: W is centered, so E[F] is its variance, namely n × s² = 2 × 4 = 8 u². Both methods give the same value.

  4. Differentiate F with respect to each coin

    Second coin, with first coin +1: F(+,+) − F(+,−) = 16 − 0 = 16, then 16 / 4 = 4 u. With first coin −1, we get (0 − 16) / 4 = −4 u.

    First coin, with second coin +1: F(+,+) − F(−,+) = 16 − 0 = 16, giving 4 u. With second coin −1, F(+,−) − F(−,−) = 0 − 16, giving −4 u. This derivative depends on the second coin, which has not yet been tossed in the first round.

  5. Obtain the stakes by conditioning

    First round: no coin outcome is known. The derivative is 4 or −4 depending on the second coin, each with probability one half. The mean is (4 − 4) / 2 = 0 u. The first-round stake is 0: the score F = W² does not distinguish backward from forward movement, so the direction of the first movement does not change its mean.

    Second round: the derivative depends only on the first coin, already tossed. No unknown coin remains to average over, and the stake equals the derivative: 4 u if V = +2 u, −4 u if V = −2 u. We recognize 2V, since 2 × 2 = 4.

  6. Reconstruct F on every path

    Path (+,+): E[F] + (first-round stake) × s + (second-round stake) × s = 8 + 0 × 2 + 4 × 2 = 8 + 8 = 16 u². This is indeed F = 16 u².

    Path (+,−): 8 + 0 × 2 − 4 × 2 = 8 − 8 = 0 u², equal to F. Path (−,+): the second-round stake is −4 and the movement is +2, giving 8 − 8 = 0. Path (−,−): −4 times −2 gives 8 + 8 = 16. All four paths are recovered exactly.

  7. Check through the variance and compare with the continuous case

    Each path differs from the mean by 8 u² in absolute value: F − E[F] is 8 or −8. Thus Var F = (8)² = 64 u⁴.

    Using the gains: the terms mkΔWk are uncorrelated, because the stake is known before the coin is tossed, and the coin is centered and independent of it. Var F is therefore the sum of s² times the mean of (stake)². First round: 0. Second round: 4 × 16 = 64 u⁴, since the stake is ±4 and its square is 16 u². The two methods agree.

    Note: in continuous time, the derivative of W² is 2W. Here the derivative with respect to the first coin is ±4 u, rather than 2W, because it excludes the effect of the coin itself. Only the stakes, which are conditional means, coincide: 0 in the first round, 2V in the second.

  8. Read the continuous-time statement

    Let W be a standard Brownian motion on [0, T], ℱ the information it generates up to time t, and F a square-integrable random variable in the domain of the Malliavin derivative (c235-m4), with a square-integrable derivative. Then:

    F=E[F]+∫0TE[DtF∣ℱt]dWt

    The integral is the Ito integral; the integrand E[DtF | ℱt] is adapted and uses only the past. Recall, to be checked in c235-m5: for an adapted integrand, the divergence and the Ito integral coincide, so F − E[F] is the divergence of the stake. Integration by parts from c236-m1 is used to identify this integrand; that proof is not repeated here.

    For F = W_T², we have DtF = 2W_T for all t ≤ T, and E[2W_T | ℱt] = 2Wt, because W_T is Wt plus a centered increment independent of the past. Hence:

    WT2=T+∫0T2WtdWt

    Our walk is an analogue: the accumulated variance n × s² = 8 u² plays the role of T and equals E[F] = 8 u²; the stake 2V corresponds to 2Wt. This exact calculation verifies the mechanism in a coin-toss model; it does not prove the continuous-time statement and says nothing about taking a limit as the step size tends to 0.

Explain it in your own words

Why is the first-round stake 0 even though the derivative with respect to the first coin is never 0? Explain what conditioning removes, and why the second-round stake has nothing left to remove.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The stake in round k is the derivative of F with respect to that round.”

    The derivative also depends on future coins, which are unknown when the stake is chosen. In the example, the derivative with respect to the first coin is ±4 u depending on the second coin, whereas the first-round stake is 0: the conditional mean removes what is not yet known.

    Review the step on obtaining stakes by conditioning

  • “The sum of the gains is enough; the term E[F] is optional.”

    Each stake × movement gain has mean 0, because the coin is centered and independent of the stake. In the example, E[F] = 8 u² and the final gain is ±8 u²: without the constant term, we would find 8 instead of 16 on path (+,+).

    Revisit the reconstruction path by path

  • “The Clark-Ocone formula applies to any random variable, without conditions.”

    The continuous-time statement requires a Brownian motion, the information it generates, and a square-integrable variable in the domain of the Malliavin derivative. The coin-toss walk has no domain difficulty; in continuous time, a discontinuous score, such as the indicator of a threshold being crossed, is not covered without a further argument.

Exercise 1 · Explained application

A larger step

Repeat the walk in the example with step size s = 3 u, still with 2 rounds and F = W². Calculate E[F], the second-round stake when the first coin is +1, and the gain when the second coin is +1, then check F on this path. Fictional values.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The first term of the formula is E[F] = n × s²: calculate s² first.

Hint 2

The second-round stake is 2V, with V = s when the first coin is +1.

Hint 3

For the check, calculate W = n × s on this path, then square it.

Compare with the solution

s² = 3 × 3 = 9 u², so E[F] = 2 × 9 = 18 u².

Second-round stake, first coin +1: 2V = 2 × 3 = 6 u. Gain with second coin +1: 6 × 3 = 18 u². The first round contributes nothing: its stake is 0.

Reconstruction: 18 + 0 + 18 = 36 u². Direct check: W = 2 × 3 = 6 u, so F = (6)² = 36 u². Both methods coincide.

Check your reasoning

  • E[F] calculated using s squared, rather than s.
  • Second-round stake equal to 2V, with V the position after the first round, rather than the final position.
  • Stake in u, gain and score in u².
  • Check against the score calculated directly.

Exercise 2 · Transfer

Another score: the product of the movements

Keep s = 2 u and 2 rounds, but use the score G = (sε) × (sη), the product of the two movements. Calculate G on the four paths, E[G], the two stakes, and the reconstruction on path (+,+). Then compare G with (F − E[F]) / 2, where F is the score in the example.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

G is s² when the two coins match and −s² otherwise.

Hint 2

For the second-round stake, fix the first coin at +1 and compare G when the second is +1 and then −1.

Hint 3

For the first round, average the derivative over the second coin, as in the example.

Compare with the solution

On the four paths, G is 4, −4, −4, and 4 u², so E[G] = 0.

Second coin, first coin +1: (4 − (−4)) / 4 = 8 / 4 = 2 u, which is V. With first coin −1, we find −2. First round: the derivative is ±2 depending on the second coin, with mean 0, so the stake is 0.

Reconstruction on (+,+): 0 + 0 × 2 + 2 × 2 = 4 u², equal to G on this path.

Comparison: (16 − 8) / 2 = 4 u², the same value as G on (+,+). On each of the four paths, G = (F − E[F]) / 2, so the stake for G is half the stake for F: 2 versus 4. This illustrates that the formula is compatible with multiplication by a constant and addition of a constant.

Check your reasoning

  • G evaluated correctly on all four paths, including the signs.
  • First-round stake found to be 0 by averaging the derivative over the second coin.
  • Reconstruction checked on one path using the mean, stakes, and movements.
  • Connection with F explained through the relation G = (F − E[F]) / 2 on all paths.

Exercise 3 · Open generalization

Extend the walk by one round

Now take 3 rounds, s = 2 u, and F = W², where W is the final position. Show that the stake in round k equals 2 times the position before that round. Calculate E[F], then Var F in two ways: using the sum of s² times the mean square of each stake, then directly from the distribution of W.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The derivative with respect to the coin in round k equals 2s times the sum of the other coins. Average over the future coins, which are centered.

Hint 2

E[W²] is the variance of a sum of independent coins: each step contributes s². For the position before the round, count only steps already taken.

Hint 3

For the direct method, |W| is 3s with probability 0.25 (all steps in the same direction) and s with probability 0.75.

Compare with the solution

Stake: DkF = 2s × (sum of the other coins). The mean given the past keeps coins already tossed and averages future coins to 0, since they are centered: what remains is 2 times the position before the round. The stakes are therefore 0 in the first round, 2V in the second, and 2 times the position after two rounds in the third.

The mean is E[F] = 3 × s² = 3 × 4 = 12 u².

First method: E[stake²] = 4 × E[(position before the round)²]. The position after one round has mean square s² = 4 u², and after two rounds 2 × s² = 8 u². Second round: 4 × 4 = 16. Third round: 4 × 8 = 32. First round: 0. Sum: 48, then Var F = s² × 48 = 4 × 48 = 192 u⁴.

Direct method: |W| = 6 u gives F = 36 u², differing from E[F] by 24 u², with probability 0.25; |W| = s gives F = 4 u², differing from E[F] by 8 u² in absolute value, with probability 0.75. Var F = 0.25 × (24)² + 0.75 × (8)² = 0.25 × 576 + 0.75 × 64 = 144 + 48 = 192 u⁴.

Both methods give 192 u⁴: the formula preserves the variance.

Check your reasoning

  • Stake in round k identified as 2 times the position before that round, with the role of future coins explained.
  • E[F] equal to n × s².
  • Variance obtained from the sum of s² times the mean square of each stake.
  • Variance recovered directly, using the probabilities of the two values of |W|, and the two results compared.
  • Confidence rating given before reading the solution.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the stake in round k, and why is it conditional?

It is the mean of the derivative of F with respect to the coin in round k, given the coins already tossed. It is conditional because it must be known before the round: future coins are averaged over.

Recall 2How do we calculate the discrete derivative with respect to the coin in round k?

Compare F with this coin at +1 and then at −1, keeping the other coins fixed, and divide the difference by 2s, the difference between the two possible movements.

Recall 3Why does the formula keep the term E[F]?

Each stake × movement gain has mean 0, so their sum cannot supply the mean of F: the constant part E[F] is necessary.

Recall 4What assumptions accompany the continuous-time statement?

W is a standard Brownian motion, the information is what it generates, and F is square-integrable and in the domain of the Malliavin derivative, with a square-integrable derivative. The integral is the Ito integral.

Recall 5What does the exact calculation on the coin-toss walk prove?

It verifies the mean, stake, and movement mechanism on all paths of a discrete model. It neither proves nor replaces the continuous-time statement.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Define the coins, information, and score; calculate the mean E[F]; differentiate F with respect to each coin; average this derivative given the past to obtain the stake; add the sum of stake × movement gains to E[F] to recover F; check through the variance; move to continuous time only with a Brownian motion, its information, and a variable in the domain of the derivative.
