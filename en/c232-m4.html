Level 4 · C232·M4

Varadhan

Your goalApply Varadhan’s lemma to a Gaussian model with small noise: find the dominant exponent of an exponential average by maximizing the balance F − I, check it with an exact calculation, and name the assumptions that justify the conclusion.

Before you start
c231-m5 : geodesics · c228-m5 : incompleteness · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c174-m5 : conditions for validity · c232-m1 : Laplace’s method · c232-m2 : steepest descent · c232-m3 : large deviations

✿01 See the idea

Take a fictional quantity X, centered at 0, whose fluctuations are controlled by a dimensionless parameter ε that is strictly positive and will become small. X almost always stays near 0. Large values can occur, but they become very rare: the probability of finding X near a point x behaves like exp(−I(x)/ε). The function I, which is nonnegative and 0 at the typical point, is the rate function introduced in c232-m3; here I(x) = x²/2.

Next, introduce an exponential weight: exp(F(X)/ε). The gain F is fictional; it rewards certain values of X. We want the average of this weight. Since 1/ε is large, two effects compete: a value far from 0 has a high rarity cost, but it can contribute a large weight.

Varadhan’s lemma resolves this competition at the scale of exponents. In the limit, the average grows like exp(S/ε), where S is the best balance of “gain minus rarity cost” over all possible values of x.

limε→0+εlnE[exp(F(X)ε)]=supx(F(x)−I(x))

This is not an identity for fixed ε: the lemma describes an exponent, rather than the value of the average. It assumes a good rate function (the sets where I stays below a threshold are closed and bounded), a continuous gain F, and controlled tails, for example when F is bounded above. Laplace’s method (c232-m1) gives the idea: the integral of an exponential with a large parameter concentrates around the maximum of the exponent, which steepest descent (c232-m2) helps locate.

This lesson uses a Gaussian example where everything can be calculated by hand. It uses neither geodesics nor the incomplete markets in the other chapter prerequisites, and it does not prove the lemma in full generality. The values in the scenario are fictional: they describe no real market or system.

The rule to remember

Varadhan: ε ln E[exp(F/ε)] tends to the maximum of F − I. Maximize the balance of “gain minus rarity cost”, never F alone or I alone, and only when the assumptions hold.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A fictional weight on a quantity with small noise

With X a centered Gaussian variable of variance ε, the rate function I(x) = x²/2, the gain F(x) = a x − b x², a = 3 and b = 0.25 (fictional, dimensionless values), find the dominant exponent of E[exp(F(X)/ε)] as ε tends to 0, then check it at ε = 0.10.

  1. Define the symbols and assumptions

    X is the random quantity; its distribution depends on ε and is Gaussian, centered, with variance ε. The parameter ε is dimensionless and strictly positive. The Gaussian rate function is I(x) = x²/2: it measures the rarity cost, in the sense that P(X near x) ≈ exp(−I(x)/ε). F is the gain; E denotes expectation, ln the natural logarithm, and sup the least upper bound, which is a maximum when it is attained.

    Assumptions used: the rate function is good, F is continuous, and the tails of F remain controlled. In this example, the last condition reduces to a strictly positive coefficient for the quadratic penalty in F − I, which we check in the next step. Nothing is measured: this is a calculation scenario.

  2. Write the balance F − I

    Subtract the rarity cost from the gain. Grouping the terms in x² gives the coefficient c = b + 0.5 = 0.75.

    F(x)−I(x)=ax−bx2−x22=ax−cx2

    Since c = 3/4 is strictly positive, the balance is a downward-opening parabola. The coefficient c of the quadratic penalty is positive: the tail condition for this example is satisfied.

  3. Find the maximum

    Set the derivative to 0, as in steepest descent in c232-m2: a − 2 c x = 0. The second derivative is −2 c, which is negative, so this critical point is the global maximum.

    x*=a2c=33/2=2

    To keep the units clear: x, ε, a, b, c, F and I are all dimensionless in this scenario.

  4. Evaluate the balance at the maximum

    First calculate the gain, then the cost at the point x* = 2. The linear term of F is 3 × 2 = 6. The square of x* is 4, so the quadratic term is 0.25 × 4 = 1. Thus F(x*) = 6 − 1 = 5. The cost is I(x*) = 4/2 = 2. The balance is 5 − 2 = 3.

    Check by another route: completing the square gives the maximum of a x − c x² as a²/(4 c), or 9/3 = 3. Both routes give the same result.

  5. Interpret Varadhan’s statement

    With the assumptions satisfied, Varadhan’s lemma gives the dominant exponent:

    limε→0+εlnE[exp(F(X)ε)]=supx(F(x)−I(x))

    In other words, the average grows like exp(3/ε). The point x* = 2 dominates even though it is rare: its rarity cost is 2/ε, but its weight exp(5/ε) is stronger.

  6. Check with the exact Gaussian integral

    Here the expectation can be calculated: completing the square in the exponent a x − c x² lets us compare the remaining Gaussian integral with the normalization constant of the distribution of X, and both π and ε cancel. The ratio is 1/√(2 c).

    εlnE[exp(F(X)ε)]=a24c−ε2ln(2c)

    The first term is exactly 3. The second tends to 0 with ε. To bound it without a calculator, use ln y ≤ y − 1: with y = 3/2, we obtain 0 < ln(3/2) ≤ 1/2. Hence, for every ε:

    3−ε2×1/2≤εlnE[…]<3

    At ε = 1/10, the maximum error is 1/10 × 1/4 = 1/40. We therefore obtain the bounds 2.975 ≤ ε ln E < 3.000. The value at fixed ε is not the limit, but it stays within 0.025 of it.

  7. Rule out mistaken interpretations

    An ordinary average of F does not give the answer: E[F(X)] = −b ε = −0.025 at ε = 0.10, close to 0, whereas the dominant exponent is 3. Rare values account for the exponential average.

    Maximizing F alone does not work either. Its slope is 0 at x = a/(2 b) = 6, but already at x = 4 the gain 8 is exactly offset by the cost 8: the balance drops back to 0 and then becomes negative. The maximum to retain is that of the balance, 3.

Explain it in your own words

Why can a rare point such as x* dominate an average? Explain what F and I represent in the exponent, then state which assumption would guarantee that the result remains valid if F were changed.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The limit is the value of F at the most probable point.”

    The most probable point is x = 0, where the balance is 0, whereas the dominant exponent is 3. Rare values account for the exponential average: revisit the interpretation and comparison steps in the example.

    Revisit the full example

  • “It is enough to maximize the gain F.”

    The gain alone ignores the rarity cost. In the scenario, F keeps rising beyond x = 4, but F − I is no longer positive there. Maximize the balance F − I; look at the first diagram.

    Review the diagrams

  • “Varadhan’s lemma works for every continuous function F.”

    Controlled tails are also needed. If F receives a quadratic term that cancels the one in I, the balance becomes linear and the average diverges. The exercise on assumptions demonstrates this numerically.

    Go to the exercises

Exercise 1 · Application with explanation

A smaller gain

Use the same rate function I(x) = x²/2 and the same coefficient b = 0.25, but take a = 1. Find the point x* where F − I is maximized, then the dominant exponent. The values are fictional and dimensionless.

Hint 1

The coefficient c = 0.75 does not depend on a: only the slope a changes.

Hint 2

Use x* = a/(2 c), then evaluate a x* − c x*² to check.

Compare with the solution

With c = 3/4, we have 2 c = 3/2, so x* = 1/3/2 = 2/3. The square of x* is 4/9 and c x*² = 1/3. The balance is therefore 2/3 − 1/3 = 1/3.

Check with the formula a²/(4 c): 1/3 = 1/3. Both routes agree, and the upper-bound assumption still holds because c is unchanged.

Check your reasoning

  • Write the balance as a x − c x² with the correct c.
  • Find x* by setting the derivative to 0 and justify that it is a maximum.
  • Check the result by a second route.

Exercise 2 · Checking an error bound

Choose ε for a given accuracy

In the example, the difference between ε ln E and the limiting exponent is bounded by ε × 1/4. What is the largest value of ε that guarantees, using this bound, an error of at most 0.02? This bound is sufficient, but not necessarily optimal.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write the inequality ε × 1/4 ≤ 0.02 and isolate ε.

Hint 2

Check by substituting the value you found for ε.

Compare with the solution

The inequality is ε × 1/4 ≤ 1/50. Isolate ε by dividing by 1/4: ε ≤ 1/50/1/4 = 2/25. Thus ε = 2/25 is the guaranteed limiting value.

Check: 2/25 × 1/4 = 1/50, equal to the tolerance. Since the bound comes from ln y ≤ y − 1, the actual error is smaller: the conclusion remains conservative.

Check your reasoning

  • Start from the established bound rather than a guessed value.
  • Solve the inequality while keeping exact fractions.
  • State that the guarantee is sufficient, rather than optimal.

Exercise 3 · Limit of validity

When the lemma no longer applies

Now take F(x) = a x + g x² with a = 3, still with I(x) = x²/2. Compare g = 0.25 and g = 0.5. For each value, calculate the quadratic coefficient in F − I, then state whether the dominant exponent is finite. The values are fictional.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Write F − I = a x − c x² with c = 0.5 − g.

Hint 2

If c = 0, examine what happens to the balance and to the integral of exp(F/ε).

Compare with the solution

For g = 1/4, the coefficient is c = 1/2 − 1/4 = 1/4, which is strictly positive. The maximum is a²/(4 c) = 9/1 = 9: the lemma applies and the dominant exponent is finite.

For g = 1/2, the coefficient is c = 0. The balance becomes a x, which is linear and has no maximum: the supremum is infinite. The average also diverges for every ε, since exp(a x/ε) is integrated over the whole real line. The formula a²/(4 c) would require division by 0; it no longer makes sense. First check that F − I is bounded above.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the coefficient c before using any formula for the maximum.
  • Distinguish a finite supremum, an infinite supremum, and a divergent average.
  • Do not apply a²/(4 c) without checking that c > 0.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the rate function I(x) measure in Varadhan’s lemma?

The rarity cost: the probability that X is near x behaves like exp(−I(x)/ε) when ε is small. I is nonnegative and 0 at the typical point.

Recall 2What does Varadhan’s lemma give for ε ln E[exp(F(X)/ε)]?

Under its assumptions, the limit as ε tends to 0 through positive values is the supremum of F − I.

Recall 3Why maximize F − I rather than F alone?

A value of x that increases the gain F is also rare: its weight is multiplied by exp(−I/ε). The dominant exponent concerns the product, hence the sum F − I.

Recall 4What should you check before writing sup(F − I)?

A good rate function, a continuous gain F, and controlled tails (in the Gaussian example: a strictly positive coefficient for the quadratic penalty in F − I). Otherwise the average may diverge, as in the exercise where the quadratic coefficient drops to 0.

Recall 5Does the lemma give the value of the average at fixed ε?

No, only its exponent. In the Gaussian example, ε ln E differs from the limit by the exact term (ε/2) ln(2 c), which tends to 0.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the method: define X, its rate function I, and the weight exp(F/ε); write the balance F − I; maximize it by setting the derivative to 0 and using concavity; interpret the limit of ε ln E as this maximum; check it with the Gaussian integral and bounds; check the rate function, continuity of F, and tails before invoking the lemma.
