Green and Stokes

Your goalState the hypotheses of Green’s theorem and then Stokes’ theorem, and verify on a rectangular pool that the circulation around the boundary equals the integral of the curl over the interior.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

In our fictional scene, a rectangular pool measuring 3 m by 2 m is viewed from above. At its surface, the water has a velocity F at each point (x, y): this is a field in the plane. We want to measure how much the water circulates around the boundary of the pool. The field is invented for the calculation: it is neither a set of readings nor a model of a real flow.

There are two ways to measure this. We can walk along the boundary counterclockwise and, at each step, add the component of velocity in the direction of travel multiplied by the length of the step: this is the circulation, in m²·s⁻¹. Alternatively, we can look inside: divide the pool into small squares and measure how much the field rotates in each one. Green’s theorem states that, under specific hypotheses, the two totals are equal.

The reason lies in the shared sides. Two neighboring squares share a side: one traverses it to the right, the other to the left, and the two contributions cancel. After adding all the squares, only the sides of the outer boundary remain. The circulation around the boundary is therefore the sum of the small circulations in the interior.

Stokes’ theorem extends the same idea to space: an oriented surface bounded by a closed curve. The circulation along the boundary equals the flux of the curl through the surface. When the surface is flat and lies in the xy-plane, we recover Green’s theorem exactly. This page develops Green’s theorem in detail; Stokes’ theorem is stated with its hypotheses and verified only in this flat case.

The rule to remember

Traversing the boundary with the region on the left adds up the local rotations in the interior: circulation around the boundary = double integral of ∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y, provided the field and boundary are sufficiently regular.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

The rectangular pool: boundary and interior

Fictional scene: a rectangular pool measuring 3 m by 2 m, with surface velocity given by an invented field. We calculate the circulation around the boundary, then the integral of the curl over the interior, and check that the two numbers agree.

  1. State Green’s theorem, symbol by symbol

    Let R be a bounded region of the plane whose boundary C is a simple closed, piecewise smooth curve, traversed so that R stays on the left. Let F = (P, Q) be a field defined on an open set containing R and C, whose components have continuous partial derivatives. Then the circulation of F along C equals the double integral of the scalar curl:

    ∮CPdx+Qdy=∬R(∂Q∂x−∂P∂y)dA

    P and Q are the components of F in the x and y directions, in m·s⁻¹. The differentials dx and dy are small displacements along C, in m. The partial derivative ∂Q/∂x is calculated with y held fixed: its unit is s⁻¹, as is that of ∂P/∂y. The area element dA is in m². Both sides are therefore in m²·s⁻¹. Hypotheses: a bounded region; a simple closed, piecewise smooth boundary; positive orientation (region on the left); a field defined with continuous partial derivatives throughout the region. If the region has a hole, the boundary of the hole is part of C and is traversed clockwise.

  2. Define the pool and the field

    The pool is the rectangle R of points (x, y) with x between 0 and 3 m and y between 0 and 2 m. The fictional field in the scene has the following components, with k = 2 m⁻¹·s⁻¹:

    P=kxy,Q=kx2

    The units are consistent: k·x·y is in m⁻¹·s⁻¹ × m² = m·s⁻¹. These are polynomials: their partial derivatives are continuous everywhere. The boundary of the rectangle is simple, closed and piecewise smooth. The hypotheses of Green’s theorem are therefore satisfied.

  3. Calculate the circulation along each side

    We traverse the boundary counterclockwise: bottom, right, top, left. On each side, one of the two variables is constant, so dx or dy is 0, leaving just a single integral. Bottom: y = 0, so P = 0 and the contribution is 0. Right: x = a, dx = 0, leaving the component Q:

    ∫0bQ(a,y)dy=ka2b=2×9×2=36

    Top: y = b, dy = 0, and x goes from a to 0, which reverses the sign compared with the integral from 0 to a:

    ∫a0kbxdx=−kba22=−18

    Left: x = 0, so Q = 0 and the contribution is 0. The sum over the four sides is:

    0+36−18+0=18

    The circulation is 18 m²·s⁻¹.

  4. Calculate the scalar curl

    To apply Green’s theorem, we now need the quantity in the interior. We differentiate each component with respect to the required variable, holding the other fixed, then subtract in the order ∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y:

    ∂Q∂x=2kx=4x,∂P∂y=kx=2x
    rot(P,Q)=∂Q∂x−∂P∂y=(4−2)x=2x

    The scalar curl is 2·x in s⁻¹ when x is in meters. It is positive: in this model, the field rotates counterclockwise, more strongly as x increases.

  5. Integrate it over the pool and compare

    The double integral of the curl is calculated as two nested single integrals. Integrating with respect to y on the inside simply gives the factor b, because the curl does not depend on y. This leaves the integral of x with respect to x, which is the square of a divided by 2, or 4.5 m²:

    ∬R2xdA=∫0a2xbdx=2×2×4.5=18

    The double integral is 18 m²·s⁻¹, exactly the circulation from the previous step. The boundary and the interior give the same number: this is what Green’s theorem states.

  6. Check direction, subdivision and units

    First check: direction. Traversing the boundary clockwise reverses the sign: the circulation becomes −18 m²·s⁻¹. Using Green’s theorem then requires a minus sign in front of 18.

    Second check: the subdivision in the first figure. For a square with sides one meter long and x-coordinates from u to v, the integral of the curl is 2, multiplied by the height 1 m, multiplied by the difference of the squares of v and u, divided by 2: it is therefore simply this difference. The three columns give 1, 3 and 5 per square. With 2 rows, the sum is (1 + 3 + 5) × 2 = 9 × 2 = 18 m²·s⁻¹, again the circulation around the boundary.

    Third check: units, m²·s⁻¹ on both sides. This check does not prove that the field describes a real pool: it only verifies the mathematical equality for this polynomial field.

  7. Apply Stokes’ theorem in the plane

    Let S be a piecewise smooth surface in space, oriented by a unit normal n, with a simple closed, piecewise smooth boundary ∂S. The boundary is oriented by the right-hand rule: the thumb points along n, and the fingers give the direction of travel. Let F = (P, Q, R) be a field in space whose components have continuous partial derivatives in a neighborhood of S. Then:

    ∮∂SF·dr=∬S(rotF)·ndS
    rotF=(∂R∂y−∂Q∂z,∂P∂z−∂R∂x,∂Q∂x−∂P∂y)

    The components of F are in m·s⁻¹, the curl in s⁻¹, and dS in m²: the result is again in m²·s⁻¹. Place our pool in the plane z = 0, with n = (0, 0, 1) as written below. Only the third component of the curl contributes, and the component R does not appear in it:

    n=(0,0,1)⇒(rotF)·n=∂Q∂x−∂P∂y

    Stokes’ theorem becomes exactly Green’s theorem: 18 = 18 for our pool, viewed as a flat surface in space. We have not verified a curved surface here: that would require parameterizing the surface and integrating a flux, beyond the scope of this page.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The direction of travel is a minor detail: the integral has the same value in both directions.”

    Reversing direction reverses the sign. For our pool, the counterclockwise direction gives 18 m²·s⁻¹ and the clockwise direction gives −18 m²·s⁻¹. Green’s theorem requires the direction that keeps the region on the left; with the other direction, a minus sign is needed in front of the double integral.

    Review the direction check in the example

  • “Green’s theorem works for any field and any closed curve.”

    The field must be defined with continuous partial derivatives throughout the region, and the boundary must be simple, closed and piecewise smooth. If the region has a hole, its boundary also counts and is traversed clockwise: on the region surrounding the island in the exercise, 12 − 2 = 10 m²·s⁻¹, equal to the double integral over 5 m².

    Redo the island exercise

  • “The curl and the divergence are the same number; their formulas are interchangeable.”

    The scalar curl ∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y measures rotation and is linked to circulation. The divergence ∂P/∂x + ∂Q/∂y measures net outflow and is linked to outward flux. For our field, the outward flux is 12 m²·s⁻¹ and the circulation is 18 m²·s⁻¹: two questions, two numbers.

    Compare flux and circulation in the transfer exercise

Exercise 1 · Direct application

A rotating field

On the same pool, a second fictional field G has components P = −y and Q = x, each multiplied by 1 s⁻¹ to obtain m·s⁻¹. Calculate its counterclockwise circulation using Green’s theorem, then along each side, and compare.

G(x,y)=(−y,x)×1s⁻¹
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate ∂Q/∂x and ∂P/∂y: is the curl constant?

Hint 2

A constant can be taken outside the double integral: what remains is the area of the pool.

Hint 3

For each side, identify the components that are 0 on each segment, and the sign on the top side.

Compare with the solution

Using Green’s theorem: ∂Q/∂x = 1 and ∂P/∂y = −1, so the curl is 1 − (−1) = 2 s⁻¹, a constant. The area of the pool is 3 × 2 = 6 m². The circulation is 2 × 6 = 12 m²·s⁻¹.

Along each side: the bottom and left contribute 0. On the right, Q = x = a is constant over a length b, giving 3 × 2 = 6. At the top, P = −b and x goes from a to 0, giving (−2) × (−3) = 6. Total: 6 + 6 = 12 m²·s⁻¹, as with Green’s theorem.

Extension: for a segment from (x, y) to (u, v), this circulation is x·v − u·y. The total for a polygon is twice its signed area, positive in the counterclockwise direction. Here, the only two nonzero terms are each 6, their sum is 12, and half of it gives the area 6 m².

Check your reasoning

  • The curl is calculated with the correct sign and remains in s⁻¹.
  • The area appears only once, with its unit.
  • The check along each side gives the same number with the unit m²·s⁻¹.

Exercise 2 · Boundary case for a hypothesis

An island in the pool

A square island with sides 1 m long, strictly inside the pool, is removed from it. The field G remains defined everywhere. Calculate the double integral of the curl over the surrounding region, then recover this number using the two oriented boundaries: the outer boundary counterclockwise, the island boundary clockwise.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

When walking along each boundary, the region must stay on the left: which way should you go around the island?

Hint 2

The clockwise circulation around the island is the negative of the counterclockwise circulation, which Green’s theorem gives using the island’s area.

Compare with the solution

The area of the surrounding region is 6 − 1 = 5 m². The double integral is 2 × 5 = 10 m²·s⁻¹.

Using the boundaries: the counterclockwise outer boundary gives 12. Going counterclockwise around the island would give 2 × 1 = 2; going clockwise gives −2. Total: 12 − 2 = 10 m²·s⁻¹, equal to 10: the check is consistent.

The field G is defined inside the island here, so Green’s theorem can be applied there. The essential hypothesis on the surrounding region remains that G is defined there with continuous partial derivatives.

Check your reasoning

  • The island boundary is traversed clockwise.
  • The two calculations give the same number.
  • The hypothesis for the region with a hole is stated.

Exercise 3 · Transfer

Outward flux and divergence

For the field F in the example, calculate the outward flux through the boundary of the pool, one side at a time, then using the double integral of the divergence. Finally, compare it with the circulation 18 m²·s⁻¹ and explain the difference.

∮CPdy−Qdx=∬R∂P∂x+∂Q∂ydA
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

On the right side, the outward normal points toward increasing x: the flux is the integral of P with respect to y.

Hint 2

On the bottom and top sides, the normal points down and up respectively: only Q contributes, with a minus sign at the bottom.

Hint 3

Q does not depend on y: what happens to the contributions from the bottom and top?

Compare with the solution

Right: P = k·a·y, so its integral from 0 to b is 6 × 2 = 12. Left: P = 0, giving 0. Bottom: the normal points down, flux = −the integral of Q(x, 0), or −k times the cube of a divided by 3 = −2 × 27/3 = −18. Top: Q does not depend on y, giving +18. Total: 12 − 18 + 18 = 12 m²·s⁻¹.

Using the divergence: ∂P/∂x = k·y and ∂Q/∂y = 0, so the divergence is 2·y. The double integral is (2 × 2) × 3 = 4 × 3 = 12 m²·s⁻¹, equal to the outward flux. This form follows from Green’s theorem applied to the field (−Q, P).

The outward flux 12 differs from the circulation 18: one measures what flows out through the boundary, the other what circulates along it.

Check your reasoning

  • The sign on each side follows the outward normal.
  • The two flux calculations agree.
  • The difference between flux and circulation is explained.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does Green’s theorem state in the plane?

For a bounded region whose boundary is simple, closed and piecewise smooth, traversed with the region on the left, and a field with continuous partial derivatives, the circulation of the field around the boundary equals the double integral of ∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y over the region.

Recall 2Which direction of travel does Green’s theorem require, and what changes when it is reversed?

The positive direction keeps the region on the left: counterclockwise for an outer boundary, clockwise for the boundary of a hole. Reversing it reverses the sign of the circulation.

Recall 3Which hypotheses should you check before applying Green’s theorem?

A bounded region; a simple closed, piecewise smooth boundary; positive orientation; a field defined throughout the region with continuous partial derivatives; holes included in the boundary.

Recall 4What is the connection between Stokes’ theorem and Green’s theorem?

Stokes’ theorem relates the circulation around the boundary of an oriented surface to the flux of the curl through it. For a flat surface in the xy-plane, with the normal pointing toward increasing z, it recovers Green’s theorem.

Recall 5What is the difference between the scalar curl and the divergence?

The scalar curl ∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y measures rotation and is linked to circulation; the divergence ∂P/∂x + ∂Q/∂y measures net outflow and is linked to outward flux.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Choose the region and its oriented boundary, with the region on the left. Check the hypotheses: a field defined with continuous partial derivatives; a simple closed, piecewise smooth boundary, including holes. Calculate the circulation one side at a time, then the scalar curl and its double integral. Compare the results with their units, and check by reversing direction and by subdivision. State that Stokes’ theorem extends the idea to an oriented surface and recovers Green’s theorem on a flat surface.
