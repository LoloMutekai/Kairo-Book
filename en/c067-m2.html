Level 2 · C067·M2

Training set

Your goalIdentify the observations that fit a model and calculate how selecting or repeating them changes the fit.

Before you start
c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c066-m5 : standard errors and diagnostics · c017-m5 : reproduction protocol · c067-m1 : empirical risk

✿01 See the idea

In a document scanning workshop, you want to report how long a standard batch of documents will take. Batches A, B and C took 8, 10 and 12 min. To start, you choose a very simple rule: report the same duration for every standard batch. What duration should you set for this rule?

The training set is the list of examples whose values are used to fit the model parameters. Here, the parameter is the reported duration; the times for A, B and C are used to choose it. The scanned documents are the objects being worked on, and the training observations are the recorded durations of their batches.

Batch R is reserved for a later assessment. Its duration remains hidden and is not involved in any choice on this page. Being stored in the same folder as A is not enough to make R a training example: what matters is how its information is used.

All these data are fictional. Before the durations are read, the protocol specifies which batches are eligible: the same size, the same device and the same definitions of when the work starts and ends. A, B and C are distinct batches. We do not keep only the fastest ones. These precautions make the calculation interpretable; they do not guarantee that these few batches represent all future work.

The rule to remember

Identify the examples that influence the fit, keep their identities and check their weights: copying a row can change the model without adding a new observation.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Fit the reported duration using the selected batches

Set D contains A: 8 min, B: 10 min and C: 12 min. Let us choose the constant duration that minimizes their mean squared error, giving each record the same weight. The duration of the reserved batch is not needed.

  1. Describe the data and the model before calculating

    D denotes the list of selected records with their identities. n is its size, here 3 observations, with no physical unit. Index i runs through the observations from 1 to n; yᵢ is the measured duration of the corresponding batch, in minutes. The letters A, B and C identify the batches and are not numbers.

    b denotes a candidate duration, in minutes. The model reports b for every standard batch: it is a constant model, with no varying input feature in this example. Its parameter is b. We fix this family of models and the squared error rule before fitting b.

    We assume that n is strictly greater than 0, durations are finite, records are comparable and each observation has equal weight: every record counts as much as the others in the mean. The minimization formula below is purely algebraic. Its validity does not show that the set is representative of other batches.

  2. Explain what training means here

    R_D(b) is the empirical risk: the mean, over D, of the squared differences between the reported duration and the measured duration. Here, a squared difference is the squared loss, meaning the cost associated with that error. The symbol Σ means adding the terms for all the indicated indices. The exponent 2 means multiplying a difference by itself. R_D is therefore expressed in minutes squared.

    RD(b)=1n∑i=1n(b−yi)2

    Training means choosing b using these errors. We write b̂, with a hat, for the chosen parameter. Comparing candidate durations using the records in D already involves D in training, even if you calculate with a pencil.

  3. Calculate the proposed fit

    For a constant model and this squared loss with equal weights, the parameter that minimizes the risk is the mean of the selected durations. We verify this below. The bar over y denotes this mean; it does not denote a new measurement.

    b^=y¯=8+10+123=303=10 min

    Adding combines the durations of all the selected examples. Dividing by their number gives the mean duration per batch. The model now reports 10 min for a standard batch; it does not reproduce the individual durations.

  4. Measure the errors remaining on the set

    Define eᵢ = b̂ − yᵢ, the signed difference in minutes. For A: 10 − 8 = 2 min; for B: 10 − 10 = 0 min; for C: 10 − 12 = -2 min. The sign indicates whether the model reports too much or too little time.

    The squares are respectively 4, 0 and 4 minutes squared. They prevent differences in opposite directions from cancelling out in the error measure. Their sum is 8 minutes squared; the mean is:

    RD(b^)=4+0+43=83≈2.67 minutes au carré

    This error is calculated on the examples used to fit b. This number does not yet measure errors on future batches. It is neither a mean waiting time nor a success rate.

  5. Check that the mean minimizes the squared errors

    First check: the signed differences add up to 2 + 0 + (-2) = 0 min. The mean also lies between the shortest duration, 8 min, and the longest, 12 min. These checks detect calculation errors, but passing them is not enough to prove the minimum.

    For this proof, write each difference b − yᵢ as (b − b̂) + (b̂ − yᵢ). Squaring a sum gives the squares of the terms and their cross product, multiplied by 2. When we take the mean, this cross product disappears because the mean of the differences b̂ − yᵢ is 0. This leaves the identity:

    RD(b)=RD(b^)+(b−b^)2

    The added term is a square, so it is greater than or equal to 0. It is 0 only when b = b̂. The mean is therefore the unique minimum in this family of constant models. This conclusion depends on using squared errors and equal weights; it does not by itself define every possible form of training.

  6. Verify using a different candidate duration

    Try b = 12 min on the same D. The differences are 4, 2 and 0 min. The squares total 16 + 4 + 0 = 20 minutes squared. The risk is 20/3 ≈ 6.67 minutes squared, higher than the risk for the 10 min fit.

    Another way to check: the shift from the mean is 2 min, whose square is 4 minutes squared. The previous identity gives 8/3 + 4 = 20/3 minutes squared. This matches the direct calculation. Training has chosen a better fit on D; this is still not enough to establish better performance outside D.

  7. Keep a record that allows the fit to be reproduced

    A useful record contains the identifiers A, B and C, their durations in minutes, the definition of a standard batch, the inclusion criterion, the version of the set, the constant model and the squared error rule with equal weights. It also keeps the fitted duration of 10 min and its exact empirical risk of 8/3 minutes squared. Recording only the mean would lose the provenance of the fit.

    Batch R stays out of this fitting record. Another training set may give another reported duration. The exercises will change only the records used for training; the role of validation will be studied in the next unit.

Explain it in your own words

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “All the rows in the folder belong to training.”

    Look for the values actually used to choose the parameter. A, B and C are involved in fitting; R is reserved and its duration stays hidden. The location of a file does not determine the role of an observation.

    Review the batches used for fitting

  • “The risk is minimal, so the model can handle future batches.”

    The minimum was obtained on the records used to choose b, within a specific family of models. It proves an optimal fit on this set under the chosen rule. Quality on other batches requires separate evidence.

    Review the minimum and what it establishes

  • “Copying A adds an observation as useful as a new batch.”

    A copy counts the same duration again. With equal weight per row, A carries more weight without any new work having been observed. Check the identifiers before fitting, then repeat the calculation keeping one record per batch under our protocol.

    Diagnose and correct the duplicate

Exercise 1 · Identifying the role of data

Fitting with a pencil

You compare candidate durations of 12 min and 10 min using the records for A, B and C, then choose the one with the lowest risk. R is stored in the same folder, but its duration remains inaccessible. Which batches were used for training? Does calculating by hand change their role?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Identify the observations that helped you choose between the candidate parameters.

Hint 2

An observation can be used for training without being passed to a software library.

Compare with the solution

A, B and C, a total of 3 distinct batches, were used to choose the reported duration. Their errors determined the choice between candidate parameters: they form the training set for this fit.

R did not influence any choice in the problem. Its presence in the folder does not change its reserved role. Calculating with a pencil is still training, since the parameter is chosen using examples.

  • Name A, B and C and justify their role by how the parameter was chosen.
  • Exclude R from the described fit without referring to its location.
  • Explain why using software is not a requirement for training.

Exercise 2 · Application and checking

Add a distinct eligible batch

Start again from the original set A, B, C. A new batch H, distinct and eligible under the protocol, took 14 min; it is assigned to training. R remains reserved. Calculate the new number of records, their sum and the constant fit. Check the direction of the shift relative to 10 min.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Add the new duration to the sum of 30 min and add 1 to the number of records.

Hint 2

The new record is above the old mean: the new fit must increase and stay below 14 min.

Compare with the solution

The set contains 3 + 1 = 4 records. The sum becomes 30 + 14 = 44 min. The new mean is 44 ÷ 4 = 11 min. Dividing by 3 again would overlook the new set size.

Independent check: H exceeds the old mean by 14 − 10 = 4 min. Spreading this difference over the 4 records shifts the mean by 1 min: 10 + 1 = 11 min. The result does increase towards H.

This change comes from a new training record. It does not prove that the model predicts better; the duration of R was not used.

Check your reasoning

  • Obtain 4 records and a sum of 44 min.
  • Justify the division giving 11 min.
  • Check the direction of the shift and distinguish a new fit from a proven improvement.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis and repair

More rows, the same batches

Start again from the original set, without H. An export contains A, A, B, C: the A rows come from the same record, with no new measurement. The tool gives each row equal weight. Calculate its reported duration, explain what changed and correct the preparation of the set according to our protocol.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The sum includes 2 times 8 min. You need to count the rows to reproduce what the tool calculates.

Hint 2

Compare the total weight given to A with its initial weight. To correct the problem, use the identity of the batch, not its duration value.

Compare with the solution

There are 4 rows, but still 3 distinct batches. The sum of the rows is 30 + 8 = 38 min. The fit becomes 38 ÷ 4 = 19/2 = 9.50 min, a shift of -1/2 min from 10 min.

A now accounts for 2 rows out of 4, giving a total weight of 1/2, instead of 1/3. B and C each have a weight of 1/4. The mean changed because the weighting of the batches changed; no new observation was added.

Another way to write the check: (16 + 10 + 12) ÷ 4 = 9.50 min. To correct the problem, keep only one copy of the record for A, then fit again: the fitted duration returns to 10 min. Distinct batches could have the same duration; deleting all equal durations would be another error.

Deliberate weighting would require a stated justification. Here, the protocol gives equal weight to each distinct batch: the export duplicate must therefore be corrected, rather than presented as more data.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish 4 rows from 3 distinct batches.
  • Calculate 9.50 min and explain the weight of 1/2 for A.
  • Correct the problem using the identity of the record and recover 10 min.
  • Do not claim that a copy reduces uncertainty or improves prediction.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What makes a record a training example?

Its information is used to fit the model parameters. Here, the durations of A, B and C are used to fit the constant reported duration. The folder name and the use of software do not define this role.

Recall 2How do you recover the constant fit, and under what assumptions?

With a nonempty set, finite durations, squared loss and equal weight per record, add the durations and divide by their number. This mean minimizes the squared error on the set; another loss or different weights may change the fit.

Recall 3Why do 10 and 8/3 describe different quantities?

10 min is the duration reported by the model. 8/3 minutes squared is the mean of its squared errors on the training set. The risk gives neither a guaranteed future duration nor a success rate.

Recall 4Why can copying the record for A shift the fit without new information?

With equal weight per row, A is counted more often. In the export A, A, B, C, its total weight changes from 1/3 to 1/2. The fit changes through reweighting the same batches.

Recall 5What does the minimum risk on D demonstrate, and what evidence is still missing?

It demonstrates an optimal fit on D within the constant model family and with the chosen loss. It does not establish the errors on new batches. A separate measurement of this quality is still needed; reading this page does not demonstrate mastery.
