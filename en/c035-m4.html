Level 1 · C035·M4

Finite joint distribution

Your goalBuild a probability distribution for a pair of delays, read its cells, and find the probability of an event by adding the appropriate cells.

Before you start
c030-m5 : Poisson · c034-m5 : mean value · c035-m1 : univariate density · c035-m2 : cumulative distribution · c035-m3 : normal and exponential distributions

✿01 See the idea

At the same stop, buses A and B can arrive on time or late. Knowing how often A is late and how often B is late does not yet tell us whether they are late on the same mornings. We need to keep the information from the same morning together.

Let us build a fictional model: a box contains 20 cards. Each card shows a pair of delays, first for A and then for B. We draw a card at random, with every card equally likely to be chosen. The numbers in the diagram count these cards; they are not records of actual bus arrival times.

In this model, A is 0 or 5 min late; B is 0 or 10 min late. X denotes the delay of A and Y the delay of B. A pair is located at the intersection of a row for X and a column for Y. The joint distribution gives the probability of each pair.

To ask whether at least one bus is late, keep all the cells except the one where both buses are on time. Each card belongs to a single cell, so adding the probabilities of these cells does not count any card twice. The cells have equal areas to make the grid easy to read; their areas do not represent the sizes of the probabilities.

The rule to remember

Keep the values from the same morning in the same pair; assign a probability to each cell; for an event, add the cells that satisfy it, each just once.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From a box of cards to a joint distribution

We draw a card from 20, with no card more likely than another. The pairs (0 ; 0), (0 ; 10), (5 ; 0) and (5 ; 10), in minutes, appear 8, 2, 4 and 6 times respectively. Let us build the distribution, then calculate the probability that at least one bus is late.

  1. Define what a cell means

    X and Y are random variables: their values depend on the card drawn. Their possible values form the sets S = { 0 ; 5 } and T = { 0 ; 10 }, in minutes. The letters x and y denote a particular value of X and of Y. The symbol ∈ means “belongs to”. P denotes the probability of an event.

    p(x,y)=P(X=x et Y=y)

    The function p assigns each pair its probability, which has no unit. The conjunction in the formula requires the values to appear on the same card. We assume that the list of cells is complete, that the cards are equally likely, and that each card is placed in a single cell. We do not assume that the delay of one bus is unrelated to the delay of the other.

    This is a finite distribution: a cell directly holds a probability. For a continuous density, a density value alone is not a probability at a single point. No integral or cell width is involved here.

  2. Count the total by rows and by columns

    The row X = 0 contains 8 + 2 = 10 cards. The row X = 5 contains 4 + 6 = 10 cards. The total is 10 + 10 = 20 cards. We add because the groups have no cards in common.

    Check in the other direction: the column Y = 0 contains 8 + 4 = 12 cards; the column Y = 10 contains 2 + 6 = 8. We again find 12 + 8 = 20 cards. This check helps spot an omission; it does not replace checking the contents of the cells.

  3. Divide each count by the same total

    Each card has a probability 1/20 of being drawn. For a cell, count its favorable cards and divide by the total number of cards. We obtain p(0 ; 0) = 8/20 = 2/5, then p(0 ; 10) = 2/20 = 1/10.

    Similarly, p(5 ; 0) = 4/20 = 1/5 and p(5 ; 10) = 6/20 = 3/10. In decimal form, the cell values are 0.40, 0.10, 0.20 and 0.30 respectively, with no unit. Dividing a number of cards by a number of cards cancels this unit.

  4. Check that the distribution accounts for all the probability

    A finite joint distribution must assign nonnegative numbers whose sum over all the cells is 1. An impossible cell may have value 0. In the formula, each ∑ sign asks for an addition: we go through the values x in S and the values y in T, covering all the cells.

    p(x,y)≥0

    Here, the top row sums to 2/5 + 1/10 = 1/2; the bottom row to 1/5 + 3/10 = 1/2. The total 1/2 + 1/2 = 1 confirms normalization: all the probability is allocated. However, a correct sum does not prove that the cells accurately describe real buses.

    ∑x∈S∑y∈Tp(x,y)=1

  5. Select the event, then check using its complement

    “At least one bus is late” keeps the pairs (0 ; 10), (5 ; 0) and (5 ; 10). A single draw cannot give several different pairs, so their probabilities can be added. Grouping the cells where B is late gives the calculation 1/5 + (1/10 + 3/10) = 1/5 + 2/5 = 3/5.

    The complement is “both buses are on time”, which is the single cell (0 ; 0). Subtracting its probability from the total gives another method: 1 − 2/5 = 3/5 = 0.60, with no unit. The methods agree, and the result is indeed in the interval from 0 to 1.

    Directly adding the row “A is late” and the column “B is late” would count their shared cell twice. Marking the cells before adding prevents this double counting.

  6. Find the distribution of each bus at the margins of the table

    To describe X alone, we discard the information about Y by adding an entire row: P(X = 0) = 1/2 and P(X = 5) = 1/2. These probabilities form the marginal distribution of X; “marginal” refers to the totals that can be written at the edge of the table.

    To describe Y alone, we add a column: P(Y = 0) = 2/5 + 1/5 = 3/5; P(Y = 10) = 1/10 + 3/10 = 2/5. Each of these marginal distributions also sums to 1. The probability that B is late is therefore 0.40, with no unit, whereas the probability that both buses are late is 0.30.

    The joint distribution lets us find the marginal distributions. The reverse is not guaranteed: identical margins can hide different pairs. The last exercise asks you to construct this difference, without calculating covariance or conditional probability.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the calculations. Explain why a card gives a pair, why all the cells have the same denominator, and why adding a row removes the information about Y. Then find “at least one delay” using the cells and using its complement.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I can read the grid with the rows and columns swapped.”

    The labels give the order: X is the delay of A, Y the delay of B. Read the row first, then the column. The cell (5 ; 0) has value 1/5, whereas (0 ; 10) has value 1/10; they describe different mornings.

    Read the grid labels again

  • “My cells are between 0 and 1, so this must be a distribution.”

    You must also go through the full list of pairs and check that the sum is 1. When drawing from the whole box, divide each cell count by 20 cards; changing the denominator for each row would change the question.

    Review the total and normalization

  • “For “B is late”, I take only the cell where both buses are late.”

    B can be late with A on time or with A late. Take the entire column: 1/10 + 3/10 = 2/5. The shared cell 3/10 answers only “A and B are late”.

    Find a marginal distribution using the columns

Compare with the solution

The row X = 5 and the column Y = 0 intersect at 4 cards: 4/20 = 1/5 = 0.20. A is 5 min late and B arrives on time.

The row X = 0 and the column Y = 10 give 2 cards: 2/20 = 1/10 = 0.10. A is on time and B is 10 min late. The delays are in minutes; the probabilities have no unit.

The cell where both buses are late satisfies “at least one”, but not “exactly one”.

Compare with the solution

Select (0 ; 10) and (5 ; 0). Their sum is 1/10 + 1/5 = 3/10 = 0.30, with no unit. Each selected card describes one bus being late while the other is on time.

The excluded cells sum to 2/5 + 3/10 = 7/10. The check gives 1 − 7/10 = 3/10, matching the direct calculation. “At least one delay” also included the cards where both buses were late, giving its probability 3/5.

Exercise 3 · Transfer within the same setting

Are the margins enough to recover the pairs?

Change the original box: remove 2 cards for each of the pairs (0 ; 0) and (5 ; 10), then add 2 cards for each of the pairs (0 ; 10) and (5 ; 0). Reconstruct the counts. Do the row and column totals change? Does the probability of both buses being late change? State what this shows about what the margins tell us.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Calculate the new counts cell by cell before dividing. The total remains 20 cards.

Hint 2

Compare the row and column sums with their previous values. Then compare only the bottom right cell.

Compare with the solution

The top row now contains 8 − 2 = 6 cards, then 2 + 2 = 4 cards. The bottom row contains 4 + 2 = 6, then 6 − 2 = 4 cards. No count becomes negative.

The rows give 6 + 4 = 10 and 6 + 4 = 10 cards, as before. The columns give 6 + 6 = 12 and 4 + 4 = 8 cards, as before. The total remains 20: with the same denominator, all the marginal probabilities are preserved.

Yet the “both late” cell gives 4/20 = 1/5 = 0.20, instead of 3/10 = 0.30. The margins describe what happens to each bus separately; they are not enough to recover the joint distribution. This is a counterexample constructed within the model, not a measurement of traffic.

Check your reasoning

  • Find the counts 6, 4, 6 and 4 in the correct order.
  • Check the sums of each row and each column separately.
  • Contrast preserved margins with a joint probability that changes from 3/10 to 1/5.
  • Justify the conclusion using the cells, without assuming that margins determine the joint distribution.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does p(x ; y) mean in the bus setting?

The probability that the delay of A is x and the delay of B is y on the same card. The values x and y are in minutes; the probability has no unit.

Recall 2What checks must a probability grid satisfy to be a finite joint distribution?

The grid must cover all possible pairs. Each cell is nonnegative, and the sum of all the cells is 1. These mathematical checks do not guarantee that the model fits the real world.

Recall 3How do you find the distribution of Y alone from the distribution of the pair?

For each value of Y, add its column over all values of X. For Y = 10 min, this gives 1/10 + 3/10 = 2/5. The probability still has no unit, and the detail about X is lost.

Recall 4How do you calculate the probability of an event such as “at least one bus is late” without counting a card more than once?

Identify all the pairs that satisfy the event and add each cell only once. Here, excluding only the “both on time” cell also gives 1 − 2/5 = 3/5.

Recall 5Why do the distributions of X alone and Y alone not always let us reconstruct the joint distribution?

They give the row and column sums, but not the allocation within them. Changing the cells as in the exercise preserves these sums while changing the probability of simultaneous delays from 3/10 to 1/5.
