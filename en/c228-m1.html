Level 4 · C228·M1

Characteristic function

Your goalDefine the characteristic function of a jump distribution with four outcomes, evaluate it exactly at four frequencies, check its bounds, apply it to a sum of independent jumps, and recognize when four frequencies no longer suffice to recover the distribution.

Before you start
c227-m5 : Ito-Tanaka · c166-m5 : spectral truncation · c173-m5 : completeness

✿01 See the idea

A fictional price sometimes jumps by an integer number of ticks during a time interval. Let J denote this jump size, measured in ticks. In our example it has four outcomes, 0, 1, 2 or 3 ticks, with probabilities p0 = 0.500, p1 = 0.250, p2 = 0.125 and p3 = 0.125. These values are fictional: they are used for calculations, not to describe a real market. All calculations are exact because each decimal given is a terminating decimal fraction.

How can we represent this distribution by an object that always exists, identifies the distribution, and behaves well when jumps are added? The idea is to examine the distribution at a frequency u. For each outcome j, place the point at angle u·j on the circle of radius 1 in the complex plane. Give this point the probability of the outcome as its weight, then take the weighted average. This weighted average, which depends on u, is the characteristic function of J, denoted by φ.

Angles are measured in radians: u is therefore expressed in radians per tick, the inverse of the unit of J, and the product u·j is an angle with no unit. The point at angle θ on the circle of radius 1 is the complex number exp(iθ) = cos θ + i sin θ, where i denotes the number whose square is −1. Its real and imaginary parts are its two coordinates.

φ(u)=E[eiuJ]=∑j=03pjeiuj

E denotes the expectation, that is, the average weighted by the probabilities. The assumptions in this lesson are that J is integer valued, its distribution is given by the table, and the jumps added later are independent. The function gives a complex number with no unit. It exists for every u, with no condition on the moments of J: the rotating point always has length 1, so the average never diverges.

Three facts make this object useful. A weighted average of points on the circle stays within the disk: the modulus of φ is at most 1. At frequency u = 0, all the points are at angle 0 and the value is exactly 1. Finally, for independent jumps, the functions multiply. A fourth fact, accepted here without proof, will only be mentioned: if two distributions on the real line have the same function at every frequency, they are equal.

We will evaluate φ at the four frequencies u = 0, π/2, π and 3π/2. The points at angle u·j then fall at just four positions: 1, i, −1 and −i. The calculation remains exact, without cosines. This also has a limitation, related to spectral truncation: with few frequencies, different distributions can become indistinguishable, as exercise three will show.

The rule to remember

At each frequency u, φ(u) is the complex weighted average, with the probabilities as weights, of the points at angle u·J on the circle of radius 1; its value is 1 for u = 0, its modulus is at most 1, and for independent jumps the functions multiply.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

A fictional jump of 0 to three ticks

J takes the values 0, 1, 2 and 3 ticks, with fictional probabilities 0.500, 0.250, 0.125 and 0.125. We calculate φ at four frequencies, check its bounds, add two independent jumps, then recover the distribution from φ.

  1. Define the symbols, units and assumptions

    J is the integer jump size, in ticks. The probabilities P(J = j) are denoted by p0, p1, p2 and p3; they are positive and sum to 1. u is the frequency, in radians per tick. i is the complex number whose square is −1. J1 and J2 will later denote two independent jumps: P(J1 = a and J2 = b) = P(J1 = a) × P(J2 = b) for all values a and b.

    For a quarter turn, u = π/2, the point associated with outcome j is exp(iπj/2), which equals i raised to the power j. This power takes only four values: 1 for j = 0, i for j = 1, −1 for j = 2 and −i for j = 3. Beyond that, the sequence repeats every 4 steps.

    eiπj2=ij

    All probabilities in this example are fictional. The calculation is used to understand the object, not to value an instrument.

  2. Check the distribution and frequency 0

    The table defines a distribution if its probabilities are positive and sum to 1. The partial sums give 0.500 + 0.250 = 0.750, then 0.750 + 0.125 = 0.875, then 0.875 + 0.125 = 1.

    At frequency u = 0, exp(i·0·j) = 1 for every outcome j. So φ(0) = p0 + p1 + p2 + p3 = 1. This is the first check: a sum other than 1 would indicate that the table does not define a distribution.

  3. Evaluate at a quarter turn

    For u = π/2, the outcomes j = 0, 1, 2, 3 have the factors 1, i, −1 and −i. Group the real factors, then those containing i:

    φ(π2)=(p0−p2)+i(p1−p3)=0.375+i·0.125

    Real part: p0 − p2 = 0.500 − 0.125 = 0.375. Imaginary part: p1 − p3 = 0.250 − 0.125 = 0.125. Outcomes 2 and 3 subtract because their factors are −1 and −i.

  4. Evaluate at a half turn and three quarters of a turn

    For u = π, exp(iπj) equals 1 if j is even and −1 if j is odd: the result is real. φ(π) = (p0 − p1) + (p2 − p3) = (0.500 − 0.250) + (0.125 − 0.125) = 0.250 + 0.000 = 0.250.

    For u = 3π/2, the angle adds to the quarter turn angle to make 4 quarters, or a full turn, and a full turn gives 1 for every integer outcome. Each point is therefore the reflection across the real axis of the corresponding point at a quarter turn. The real part remains 0.375 and the imaginary part becomes the negative of 0.125, that is, 0 − 0.125, a negative value.

  5. Check the modulus

    The modulus of a + ib is the square root of a² + b². To avoid a square root, compare the squares. At u = π/2: 0.375² = 0.140625 and 0.125² = 0.015625, so |φ|² = 0.140625 + 0.015625 = 0.15625. This square is less than 1, so the modulus is too.

    The weighted average does lie inside the disk, as in the second diagram. At a half turn, φ equals 0.250, also with modulus less than 1. A modulus greater than 1 would indicate a calculation error or a table that does not define a distribution.

  6. Add two independent jumps

    Let S = J1 + J2, where J2 is an independent copy of J1 with the same distribution, and let ψ denote the characteristic function of S, just as φ denotes that of J. Since exp(iu(J1+J2)) = exp(iuJ1) × exp(iuJ2) and the expectation of a product of two independent quantities is the product of their expectations, we obtain ψ(u) = φ(u) × φ(u).

    The square of a + ib is (a² − b²) + i·2ab. At u = π/2, the real part is therefore 0.140625 − 0.015625 = 0.125. The imaginary part will be calculated in exercise two.

    Check by another method: the distribution of S. It takes values from 0 to 6 ticks. For example, P(S = 2) = p0p2 + p1p1 + p2p0 = 3 × 4/64 = 12/64. As weights over 64, the distribution of S is 16, 16, 12, 12, 5, 2, 1, summing to 64.

    The direct calculation of the real part assigns the factor 1 to outcomes 0 and 4, and −1 to outcomes 2 and 6: (16 − 12 + 5 − 1)/64 = (4 + 4)/64 = 8/64 = 0.125. Both methods give 0.125.

  7. Recover the distribution from the four values

    To recover p0, average the four values of φ: p0 = (φ(0) + φ(π/2) + φ(π) + φ(3π/2))/4. The reason is that, for each outcome j, the sum of the four points exp(iπjk/2), with k from 0 to 3, equals 4 if j = 0 and 0 otherwise. For j = 1: 1 + i − 1 − i = 0. For j = 2: 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 = 0. For j = 3: 1 − i − 1 + i = 0.

    The values at frequencies π/2 and 3π/2 are complex conjugates: their imaginary parts cancel and their real parts add. So 2 × 0.375 = 0.750, then 1 + 0.750 = 1.750, then 1.750 + 0.250 = 2.000. Finally, 2.000/4 = 0.500.

    Check: we recover exactly p0 = 0.500. This recovery works because J has only four consecutive outcomes; exercise three shows what changes when the distribution covers more values.

Explain it in your own words

Why is independence essential for writing the characteristic function of the sum as the square of φ? And why is recovery from four frequencies exact for J but in need of revision for S?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The characteristic function is a probability: a real number between 0 and 1.”

    φ is generally a complex number. At u = π/2, it equals 0.375 + i·0.125, with a nonzero imaginary part. Only its modulus is bounded by 1; the square of that modulus is 0.15625 here.

    Review the evaluation at a quarter turn

  • “For a sum of independent jumps, add the characteristic functions.”

    Multiply them. At frequency 0, adding would give 2, whereas every characteristic function equals 1 there: only the product 1 × 1 remains consistent. At π/2, the product gives a real part of 0.125, confirmed by the distribution of the sum.

    Review the step on the independent sum

  • “Four values of φ always allow us to recover the distribution.”

    They suffice for J, which has four consecutive outcomes. For S, which reaches 6 ticks, recovering the probability in position 0 gives 0.328125 instead of 16/64, because S = 0 and S = 4 become indistinguishable. Recall spectral truncation: removing frequencies removes information.

    Repeat the diagnosis in exercise three

Exercise 1 · Guided application

A uniform distribution on four outcomes

Another fictional jump size J′ takes the values 0, 1, 2 and 3 ticks, each with the same probability 0.250. Calculate φ(π/2) and φ(π), check φ(0), then recover p0 using the average in the last step of the example.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

First write the four differences p0 − p2, p1 − p3, p0 − p1 and p2 − p3: they involve equal probabilities.

Hint 2

If everything cancels, only φ(0) remains in the final average.

Compare with the solution

Real part at π/2: 0.250 − 0.250 = 0.000. Imaginary part: the same difference, so 0.000. At a half turn: (0.250 − 0.250) + (0.250 − 0.250) = 0.000. At u = 0, the sum of four probabilities each equal to 0.250 is 1.

Recovery: (1 + 2 × 0.000 + 0.000)/4 = 0.250, which is indeed the original probability. A uniform distribution on four consecutive outcomes does not oscillate at these three frequencies: φ vanishes there, and all the information is carried by φ(0).

Check your reasoning

  • The four differences are written with the correct pairs of probabilities.
  • φ(0) = 1 is checked before recovery.
  • The recovered probability is compared with the original value.

Exercise 2 · Cross-check

Imaginary part of a sum

For the distribution in the example and two independent jumps, calculate the imaginary part of ψ(π/2) in two ways: by squaring φ(π/2), then using the table of the distribution of S. Compare the two results.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For the square of a + ib, the imaginary part is 2ab.

Hint 2

In the table, only the odd outcomes have a factor containing i: +i for j = 1 and j = 5, −i for j = 3. Use the weights w1, w3 and w5.

Compare with the solution

Using the product: 0.375 × 0.125 = 0.046875, then 2 × 0.046875 = 0.09375.

Using the table: (16 − 12 + 2)/64 = (4 + 2)/64 = 6/64 = 0.09375. Both methods give 0.09375, so ψ(π/2) = 0.125 + i·0.09375. The agreement confirms the product rule; it does not remove the need to check independence in a real case.

Check your reasoning

  • The formula for the square of a complex number is written before substituting numbers.
  • The factors +i, −i and +i for the odd outcomes are assigned to the correct weights.
  • The two methods are explicitly compared.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis

Four frequencies for a sum

We know ψ at frequencies 0, π/2, π and 3π/2 for the sum S in the example. Recover the probability of the outcome S = 0 by averaging the four values, compare it with the true value in the table for S, then explain the difference.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Use ψ = φ² at each frequency: at a half turn, the square of 0.250; at a quarter turn, the real part already found.

Hint 2

The true probability of S = 0 is 16/64. Find the outcome of S that produces the same points at the four frequencies: two outcomes become indistinguishable when they differ by 4.

Compare with the solution

At a half turn: ψ(π) = 0.250² = 0.0625. Average: 2 × 0.125 = 0.250, then 1 + 0.250 = 1.250, then 1.250 + 0.0625 = 1.3125, finally 1.3125/4 = 0.328125.

The true value is 16/64, so the recovered probability is too large. The difference comes from S = 4: 16 + 5 = 21, and 21/64 = 0.328125. The outcomes S = 0 and S = 4 give the same four points, because four quarter turns make a full turn: their probabilities add.

This is not a flaw in φ, which identifies the distribution when all frequencies are available. These four frequencies do not suffice to distinguish the outcomes of a distribution covering the values from 0 to 6.

Check your reasoning

  • ψ is obtained as a product of φ at each frequency used.
  • The difference is explained by the inability to distinguish the outcomes S = 0 and S = 4.
  • The conclusion does not claim that φ fails to identify the distribution.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How is φ(u) defined for a jump size J, and in what unit is u expressed?

φ(u) is the average of exp(iuJ): the sum, over outcomes j, of the probability of j times exp(iuj). It is a complex number with no unit; u is in radians per unit of J, here per tick.

Recall 2What guarantees do we always have for φ(0) and for the modulus of φ(u)?

φ(0) equals 1, because all the points are at angle 0. The modulus is at most 1, because φ is a weighted average of points on the circle of radius 1. No moment assumption is needed.

Recall 3What is the characteristic function of a sum of two independent jumps?

The product of the two functions, not their sum: the expectation of a product of independent quantities is the product of their expectations.

Recall 4Why do four frequencies suffice to recover the distribution of J in the example?

J has four consecutive integer outcomes. At quarter turns, the sum of the four points for an outcome equals 4 for outcome 0 and 0 for the other three: each probability can be recovered by an average.

Recall 5What happens if the distribution has more than four outcomes and we keep the same four frequencies?

Two outcomes separated by 4 produce the same points and their probabilities add: for the sum S, we read 0.328125 instead of 16/64 for outcome 0.

Reconstruct the reasoning

State the jump distribution and check that its probabilities sum to 1; for each frequency u, place outcome j at angle u·j on the circle of radius 1, weight it by its probability and take the weighted average; check φ(0) = 1 and modulus at most 1; multiply the functions for independent jumps; recover a distribution only if the retained frequencies distinguish its outcomes.
