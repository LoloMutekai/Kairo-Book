Level 1 · C048·M4

Quadratic forms

Your goalConvert a quadratic expression into its symmetric matrix, calculate its value and explain the role of the cross term for the same deformed plate.

Before you start
c040-m5 : least squares · c048-m1 : eigenvalues · c048-m2 : diagonalization · c048-m3 : symmetric matrices

✿01 See the idea

Imagine a small elastic plate with a point that can move horizontally and vertically. Let x denote the horizontal displacement and y the vertical displacement, measured from the rest position. Moving this point in both directions at once may require an energy different from the sum of the costs of each separate displacement: the deformations are coupled.

We use a fictional model for this plate. The coordinates are in millimetres, written mm, and the energy is in millijoules, written mJ. The model assigns an energy to each displacement; its coefficients remain fixed. It is assumed to be valid for all the small deformations considered on this page. No physical test has validated it.

For the displacement x = 1 mm and y = -2 mm, the separate contributions are 2 mJ and 8 mJ. The coupling adds a contribution of -4 mJ. The total is therefore 6 mJ. This negative contribution expresses an interaction in the formula; it does not by itself represent energy stored separately.

The feature to track is the square: if all displacements are multiplied by the same factor, each product of displacements receives this factor twice. This is the idea of a quadratic form. We will recover the total using the coordinates, then using a matrix.

The rule to remember

A quadratic form combines squares and products of coordinates. In a symmetric matrix, the matching off-diagonal entries together contribute twice their common value to the coefficient of the cross term.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Calculate and check the energy of the plate

The model combines the square of x weighted by a, the product xy weighted by 2b and the square of y weighted by c. The coefficients are a = 2, b = 1 and c = 2, in mJ/mm². For x = 1 mm and y = -2 mm, let us calculate q while keeping the sign of y. Here the letter q directly denotes the energy of the model, with no additional factor.

  1. Define the form and the objects

    A real quadratic form assigns a scalar, meaning a number, to a column vector z through q(z) = zᵀAz. The matrix A is real, fixed and symmetric: Aᵀ = A. The superscript T means transpose; here it turns the column z into the row zᵀ. It does not mean a square or an inverse.

    If z has n components, A has n rows and n columns. The product Az is a column, then zᵀ(Az) is a scalar. For our plate, n = 2, z contains x and y in mm, and each coefficient of A is in mJ/mm². Az is therefore in mJ/mm; the final product is in mJ.

    You can also recognize the form in its expanded expression: each term is a product of two coordinates, possibly the same coordinate twice. It is a homogeneous polynomial of degree 2. Adding a term proportional to x alone, or a nonzero constant, would make the expression cease to be a quadratic form.

    z=[xy],A=[abbc]

  2. Understand the two cross contributions

    In the matrix product, the top right entry provides bxy, and the bottom left entry provides byx. Since x and y are real numbers, xy = yx: these contributions add together. The coefficient of the monomial xy is therefore 2b, while each off-diagonal entry contains only b.

    q(z)=ax2+bxy+byx+cy2=ax2+2bxy+cy2

    The plate data therefore correspond to the following matrix. The cross coefficient in the expanded expression is 2 mJ/mm²; b is 1 mJ/mm².

    A=[2112] mJ/mm²

  3. First multiply the matrix by the column

    The first row of A takes a times x and b times y: 2 × 1 + 1 × (-2) = 2 + (-2) = 0 mJ/mm. The second takes b times x and c times y: 1 × 1 + 2 × (-2) = 1 + (-4) = -3 mJ/mm.

    Az=[ax+bybx+cy]=[0-3] mJ/mm

    This intermediate column is not yet the energy: its units and its two components show that it still needs to be multiplied on the left by zᵀ.

  4. Complete the product and check the expansion

    The row zᵀ contains x followed by y. It multiplies the corresponding components of Az, then adds the products: 1 × 0 + (-2) × (-3) = 0 + 6 = 6 mJ. The result is indeed a single number.

    Let us check the polynomial term by term. The square of x is 1 mm² and the square of y is 4 mm²; the product xy is -2 mm². The square of a negative coordinate is positive, but the product of coordinates with opposite signs is negative.

    q(z)=2×1+2×(-2)+2×4=2+(-4)+8=6 mJ

    The two pure contributions total 10 mJ; here the coupling subtracts 10 − 6 mJ. The matrix and expanded calculations give the same energy, 6.00 mJ.

  5. Check using another expression

    Let κ = 1 mJ/mm², where κ is read as kappa. For the particular coefficients of this plate, we can regroup the squares differently:

    q(z)=κ[(x+y)2+x2+y2]

    Expanding the square of x + y gives the sum of the squares of x and y, plus 2xy. Adding the square of each coordinate once more recovers exactly the original coefficients. This identity is specific to the choice a = c = 2κ and b = κ, rather than to every quadratic form.

    Numerically, x + y = -1 mm, so its square is 1 mm². The other squares total 5 mm². The expression inside the brackets is 6 mm²; multiplying it by κ gives 6 mJ. This regrouping checks the total and its unit again.

  6. Recognize the scaling law and distinguish it from linearity

    Let t be a real factor with no unit. Replacing z with tz multiplies each coordinate by t and keeps A fixed. There is then a factor t on each side of A:

    q(tz)=(tz)TA(tz)=t2zTAz=t2q(z)

    This property is called homogeneity of degree 2. Returning to rest gives q(0, 0) = 0 mJ. Reversing the entire displacement keeps the same energy, because the negative factor is squared. Doubling the entire displacement multiplies the energy by the square of 2, which you will check in the exercises.

    The diagonalization studied earlier also lets us change coordinates to remove the cross terms of a symmetric matrix. We do not need it to evaluate q here. Systematically determining its sign for different directions belongs to the next unit, on positivity.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the formulas. Explain why the matrix contains b in two entries and why Az is not yet the energy, then reconstruct both calculations of the total. Which step would fail if you replaced the sign of y with the sign of its square?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The coefficient of xy is copied into each off-diagonal entry.”

    Both entries contribute to the same monomial. For a cross coefficient equal to 2, each symmetric entry must contain 2 ÷ 2 = 1, in mJ/mm². Otherwise you count the coefficient in the expanded expression twice.

    Revisit the two cross contributions

  • “Since there are squares, I can ignore the signs of the coordinates.”

    The product xy is not a square. With x = 1 mm and y = -2 mm, it is -2 mm² and the cross contribution is -4 mJ. Keep each coordinate in parentheses until you perform its operation.

    Revisit the polynomial calculation with signs

  • “An expression involving a matrix must be linear.”

    Az depends linearly on z, but q also uses z on the left. The factor therefore acts twice: q(tz) = t × t × q(z). Also, coupling generally prevents us from adding the energies of separate displacements.

    Test scaling, then addition of displacements

Exercise 1 · Recognition with explanation

Reconstruct the specification for the plate

Hint 2

The total coefficient of xy is 2b; therefore divide the stated coefficient by 2.

Compare with the solution

Exercise 2 · Application and two checks

Double the entire displacement

The same plate goes from the displacement (1 mm; -2 mm) to the displacement (2 mm; -4 mm). Predict the energy using the scaling law, then check it by calculating the two squares and the cross term separately. The matrix stays the same.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The same factor t = 2 multiplies both coordinates; the energy receives the square of t.

Hint 2

In the direct calculation, keep the sign of -4 for the product in the cross term and square the entire coordinate for the vertical term.

Compare with the solution

The factor is 2, with no unit. Its square is 4. The prediction is therefore q(tz) = 4 × 6 = 24 mJ.

Direct check: the square of x is 4 mm², the square of y is 16 mm² and xy is -8 mm². The contributions are 2 × 4 = 8 mJ; 2 × (-8) = -16 mJ; 2 × 16 = 32 mJ.

The total gives 40 + (-16) = 24 mJ. Both methods agree. Applying the factor to q only once would have treated a quadratic energy as a linear function.

Check your reasoning

  • The factor multiplies all coordinates and keeps the matrix fixed.
  • The prediction uses the square of the factor.
  • The direct calculation keeps the negative cross contribution and recovers 24 mJ.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and objection

Can the separate costs be added?

For the same plate, a worksheet calculates the horizontal displacement u = (1 mm; 0 mm), then the vertical displacement v = (0 mm; -2 mm). It states: “For u + v, simply add q(u) and q(v).” Evaluate these quantities, recover the missing term and explain the error without invoking a new physical measurement.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For each separate displacement, one coordinate is 0; the product in the cross term disappears.

Hint 2

The displacement u + v is the one in the example. Compare its energy with the sum of the pure contributions.

Compare with the solution

u and v are displacement vectors; their sum adds the coordinates. In u, the vertical term and the cross term vanish, so q(u) = 2 mJ. In v, the horizontal term and the cross term vanish, so q(v) = 8 mJ. The sum is 10 mJ.

But u + v = (1 mm; -2 mm), so q(u + v) = 6 mJ. The difference q(u + v) − q(u) − q(v) is -4 mJ: this is the cross contribution 2xy = -4 mJ that the worksheet omitted.

A quadratic form is therefore not additive in general. Here the model evaluates the final combined displacement; the energies of the separate configurations are not successive amounts of work to be added. The disagreement is algebraic and proves nothing about a real plate.

Check your reasoning

  • You explain why the cross term is 0 for each separate displacement.
  • You compare 10 mJ with 6 mJ and recover -4 mJ.
  • You relate the difference to coupling and distinguish the fictional model from physical observation.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does q(z) = zᵀAz represent, and which objects are fixed or variable?

A is a fixed real symmetric matrix; z is a variable column vector. Az is a column; multiplication on the left by the row zᵀ gives the scalar q(z). The expansion combines products of two coordinates.

Recall 2Why does a symmetric entry b give the coefficient 2b in front of xy?

The two off-diagonal entries produce bxy and byx. Real numbers satisfy xy = yx, so their sum is 2bxy. To recover each entry from the polynomial, divide the cross coefficient by 2.

Recall 3What happens to the energy if a factor t multiplies all coordinates without changing A?

q(tz) = t × t × q(z), because t appears on each side of A. t has no unit. This does not imply additivity: cross terms generally prevent us from adding the values for separate displacements.

Recall 4How can you check the calculation for the plate without simply rereading the same addition?

Compare the product zᵀ(Az) with the polynomial, then add the squares of x + y, x and y and multiply by κ. This identity is specific to the chosen coefficients. The results must agree in mJ; Az alone remains in mJ/mm.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the sequence: coordinates and units → symmetric matrix → double off-diagonal contribution → column Az → scalar zᵀ(Az) → check using squares → squared scaling factor. Then explain why adding the separate costs loses the coupling.
