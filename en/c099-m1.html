Level 2 · C099·M1

Logistic regression

Your goalTransform an affine score into a probability using the sigmoid, then distinguish this probability from the decision made using a threshold.

Before you start
c067-m5 : pipeline · c060-m5 : approximations and assumptions · c059-m6 : deterministic discrete Bellman

✿01 See the idea

A workshop receives requests for assistance. For each request, a checklist set in advance counts signs of urgency: we find 2 on the request we will follow. We want to estimate whether it is urgent, then decide whether it enters the priority queue. The count alone does not yet give either a probability or a decision.

Logistic regression starts by constructing a score: here each sign adds the same contribution, starting from an initial offset. This score can be negative or positive. A curve called the sigmoid transforms it into a value between 0 and 1, interpreted by the model as a probability of urgency.

For our request, this probability will be 2/3 ≈ 0.67, with no unit. The workshop rule then compares it with the threshold 3/5 = 0.60. The estimated value and the priority ranking rule are separate objects: changing the threshold can change the queue without changing the model.

This entire scenario is fictional: the requests, checklist, coefficients and threshold are chosen for the calculation. No training on observations has taken place. A bounded output does not guarantee that the probabilities reflect actual frequencies of urgency. Here we study the form of the model and a prediction, rather than all the metrics in the chapter.

The rule to remember

Construct the affine score, apply the sigmoid, then compare the probability with the stated threshold. Keep the score, probability, predicted class and observed urgency separate.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From the urgency checklist to the priority queue

Our request has x = 2 signs of urgency. The fictional model sets b = −ln(2) and w = ln(2). The threshold is t = 3/5. Let us find the probability and class, keeping the expressions exact.

  1. State what we want to predict

    X denotes the count of signs that a request could have; x is its observed value. Y denotes its urgency label, according to a fixed annotation rule: Y = 1 for urgent, Y = 0 otherwise. An observed label is written y. It must be possible to assign it independently of the classification produced by the model.

    p(x) denotes the conditional probability P(Y = 1 | X = x) assumed by the model; the vertical bar means “given”. We abbreviate it to p for our request. The predicted class is written ŷ, with a hat. Y remains unknown when the request is assigned to a queue.

    b is the initial offset, w the contribution per sign, z the score and t the threshold. All have no unit: x is a pure count and z must be dimensionless to be used in an exponential. The model assumes that the score is affine in x, with coefficients fixed during this prediction; it does not assume that p itself is affine. The checklist and the meaning of the label must stay the same between training and use.

  2. Construct the affine score

    Affine here means an offset b plus a contribution proportional to the count x, with factor w.

    z=b+wx=(x−1)ln(2)

    ln is the natural logarithm; exp is its inverse function. Thus exp(ln(a)) = a for positive a. In our choice, b = −ln(2) offsets the contribution of the first sign. With x = 2, z = −ln(2) + 2 × ln(2) = 1 × ln(2) = ln(2).

    The score is positive, but it is not a probability. In general, it is not bounded between 0 and 1. The logarithmic expression is kept exact: we have not rounded a coefficient to obtain a convenient fraction.

  3. Define the sigmoid and the odds

    p=σ(z)=11+exp(−z)

    σ, the Greek letter sigma, denotes the logistic sigmoid here. When z increases, exp(−z) decreases, so the denominator decreases and p increases. exp(z) is always positive. Let q = exp(z), the odds assigned to urgency. Multiplying the numerator and denominator of the formula by exp(z) gives a form that is more convenient for calculation:

    p=q1+q

  4. Calculate the odds without approximation

    For the score z = ln(2), q = exp(ln(2)) = 2. Odds of 2 compare the “urgent” weight with the “non-urgent” weight: 2 to 1. This ratio is not a probability.

    Another exact interpretation: with no signs, the odds would be exp(−ln(2)) = 1/2. The exponential of a sum is the product of the exponentials of its terms. Each sign adds ln(2) to the score, so it multiplies the odds by 2. With 2 signs, we obtain 1/2 × 2 = 1, then 1 × 2 = 2. This doubling is an assumption encoded by our coefficients, rather than a fact measured on the requests.

  5. Express the weights relative to a common total

    The relative weights are 2 for urgent and 1 for non-urgent. Their sum is 3. Dividing the urgency weight by this sum makes the two probabilities complementary:

    p=21+2=23=2/3≈0.67

    The complementary probability is 1 − 2/3 = 1/3. The sum is 1, with no unit. The denominator 1 + q is strictly greater than q: for any finite score, p stays strictly between 0 and 1.

  6. Add an explicit decision rule

    Set ŷ = 1 if p ≥ t; otherwise ŷ = 0. By convention, equality therefore places the request in the priority queue. With t = 3/5 = 0.60, the exact difference p − t is 1/15, which is positive. We classify this request as a priority.

    Check without rounding: comparing 2/3 with 3/5 amounts to comparing 2 × 5 = 10 and 3 × 3 = 9, because the denominators are positive. The decision is indeed ŷ = 1. This still does not reveal the actual value of Y. The threshold is given here; choosing a threshold according to the costs of errors requires further work.

  7. Work backwards to check

    Reconstruct the odds from the probabilities: p/(1 − p) = (2/3)/(1/3) = 2. Their logarithm is ln(2), the original score. This is the characteristic link in the model: the logarithm of the odds, ln(p/(1 − p)), is affine in x.

    With x = 1, the score would become 0 and the probability 1/2 = 0.50. With x = 0, the score would be −ln(2), the odds 1/2 and the probability 1/3. These opposite scores give 1/3 + 2/3 = 1. The direction of change is consistent with positive w: more signs increase p.

  8. Separate the form of the model from its training

    Why the word regression? The model makes the logarithm of the odds affine, even when the final output is used for classification. In actual training, b and w would be fitted to requests with observed labels y, rather than chosen for this page.

    A common way to fit them minimizes the sum of logarithmic losses over the training examples: for y = 1, the loss is −ln(p); for y = 0, it is −ln(1 − p). The loss penalizes a low probability assigned to the observed label. This criterion trains the coefficients; comparison with t then determines the queue.

    We do not perform this fitting here. Before any real use, defensible labels and evaluation on requests held out from fitting would be needed, among other things. The formula alone establishes neither the quality of the predictions nor a benefit from sorting requests.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The odds are 2, so the probability is 2.”

    The odds compare two weights; a probability relates a weight to the total. Add the weight 1 of the other class: 2/(1 + 2) = 2/3. Calculating p/(1 − p) again must then give 2.

    Review the odds, normalization and reverse check

  • “A probability of 2/3 means that this request is actually urgent.”

    The model assigns a probability; the threshold gives a predicted class. The observed label comes from the annotation rule. An output between 0 and 1 does not prove that it reflects actual frequencies and does not guarantee the outcome of an individual case.

    Review what the blocks along the path establish

  • “Raising the threshold requires retraining the regression.”

    If the request and coefficients stay fixed, its score and probability stay fixed. The threshold belongs to the decision rule; raising it can only change the assigned class. The consequences of this choice will need to be evaluated separately.

    Work through the rule change in the last exercise

Exercise 2 · Application · exact calculation on paper

One additional sign

Start again with the request in the example and add a sign: x = 3. Keep b, w and t. Calculate z, q, p, then the class. Check your probability by reconstructing its odds. You may use exp(2 × ln(2)) = 2 × 2.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The score is (3 − 1) × ln(2). Each added sign multiplies q by 2.

Hint 2

The step q → p divides q by 1 + q, never by q alone.

Compare with the solution

z = 2 × ln(2) = ln(4). The odds are therefore q = 4. We can also start again from the odds 2 in the example and multiply them by 2: 2 × 2 = 4.

p = 4/(1 + 4) = 4/5 = 4/5 = 0.80. This probability exceeds t = 0.60, so ŷ = 1.

Reverse check: the complement is 1/5. Dividing 4/5 by 1/5 gives 4, the expected odds. The probability increases without exceeding 1; it did not double when the odds doubled.

Check your reasoning

  • I reconstruct the exact score and odds of 4.
  • I normalize by the total 5 before comparing with the threshold.
  • I check using p/(1 − p) and distinguish doubling the odds from the change in p.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does the model calculate before producing a probability?

An affine score z = b + wx, where b is the offset, w the contribution per unit of x and x the observed feature. The score is dimensionless and is not bounded like a probability.

Recall 2Reconstruct the sigmoid, then its value for a score of 0.

p = 1/(1 + exp(−z)). If z = 0, exp(−z) = 1, so p = 1/2. For a finite score, the probability is strictly between 0 and 1.

Recall 3Why do odds of 2 give a probability of 2/3?

The odds q compare the weights of the urgent class and the other class. We normalize: p = q/(1 + q), so 2/(1 + 2) = 2/3. Conversely, q = p/(1 − p), and ln(q) is the affine score.

Recall 4What can change when only the threshold is modified?

The predicted class ŷ can change. The score, model probability and actual label stay the same. The handling of equality must be stated; here ŷ = 1 for p ≥ t.

Recall 5What distinguishes our fictional coefficients from learned coefficients?

Ours are chosen for exact calculation. Learned coefficients would be fitted to features and observed labels, for example by minimizing a logarithmic loss. Quality must then be evaluated separately; reading the formula proves no gain.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the chain for the request: feature x, score b + wx, odds exp(z), probability q/(1 + q), then comparison with t. Reconstruct the exact fraction in the example and its reverse check. Finish by distinguishing learned coefficients, the classification rule and the observed label: none of these terms replaces the other two.
