Level 2 · C143·M2

Compactness

Your goalSelect a finite cover of a closed rail, check that it covers every position, and understand why removing an endpoint changes the guarantee.

Before you start
c142-m5 : convergence criteria · c045-m5 : introductory quadrature · c046-m5 : approximation error · c143-m1 : rigorous continuity

✿01 See the idea

A trolley can stop anywhere on a rail running from 0 m to 6 m, including the end stops. For each possible camera position, imagine a viewing window around its centre. The practical question is: can we choose finitely many of these windows and keep the entire rail visible?

In this fictional scene, a camera sees positions at a distance strictly less than 2 m from its centre. At a distance of exactly 2 m, visibility is not guaranteed. We therefore represent each window by an open interval: its endpoints are excluded. The model ignores obstacles and the dimensions of the trolley; it considers only its position on a line.

A family of windows covers the rail if every position on the rail belongs to at least one window. Selecting a finite subcover means keeping only finitely many windows from that same family, without losing a single position. The rail still has infinitely many positions, even when the list of cameras becomes finite.

Compactness guarantees this selection for every cover by open sets, even when the windows have different sizes. It does not specify their number, choose their centres, or promise the smallest possible set of equipment. The diagrams show a successful choice, followed by a difficulty when the left end stop is removed.

The rule to remember

On the real line, being closed and bounded is equivalent to being compact. For every cover by open sets, a compact set allows a finite selection that still covers it; each proposed selection must still be checked.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Choose cameras, then prove coverage

The coordinates are expressed in metres on a real line. The set of positions is K = [0 ; 6]. For each centre t belonging to K, a window Uₜ contains the x satisfying |x − t| < r, with r = 2 m. All these windows are available; let us choose those centred at 0 m, 3 m and 6 m.

  1. Move from windows to the definition

    x is a position to observe, t a possible centre, and r the viewing radius: these quantities are lengths. |x − t| is the distance between x and t. Ut = ]t − r ; t + r[ is open. More generally, a set U is open if, around each of its points y, it contains an interval ]y − ρ ; y + ρ[ for a strictly positive radius ρ, in metres here.

    A family (Ut), indexed by a set I, covers K when every x in K is in at least one Ut. The symbol ⋃ means taking all the points in all the indicated sets together; ⊂ means “is contained in”. K is compact if, for every family of open sets covering it, there are finitely many members, say m, that still cover it. For nonempty K, m is a positive integer with no unit; the selected indices in the formula identify these members.

    K⊂⋃t∈IUt⇒K⊂Ut1∪⋯∪Utm

    In our family, each position x is covered by the window centred at t = x: the distance is 0 m, strictly less than r. This checks the initial cover, but does not yet check a finite selection. The word “every” in the definition is essential: showing one particular finite cover does not prove compactness.

  2. Identify the domain where the guarantee applies

    The Heine–Borel theorem on the real line states that a set is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded. Bounded means contained between finite bounds. Closed means that it contains every real limit of a convergent sequence of its points. This criterion applies here to the real line with its usual distance; it cannot be transferred to another space without assumptions.

    K is bounded: 0 ≤ x ≤ 6 for every position, with coordinates in the fixed unit m. It is closed: if positions in K converge to a position ℓ, taking limits in the inequalities gives 0 ≤ ℓ ≤ 6. The end stops are therefore included. The criterion establishes the compactness of K and guarantees a finite subcover from our family of open sets.

    The general criterion is used as a theorem. Here is why the guarantee holds for the segment we are considering: the following proof uses the completeness of the real numbers, encountered earlier, and does not depend on the particular radius of the cameras.

  3. Understand the proof for a segment

    Take a segment [a ; b] with a < b and any cover by open sets. The letters a and b denote its endpoints. Let E be the set of positions s belonging to [a ; b] such that [a ; s] is covered by finitely many open sets from this cover. E contains a, since an open set covers a; E is bounded above by b. Completeness provides c = sup E, the least upper bound of E.

    An open set U in the cover contains c, because c belongs to [a ; b]. It therefore contains ]c − η ; c + η[ for a length η > 0. Choose s in E with c − η/2 < s ≤ c; such an s exists by the definition of the least upper bound, and if c = a we can take s = a. A finite cover of [a ; s], supplemented by U, covers up to d = min(b, c + η/2). The symbol min selects the smaller of the two numbers. All the symbols s, c, d and η are lengths in the same unit here.

    If c < b, then d > c: we would have d in E beyond its upper bound, which is impossible. Thus c = b. The same construction then gives a finite cover of the entire segment [a ; b]. The openness of the windows provides the small extension; the finite bound and the inclusion of the endpoints prevent the reasoning from escaping the segment. This proof establishes the result for segments, without claiming to prove the general criterion for all closed and bounded sets here.

  4. Calculate the selected windows

    We try the centres 0 m, 3 m and 6 m. For each centre, subtracting r gives the left endpoint and adding r gives the right endpoint. The windows remain open: seeing a point exactly at a boundary is outside the guarantee.

    A: 0 − 2 = -2 m and 0 + 2 = 2 m, so A = ]-2 ; 2[. B: 3 − 2 = 1 m and 3 + 2 = 5 m, so B = ]1 ; 5[. C: 6 − 2 = 4 m and 6 + 2 = 8 m, so C = ]4 ; 8[.

    K⊂A∪B∪C

    This inclusion is the result to establish, rather than an automatic consequence of our choice of centres. We have kept 3 windows from the initial family; K still contains all the positions between the end stops.

  5. Check the junctions and end stops

    A and B overlap because the left endpoint of B is strictly before the right endpoint of A. Their overlap is ]1 ; 2[, with length 2 − 1 = 1 m. B and C overlap on ]4 ; 5[, with length 5 − 4 = 1 m. There is therefore no gap between successive windows.

    The end stops are inside windows: -2 < 0 < 2 and 4 < 6 < 8, in the unit m. The union is the interval ]-2 ; 8[, which contains K. The points 2 m and 4 m, excluded from an outer window, are in B. The strict inequalities account for the junction points, which would be overlooked by merely adding lengths.

  6. Check using the distance to the nearest centre

    Another check: between the centres 0 m and 3 m, the distance to the nearer of the two is at most half their separation, namely 3 ÷ 2 = 3/2 m = 1.50 m. The same reasoning applies between 3 m and 6 m, because the separation is the same. The positions farthest from the centres are the midpoints of these intervals.

    The maximum distance to the nearest selected centre is therefore 1.50 m. It is strictly less than the radius 2 m; the difference is 2 − 1.50 = 0.50 m. Every position is thus strictly inside at least one window. This margin is a geometric property of the fictional example, rather than a validated safety margin for a real camera.

    Compactness guaranteed the existence of a finite subcover. The calculation checked this particular subcover using the junctions, then the distances. We have not sought the minimum number of cameras or checked a physical installation.

Explain it in your own words

Without looking, distinguish the property of K, the family of windows, and the chosen selection. Why does the theorem allow a finite selection without guaranteeing that any choice of centres will work? Then repeat the check using distances.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The rail is bounded, so it is compact.”

    On the real line, it must also be closed. The rail with its left end stop removed remains bounded, but it lacks a limit of positions that it contains. The final exercise constructs an open cover for which no finite selection suffices.

    Reconstruct the counterexample of the rail open at the left end

  • “I found windows that work, so I proved compactness.”

    You checked one particular cover. Compactness concerns every cover by open sets. Return to the definition and the reasoning with an arbitrary family: our single diagram cannot replace the quantifier.

    Reread the definition and the proof for the segment

  • “The windows touch: that is enough to cover the rail.”

    The windows are open. If their endpoints meet exactly without overlapping, the contact point may belong to the rail without belonging to any window. Reducing the radius to 1.50 m in the calculation exercise reveals these overlooked points.

    Test the contact points with a reduced radius

Exercise 1 · Direct application

Is the middle camera unnecessary?

Keep K = [0 ; 6] m and the radius 2 m. Someone keeps only A and C. Give a position on the rail that this selection misses, then state which window covers the positions 2 m and 4 m in the full selection.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Test the centre of the rail: compare its distance from the centres of the remaining cameras with the radius.

Hint 2

An endpoint of A or C may be inside B. Write the strict inequalities.

Compare with the solution

The position 3 m is 3 m from the centre of A and 3 m from the centre of C. These distances exceed 2 m: A and C therefore do not cover K. More precisely, they miss the positions in [2 ; 4] m, including the endpoints.

In the full selection, B = ]1 ; 5[ m contains 2 m and 4 m, since 1 < 2 < 5 and 1 < 4 < 5. A excludes 2 m; C excludes 4 m. B covers both of these junction points.

Check your reasoning

  • Give a position belonging to K but to neither A nor C.
  • Justify your answer using distances or interval bounds.
  • Check the two junction points separately using B.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and diagnosis

A radius that is just too small

The centres remain 0 m, 3 m and 6 m, but the radius becomes 1.50 m. Calculate the right endpoint of the first window, both endpoints of the middle window, and the left endpoint of the last window. Does the selection cover K? Fix it by restoring the radius from the example. Does this failure prove that K is not compact?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare 0 + 1.50 with 3 − 1.50, then 3 + 1.50 with 6 − 1.50.

Hint 2

The visibility inequality is strict: a distance equal to the radius is not enough.

Compare with the solution

The first window ends at 1.50 m. The middle window starts at 1.50 m and ends at 4.50 m. The last starts at 4.50 m. Successive windows therefore touch at the positions 1.50 m and 4.50 m, without containing them.

At each of these positions, the nearest centres are exactly 1.50 m away. The positions belong to the rail, but the strict visibility condition fails: the selection does not cover K. Returning to the radius 2 m restores the overlaps of 1 m and 1 m calculated in the example.

K remains the same closed and bounded segment: it remains compact. The family of all windows centred at t belonging to K still covers K, even with the reduced radius, since the window centred at x covers x. Compactness guarantees a finite subcover from this family; it does not guarantee our selection of the centres 0, 3 and 6.

Check your reasoning

  • Calculate the four bounds in metres, using the French decimal comma.
  • Name the missed points and use the exclusion of endpoints.
  • Restore the radius and distinguish the failure of a selection from the property of K.

Exercise 3 · Proof and counterexample

Remove the left end stop

The rail becomes K′ = ]0 ; 6] m. This time the proposed windows are Vₙ = ]6/n ; 7[ m, for every integer n ≥ 2. Show that they cover K′. For a nonempty finite selection, let N be its largest index and find a point on the rail that is still missed. Perform the calculation for N = 4. N is an index, rather than the number of selected windows.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For x > 0, choose an integer n greater than 6/x: what happens to the inequality 6/n < x?

Hint 2

As n increases, the left bound decreases. The window with the largest index therefore contains the other selected windows.

Hint 3

Test x = 3/N m and compare it with 6/N m.

Compare with the solution

Each Vn is an open interval of the line. If x is a coordinate in K′, then x > 0 and x ≤ 6 < 7. There is an integer n ≥ 2 with n > 6/x, because the integers are not bounded above. The quotient of coordinates in the same unit has no unit. Then 6/n < x < 7, so x belongs to Vn: the entire family covers K′.

Now take any nonempty finite selection and let N be its largest index. The windows are nested: their union is V_N = ]6/N ; 7[ m. The point x = 3/N m is strictly positive, at most 6 m, and strictly less than 6/N m. It is on K′, but outside the union. The empty selection does not cover this nonempty rail either. No finite selection suffices.

With N = 4, the left bound is 6/4 = 3/2 m = 1.50 m; the missed point is 3/4 = 3/4 m = 0.75 m. Adding the window with index 9, greater than 8, covers this point since 6/9 = 2/3 m < 3/4 m. But a new finite selection still has a largest index and misses a point closer to the left end: the reasoning repeats for every selection, rather than just our value of N.

This cover with no finite subcover proves that K′ is not compact. K′ remains bounded but is not closed: the positions 6/n m, for n ≥ 2, belong to it and converge to 0 m, which does not belong to it. We are not asking to see the removed point; positive positions that are present on the rail escape every finite selection.

Check your reasoning

  • Consider an arbitrary position on the rail to prove that the initial family covers it.
  • Consider an arbitrary finite selection, rather than only the first few indices.
  • Check that the missed point belongs to K′ and remains outside all the selected windows.
  • Relate the counterexample to the excluded limit, without confusing N with a count.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the essential quantifier in the definition of compactness?

Every cover by open sets must have a finite subcover made up of members of the given family. A single successful choice does not establish this universal property.

Recall 2What criterion identifies compact sets on the real line, and what does closed mean?

With the usual distance, being compact is equivalent to being closed and bounded. Closed means containing every real limit of a convergent sequence of its points; bounded means remaining between finite bounds.

Recall 3In the proof for the segment, what roles do the least upper bound and openness play?

We take the least upper bound c of the positions reachable with a finite cover from the left. An open set containing c allows a finite cover reaching just before c to be extended. If c were before the right endpoint, we would exceed this upper bound; at the right endpoint, the same open set completes the cover.

Recall 4Why can open windows that touch miss a point?

The contact point is excluded from both windows. Our selection needs a strict overlap or another window covering that point, followed by a check of the end stops as well.

Recall 5On the rail with its left end stop removed, why do the windows Vₙ allow no finite subcover?

A finite selection has a largest index N. Its union starts strictly after 6/N m and still misses 3/N m, a positive position on the rail. Yet every positive position is covered by a window with a sufficiently large index.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct the reasoning without support: define an open cover; put “every” before the choice of family and “there exists” before the finite selection; identify the closed and bounded segment; explain the proof using the least upper bound; calculate and check the chosen windows; show how removing an endpoint allows a cover with no finite subcover.
