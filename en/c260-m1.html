Level 4 · C260·M1

Conditional intensity

Your goalCalculate an arrival rate from the available history and distinguish this rate from a count and a probability.

Before you start
c226-m5 : integration with jumps · c061-m5 : identifiability and uncertainty · c259-m5 : simulation and limits · c090-m5 : stationarity

✿01 See the idea

A technical support team receives incident reports. After several arrivals close together, should it expect the same rate as after a long quiet period? Counting everything that has arrived since opening does not, on its own, tell us what is plausible right now.

The conditional intensity is the arrival rate that the model assigns to the next short interval, given the available history. Here it is expressed in reports per minute. The word conditional means that this rate depends on what is known; it can change when a report arrives or when information becomes too old.

Our scenario and all its parameters are fictional. We set a background rate of 0.50 reports/min and an increase of 0.25 reports/min for each arrival in the last 2 minutes. The window includes its earlier boundary and excludes the current instant. This rule helps us understand the rate; it has not been fitted to a real service.

An event can increase the rate in this model without guaranteeing another arrival. Reports close together may also have a common cause: their proximity does not demonstrate that they cause one another. Here we will learn to read and calculate a given intensity before studying how to choose its dependence on the past.

The rule to remember

Fix the information available before the instant of interest; calculate a rate in a stated unit of time; multiply by a short duration to obtain only a local probability approximation.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

What is the rate just before minute 10?

Since opening at minute 0, the only arrivals have been at minutes 7, 8.5, 9 and 9.5. We consider t = 10 min. With background rate b = 0.50 reports/min, increase a = 0.25 reports/min per retained arrival and window width w = 2 min, let us calculate the rate and its interpretation over h = 0.01 min.

  1. Define the count and the available history

    N(t) counts reports that have arrived by t, starting from opening; N(t−) counts them strictly before t. The − sign denotes the left-hand limit. Each arrival increases N by 1; N(0) = 0. The times t and tᵢ are in minutes; i numbers the arrivals. N is a count with no physical unit, expressed in reports.

    The filtration ℱ collects the information revealed over time. ℱt- denotes everything available strictly before t: earlier arrivals and known parameters. Predictable means here that the rate at t is determined by this information, before learning whether an event occurs at t. We assume that arrivals are observed immediately, with no delays or omissions. This assumption would need to be checked on real data.

    The process is simple: there are no simultaneous arrivals. It is nonexplosive: every interval of finite duration contains a finite number of arrivals. We work over a finite horizon with a finite expected number of arrivals; the intensity is nonnegative and its integral over this horizon has finite expectation. Stationarity is not required to define this intensity.

  2. Give the rate a precise meaning

    We write λ(t), lambda, for the intensity relative to this history. Its definition through compensation requires the count minus the cumulative rate to be a martingale. Here this word means that the future increment of M has conditional expectation 0, given the present information.

    A(t)=∫0tλ(s)ds;M(t)=N(t)−A(t)

    s is a time variable running through the integral. A(t), the compensator, is in reports, like N(t) and M(t). A follows the predicted rate; it is not the realized count. An intensity is defined up to changes on a set of times of measure 0; here we choose a predictable version that is continuous from the left.

    To read this rate as a local probability, we add the usual regularity conditions: at a regular instant, the rate varies sufficiently little over a shrinking interval, and the probability of multiple arrivals is negligible relative to its duration. Let ΔN = N(t + h) − N(t) be the number of arrivals in the future interval, open on the left and closed on the right, of positive duration h. Then, at the regular instants under consideration:

    ℙ(ΔN=1∣ℱt−)=λ(t)h+o(h)
    ℙ(ΔN≥2∣ℱt−)=o(h)

    ℙ means probability; the bar means “given”. Each remainder o(h) denotes a term whose quotient by h tends to 0 as h tends to 0 through positive values. These remainders are not necessarily identical. The probability of at least 1 event therefore has the same leading term λ(t)h. At the precise time of an arrival, we retain the prior rate to evaluate that arrival, then update the history to predict what follows.

  3. Select the arrivals that count under the rule

    R(t) denotes the number of retained arrivals, and w the window width. Our convention is t − w ≤ tᵢ < t. The earlier boundary is included; the current instant is excluded. This convention gives a version that is continuous from the left, including when arrivals leave the window.

    At t = 10 min, the earlier boundary is 10 − 2 = 8 min. The arrival at 7 min is 3 min old: it is too old. The following arrivals are respectively 1.5 min, 1 min and 0.5 min old. All lie within the window: R(t) = 3.

    Check using the boundaries in the diagram: 8 ≤ 8.5 < 9 < 9.5 < 10, whereas 7 < 8. The total count N(t−) is 4, but this is not the recent count R(t).

  4. Add the background and additional rates

    We specify the model by λ(t) = b + aR(t). b is the background rate; a is the increase in rate contributed by a retained arrival. R is a count, so aR and b have the same unit, reports per minute. These parameters are given; calculating with them here does not mean estimating them.

    λ(t)=12+14×3=1.25 signalement/min

    Each retained arrival adds 0.25 reports/min. The 3 arrivals contribute 0.75 reports/min; adding the background rate 0.50 gives λ(t) = 1.25 reports/min. The rate exceeds 1: this is allowed because it is not a probability.

    With no retained arrival, the rule would give the background rate b. After a new arrival, the future rate would increase; when arrivals leave the window, it would decrease. This positive dependence on the past is the model’s self-exciting mechanism. It is not evidence of causality between incidents.

  5. Move to the short interval while retaining the approximation

    We choose h = 1/100 min, or 0.01 min. With 60 seconds per minute, this duration is 0.01 × 60 = 0.6 s. The rate is per minute: we must multiply by the duration in minutes, not directly by the number of seconds.

    λ(t)h=1.25×0.01=0.0125

    The exact product is 1/80, or 0.0125, and is dimensionless. It is the leading term for the probability of at least 1 arrival over this short interval. Writing an approximate probability of 0.0125 assumes the stated local conditions. We have not bounded the remainder for this duration: the decimal places of the product do not guarantee the precision of the probability.

    Another way to check: the background rate contributes 0.0050 and the additional rate contributes 0.0075 to the product rate × duration. Their sum is 0.0125, as with direct multiplication. This equality checks the arithmetic; it does not measure the approximation error.

  6. Check what the result claims

    Since opening, the observed average rate is 4/10 = 0.40 reports/min. The current intensity is 1.25 reports/min. These values answer different questions: the observed average pace and the conditional rate given by the model.

    Neither 1.25 nor 0.0125 is a guaranteed number of arrivals. The future remains random. The homogeneous Poisson process would be the special case of a constant rate independent of past arrivals; our rule changes when the recent count changes.

    Over 1 min, the naive product would give 1.25, greater than 1. It cannot be a probability. Over a long interval, the relevant history evolves, and new arrivals can themselves change the rate. For a future expectation, we would need to consider the conditional expectation of the integral of the future rate, rather than automatically holding the current rate fixed.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the calculations. Reconstruct the window, the background rate, the additional rate and the unit of λ. Then explain why a rate can exceed unity while a probability cannot, and what we still do not know about the error for the short interval.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The intensity is 1.25, so it is an impossible probability.”

    1.25 is a rate in reports per minute. A probability is dimensionless; for a short duration, the leading term is rate × duration. Check the units before comparing the result with 1.

    Review the transition from the rate to the short interval

  • “To predict the arrival at t, I already include it in the history.”

    The rate used to evaluate this arrival must be determined before it occurs. Apply t − w ≤ tᵢ < t, then distinguish this rate from the one used just after the arrival. Including the event of interest uses the answer to predict it.

    Work through the exercise “An arrival exactly now”

  • “The count divided by elapsed time must give the current intensity.”

    The quotient 4/10 describes the entire observed period. The current rule retains only the last 2 minutes, then adds a background rate. With the same history, 0.40 and 1.25 reports/min therefore have different meanings.

    Identify the window and the excluded arrivals again

Exercise 1 · Application · let the history age

Wait without a new report

In the same scenario, suppose that no additional report arrives between 10 and 11.25 min. At t = 11.25 min, give the window, R(t), the intensity and the leading probability term for h = 0.01 min.

Shift both boundaries of the window; do not mechanically keep the old R.

Hint 2

The window starts at 9.25 min, inclusive. Test each time before using b + aR.

Compare with the solution

The window becomes 9.25 ≤ tᵢ < 11.25 min. Only the arrival at 9.5 min is retained; those at 8.5 and 9 min have left the window. R(t) = 1.

The additional rate is 0.25 × 1 = 0.25 reports/min. The rate is 0.50 + 0.25 = 0.75 reports/min. The local product is 0.75 × 0.01 = 0.0075, dimensionless.

The rate decreases without a new event because arrivals have aged. This instant is neither an arrival nor a window exit: the local interpretation of the product applies under the assumptions in the lesson. The total arrival count, however, does not decrease.

Exercise 2 · Reasoning · information and time order

An arrival exactly now

Start again from the initial history and add a report exactly at minute 10. A colleague includes it when calculating the rate that was supposed to evaluate its own arrival. Correct this approach, then calculate the rate just after this event and its product with h = 0.01 min.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Compare the condition tᵢ < t with tᵢ ≤ t. What information was available before the event?

Hint 2

After the arrival, and before any other arrival or window exit, R increases by 1.

Compare with the solution

The predictable rate at the instant of this arrival uses the 3 recent events strictly before it. It remains 1.25 reports/min. The event at t cannot be used to explain its own arrival rate.

Just after it, the updated history contains 4 recent arrivals. The additional rate is 0.25 × 4 = 1.00 reports/min, so the rate on the right is 0.50 + 1.00 = 1.50 reports/min.

Check: the jump in rate is 1.50 − 1.25 = 0.25 reports/min, exactly the increase a from an arrival. For a short interval after the event, the product is 0.0150, or 0.0025 more than before. This increase concerns a leading probability term; it does not guarantee another arrival.

Check your reasoning

  • Distinguish the rate before the event from the rate on the right, after the update.
  • Recover the increase a by subtracting the rates.
  • Do not interpret the jump in rate as evidence of causality or as a certain arrival.

Exercise 3 · Critique · defend a limited conclusion

An exact product, a false conclusion

A note about the same service claims: “At minute 10, λ = 1.25; therefore the probability over the next minute is exactly 1.25, and we will receive that number of reports.” Correct each part and give a numerical conclusion that the example actually supports.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Check, in order, the unit of the rate, the bounds of a probability and the integer nature of a realized count.

Hint 2

Return to h = 0.01 min. Separate the exactness of the product from the error in interpreting it as a probability.

Compare with the solution

The unit reports/min is missing after λ. The product 1.25 × 1 min is 1.25, but exceeds 1: it is not a probability. A formula valid to first order as h tends to 0 does not become an identity over a minute.

A realized count is an integer and is random. Even a correctly calculated expectation would not be a guaranteed number of arrivals. Over a finite duration, the rate can change after events or window exits; holding λ fixed does not automatically calculate the future conditional expectation.

A defensible conclusion: with the initial history and h = 0.01 min, the local leading term is exactly 1/80, or 0.0125. Under the local conditions, the probability is approximately equal to it; no numerical error bound has been provided. Displaying a value with several decimal places does not supply that evidence.

Check your reasoning

  • Correct the unit, the probability greater than 1 and the supposedly guaranteed count.
  • Give the exact local product and retain the word approximation for the probability.
  • Explain why a prediction over a minute requires accounting for changes in the rate.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What does λ(t) represent for the reports, and what is its unit?

The arrival rate predicted by the model, given the information available before t. Its unit is reports per minute. It can exceed 1 without being a probability.

Recall 2Why exclude an arrival exactly at t from R(t)?

The rate at t must be determined before this arrival. Our rule retains t − w ≤ tᵢ < t. The arrival at t updates the rate used afterwards, without retrospectively determining its own rate.

Recall 3What precise relationship links the count, the intensity and the compensator?

A(t) is the integral of λ since opening. Under the stated integrability assumptions, N(t) − A(t) is a martingale: its future increments have conditional expectation 0. The count N alone is not centered in this way.

Recall 4What does the product λ(t)h allow us to do, and what qualification must we state?

At a regular instant and under the local conditions, it is the leading term of the probability of at least 1 event in a short duration h. The product is exact; the probability includes a remainder o(h), for which no numerical bound is given here. At a known jump, we must distinguish the rate on the left from the rate on the right.

Recall 5Does a rate that rises after an arrival prove that the reports cause one another?

No. This is the dependence imposed by our model. A common cause, omissions or observation delays could produce clustering. A separate study would be needed to support a causal interpretation.

Define the available history before calculating a rate

What you understand
An intensity depends on the information available before the event of interest. The time of an event and the time when it becomes known may differ; a history reconstructed afterwards is not automatically the history available at the time of prediction.
