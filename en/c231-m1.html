Level 4 · C231·M1

Multilinearity and tensors

Your goalReconstruct a bilinear form from its values on basis vectors, then change its components without changing the calculated physical work.

Before you start
c127-m5 : Green and Stokes · c059-m6 : deterministic discrete Bellman · c143-m5 : uniform convergence · c049-m5 : series solutions · c165-m5 : duality

✿01 See the idea

A slider can move in a plane. You apply a constant force F during its displacement d. The work done by this force measures the energy it transfers through this displacement. A component pushing in the direction of motion contributes positively; a component opposing it contributes negatively.

Keep the displacement fixed and multiply the force by 2: its work is multiplied by 2. Keep the force fixed and multiply the displacement by 2: the effect is the same. If you change the 2 inputs at once, the factors multiply. The rule is linear in each input separately: this is bilinearity.

In an orthonormal coordinate system, whose axes are perpendicular and have the same spatial scale, our force is (3 ; 4) N and our displacement is (2 ; -1) m. The newton, denoted N, is the unit of force; the metre, denoted m, is the unit of length; the joule, denoted J, is the unit of work, with N·m = J. The diagram separates the contributions from the axes.

The scene and its values are fictional. We calculate the work done by this force alone, assumed constant throughout the displacement; we do not claim to calculate the work of all forces or a measured gain on a machine. The axes remain fixed. This unit explains the multilinear rule and its components; manifolds, metrics and geodesics come later.

The rule to remember

To recognize bilinearity, fix one input, then check linear combinations in the other. To recognize the tensor behind the numbers, reconstruct the same rule after a change of bases.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

From work done by a force to a bilinear tensor

For the same slider, let us calculate the work, then identify what depends on the choice of coordinates. We will use u for the normalized coordinates of the force, v for those of the displacement, and T for the rule that combines them.

  1. Separate quantities from their coordinates

    Choose a reference force Fref = 1 N and a reference length Lref = 1 m, both strictly positive. Define u = F/Fref and v = d/Lref. The components ux = 3, uᵧ = 4, vx = 2 and vᵧ = -1 are dimensionless. The subscripts x and y name the axes. The spaces U and V are copies of the real vector plane: U contains normalized forces and V contains normalized displacements.

    E is the reference energy Fref × Lref = 1 J. W denotes the work in joules. The rule T takes dimensionless real values; the factor E restores the physical unit. In the chosen orthonormal coordinate system, the work done by the constant force is the dot product of F and d:

    T(u,v)=uxvx+uyvy;W=ET(u,v)

    The scalar coefficients that will multiply u or v are therefore dimensionless. Adding forces, or adding displacements, remains meaningful; adding a force to a displacement does not.

  2. Prove linearity in each input

    A map is linear if it preserves combinations αa + βb, for all vectors a, b and all real numbers α, β. Here α and β are dimensionless. Fix v and choose another normalized force ũ in U; the tilde distinguishes this vector from u. By distributivity:

    T(αu+βu~,v)=(αux+βu~x)vx+(αuy+βu~y)vy
    T(αu+βu~,v)=αT(u,v)+βT(u~,v)

    For the other input, fix u and choose ṽ in V. Expanding ux(αvx + βṽx) + uᵧ(αvᵧ + βṽᵧ) gives α(uxvx + uᵧvᵧ) + β(uxṽx + uᵧṽᵧ), so T(u, αv + βṽ) = αT(u, v) + βT(u, ṽ). These identities for arbitrary inputs prove bilinearity; a few numerical trials would not suffice.

    In particular, T(0, v) = T(u, 0) = 0. The 0 symbol in an input denotes the 0 vector of its space; the 0 result is a real number. Finally, T(αu, βv) = αβT(u, v): being linear separately does not mean being linear in the whole pair.

  3. Name the tensor and its elementary product

    Multilinearity extends this definition: a map with several vector inputs is linear in each input when the others are fixed. In the case studied here, tensors are real multilinear forms on finite-dimensional spaces. The order counts the inputs; T has order 2. In general, the definition requires neither symmetry nor positivity. We do not cover all types of tensors here.

    Take the basis (ex, eᵧ) of U and the basis (hx, hᵧ) of V, both aligned with the initial axes. The dual space U* is the space of linear maps from U to the real numbers. Its dual basis (eˣ, eʸ) extracts coordinates: eˣ(u) = ux, eʸ(u) = uᵧ. Similarly, hˣ(v) = vx and hʸ(v) = vᵧ for the dual space V*. These superscripts name linear forms; they are not powers.

    If p belongs to U* and q to V*, their elementary tensor product p ⊗ q is defined by (p ⊗ q)(u, v) = p(u)q(v). The symbol ⊗ combines forms acting on distinct inputs; it does not denote a dot product. In finite dimensions, the space U* ⊗ V* is identified with the bilinear forms on U × V through sums of such products. Our rule is written as:

    T=ex⊗hx+ey⊗hy

    Evaluating the first term gives uxvx; evaluating the second gives uᵧvᵧ. A tensor can therefore be a sum of elementary products; we do not assume that it equals a single p ⊗ q.

  4. Find the components while preserving the rule

    For i and j chosen from the labels x and y, set Aᵢⱼ = T(eᵢ, hⱼ). These are the components of T in the chosen bases. Row i corresponds to the input in U, column j to the input in V. Here the matrix A and the calculation are written as:

    A=[1001];T(u,v)=utAv

    In this notation, u and v are coordinate columns, and the superscript t denotes the transpose: the column u becomes a row. All components of A are dimensionless. For example, T(ex, hx) equals 1 because these vectors have coordinates (1 ; 0), whereas T(ex, hᵧ) equals 0.

    Why is the matrix sufficient? Expand u = uxex + uᵧeᵧ and then v = vxhx + vᵧhᵧ using linearity in each input. You obtain T(u, v) = uxvxAxx + uxvᵧAxᵧ + uᵧvxAᵧx + uᵧvᵧAᵧᵧ. Each coefficient multiplies a value of T on a pair of basis vectors. This expansion proves that a bilinear form is entirely determined by these components.

    Conversely, any fixed matrix of this size defines a bilinear form through this expression. The array becomes a tensor representation when its spaces, its bases and the way its components change with them are specified.

  5. Calculate the work done on the slider

    Return to the dimensionless data u = (3 ; 4) and v = (2 ; -1). The horizontal contribution to T is uxvx = 3 × 2 = 6. The vertical contribution is uᵧvᵧ = 4 × (-1) = -4. The negative sign comes from opposite vertical directions; we do not take an absolute value.

    T(u,v)=6+(-4)=2

    To recover the physical work, multiply by E, which equals 1 J:

    W=ET(u,v)=1 J×2=2 J

    The positive result means that this force does positive work during this displacement. It gives neither the duration of the motion nor the complete energy balance of the slider.

  6. Check using a physical decomposition

    Decompose the actual force into Fx = (3 ; 0) N and Fᵧ = (0 ; 4) N, then calculate their work separately over the same displacement. Fx does 6 J of work; Fᵧ does -4 J. Their sum is 2 J. This check recovers the calculation without assigning a positive sign to every contribution.

    Unit check: each physical term is the product of a force in N and a displacement in m, so it is in J. Structure check: a 0 force would give 0 work; so would a 0 displacement. Multiplying only the force by 2 must give 4 J. You check this last point in the first exercise.

  7. Change the bases and preserve the tensor

    Different bases give different coordinates for the same vectors. Denote their new columns by u′ and v′. The invertible matrices P and Q are defined by u = Pu′ and v = Qv′; their columns are the new basis vectors expressed in the old bases. The prime means “new coordinates”, without changing the physical object. P and Q are dimensionless here.

    The rule T must assign the same real number to the same pair of vectors. Substituting for the old coordinates in uᵗAv gives u′ᵗPᵗAQv′. Its new matrix is therefore A′:

    A′=PtAQ;utAv=u′tA′v′

    The components change to compensate for the change of coordinates. Keeping A while replacing only u and v with their new coordinates would generally change the rule. The second exercise checks this compensation for our slider.

  8. State what has been established

    We have constructed a bilinear form, its decomposition in the dual bases and its matrix of components. The calculated work is 2 J under the stated assumptions alone. The initial identity matrix A comes from the choice of orthonormal axes; no universal identity for all tensor matrices follows from it.

    A series of measured values arranged in an array does not prove multilinearity. You must define the inputs, prove the identities and give a consistent interpretation of changes of bases. The third exercise shows that a simple constant offset is enough to break the property, even if the results still look like work values.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “Since it is bilinear, multiplying both inputs by 2 multiplies the result by 2.”

    First fix v: T(2u, v) = 2T(u, v). Then fix 2u and scale v: the second factor 2 is included by multiplication. The total factor is 4; in our scene, the work becomes 8 J.

    Revisit the proof in each input

  • “A tensor is its array of numbers; I can keep that array when changing basis.”

    The array A gives values on pairs of basis vectors. If these vectors change, those values change too: A′ = PᵗAQ under the convention u = Pu′ and v = Qv′. The work calculated for the same objects must remain identical.

    Check the change of basis for the slider

  • “Adding a constant offset cannot interfere with bilinearity.”

    A linear map must send the 0 vector to the scalar 0. If you add 1 J to the work, a 0 force still gives 1 J. The 0 test is enough to refute bilinearity here; passing this test alone would not be enough to prove it.

    Examine the offset of the device

Exercise 1 · Application · predict then calculate

Change one input, then the pair

For the same slider, multiply the force by 2 without changing the displacement. Predict the work, then recalculate it using components. Next, also multiply the displacement by 2. Give the new components and the work; explain why the rule is not linear in the pair treated as a single input.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Apply linearity in the force while keeping v fixed. For the second trial, apply linearity in the displacement separately.

Hint 2

In the direct calculation, each product of components then contains the 2 scaling factors.

Compare with the solution

Only the force is scaled: F becomes (6 ; 8) N. The predicted work is 2 × 2 = 4 J. Direct calculation gives 6 × 2 + 8 × (-1) = 4 J.

If the displacement also changes, d becomes (4 ; -2) m. The work is 6 × 4 + 8 × (-2) = 8 J. The total factor is 2 × 2 = 4.

In the space of pairs, multiplying (u, v) by 2 multiplies u and v simultaneously. Here the value of T is multiplied by 4, instead of the factor 2 required by a linear map of the pair. Separate linearity still holds.

Check your reasoning

  • You state the factor before doing the operations and distinguish the fixed inputs from the changed inputs.
  • You obtain 4 J and then 8 J, preserving the sign of the vertical component.
  • You explain the difference between separate linearity and linearity on the space of pairs.

Exercise 2 · Reconstruction · coordinates and components

A different force scale

The physical objects remain those of the example. In U, replace only eₓ with e′ₓ = 2eₓ and keep e′ᵧ = eᵧ. In V, keep hₓ and hᵧ. Find u′, then the values A′ᵢⱼ = T(e′ᵢ, hⱼ). Recalculate the work and also calculate the incorrect result obtained by keeping A. Give a check explaining which result corresponds to the same objects.

Hint 1

The first division on the force scale is 2 times as large; you must divide the number of divisions along that axis by 2. The displacement components remain the same.

Hint 2

For A′ₓₓ, use T(2eₓ, hₓ) = 2T(eₓ, hₓ). Do the same for the other entries. Here Q is the identity matrix.

Compare with the solution

The equality u = u′xe′x + u′ᵧe′ᵧ requires u′x = 3/2 = 3/2 = 1.50 and u′ᵧ = 4. These numbers remain dimensionless. The check 2 × 3/2 = 3 reconstructs the old force coordinate.

Pt=P=[2001];A′=[2001]

The new first entry is 2; the off-diagonal entries remain 0, and the last entry is 1. The product u′xvx is 3/2 × 2 = 3, and its contribution then becomes 2 × 3 = 6. The vertical contribution remains -4. Thus T is 2 and W = 1 × 2 = 2 J, as before.

Keeping A would give T = 3 + (-4) = -1, so W = -1 J. Since the physical displacement and force have not changed, this disagreement reveals an incorrect transformation of the components. A new scale division for the basis in U has not changed Fref or the physical newtons.

Check your reasoning

  • You divide the first coordinate of u and multiply the first row of A by 2.
  • You state that V and its coordinates remain identical and reconstruct the old force.
  • You recover 2 J and identify why -1 J comes from an outdated array.

Exercise 3 · Diagnosis · refute then repair

The offset of the device

A fictional device displays G(u, v) = E T(u, v) + c, where c = 1 J is a constant offset and E remains the reference energy. Is its rule bilinear? First look for a test with a 0 force; then compare G(2u, v) with 2G(u, v) using the slider data. Propose a repair and justify it for arbitrary inputs.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

A bilinear map is 0 whenever one of its inputs is the 0 vector.

Hint 2

The offset c is added after each work calculation. It does not automatically follow the factor multiplying the force.

Compare with the solution

For a 0 force, T(0, v) = 0; yet the device displays G(0, v) = 1 J. Since c is nonzero, the rule is not linear in the force, so it is not bilinear. This counterexample is enough to refute it.

For our initial pair, G(u, v) = 2 + 1 = 3 J. With the force scaled, G(2u, v) = 4 + 1 = 5 J. But 2G(u, v) = 2 × 3 = 6 J. The difference is 1 J, exactly the offset added once too many in the second expression.

If c is known and truly constant in this model, define H(u, v) = G(u, v) − c. Then H(u, v) = E T(u, v) for all inputs. The factor E is fixed, and T is bilinear by the proof, so H is bilinear too. This algebraic repair does not prove that a real device would have no other fault.

Check your reasoning

  • You give the counterexample with a 0 input and state that passing a 0 test would not be sufficient proof.
  • You distinguish 5 J from 6 J and explain the origin of 1 J.
  • You subtract the offset and justify the repair through an identity valid for all inputs.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1Which identities must be proved for a bilinear form T on U × V?

With v fixed, T(αu + βũ, v) = αT(u, v) + βT(ũ, v). With u fixed, T(u, αv + βṽ) = αT(u, v) + βT(u, ṽ), for all relevant vectors and real numbers. Testing a few values is not enough to prove these identities.

Recall 2Why does T(2u, 2v) equal 4T(u, v)?

Linearity in the force supplies a factor 2, then linearity in the displacement supplies the other. The factors multiply. A bilinear form is generally not linear in the pair treated as a single input.

Recall 3What does p ⊗ q mean, and how do we recover the tensor for the slider?

p and q are linear forms on the respective spaces; (p ⊗ q)(u, v) = p(u)q(v). The dual forms extract coordinates, so T = eˣ ⊗ hˣ + eʸ ⊗ hʸ. The order 2 counts the inputs, not the rows of an arbitrary array.

Recall 4What is Aᵢⱼ, and why is A′ = PᵗAQ?

Aᵢⱼ = T(eᵢ, hⱼ) gives the value on a pair of basis vectors. Bilinearity allows us to reconstruct T(u, v) = uᵗAv. With u = Pu′ and v = Qv′, substitution gives u′ᵗPᵗAQv′. The new matrix therefore compensates for the new coordinates.

Recall 5What units do u, v, T and W have? What does a constant offset reveal?

u = F/Fref, v = d/Lref and T(u, v) are dimensionless. E = Fref × Lref is in J; W = ET(u, v) is in J. Adding a nonzero constant offset makes the 0-input test fail and destroys bilinearity.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, reconstruct the path from force and displacement → normalized coordinates → linearity in each input → products of dual forms → values on basis vectors → matrix → work in joules. Then explain how A′ = PᵗAQ preserves the result, and invent a counterexample based on an offset using the same device.
