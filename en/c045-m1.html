Level 1 · C045·M1

Integration by parts

Your goalReconstruct integration by parts from the derivative of a product, then calculate and check the volume received by a tank.

Before you start
c034-m5 : average value · c010-m5 : logarithmic scales

✿01 See the idea

A pump fills a tank. Its flow rate starts at 0 L/min, increases, then returns to 0 L/min after 2 min. The final flow rate is 0, but the water poured in over that time has stayed in the tank. To find this volume, we need to accumulate the flow rate over the whole period.

Our flow rate model is the product of an increasing time and a decreasing factor. We will shift the work: differentiate the time factor, integrate the other factor, then adjust the resulting product. This rearrangement is called integration by parts. It comes from the product rule for differentiation.

In the flow rate diagram, the area under the curve represents the volume received. The calculation diagram previews the calculation: a term of 12 L from which we subtract 8 L. The terms in this calculation are mathematical constructions; they do not describe pouring water in and then draining it out.

The pump, its model and all the values are fictional. We assume the model is exact over the interval studied, with no leaks, no water leaving and no overflow. The calculation will give the volume added, whatever volume is already present.

The rule to remember

Differentiate one factor to simplify it, find an antiderivative of the other, then write the product evaluated at the limits minus the new integral. Check the result by differentiation.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Filling the tank over the full interval

Time t is measured from the start. During the interval from 0 to T = 2 min, the flow rate follows q(t) = c t(T − t), with c = 3 litres per minute cubed. Let us calculate the volume I added during this interval, justifying each transformation.

  1. Define the quantity and the assumptions

    t and T are durations in minutes. c is a constant coefficient, in litres per minute cubed. Since t(T − t) has units of minutes squared, q(t) is in litres per minute. I is the volume received, in litres. The symbol ∫ adds up the small contributions q(t) dt; dt represents the time element, in minutes. The limits specify the interval over which this accumulation takes place.

    I=∫0Tq(t) dt=∫0Tt·c(T−t) dt

    The model is polynomial, so it is continuous with a continuous derivative on this finite interval. The flow rate stays nonnegative because t and T − t are nonnegative throughout it. With our assumptions about the tank, this integral is the volume added.

  2. Choose what to differentiate and what to integrate

    We call the factor t u and the other factor v′. The prime denotes the derivative with respect to t; v will be an antiderivative of v′, meaning a function whose derivative gives v′. Choosing u = t replaces this factor with the constant 1 when we differentiate it.

    u(t)=t;u′(t)=1;v′(t)=c(T−t)
    v(t)=c(Tt−t22)

    To obtain v, we integrate the constant term cT to get cTt. The antiderivative of −ct is −c multiplied by t squared, all divided by 2. Differentiating this expression gives cT − ct = c(T − t). We choose the additive constant to be 0 to simplify the notation.

    u is in minutes, u′ is dimensionless; v′ is in litres per minute squared and v is in litres per minute. Thus, uv is in litres and the integral of u′v is also in litres. The new integral contains only the polynomial v; the outer factor t has disappeared.

  3. Recover the minus sign from the derivative of the product

    For functions u and v whose derivatives are continuous on a closed interval [a, b], with a < b, the derivative of the product is the sum u′v + uv′. a and b are the time limits, in minutes. Let us integrate this equality and then isolate the required term uv′.

    (uv)′=u′v+uv′
    ∫abuv′ dt=[uv]ab−∫abu′v dt

    The brackets [uv] evaluated from a to b mean u(b)v(b) − u(a)v(a), end minus start. The fundamental theorem replaces the integral of the derivative of the product with this difference. The minus sign comes from moving the integral of u′v to the other side of the equality.

    We also encounter the notation d u = u′(t) dt and d v = v′(t) dt. With the same limits, the identity then reads: the integral of u d v equals the product [uv] evaluated from a to b minus the integral of v d u. Here, we keep the derivatives and the limits visible in the formula.

  4. Evaluate the product at the limits

    Let B be the difference [uv] evaluated from 0 to T. With the stated units, cT is 6 litres per minute squared and c/2 is exactly 3/2, or 1.50 litres per minute cubed. At the start, u(0) and v(0) are 0: the product is 0 L.

    At the end, T squared is 4 minutes squared. The linear contribution to v(T) is 6 × 2 = 12 L/min; the quadratic contribution is (3/2) × 4 = 6 L/min. Their difference gives v(T) = 6 L/min.

    B=u(T)v(T)−u(0)v(0)=2×6−0=12 L

    The flow rate q(T) is 0 because v′(T) is 0. This does not make v(T) 0: v(T) is the value of an antiderivative. B therefore equals 12 L, but it is not yet the required volume.

  5. Calculate the integral to subtract

    Let R be the integral of u′v from 0 to T. Since u′ = 1, we still need to integrate v. Let A be an antiderivative of v, in litres. We increase each exponent by 1 and divide by the new exponent:

    A(t)=cT2t2−c2×3t3

    The coefficient of the squared term is cT/2 = 3 litres per minute squared. The coefficient of the cubed term is (c/2)/3 = 1/2, or 0.50 litre per minute cubed. Their units ensure that each term in A is a volume. Differentiating A gives v.

    T cubed is 8 minutes cubed. At the end, the contributions are 3 × 4 = 12 L and (1/2) × 8 = 4 L. At the start, A(0) = 0 L.

    R=A(T)−A(0)=(12−4)−0=8 L

  6. Combine the terms in litres

    I=B−R=12−8=4 L

    The tank received 4 L over 2 min. The 12 L in the boundary term included the other accumulation; subtracting 8 L isolates exactly the original integral. All the terms have the same unit. The result is positive, which is consistent with the nonnegative flow rate, but this sign alone does not prove its value.

  7. Check using an antiderivative obtained another way

    Now expand the flow rate: from the term cTt, subtract c multiplied by t squared. We can integrate this polynomial directly and call the resulting antiderivative F, in litres. This check starts again from the original model without using the subtraction B − R.

    F(t)=cT2t2−c3t3;F′(t)=cTt−ct2=q(t)

    In the derivative of the squared term, the factor 2 cancels its divisor; for the cubed term, the factor 3 cancels its divisor. This verifies the antiderivative for every t in the interval, rather than just comparing a few values.

    The coefficient of the cubed term in F is c/3 = 1 litre per minute cubed. Therefore F(T) − F(0) = 12 − 8 − 0 = 4 L. The difference from the result obtained by parts is 0 L. We can also verify algebraically that uv − A = F.

    For this polynomial, expanding would have been enough from the start. The factored form was chosen to make the choice of u, the antiderivative v, the boundary term and the adjustment visible. To choose the method, ask yourself whether the new integral is simpler and whether you know how to calculate it.

Explain it in your own words

Hide the equations. Recover the minus sign from the derivative of the product, explain the units of u and v, then justify why the final flow rate being 0 does not make B 0. Then reconstruct the volume without consulting the result.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I take an antiderivative of each factor and multiply them.”

    The derivative of a product has 2 terms. Multiplying antiderivatives therefore does not automatically give back the original product. Go through (uv)′ = u′v + uv′ again, then isolate the integral of uv′: subtracting the other term gives the correct identity.

    Reconstruct the identity before making substitutions

  • “The final flow rate is 0, so the product at the limits is 0.”

    The flow rate is uv′, whereas the brackets apply to uv. Here v′(T) is 0, but v(T) is 6 L/min. The final product is 2 min × 6 L/min = 12 L. Read the names again before substituting the values.

    Distinguish the flow rate curve from the calculation

  • “For the brackets, I replace t only with the upper limit.”

    The brackets always mean the value of the product at the end minus its value at the start. The 0 starting value hid the second term in the example. For the interval starting at 1 min, we need to subtract 9/2 L from the final product and also evaluate the antiderivative A at both limits.

    Test an interval with a nonzero starting time

Exercise 1 · Warm-up · a shorter interval

Stop the pump before the end

Using exactly the same model q(t) = c t(T − t), calculate the volume received between 0 and 1 min. The parameter T stays at 2 min: only the observation limit changes. Keep u = t and the antiderivative v from the example. Write the product at the limits and the integral to subtract before subtracting.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

At the new limit, the time squared is 1 minute squared and its cube is 1 minute cubed.

Hint 2

Calculate v, then the product uv. For the other integral, use A at this same limit.

Compare with the solution

v(1) = 6 × 1 − (3/2) × 1 = 6 − 3/2 = 9/2 L/min, or 4.50 L/min. The final product is 1 × (9/2) = 9/2 L. The initial product is 0.

A(1) = 3 × 1 − (1/2) × 1 = 3 − 1/2 = 5/2 L, or 2.50 L. Since A(0) is 0, this value is the integral to subtract.

The volume is therefore 9/2 − 5/2 = 2 L. Check with F: the quadratic term is 3 L and the cubic term is 1 × 1 = 1 L; their difference gives 2 L again.

Check your reasoning

  • Keep T = 2 min in the flow rate model.
  • Distinguish v, in L/min, from the product uv, in litres.
  • Show the exact values 9/2 L and 5/2 L, then the volume 2 L.
  • Check with the antiderivative F.

Exercise 2 · Application · both limits count

Count only the end of the filling process

Now calculate the volume received between 1 and 2 min. Someone keeps B = 12 L because they substitute only the upper limit. Explain the error, then calculate the difference at the limits for uv and for A. Finally, verify that the volumes over successive intervals give the full filling volume.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The value of the product at 1 min is 9/2 L, already calculated in the previous exercise.

Hint 2

The integral to subtract is A(2) − A(1). It is no longer simply A(2).

Compare with the solution

The boundary term is 12 − 9/2 = 15/2 L, or 7.50 L. The person counted the product at the end without subtracting the product at the start.

The integral of u′v is 8 − 5/2 = 11/2 L, or 5.50 L. We then subtract the complete results: (15/2) − (11/2) = 2 L.

Independent check: F(2) − F(1) = 4 − 2 = 2 L. Finally, 2 + 2 = 4 L: the intervals meet at the same limit without overlapping, and their sum gives the full volume.

Check your reasoning

  • Evaluate uv and A at each limit, keeping the order end minus start.
  • Put parentheses around the differences before the final subtraction.
  • Obtain 2 L and justify the check using successive intervals.

Exercise 3 · Transfer · defend the identity

Does another antiderivative give a different filling volume?

For the full interval from 0 to 2 min, another person chooses ṽ(t) = v(t) + C, with C = 2 L/min constant. They claim that the result must increase because ṽ is larger. Answer without changing the flow rate: check the derivative, recalculate the product at the limits and the integral to subtract, then explain the cancellation using symbols.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The derivative of C is 0. An extra term is therefore added to the product at the limits, but also to the integral of u′v.

Hint 2

Here u = t and u′ = 1. The addition to the integral is C multiplied by the duration of the interval.

Compare with the solution

The derivative of ṽ is v′, because C is constant. The product uv′ and therefore the flow rate stay the same. At the end, ṽ(T) = 6 + 2 = 8 L/min; at the start, u(0) is 0, even though ṽ(0) equals C.

The new boundary term is 2 × 8 − 0 = 16 L. The integral of u′ṽ gains the addition C × T = 2 × 2 = 4 L; it therefore equals 8 + 4 = 12 L. The final calculation gives 16 − 12 = 4 L, as before.

Using symbols, the addition to the boundary term is [Cu] evaluated from a to b, or C(u(b) − u(a)). The addition to the other integral is C times the integral of u′, so it is the same difference C(u(b) − u(a)). These additions cancel in the subtraction. Changing the constant of the antiderivative changes the intermediate terms without changing the original integral.

Check your reasoning

  • Justify that the flow rate is preserved by the derivative of ṽ.
  • Calculate 16 L and 12 L before recovering 4 L.
  • Explain the cancellation of C in both terms, beyond the numerical equality alone.
  • Do not interpret the change of antiderivative as an action on the pump.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1How can you recover integration by parts and its minus sign?

Start from (uv)′ = u′v + uv′, integrate from a to b, then isolate the integral of uv′: it equals [uv] evaluated from a to b minus the integral of u′v. Continuous derivatives on the closed interval are a sufficient assumption here.

Recall 2In the tank example, what do we choose for u and v′, and what do we gain?

u = t, so u′ = 1; v′ = c(T − t), so we can take v = c(Tt − t squared divided by 2). The outer factor t disappears from the new integral. The choice must allow us to calculate that integral; the presence of a product is not enough to require this method.

Recall 3Which units confirm that I = B − R is consistent?

u is in minutes; v is in L/min; uv is therefore in litres. u′ is dimensionless and u′v dt is also in litres. The product at the limits B, the integral R and the volume I therefore have the same unit.

Recall 4What do the brackets [uv] mean, and why does the final flow rate being 0 not make their value 0?

They mean u(b)v(b) − u(a)v(a). The flow rate is uv′; the brackets apply to uv. Over our full interval, q(T) is 0 but v(T) is 6 L/min and the product at the limits is 12 L.

Recall 5Why does adding a constant to v leave the integral unchanged?

The derivative v′ does not change. The boundary term and the integral to subtract both increase by C(u(b) − u(a)); these additions cancel. The volume of 4 L stays the same.

Method guide · state the model before accumulating

What you understand
A rate multiplied by a duration describes an accumulation when the rate is constant. For the pump on this page, the flow rate varies: we integrate its model. Integration by parts rearranges this integral without changing the physical model.
