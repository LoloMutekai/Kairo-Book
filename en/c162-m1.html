Formal practices

Your goalDescribe a formal session of attention to the breath, work through the act of returning, and distinguish reported practice, subjective experience, and evidence of effectiveness.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

Camille sits down before studying. She chooses to feel the air passing through her nostrils. Soon, she catches herself mentally preparing her next assignment. She notices this thought, sets her planning aside, and returns to the sensation of the breath happening now. This return is the action we will describe.

A practice is formal when time is set aside for it, with a beginning, an end, and an explicit instruction. The word implies neither an unusual posture nor a necessarily long session. Informal practice means bringing this quality of attention into an ordinary activity; here, Camille sets aside a time slot for the exercise itself.

Here, voluntary attention means directing attention toward a chosen object. The anchor is the concrete object to return to: for Camille, the sensation of air at her nostrils. Mindfulness adds a way of encountering whatever arises: noticing present experience without immediately treating a distraction as a fault to punish. Attention to the breath is one form of practice, rather than a definition of all formal practices.

Observing a sensation, scanning the sensations of the body, and allowing whatever events arise involve different instructions. We follow only the focused form: keeping a chosen anchor and returning to it after noticing that attention has wandered. We do not develop a programme of multiple sessions, measure attentional abilities, or offer a contemplative interpretation here.

Camille and the data are fictional. Her 3 min time slot makes it possible to check the sequence on paper; it is neither an optimal duration nor a prescription. You can understand the scene without doing the exercise. If you choose to do it, you remain free to stop if you feel uncomfortable.

The rule to remember

Set aside a time frame, choose a sensation, notice when attention wanders, return without scolding yourself, then describe what happened. The length of the time frame and subjective experience do not measure effectiveness.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Camille’s session sheet

Camille plans 30 s to settle in, 120 s to follow the instruction, and 30 s to finish. The chosen durations are fictional. We calculate the schedule for this session, then describe a moment in the practice; no psychological outcome is calculated.

  1. Define what the numbers count

    I is the settling-in duration, O the duration set aside for observation, and C the closing duration. T is the total duration of the time slot. All these quantities are in seconds, abbreviated s. The letter t denotes the time elapsed since the start; it is also read in seconds. A duration tells us how long a stage takes; t locates its boundary within the time slot.

    We assume successive stages, with no overlap or added pause. The clock starts at t = 0 s. Time spent noticing wandering and returning belongs to O. O must therefore not be identified with a duration of uninterrupted attention. An actual interruption would require a description of what was actually done.

  2. Set up an instruction that can be followed

    During I = 30 s, Camille sits on a chair, with her feet resting on the floor, in a posture she can maintain. She keeps her eyes open with her gaze resting in place. She gives herself this instruction: “feel the air passing through my nostrils; if I notice that my attention is elsewhere, return to this sensation”. A signal set in advance marks the end of the window; she does not have to check the clock with every breath.

    The sensation is the object; breathing remains spontaneous. Camille does not try to slow it down or lengthen her exhalations. Nor does she seek a striking sensation: cooler or warmer air, if perceptible, is enough as a reference point. These details make the instruction observable to her; they do not guarantee any particular state.

  3. Calculate the boundaries before starting

    Observation begins after settling in, at t = 30 s. To find its end, add the time already elapsed, I, to its duration, O: 30 + 120 = 150 s. To find the end of the whole time slot, then add C. Addition is appropriate because these stages follow one another without overlapping.

    T=I+O+C
    T=30+120+30=180 s

    A minute contains 60 s. To convert a duration into minutes, divide its number of seconds by 60. Settling in: 1/2 min, or 0.5 min; observation: 2 min; closing: 0.5 min. The whole time slot is 180 ÷ 60 = 3 min.

  4. Work through a return without restarting the session

    Between t = 30 s and t = 150 s, Camille feels the air passing. She then finds herself planning her assignment. Here, noticing means recognising this shift of attention; she does not need to recover the exact moment when it began.

    She can briefly label it “thought”, without analysing the assignment or arguing against it. Then she sets the content aside and returns to the sensation of the breath that is here now. She does not wait for the next inhalation before allowing herself to return. If she later notices that attention has wandered again, she applies the same instruction.

    The complete action therefore includes noticing and redirecting attention. Merely saying “I am distracted” while continuing to plan the assignment leaves out the return. Scolding herself at length adds another activity. Camille reconnects with the anchor without resetting the clock to the beginning; returns are part of the planned window.

  5. Close the window and write a modest record

    At t = 150 s, Camille leaves the focusing instruction behind. During C = 30 s, she broadens her attention to the room, notices the points supporting her body again, and finishes the time slot at t = 180 s. This stage gives the exercise an explicit ending.

    One possible fictional note would be: “I noticed that I was planning my assignment, then returned to the air at my nostrils; I still feel restless.” She distinguishes an action she reports from a feeling at that moment. Restlessness is not enough to say that the instruction was not followed; a feeling of calm would likewise not be enough to demonstrate improved attention.

  6. Check the time budget and what it establishes

    Check by subtraction: settling in and closing take 30 + 30 = 60 s. That leaves 180 − 60 = 120 s for O, as stated. This is another way to recover the window without merely repeating the same addition.

    After settling in alone, the remaining time is 180 − 30 = 150 s. This remainder still includes closing: assigning all of it to observation would remove closing. Checking in the other unit gives 0.5 + 2.0 + 0.5 = 3.0 min.

    These operations check the consistency of a session plan. An end signal tells us about elapsed time, rather than the continuous presence of attention. Describing the return helps explain the instruction; it establishes neither a lasting skill nor an effect on studying. Methods for evaluating effects belong to other units.

Explain it in your own words

Cover the page. Describe the moment when Camille thinks about the assignment, and act out the return to the anchor in words. Then reconstruct the time budget, naming what each duration includes. Why can an accurate clock not certify the quality of this practice?

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “A thought appears: my session has failed.”

    In this instruction, a noticed thought provides an opportunity to return to the chosen sensation. Describe the noticing and the return instead of judging the session solely by the presence of thoughts. An absence of reported thoughts does not demonstrate continuous attention either.

    Review the return loop

  • “To follow the breath properly, I must make it slow and regular.”

    Return to Camille’s exact instruction: feel spontaneous breathing at the nostrils. Deliberately changing its rhythm would introduce another task. The reference point is a perceived sensation, rather than a target number of breaths.

    Reread settling in and the shift to the assignment

  • “I kept going for 3 min and feel calm: my attention has improved.”

    The time slot includes 60 s of settling in and closing; even the observation window does not measure continuous attention. Calm is a feeling. Restate the time frame, reported actions, and subjective experience separately before drawing any conclusion about a lasting effect.

Exercise 2 · Calculation and checking

Recalculate the same sheet

In a fictional variation of Camille’s sheet, add 60 s to O and keep I and C. Calculate the new end of observation and the total in seconds and minutes, then recover O by subtraction. This variation is for calculation; it does not recommend lengthening a session.

Hint 1

First calculate the new observation duration. Its end boundary also includes the settling-in time.

Hint 2

To check the result, subtract everything outside the observation window from the total.

Compare with the solution

Observation becomes 120 + 60 = 180 s. It ends at 30 + 180 = 210 s after the start. A duration and its end boundary differ because settling in precedes the window.

The total is 210 + 30 = 240 s, or 240 ÷ 60 = 4 min. Another route gives the same result: the previous total of 180 s increased by 60 s also gives 240 s.

Reverse check: 240 − (30 + 30) = 180 s. This recovers the new observation duration. The result describes a schedule; no duration of actual attention has been deduced.

Check your reasoning

  • You separate the duration of 180 s from the boundary at 210 s.
  • You obtain 240 s, or 4 min, while keeping closing.
  • You present the subtraction check with its units.
  • You do not turn the planned duration into a validated dose or an attention score.

Compare with the solution

Time frame: the sheet reports the end of the time slot. Action: Camille says she noticed a thought, then returned to the anchor. Feeling: she says she is calm at the time of the note. This information does not establish that she remained attentive throughout O.

A proportionate statement would be: “I finished the planned time slot. I report noticing the thought about the assignment, then returning to the air at my nostrils. I feel calm now; the effect on my studying remains to be established.” The reported return describes an application of the instruction without constituting an independent measurement.

The restless version of the note in the example and this calm version are fictional variations of the same case, rather than compared observations. Neither their contrast nor the time calculation demonstrates a benefit. Describing a practice correctly remains distinct from evaluating its effects.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What makes Camille’s session formal?

She sets aside time for practice, with an instruction, a beginning, and an end. Its formal nature comes from this explicit structure, rather than a supposedly ideal duration or an unusual posture.

Recall 2What is the chosen anchor, and how does Camille use it?

The sensation of air at her nostrils. She directs attention to it while allowing breathing to remain spontaneous, and returns to it after noticing wandering. Breathing is not a rhythm to get right.

Recall 3Recall the complete action after the thought about the assignment.

Notice that attention is occupied with the assignment, optionally label it thought, set the content aside, and return to the sensation of the breath happening now. Continue without scolding yourself or restarting the timer.

Recall 4Why is the 3 min time slot not a measure of continuous concentration?

It includes 30 s of settling in, 120 s of observation, and 30 s of closing. The observation window includes wandering and returns; the clock measures elapsed time, rather than the quality of attention.

Recall 5What can we conclude from the calm reported at the end?

Camille says she is calm at that moment. This demonstrates neither continuous attention during the session nor lasting improvement or an effect on studying. A description of practice and evidence of effectiveness answer different questions.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Without rereading, draw the structure of the session and the return loop. Reconstruct the durations and their check. Then explain why a noticed thought belongs to the proposed work, and why finishing the time slot or feeling calm is not enough to demonstrate an effect.
