Level 4 · C235·M4

Derivative

Your goalCalculate the Malliavin derivative of a simple functional of two accumulations of Gaussian noise, read it as a random function of time, check it using a centered difference, and specify the domain on which the formula holds.

Before you start

✿01 See the idea

A fictional sensor is subject to Gaussian white noise over two successive time intervals. Call the noise accumulated over the first interval ξ1 and that over the second ξ2. A fictional, dimensionless quantity, F = ξ1² + 3 ξ1 ξ2, depends on these two accumulations. Once the noise has been sampled, F is just a number. The question in this unit is: if we shifted the noise slightly, how much would F change?

For an ordinary function f of x, the derivative tells us how much f changes when we shift x. Here, what we shift is the whole noise, a function of time, rather than a number. We shift it by an amount ε in a direction h, itself a square integrable function of time. The admissible directions belong to the space H defined in the example.

The Malliavin derivative, written D, collects all these sensitivities into a single object: a function of time that depends on the noise sample. Its dot product with a direction h gives the number we want: the sensitivity of F to a shift in the direction h. For the functional that simply reads the noise in the direction h, the derivative is the direction itself; for a polynomial in these noise readings, it is the ordinary derivative of the polynomial, combined with its directions.

This is not a derivative with respect to time: the value DF(s) at time s measures the influence of the noise around s on F, rather than the slope of a path. The numbers in this scenario are fictional: they illustrate the calculation and describe neither a real sensor nor a recorded reading.

The course is restricted to simple functionals that are polynomials in finitely many noise accumulations. Extending D to less regular functionals requires an additional construction, which this unit does not teach.

The rule to remember

DF is the sum of the ordinary partial derivatives of f, evaluated at the noise accumulations, each attached to the direction that produced that accumulation; in particular, the derivative of the noise read in the direction h is h.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Differentiate F = ξ₁² + 3 ξ₁ ξ₂ along the noise

In a fictional scenario, the noise has been sampled: ξ₁ = 2 and ξ₂ = −1. Calculate the Malliavin derivative of F = ξ₁² + 3 ξ₁ ξ₂ at this sample, read it as a function of time, measure its size, apply it to a direction, then check the result without using its formula.

  1. Set out the framework, symbols, and assumptions

    Fictional time runs from 0 to 2. H denotes the set of square integrable functions g on [0 ; 2], equipped with the dot product ⟨g, k⟩ = ∫ g(s) k(s) ds and the norm ‖g‖, with ‖g‖² = ⟨g, g⟩. A direction is an element h of H.

    W is Gaussian white noise: it associates each g in H with a real random variable W(g), dimensionless here. Assumptions from the prerequisite on Gaussian noise, recalled without proof: W(g) is linear in g, centered, Gaussian, and has variance ‖g‖²; if ⟨g, k⟩ = 0, then W(g) and W(k) are independent.

    We take h1 equal to 1 on [0 ; 1] and 0 elsewhere, and h2 equal to 1 on ]1 ; 2] and 0 elsewhere. Their supports are disjoint: ⟨h1, h2⟩ = 0, and ‖h1‖² = ‖h2‖² = 1. Thus ξ1 = W(h1) and ξ2 = W(h2) are two independent variables with the standard normal distribution.

    A simple functional is a random variable F = f(W(h1), …, W(hn)) where f is a polynomial in n variables. Here n = 2 and f(x1, x2) = x1² + 3 x1 x2. The partial derivatives ∂1f and ∂2f are the ordinary derivatives of f with respect to its first and then its second variable, holding the other fixed.

    Definition of the Malliavin derivative of a simple functional, summed over indices i from 1 to n:

    DF=∑i=1n∂if(W(h1),…,W(hn))hi

    This is an H-valued random variable. The special case where f is just the first variable gives the derivative of the noise read in the direction h:

    DW(h)=h

    Duality rule, stated without proof: for any simple functional F and any direction h in H, the expectation of ⟨DF, h⟩ equals that of F W(h). Exercise three checks it in one case.

    E[⟨DF,h⟩]=E[FW(h)]

    Limits: f is assumed to be a polynomial. We accept without proof that DF does not depend on how F is written using the variables W(h1), …, W(hn). Extending D to less regular functionals involves taking the closure of the operator, which is not covered here: the formula does not apply as it stands to a nondifferentiable functional such as an indicator function.

  2. Evaluate F at the fictional sample

    Data: ξ1 = 2 and ξ2 = −1. Reason: DF will be evaluated at a specific sample; knowing the value of F then lets us say how much it changes.

    F=ξ12+3ξ1ξ2=(2)2+3×2×(−1)=4−6=−2

    Check: the cross term is negative because ξ2 is negative; F is the difference 4 − 6 between two contributions of opposite signs, giving −2.

  3. Differentiate f, then attach the directions

    Given: f(x1, x2) = x1² + 3 x1 x2. Reason: the definition first requires the ordinary partial derivatives of f, then their evaluation at the sample.

    ∂1f(x1,x2)=2x1+3x2
    ∂2f(x1,x2)=3x1

    Evaluation at the fictional sample:

    ∂1f=2×2+3×(−1)=4−3=1
    ∂2f=3×2=6

    Each coefficient becomes the coefficient of its direction:

    DF=1h1+6h2

    Check: if f is just the first variable, its partial derivative is 1 and we recover D W(h1) = h1. The coefficients 1 and 6 hold only for this sample; another sample of ξ1 and ξ2 would give different coefficients.

  4. Read DF as a function of time and measure its size

    Result: DF is the function of time equal to 1 on [0 ; 1] and 6 on ]1 ; 2], as in the second diagram. It is random: another sample would change these two values.

    Its size is its norm in H. When we expand the squared norm, the cross term contains ⟨h1, h2⟩ = 0 and vanishes; ‖h1‖² and ‖h2‖² each equal 1.

    ‖DF‖2=(1)2+(6)2=1+36=37

    Check: adding the coefficients would give 7, which is not the squared norm in H; the correct quantity is 37. Everything here is dimensionless.

  5. Apply a direction

    Given: the direction h = 1 h1 − 1 h2, meaning c1 = 1 and c2 = −1. Reason: the dot product of DF with h is a single number, the sensitivity of F to a shift in this direction. By orthogonality of h1 and h2, whose squared norms equal 1, ⟨DF, h⟩ = c1 d1 + c2 d2, where d1 = 1 and d2 = 6 are the coefficients of DF.

    ⟨DF,h⟩=c1d1+c2d2=1×1+(−1)×6=1−6=−5

    Interpretation: shifting the noise by ε in this direction adds ε c1 to ξ1 and ε c2 to ξ2, since ⟨h1, h⟩ = c1 and ⟨h2, h⟩ = c2. To first order in ε, F therefore changes by −5 ε.

  6. Check using a centered difference

    The centered difference quotient does not differentiate the formula: it simply evaluates F at two points near the sample. For a polynomial of degree two, it is exact for any ε, because the higher order terms, which alone cause the error, are absent. We take ε = 1 to keep integer values; this would not justify claiming exactness for a polynomial of higher degree.

    Point shifted forward, ξ + εc = (3, −2). The product 3 × 3 is 9, then 9 × 2 is 18:

    F(3,−2)=32+3×3×(−2)=9−18=−9

    Point shifted backward, ξ − εc = (1, 0):

    F(1,0)=12+3×1×0=1+0=1

    The centered difference quotient is then:

    F(ξ+εc)−F(ξ−εc)2ε=−9−12×1=−102=−5

    Result: −5, identical to ⟨DF, h⟩ = −5. Limit of the check: it checks one combination of the two coefficients; a direction consisting of h1 alone would check d1 separately, and exercise two applies this idea, for h2 alone, to another functional. The same quotient, applied coordinate by coordinate to a function of real numbers, is what the milestone at the end of the page examines; it does not become a Malliavin derivative there.

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “The Malliavin derivative is a number, like an ordinary derivative.”

    DF is an element of H: here a random function of time, equal to 1 on the first interval and 6 on the second. A number appears only after taking a dot product with a direction h, for example −5 for h = h₁ − h₂.

    Review how to read DF in the example

  • “If DF vanishes on an interval, F does not depend on the noise from that interval.”

    Vanishing is local to the sample: the partial derivative ∂₂K = x₁ + 2 x₂ equals 0 for ξ = (2, −1) but 2 for ξ = (2, 0). K does depend on ξ₂.

    Redo the exercise on local vanishing

  • “‖DF‖² is the square of the sum of the coefficients of DF.”

    The norm is the norm in H. The directions h₁ and h₂ are orthogonal: the cross terms vanish, leaving the sum of squares, 37, rather than the sum of coefficients, 7, or its square. Without orthogonality, these cross terms would reappear.

    Review the norm calculation

Exercise 1 · Direct application

A linear functional

Using the same h₁ and h₂, let G be as given below (fictional coefficients). Calculate DG, then ‖DG‖². Then compare with the variance of G, obtained using independence of the ξ variables and variance 1 for each.

G=3W(h1)−4W(h2)
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

G can be written as f(W(h₁), W(h₂)) with f affine: its partial derivatives are constants.

Hint 2

As in the example, h₁ and h₂ are orthogonal and have squared norm 1: the squared norm of DG is the sum of the squares of its coefficients.

Hint 3

For the variance, add the squared coefficients multiplied by the variance of each ξ; the cross term is 0 because of independence and centering.

Compare with the solution

The function is f(x1, x2) = 3 x1 − 4 x2. Its partial derivatives are 3 and −4, with no ξ: DG = 3 h1 − 4 h2 is the same function of time for every sample.

Norm: ‖DG‖² = 9 + 16 = 25. Variance: 3² × 1 + 4² × 1 = 9 + 16 = 25, since the cross term is 0. The two methods agree because G is linear; do not assume this equality for the functional F in the example.

Check your reasoning

  • The partial derivatives are constants; DG does not depend on the sample.
  • The squared norm is a sum of squares, thanks to orthogonality.
  • The variance is calculated by another method, without using D, and compared.

Exercise 2 · Application with a check

A derivative that vanishes locally

At the same sample (ξ₁ = 2, ξ₂ = −1), let K be as given below. Calculate DK and ‖DK‖², check the second partial derivative using a centered difference quotient with step size 1 in the direction h₂ alone, then say whether K is insensitive to the noise from the second interval.

K=ξ1ξ2+ξ22
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

f(x₁, x₂) = x₁ x₂ + x₂². Differentiate each term while holding the other variable fixed.

Hint 2

For the direction h₂ alone, c₁ = 0 and c₂ = 1: shift ξ₂ by +1, then by −1, and keep ξ₁ unchanged.

Hint 3

To conclude, also calculate ∂₂K at a sample where ξ₂ = 0.

Compare with the solution

∂1K = x2 equals −1; ∂2K = x1 + 2 x2 equals 2 − 2 = 0. Thus DK = −1 h1 + 0 h2 and ‖DK‖² = 1 + 0 = 1.

Check with step size 1: K(2, 0) = 2 × 0 + 0² = 0; K(2, −2) = 2 × (−2) + (−2)² = −4 + 4 = 0. The quotient is (0 − 0) / 2 = 0, matching ∂2K. The step size 1 gives an exact result because K has degree two.

Conclusion: K is not insensitive to ξ2. When ξ2 = 0, ∂2K = x1 = 2, a number different from 0. At the original sample, ξ2 is simply a point where K, viewed as a function of ξ2, is stationary: the vanishing of DK on an interval is local information.

Check your reasoning

  • ∂₂K is calculated with two terms, one per monomial.
  • The check evaluates K at two points and does not reuse the derivative formula.
  • The conclusion distinguishes vanishing at one sample from independence of ξ₂.

Exercise 3 · Checking a rule

Duality in one case

Let C be as given below and take the direction h = h₁. For ξ₁ = W(h₁) with the standard normal distribution, we are given: the moment of order 2 is 1 and the moment of order 4 is 3. Calculate DC, then the two expectations E of ⟨DC, h₁⟩ and of C W(h₁), and compare. This equality illustrates, in one case, the duality rule stated in the first step of the example.

C=W(h1)3
Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The ordinary derivative of the cube is 3 times the square; the direction remains h₁.

Hint 2

In ⟨DC, h₁⟩, the only random part is ξ₁²: take the constants outside the expectation.

Hint 3

C W(h₁) = ξ₁ times ξ₁ cubed: count the factors ξ₁ and read the given moment of order 4.

Compare with the solution

DC = 3 ξ1² h1, the ordinary derivative of the cube attached to h1. Thus ⟨DC, h1⟩ = 3 ξ1² ‖h1‖² and its expectation is 3 × 1 × 1 = 3.

On the other side, C W(h1) = ξ1 times ξ1 cubed, giving ξ1 to the power 4, whose expectation is the moment of order 4: 3. The two expectations are 3 and 3: they are equal.

Limit: this is one case, with moments supplied in the problem, rather than a proof of the rule E⟨DF, h⟩ = E[F W(h)]. The divergence, the subject of the next unit, relies on this rule; it is not taught here.

Check your reasoning

  • The derivative of the cube is written with its direction h₁.
  • The two expectations are calculated separately before being compared.
  • The answer states that checking one case does not prove the general rule.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1What is the Malliavin derivative of W(h), the noise read in the direction h?

It is h itself: f is just the first variable, its partial derivative is 1, and 1 times h gives h. The result is deterministic.

Recall 2What kind of object is DF for a simple functional F?

An H-valued random variable: here a random function of time, piecewise constant over the two intervals, rather than a number.

Recall 3Why does ‖DF‖² equal d₁² + d₂² in the example?

Because h₁ and h₂ are orthogonal, so the cross term vanishes, and each has squared norm 1. Without orthogonality, cross terms would appear.

Recall 4What does ⟨DF, h⟩ measure, and how can we check it without the formula?

The sensitivity of F to a shift of the noise in the direction h. We check it using a centered difference quotient of F; for a polynomial of degree two, it is exact for any step size.

Recall 5What does the duality rule say, and to which objects does our calculation apply?

E⟨DF, h⟩ = E[F W(h)], checked here in one case, without proof. The calculation applies to simple functionals that are polynomials in finitely many noise readings W(h₁), …, W(hₙ); anything beyond this requires an extension not covered here.

Reconstruct the reasoning

Reconstruct without looking: define H, the noise W, and its distributional assumptions; write F as a function of a few noise readings W(h₁), …, W(hₙ); take the partial derivatives of f; attach each derivative to its direction; evaluate at the sample and read DF as a function of time; calculate its norm in H and its dot product with a direction; check using a centered difference quotient; state the domain and the duality rule without proving them.
