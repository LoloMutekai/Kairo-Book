Level 3 · C102·M4

Backpropagation

Your goalCompute the gradient of a scalar loss by working backwards through a graph, then correctly add the contributions to a shared parameter.

Before you start
c057-m5 : Hessian · c100-m5 : learning curves · c059-m6 : deterministic discrete Bellman · c015-m5 : diagnosis · c102-m1 : matrix chain rule · c102-m2 : computational graph · c102-m3 : automatic differentiation

✿01 See the idea

In a workshop, a small model estimates the water needed for a cleaning task. It includes a component proportional to activity, then a fixed component using the same setting w, and finally an offset b. The data and model are fictional: they let us follow a calculation and do not claim to describe an actual installation.

The activity x is 3, with no unit. The shared setting w is 2 L and the offset b is 1 L. The prediction p reaches 9 L; the reference quantity y is 7 L. We will measure the difference, then ask: how strongly does each setting affect the loss?

In the forward pass, each operation produces a value. In the backward pass, it receives the sensitivity of the loss to its output and passes it to its inputs using its local derivatives. This is backpropagation: an organized application of the chain rule, in the reverse order of the value calculations.

The difficult point is sharing. The same w acts through both the multiplication and the fixed addition. Changing w changes both branches together. Its total sensitivity must therefore combine their contributions. The lines in the diagrams show dependencies; their positions do not represent any physical scale.

The rule to remember

Compute and store the forward values; initialize the sensitivities; work backwards through the operations in reverse order; multiply along each branch and add wherever branches meet.

✿02 Follow an example

Worked example

Work backwards from the loss of the cleaning model

Keep x = 3, w = 2 L, b = 1 L and y = 7 L. The model is p = wx + w + b; the loss ℒ is (p − y) squared divided by 2. We seek the derivatives with respect to the settings w and b. We will also compute the sensitivity to x, solely to understand the path; x and y remain fixed data.

  1. Define the symbols, units and assumptions

    x is a unitless activity index. w is a coefficient in liters, shared between the variable component wx and the fixed component w. b is an offset in liters. p is the predicted quantity, y the reference and e = p − y the signed residual, all in liters. ℒ is a loss in L²: we penalize the squared difference and divide it by 2 to simplify its derivative.

    p=wx+w+b;ℒ=(p−y)22

    a = wx and s = a + w name the intermediate results, in liters. The graph is finite, has no loops, and all its operations are differentiable at the values considered. No rounding, threshold or positivity constraint is imposed during differentiation here. The parameters and data remain unchanged between the forward and backward passes.

    For any variable v in the graph, v̄ denotes ∂ℒ/∂v: the bar indicates a loss sensitivity, not an average. ∂ is the partial derivative symbol: the other independent inputs remain fixed. The unit of v̄ is L² divided by the unit of v. Thus w̄ and b̄ are in L, x̄ in L² and ℒ̄ is unitless. The gradient we seek is the pair (w̄, b̄).

  2. Run the forward pass and store its results

    Multiply the coefficient by the activity: a = 2 × 3 = 6 L. Add the fixed component that shares w: s = 6 + 2 = 8 L. The offset gives p = 8 + 1 = 9 L.

    Subtract the reference to find the direction of the model’s error: e = 9 − 7 = 2 L. Its square is 2 × 2 = 4 L²; the loss is 4/2 = 2 L². Its value alone does not yet tell us how each parameter affects it.

    Store these values: the backward pass uses the derivatives of the operations at this same calculation point. A new value of w would require a new forward pass.

  3. Initialize, then apply a single rule

    Initialize all sensitivities to 0, then ℒ̄ to 1, since the derivative of the loss with respect to itself is 1. This initial sensitivity is called the seed of the backward pass. For each operation with output v and an input u, add the following contribution to ū:

    u¯←u¯+v¯∂v∂u

    Here u and v are scalar variables linked by an operation; the local derivative ∂v/∂u concerns only that operation, with its other inputs fixed. Process the operations in the reverse order of their dependencies: all operations using v must have contributed to v̄ before v propagates this sum to its inputs. Contributions accumulate in the same location when a parameter is shared.

    This is reverse mode automatic differentiation applied to a scalar loss. The pass stores the useful forward values and propagates local derivatives; it does not symbolically build the entire expanded expression or numerically perturb each parameter.

  4. Start from the loss and reach the prediction

    For the loss equal to e squared divided by 2, the local derivative is (2e)/2 = e. The factor from the square and the divisor cancel. Thus ē = ℒ̄ × e = 1 × 2 = 2 L.

    ∂ℒ∂e=e;p¯=e¯×1=2 L

    In e = p − y, the local derivative with respect to p is 1, giving p̄ = 2 L. y remains a fixed reference: no update to y is requested. The information flowing backwards is signed; the positive squared loss does not require a positive gradient in every case.

  5. Pass through the additions and the product

    In p = s + b, the local derivatives with respect to s and b are 1. Thus s̄ = 2 × 1 = 2 L and b̄ = 2 × 1 = 2 L. Addition passes the sensitivity to each of its inputs; it does not divide it into equal shares.

    In s = a + w, we obtain ā = 2 × 1 = 2 L and a direct contribution to w̄ of 2 L. This contribution must remain in memory while the product is processed.

    In a = wx, the local derivatives are ∂a/∂w = x, which is unitless, and ∂a/∂x = w, in L. The product path therefore contributes 2 × 3 = 6 L to w̄. For x, it gives x̄ = 2 × 2 = 4 L². Multiplying by w instead of x when computing w̄ would confuse the two inputs and their units.

  6. Combine the branches and interpret the gradient

    w is a single independent variable. Its contributions add up: w̄ = 2 + 6 = 8 L. The parameter gradient, in the order (w, b), is therefore (8 L, 2 L). The result 6 L alone would count only the variable branch.

    ∂ℒ∂w=e(x+1)=8 L

    The gradient describes a local change: for small changes δw and δb, the approximation is δℒ ≈ w̄δw + b̄δb. Here δ means “small change”, with the unit of the corresponding variable; each product is in L². Positive sensitivities indicate that increasing either parameter on its own locally increases the loss. They are neither an amount of water to remove directly nor a training rule.

    Backpropagation computes these derivatives. Choosing a step size and changing the parameters are then tasks for an optimization algorithm; here we keep w and b at their initial values.

  7. Check using an independent expression

    Combine the branches by hand: p = (x + 1)w + b, with x + 1 = 4. With x and y fixed, the loss is (4w + b − 7 L) squared divided by 2. Its direct derivative with respect to w is (4w + b − 7 L) × 4 = 2 × 4 = 8 L. With respect to b, it is 2 L. We recover the gradient from the backward pass without following its intermediate operations.

    This check specifically tests the combination of branches. The units also agree: a derivative of L² with respect to a parameter in L is in L. Finally, if the reference were exactly the prediction 9 L, the residual would be 0 L, the loss 0 L² and w̄ would be 0 L. This case checks that the derivative vanishes at a 0 residual, without proving the model’s quality on other cleaning tasks.

Explain it in your own words

✿03 Common mistakes

  • “I have a contribution to w; I can replace the old one.”

    The parameter is shared: its two uses change together. Keep the direct contribution 2 L, then add 6 L from the product. Replacing it would lose a branch. Complete all downstream paths before propagating a sensitivity upstream.

    Reread how the paths combine in the second diagram

  • “The derivative of a with respect to w is already the loss gradient.”

    ∂a/∂w = x concerns only the product. You still need to multiply by ā, then include the other path to w. Always state which output is being differentiated: a or ℒ. Here the units distinguish the unitless local derivative from the gradient in L.

    Review the propagation rule and the product

  • “As soon as I know b̄, I change b and continue the backward pass.”

    The backward pass must use the same state as the forward pass. Changing a parameter while working backwards mixes several calculation points. Finish and check the entire gradient; any subsequent change then requires a new forward pass and reinitialized sensitivities.

    Test reinitialization in the last exercise

Exercise 1 · Local application

Pass from the output to the inputs

In the initial model, p̄ = 2 L and ā = 2 L. Find b̄ and x̄, naming the local derivative used and the unit of the result. Should p̄ be divided between s and b?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

For b, reread p = s + b with s fixed. For x, reread a = wx with w fixed.

Hint 2

A contribution is the received sensitivity multiplied by the local derivative; addition has derivative 1 with respect to each of its inputs.

Compare with the solution

∂p/∂b = 1, so b̄ = 2 × 1 = 2 L. Changing b alone by a small volume changes p by the same volume; no division between the inputs occurs.

∂a/∂x = w = 2 L. Thus x̄ = 2 × 2 = 4 L². Since x is unitless, differentiating the loss with respect to x retains the unit L². Being able to compute this sensitivity does not turn x into a parameter to train.

Check your reasoning

  • Name the derivative of the addition and that of the product.
  • Obtain b̄ = 2 L and x̄ = 4 L² with their units.
  • Explain why addition does not divide the received sensitivity.

Exercise 2 · Reconstruction on paper

Diagnose a forgotten path

Someone computes w̄ = āx = 6 L and declares the calculation finished. Without copying the solution, reconstruct the full sensitivity to w using the graph, then check it by combining wx + w. Explain why two independent parameters would not describe the same model.

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

Identify every occurrence of w in p = wx + w + b.

Hint 2

Rewrite p as (x + 1)w + b; if w changes, both occurrences change together.

Compare with the solution

The product contributes 6 L, but s = a + w also contributes 2 L directly. Their sum is 8 L. The rule must accumulate these values on the same parameter.

Combining the terms gives ∂p/∂w = x + 1 = 4. Thus ∂ℒ/∂w = e × 4 = 8 L. This alternative expression reveals exactly the missing contribution.

If the occurrences were replaced by independent parameters u and v, each in liters, in p = ux + v + b, we would obtain two separate derivatives. Then imposing u = v = w requires adding their contributions to recover the derivative of the shared parameter; changing only u does not represent the change to w requested here.

Check your reasoning

  • Draw and count the two uses of the same parameter.
  • Add 6 L and 2 L.
  • Recover 8 L using the combined expression and distinguish sharing from independence.

Exercise 3 · Transfer and diagnosis

A new reference, a new backward pass

For the same cleaning task and the same x, w and b, replace only the reference with y = 10 L. Predict the sign of the gradient before calculating. Recompute the residual, the loss and the gradient (w̄, b̄). What would w̄ be if the program kept its old value 8 L instead of starting again from 0?

Rate your confidence before comparing

Assisted answer: try this exercise without help later.

Hint 1

The prediction p remains 9 L: the reference enters only in the final subtraction.

Hint 2

The new e is negative. Multiply it by the same local derivatives, then combine the branches.

Hint 3

Any intended accumulation across several examples must be stated. Here, the requested gradient is for the new example alone.

Compare with the solution

The prediction remains 9 L. The residual becomes e = 9 − 10 = -1 L. Its square is 1 L² and the loss is 1/2 L², or 0.50 L². The loss is positive despite a negative residual.

The seed remains 1 and the sensitivities start again from 0. The backward pass gives p̄ = s̄ = ā = -1 L. Towards w, the contributions are -3 L through the product and -1 L directly: w̄ = -4 L. For b, b̄ = -1 L. These signs make sense: slightly increasing either parameter brings the prediction closer to the reference here.

Keeping the old sensitivity, the program would display 8 + (-4) = 4 L. This would be the sum of two gradients, rather than the one requested. It could suit an objective explicitly defined as a sum of losses; no such sum is requested in this exercise.

✿05 Recall from memory

Close the lesson, answer each question, then reveal the answer. Explain any missing steps in your own words.

Recall 1In what order is backpropagation performed, and why?

After the forward pass, initialize the sensitivities to 0 and the loss sensitivity to 1. Process the operations in reverse dependency order so that each output has received all its contributions before passing them to its inputs.

Recall 2What distinguishes the local derivative ∂a/∂w from the sensitivity w̄?

The first describes only the product a = wx, with its other inputs fixed. w̄ = ∂ℒ/∂w describes the final loss: you must propagate the received sensitivity and add the contributions from all uses of w.

Recall 3Why do the two branches of our w add up?

w is a single independent variable: its two occurrences change together. The product contributes 6 L and the direct use contributes 2 L; the total derivative is 8 L.

Recall 4Why store the forward values and reinitialize the sensitivities?

The local derivatives must correspond to the same calculation point. Keeping an old gradient or changing a parameter during the backward pass mixes calculations. Accumulation across different losses must be an explicit objective.

Recall 5What does the backward pass produce, and what decision remains afterwards?

It produces the derivatives of the loss at the current point. It chooses neither the step size for changing the parameters nor a training procedure. A computed gradient does not demonstrate better predictions on other examples.
